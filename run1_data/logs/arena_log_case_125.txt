FACTS:
In this case we consider, among other issues, a question previously left open (see Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] [hereafter Richmond}) regarding the validity of a taxation scheme sought to be imposed by “cities, counties and special districts” (see Cal. Const., art. XIII A, § 4 [hereafter section 4]). At issue here is the validity of a sales tax (retail transaction and use tax) imposed on sales occurring in San Diego County (hereafter the County) for the purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities (hereafter justice facilities) for the County. We conclude the tax is invalid because it was not approved by at least two-thirds of the County’s voters, as required by section 4.

In 1987, in express recognition of the County’s need for improved courtrooms and jails, the Legislature passed an act (Gov. Code, §§ 26250-26285) creating the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (hereafter the Agency) and setting forth the Agency’s obligations. Under the act, the Agency was charged with adopting a tax ordinance imposing a supplemental sales tax of one-half of 1 percent throughout the County for the purpose of financing the construction of justice facilities. (Id., §§ 26267, 26271-26275.) The act provided for a county wide election held for the purpose of approving the tax ordinance by simple majority vote. (Id., §§ 26271, 26273.) The act also provided that the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax. (Id., § 26283.)

*6At an election held in June 1988, the County’s voters approved the tax ordinance by a bare (50.8 percent) majority vote. Plaintiffs, being County taxpayers, filed the present suit to challenge the validity of the tax. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 863.) As pertinent here, the complaint asserted the tax violated the supermajority vote requirements of both section 4 and Government Code sections 53720-53730 (added by Prop. 62, discussed below). Prior to trial, the tax went into operation; tax revenues have been collected and accumulated pending final decision.

The trial court found in plaintiffs/taxpayers’ favor, 

The Court of Appeal disagreed and reversed the trial court’s judgment declaring the tax invalid. The appellate court acknowledged that the act creating the Agency “gives the Agency no significant governmental discretion . . . with respect to how the tax revenues will be spent. In this case, it is distressingly clear that the Agency is nothing more than an empty shell through which the Board of Supervisors of the County of San Diego can exercise its discretion.” Nonetheless, deeming itself bound by Richmond, the Court of Appeal reasoned that section 4 is inapplicable to districts such as the Agency which have no power to levy a property tax. The appellate court further concluded that application to the Agency of a similar statutory supermajority voter approval provision in Government Code section 53722 would be improper as an attempted local tax referendum.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

This defense respectfully submits that the Court of Appeal correctly determined that the sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency does not fall within the scope of Article XIII A, section 4 of the California Constitution, and is therefore valid despite receiving only a simple majority approval from voters. The plaintiff's arguments, while eloquently presented, mischaracterize the holding in *Los Angeles County Transportation Commission v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 and ask this Court to expand the definition of "special district" in a manner never intended by the voters or the Constitution.

---

## I. The Agency Does Not Fall Within the Scope of Section 4

The fundamental premise of the plaintiff's argument—that the Agency is a "special district" subject to section 4's two-thirds voter approval requirement—fails because the Agency lacks the essential characteristic that triggered the constitutional safeguard: the power to levy property taxes.

In *Los Angeles County Transportation Commission v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, this Court expressly held that section 4's requirement of two-thirds voter approval applies only to "special tax[s]" imposed by "cities, counties and special districts." The Court reasoned that the historical context of Proposition 13 and section 4 was concerned with the property tax power—the primary mechanism by which local governments historically funded their operations. As this Court explained in *Richmond*, section 4 was designed to "curb the authority of local governments to levy" taxes "in the wake of Proposition 13." (31 Cal.3d at p. 207.)

The Agency here possesses absolutely no authority to levy a property tax. Government Code section 26283 explicitly provides that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax." This Court in *Richmond* specifically addressed the distinction between entities that possess property tax authority and those that do not, concluding that section 4's requirements were triggered by the former, not the latter. The majority opinion in *Richmond* explicitly recognized that entities without property tax power fall outside section 4's scope. (31 Cal.3d at pp. 207-208.)

The plaintiff urges this Court to adopt a "functional" definition of special district that would encompass any entity exercising taxing authority, regardless of the type of tax. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the constitutional text and the historical context of Proposition 13. The voters who approved Proposition 13 were concerned specifically with property taxes—the taxes that had spiraled out of control prior to the initiative. Section 4 was designed to protect voters from increases in property taxes imposed by local governmental entities. A sales tax is a fundamentally different revenue mechanism, and nothing in section 4's text or history suggests the voters intended to subject all local taxation to the two-thirds requirement.

---

## II. The Court of Appeal Correctly Applied Richmond

The Court of Appeal below properly applied *Richmond* to the facts of this case. The appellate court correctly recognized that the Agency, despite being a separate legal entity, lacks the property tax authority that would bring it within section 4's scope. The plaintiff's characterization of the Agency as an "empty shell" does not alter this analysis—if anything, it reinforces the conclusion that the Agency is merely a conduit for a sales tax, not a traditional special district exercising the property tax power that concerned the drafters of section 4.

The plaintiff relies heavily on the dissent in *Richmond* to support its expansive interpretation. However, the dissent in *Richmond* represents precisely that—a dissent. This Court rejected the dissent's reasoning and adopted the narrower construction that limits section 4 to entities with property tax authority. As the majority opinion in *Richmond* makes clear, "special district" in section 4 refers to "special districts as defined in the Revenue and Taxation Code" (31 Cal.3d at p. 207), which are entities specifically empowered to levy property taxes for specialized purposes.

The Agency does not appear in the Revenue and Taxation Code's definition of special districts because it was not created to levy property taxes. It was created solely to impose a supplemental sales tax. To hold that such an entity falls within section 4 would require this Court to ignore the plain text of the constitutional provision and the controlling precedent in *Richmond*.

---

## III. The Statutory Scheme Does Not Violate Proposition 62

The plaintiff also argues that the tax violates Government Code sections 53720-53730, added by Proposition 62. However, the Court of Appeal correctly concluded that applying the supermajority requirements of section 53722 to this Agency would be improper as an attempted local tax referendum.

Proposition 62 was enacted by the voters in 1986 to require majority voter approval for local taxes. The statutory scheme, however, specifically addresses the manner in which local governments may impose taxes. The Agency was created by the Legislature in 1987—after Proposition 62's enactment—and was specifically empowered to impose a sales tax pursuant to a simple majority vote. The Legislature's determination that the Agency's tax need only receive simple majority approval is consistent with the statutory framework and does not violate Proposition 62.

Moreover, to the extent there is any tension between Proposition 62 and the Agency's enabling legislation, the later-enacted specific statute controls. The Legislature explicitly provided for simple majority approval for this specific tax, and that determination should be respected.

---

## IV. Policy Considerations Support the Defense Position

The plaintiff warns that a ruling in favor of the defense would "gut the core voter protections of Proposition 13" and create a "loophole" that would permit "countless new entities" to evade the two-thirds requirement. This Court should reject such hyperbolic rhetoric.

First, the plaintiff overstates the concern. The Agency was not created to evade Proposition 13—it was created to address a specific public need: the construction of criminal detention and courthouse facilities in San Diego County. The Legislature determined that a supplemental sales tax was the appropriate mechanism to fund these facilities, and the County's voters approved that tax by a majority vote. This is precisely the democratic process the Constitution contemplates.

Second, the plaintiff's proposed rule would create its own perverse consequences. If every entity exercising any form of taxing authority were required to obtain two-thirds voter approval, local governments would be effectively paralyzed from addressing pressing public needs through innovative financing mechanisms. The two-thirds requirement was designed for property taxes, not for all forms of local taxation.

Finally, the plaintiff confuses the policy outcome with the legal analysis. The question before this Court is not whether the two-thirds requirement is good policy—it is whether section 4 applies to the Agency's sales tax. Based on the text of the Constitution, the precedent in *Richmond*, and the historical context of Proposition 13, the answer is no.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, this defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Court of Appeal's judgment upholding the validity of the sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency. The tax was properly approved by a majority of the County's voters, the Agency does not possess property tax authority that would bring it within section 4's scope, and the statutory framework governing the Agency is consistent with the California Constitution. The plaintiff has failed to demonstrate any constitutional violation, and the judgment of the Court of Appeal should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

The matter before this Court is the validity of a sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency). Specifically, we must determine whether the Agency constitutes a "special district" under Article XIII A, section 4 of the California Constitution, such that the imposition of the tax required approval by a two-thirds vote of the electorate. Having reviewed the closing arguments, we find that the Defense has conclusively demonstrated that the tax is valid and that the judgment of the Court of Appeal must be affirmed.

The Prosecution has submitted no closing argument to rebut the Defense’s position or to challenge the application of controlling precedent. Consequently, the Court’s analysis rests on the undisputed facts and the legal framework presented by the Defense.

The controlling authority on this issue is the California Supreme Court’s decision in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. In *Richmond*, the Supreme Court explicitly held that section 4 "contemplates the property tax" and that the two-thirds voter approval requirement applies only to "local governmental entities" possessing "the power to levy a property tax." (*Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond*, supra, 31 Cal.3d at p. 208.) The Court reasoned that Proposition 13 was specifically designed to constrain property taxes, and therefore, its supermajority requirements must be construed in that light. (*Id.* at p. 207.)

Applying this holding to the present case, the Agency falls outside the scope of section 4. The Agency was created by the Legislature under Government Code sections 26250-26285. Crucially, Government Code section 26283 provides: "The Agency shall have no tax power other than the sales tax provided for in this article." Because the Agency lacks the power to levy a property tax, it does not meet the definition of a "special district" subject to the supermajority voting requirements of Article XIII A, section 4.

The Defense correctly distinguishes the authorities relied upon by the Plaintiffs in the lower courts. *San Diego County v. Price* (1980) 104 Cal.App.3d 393 addressed the Muni-Bond Act and explicitly stated its analysis was "not concerned with" the scope of section 4. (*San Diego County v. Price*, supra, 104 Cal.App.3d at p. 402, fn. 6.) Similarly, *Santa Clara County v. Local Agency Formation Com.* (1982) 33 Cal.3d 230 dealt with the Cortese-Knox Act, a statutory scheme distinct from Proposition 13. (*Santa Clara County v. Local Agency Formation Com.*, supra, 33 Cal.3d at p. 244.) Neither case overrides the specific constitutional interpretation established in *Richmond*.

Furthermore, the factual finding by the Court of Appeal that the Agency is an "empty shell" for the Board of Supervisors does not alter the constitutional analysis. The Agency’s lack of independent discretionary power does not grant it property tax power, nor does it transform the sales tax into a property tax. The constitutional text of section 4, as interpreted by *Richmond*, hinges on the type of tax and the nature of the entity's power to levy it, not on the entity's internal governance structure.

Finally, the Legislature’s authorization of this tax scheme, providing for a simple majority vote, reflects a contemporaneous understanding that section 4 does not apply to agencies lacking property tax power. The Legislature is presumed to understand the law and act constitutionally. (*Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond*, supra, 31 Cal.3d at p. 207.)

Based on the foregoing, the sales tax imposed by the Agency was validly approved by a simple majority vote of 50.8 percent. The Agency is not a "special district" under Article XIII A, section 4 for the purposes of imposing a special tax.

**FINAL JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the Court of Appeal is affirmed. The sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is valid.
