# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Procedural Posture

The defense respectfully submits this opening response to address the prosecution's arguments regarding the admissibility of the NPLEX (National Precursor Log Exchange) records. The defense maintains that the trial court erred in admitting these records into evidence, as they constitute testimonial statements in violation of Ms. Kittle's Sixth Amendment right to confrontation. The prosecution's arguments regarding business records and proper foundation are without merit, and this Court should reverse the trial court's decision.

## Factual Context

The facts establish that Ms. Kittle was charged based upon her alleged involvement in methamphetamine production. Officer Lemonier testified that he conducted surveillance on an apartment, observed Ms. Kittle and J.S. leave together, performed a traffic stop, discovered methamphetamine on J.S., searched the apartment (where they found drug manufacturing materials), and then later interviewed Ms. Kittle. During the interview, Ms. Kittle admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.

The NPLEX records at issue presumably document pseudoephedrine purchases—information that the prosecution seeks to use to establish that Ms. Kittle purchased the precursor chemicals used in the methamphetamine manufacturing operation. These records were admitted over the defense's Confrontation Clause objection.

## Legal Analysis

### I. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial and Trigger the Confrontation Clause

The prosecution incorrectly argues that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial business records falling outside the scope of the Confrontation Clause. This characterization is fundamentally flawed.

In Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S. Ct. 1354, 158 L. Ed. 2d 177 (2004), the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause applies to testimonial statements of witnesses who are absent from trial. The Court explicitly left open the question of whether business records fall within the testimonial category, noting that "most [business] records are not testimonial." However, the Court emphasized that records created specifically for litigation purposes—rather than in the regular course of business—would be testimonial.

The NPLEX database represents a government-maintained system specifically designed to track pharmaceutical purchases for law enforcement purposes. These records are not mere routine business records created in the regular course of commercial transactions. Rather, they are compiled specifically for investigative and prosecutorial use. In Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), the Supreme Court held that forensic analysis certificates are testimonial because they are "functionally identical to the live, in-court testimony of a forensic analyst" and are "created specifically to serve as evidence at trial."

Similarly, the NPLEX records were created specifically to serve as evidence against individuals suspected of drug manufacturing. The database exists precisely because law enforcement uses it to track pseudoephedrine purchases to build cases against individuals involved in methamphetamine production. These records are the functional equivalent of a law enforcement officer's testimony about what they observed during surveillance—except that the records are prepared by database custodians who never testify and cannot be cross-examined.

The prosecution's reliance on the business records exception is misplaced. The NPLEX records are fundamentally different from traditional business records such as invoices, receipts, or payroll records. Those records are created for the primary purpose of conducting business, not for proving guilt in criminal proceedings. The NPLEX records, by contrast, exist specifically to assist law enforcement in identifying individuals who purchase precursor chemicals. As such, they are testimonial and implicate the Confrontation Clause.

### II. Officer Lemonier Was Not Qualified to Lay the Foundation for the NPLEX Records

Even assuming arguendo that the NPLEX records could be admitted as business records, the trial court erred in allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for their admission.

Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) requires that a qualified witness testify that the records were "(a) made at or near the time of the act, condition, or event, by—or from information transmitted by—a person with knowledge of those acts, conditions, or events, (b) kept in the regular course of that business, (c) made by that business at or near the time of the acts, conditions, or events, by a person with knowledge."

In State v. Frazier, 115 Ohio St. 3d 139, 2007-Ohio-5048, 873 N.E.2d 1233, the Ohio Supreme Court held that "the witness need not be the custodian of the records, but must have knowledge of the record-keeping practices." The prosecution cites this case for the proposition that Officer Lemonier was qualified, but this reading misapplies Frazier.

Officer Lemonier's testimony established that he was a police officer who conducted surveillance, performed a traffic stop, searched an apartment, and conducted an interview of Ms. Kittle. There is no indication in the record that Officer Lemonier had any knowledge of the NPLEX database's record-keeping practices, the procedures by which pharmacies report pseudoephedrine purchases, or the accuracy of the data maintained in that system. His role in the investigation does not qualify him to testify about the regularity of a pharmacy record-keeping system maintained by a private database company.

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier's role in the investigation and the "circumstances of the records' production" qualified him. This argument fails. The "circumstances of the records' production" that Officer Lemonier could testify about involve when and how he accessed the records—not how the records were created and maintained by pharmacies and the NPLEX system. These are distinct concepts. The foundational requirement under Evidence Rule 803(6) goes to the creation and maintenance of the records themselves, not to how law enforcement accesses them.

Without proper foundation from a witness with actual knowledge of the record-keeping practices, the NPLEX records were inadmissible.

### III. The Error Was Not Harmless

The erroneous admission of the NPLEX records cannot be considered harmless error. The core of the prosecution's case depended upon establishing that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine, the essential precursor chemical for methamphetamine manufacturing. Without the NPLEX records, the prosecution's evidence consisted primarily of Ms. Kittle's custodial statement—which itself was obtained after she was brought to the police station for questioning—and the officers' observations of the apartment and its contents.

While Ms. Kittle's statement acknowledged purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S., the NPLEX records provided independent, documentary proof of these purchases. This corroboration was critical to the prosecution's case. The admission of these records therefore affected Ms. Kittle's substantial rights and constituted reversible error.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's decision admitting the NPLEX records into evidence, find that the admission violated Ms. Kittle's Sixth Amendment right to confrontation, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The prosecution's arguments regarding the business records exception and Officer Lemonier's qualification to lay foundation are without merit. The NPLEX records are testimonial statements created specifically for use in criminal prosecutions, and their admission without opportunity for cross-examination of the database custodians violated fundamental constitutional protections.

*Defense Counsel*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Based on the review of the prosecution and defense opening arguments, the Court finds that the defense’s arguments regarding the admissibility of the NPLEX records are more persuasive. Specifically, the defense has demonstrated that the trial court likely abused its discretion in admitting the records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) due to a lack of proper foundation, and that the records raise significant Confrontation Clause concerns under *Crawford v. **ANALYSIS**

**I. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting the NPLEX Records Under Ohio Evid.R. 803(6)**

The admission of evidence is subject to the trial court's discretion, and an appellate court will not reverse absent an abuse of that discretion. See *State v. Hymore*, 9 Ohio St. 3d 122, 459 N.E.2d 514 (1984). However, for a business record to be admissible under Ohio Evid.R. 803(6), the proponent must establish a foundation through the testimony of a qualified witness that the record was made at or near the time of the event by a person with knowledge, and that it was kept in the regular course of business.

The prosecution relies on *State v. Frazier*, 115 Ohio St. 3d 139, 2007-Ohio-5048, 873 N.E.2d 1233, to argue that Officer Lemonier was a qualified witness. While *Frazier* correctly states that the witness need not be the custodian, it requires that the witness have "knowledge of the record-keeping practices." The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier’s role in the investigation provided him with this knowledge. The defense correctly identifies the logical flaw in this assertion. Officer Lemonier’s testimony, as described in the facts, pertains to surveillance, a traffic stop, and a custodial interview. There is no indication that Officer Lemonier possesses knowledge regarding how the NPLEX database is maintained, how pharmacies input data, or the internal procedures of the database administrators. An officer's ability to *retrieve* a record is distinct from having knowledge of the *record-keeping practices* required to authenticate it. Therefore, the prosecution failed to meet the standard set forth in *State v. Frazier*, and the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the records without a proper foundation.

**II. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial and Subject to the Confrontation Clause**

The Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause guarantees a defendant the right to confront witnesses against them. This right applies only to "testimonial" statements. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S. Ct. 1354, 158 L. Ed. 2d 177 (2004). The prosecution argues that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial business records. However, the defense provides a compelling argument that these records fall outside the traditional business records exception because they are created specifically for law enforcement purposes.

In *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), the Supreme Court held that forensic certificates are testimonial because they are "created specifically to serve as evidence at trial." The defense argues that NPLEX records are analogous, as they are maintained by a government system designed to track precursor chemicals for the express purpose of criminal investigations. Unlike a standard receipt or invoice generated in the regular course of commerce, the NPLEX records are compiled to assist law enforcement in identifying suspects. Consequently, the defense correctly argues that these records are "functionally identical to the live, in-court testimony" of the individuals who compiled the data. The prosecution’s blanket assertion that they are "routine business records" fails to address the specific nature of the NPLEX system as highlighted by the defense.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense has successfully demonstrated that the prosecution failed to lay an adequate foundation for the NPLEX records under Ohio Evid.R. 803(6) and *State v. Frazier*. Furthermore, the defense has raised a substantial constitutional claim that the records are testimonial under *Crawford* and *Melendez-Diaz*. Therefore, the preliminary ruling is that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records, and the conviction should be reversed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. Officer Lemonier Lacked the Requisite Knowledge of Record-Keeping Practices to Establish Foundation Under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6)

The prosecution's rebuttal conflates two distinct concepts: (1) knowledge of what a record contains, and (2) knowledge of how and when the record was made in the regular course of business. This distinction is fatal to the prosecution's foundation argument.

In *State v. Frazier*, 115 Ohio St. 3d 139, 2007-Ohio-5048, 873 N.E.2d 1233 (2007), this Court emphasized that a qualified witness must have "knowledge of the record-keeping practices" of the business creating the records. The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier's role as investigating officer provided him with this knowledge. However, the record, as stipulated in the facts, demonstrates otherwise.

Officer Lemonier's testimony, as described in the case facts, established his involvement in surveillance, a traffic stop, and a custodial interview of Ms. Kittle. There is no evidence in the record that Officer Lemonier ever testified regarding:
- The procedures by which pharmacies input data into the NPLEX system
- The time frame in which individual purchase records are created
- The internal protocols of the database administrators
- The regular practice of participating pharmacies in maintaining these records

The prosecution claims Officer Lemonier "confirmed that NPLEX operates as a system for tracking pseudoephedrine sales in compliance with federal and state regulatory requirements." This characterization stretches the record beyond what the facts establish. Even if Officer Lemonier could testify about the general purpose of NPLEX, this does not establish the foundational requirements of Rule 803(6), which require testimony that the specific records at issue were "made at or near the time of the act, condition, or event" by "the person with knowledge" of the act, and that "it was the regular practice of that business enterprise to make the memorandum."

As this Court noted in *State v. Hymore*, 9 Ohio St. 3d 122, 459 N.E.2d 514 (1984), the admission of evidence is subject to the trial court's discretion, but that discretion must be exercised within the bounds of the Evidence Rules. The prosecution's reliance on *Frazier* is misplaced because that case involved a hospital records custodian who personally knew the hospital's record-keeping practices. Here, Officer Lemonier's "knowledge" derives from his role as a law enforcement officer retrieving records during an investigation—a fundamentally different scenario.

The prosecution's argument that retrieving a record provides the necessary foundation confuses the ability to produce a document with the ability to authenticate its creation. The defense does not dispute that Officer Lemonier could lay a foundation for how the records were retrieved; rather, the defense correctly argues that foundation requires testimony about how the records were *made*, not how they were *obtained*.

## II. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial and Violate the Confrontation Clause

The prosecution's characterization of NPLEX records as "non-testimonial business records" fails to account for the unique nature of this database and the specific purpose for which its data is compiled.

### A. The Primary Purpose of the NPLEX Records Is Law Enforcement, Not Business

The prosecution relies on *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), for the proposition that records created for business purposes are not testimonial. However, the prosecution overlooks a critical distinction: the *primary purpose* of the NPLEX system must be examined, not merely the general nature of the database.

In *Melendez-Diaz*, the Supreme Court explained that forensic certificates are testimonial because they are "created specifically to serve as evidence at trial." The prosecution argues that NPLEX was established under the Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act to facilitate "regulatory compliance by pharmacies." This characterization is incomplete. While the statutory framework may impose compliance requirements on pharmacies, the *primary purpose* of the database from a functional standpoint is to provide law enforcement with a tool to identify individuals who purchase pseudoephedrine in quantities suggesting methamphetamine production.

The NPLEX system is not analogous to a routine sales receipt or invoice generated in the ordinary course of commerce. Unlike standard business records, NPLEX records are compiled specifically to track the purchase of precursor chemicals—chemicals that have no legitimate purpose other than the production of illegal drugs. The database exists because law enforcement needs to monitor these specific purchases. The records are created not merely to document a commercial transaction, but to create a tool for criminal investigation.

### B. The NPLEX Records Are Functionally Identical to the Certificates at Issue in Melendez-Diaz

The prosecution cites *Palmer v. Hoffman*, 318 U.S. 109, 63 S. Ct. 477, 87 L. Ed. 645 (1943), for the proposition that records created for business purposes remain non-testimonial even if later used in litigation. However, *Palmer* is distinguishable. In *Palmer*, the records at issue were accident reports created by a railroad for its own business purposes—specifically, to evaluate claims and improve safety. The fact that these records later became relevant to litigation was incidental to their primary business purpose.

The NPLEX records are fundamentally different. They are created specifically to track the purchase of regulated chemicals that can be used to manufacture illegal drugs. The very existence of the database is a response to law enforcement concerns about methamphetamine production. The records do not document routine commercial activity; they document purchases that trigger automatic reporting because of their potential connection to criminal activity.

As the defense argued in opening, these records are "functionally identical to the live, in-court testimony" of the individuals who compiled the data—namely, the pharmacy employees who input the purchase information and the database administrators who maintain the system. These individuals are witnesses against Ms. Kittle, and she has not had the opportunity to confront them.

### C. The Prosecution's Argument That NPLEX Is Primarily Regulatory Is Contradicted by Its Use in This Case

The prosecution argues that the "primary purpose of the system is regulatory, not testimonial." However, the use of NPLEX records in this prosecution directly contradicts this assertion. The records were not introduced to demonstrate regulatory compliance; they were introduced to prove that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine, which she allegedly used to obtain methamphetamine from J.S. The records were introduced as evidence of her criminal conduct.

This case illustrates the precise concern articulated in *Melendez-Diaz*: when records are "created specifically to serve as evidence at trial," they are testimonial regardless of the label attached to them. The NPLEX records were created to track pseudoephedrine purchases precisely because such purchases are relevant to criminal investigations. Their use in this prosecution confirms their testimonial character.

## III. The Cumulative Error Requires Reversal

Even if either foundation or the Confrontation Clause issue alone might not require reversal, the combination of both errors requires the conviction to be overturned. The NPLEX records were the only evidence directly connecting Ms. Kittle to the methamphetamine manufacturing operation. Without these records, the prosecution's case rests on Ms. Kittle's custodial statement, the validity of which raises separate concerns not addressed here.

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the NPLEX records satisfy both the foundational requirements of Rule 803(6) and the constitutional requirements of the Confrontation Clause. The prosecution has failed to meet this burden.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the preliminary ruling and reverse the conviction. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting the NPLEX records without proper foundation, and the admission of these testimonial records violated Ms. Kittle's Sixth Amendment confrontation rights. The prosecution's rebuttal fails to address the fundamental deficiencies in its case, and the conviction must be reversed.

**FINAL RULING**

After reviewing the closing arguments from both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting the NPLEX records. The prosecution's arguments fail to demonstrate that the evidentiary foundation required by Ohio law was met, nor do they overcome the constitutional concerns regarding the Confrontation Clause.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting the NPLEX Records Under Ohio Evid.R. 803(6)**

The admissibility of business records under the hearsay exception is contingent upon the proponent establishing a proper foundation. Ohio Evid.R. 803(6) requires testimony from a witness who is qualified to testify that the record was made in the regular course of business, at or near the time of the event, by a person with knowledge, and that it was the regular practice of that business activity to make such a record. As established in *State v. Frazier*, 115 Ohio St. 3d 139, 2007-Ohio-5048, 873 N.E.2d 1233 (2007), the witness need not be the custodian of records but must possess "knowledge of the record-keeping practices" of the entity that created the records.

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier satisfied this requirement by testifying to the regulatory purpose of the NPLEX system and the manner in which pharmacies generate records. However, this argument is not supported by the facts provided in the case background. The provided facts indicate that Officer Lemonier's testimony was limited to his surveillance of the apartment, the traffic stop, the search of the apartment, and his custodial interview of Ms. Kittle. There is no indication in the provided facts that Officer Lemonier testified regarding the specific procedures pharmacies use to input data into the NPLEX system, the timing of such entries, or the internal protocols ensuring the accuracy of those records.

The defense correctly identifies that the prosecution conflates the retrieval of records with the authentication of their creation. While Officer Lemonier may have obtained the records during his investigation, the provided facts do not show that he possessed the requisite knowledge of the pharmacy's record-keeping practices to lay the foundation for their admission under *State v. Frazier*. Because the prosecution failed to establish that the records were made in the regular course of business through a qualified witness, the trial court abused its discretion in admitting them.

**II. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial and Subject to the Confrontation Clause**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to be confronted with the witnesses against them, which applies to "testimonial" statements. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S. Ct. 1354, 158 L. Ed. 2d 177 (2004). The prosecution contends that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial business records created for regulatory compliance, relying on *Palmer v. Hoffman*, 318 U.S. 109, 63 S. Ct. 477, 87 L. Ed. 645 (1943). However, the defense's argument that these records are "functionally identical" to the forensic certificates at issue in *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), is persuasive.

In *Melendez-Diaz*, the Supreme Court held that documents "created specifically to serve as evidence at trial" are testimonial. The defense correctly argues that while the NPLEX system operates under a regulatory framework, its primary functional purpose is to assist law enforcement in tracking the purchase of precursor chemicals used in the manufacture of methamphetamine. Unlike the railroad accident reports in *Palmer*, which were created for internal business safety evaluations, NPLEX records are compiled specifically to identify potential criminal activity. The records document purchases that trigger automatic reporting precisely because of their potential connection to drug manufacturing.

The prosecution's reliance on the "regulatory" nature of the database cannot obscure the reality that the records were introduced in this case to prove Ms. Kittle's criminal conduct—specifically, her purchase of pseudoephedrine—rather than to demonstrate a pharmacy's internal business compliance. Because the records were created to serve as evidentiary tools for law enforcement, they are testimonial in nature. Consequently, their admission without the opportunity for cross-examination violated Ms. Kittle's rights under the Confrontation Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense has demonstrated that the prosecution failed to establish a proper foundation for the NPLEX records under Ohio Evid.R. 803(6) and *State v. Frazier*, as the witness lacked the requisite knowledge of the record-keeping practices. Furthermore, the defense has shown that the records are testimonial under *Crawford* and *Melendez-Diaz*, as their primary purpose is law enforcement tracking rather than routine business documentation. The prosecution's rebuttal does not provide sufficient evidence to overcome these deficiencies. Therefore, the preliminary ruling is affirmed: the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records, and the conviction is reversed.
