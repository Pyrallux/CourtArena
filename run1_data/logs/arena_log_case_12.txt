COURT ARENA LOG - Case 12
FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1

The letters to the editor, on the other hand, are more opinionated. They do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt. Some are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race. We refuse, however, to find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society. But see Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. These few letters do not represent a society scornful of outsiders, as counsel Reardon would have us believe. Instead, by attempting to influence the public, these letters affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence.
One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

Your Honor, the prosecution respectfully submits that defendants have utterly failed to meet the extraordinary burden required to justify a change of venue. As this Court correctly recognized in its thorough Order denying the motion, the legal standard for granting a change of venue based on pretrial publicity is exceptionally stringent—reserved only for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); *see also Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985) (requiring "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage"). Defendants have presented no evidence approaching this threshold.  

Crucially, defendants cite only six newspaper articles and editorials as evidence of prejudicial publicity—none of which this Court found "inflammatory." As the Order correctly noted, while some letters to the editor contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and "embarrassingly slanted" race-based remarks, these "few rogue letters do not represent the beliefs of an entire society." This Court rightly refused to attribute isolated opinions to the community at large, emphasizing that "by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all." This aligns with established precedent: isolated expressions of bias, without evidence of community-wide contamination, cannot establish presumed prejudice. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537 (finding that "a few newspaper articles expressing prejudice" do not suffice to warrant venue change).  

The Supreme Court has reversed convictions only once on presumed prejudice grounds—*Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963)—where a defendant’s televised, unconstitutionally obtained confession was broadcast *multiple times* to "tens of thousands" of community members. Here, the record contains no comparable "sensationalized" coverage. Defendants cite no evidence of saturation, no evidence of community-wide hysteria, and no evidence that jurors were exposed to prejudicial material beyond the six articles. As this Court observed, "a showing of presumed prejudice is rare" and requires "an atmosphere [that] belies any claim of impartiality." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272. The record before this Court simply lacks such an atmosphere.  

Defendants’ cultural bias argument is equally unavailing. While they claim "bigotry from a 'culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here,'" the record contains no evidence—let alone proof—that this alleged bias permeated the community or influenced potential jurors. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961), requires a showing that "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." Defendants offered no voir dire testimony, no polling data, and no objective evidence of systemic bias—only conclusory assertions. The law demands more. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (due process requires a "tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power"); *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994) (actual prejudice must be "discerned by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire"). Without voir dire, such a showing is impossible—and defendants did not even attempt it.  

Moreover, a change of venue is legally and practically unworkable in American Samoa. As this Court correctly held, "we have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll." Transferring the case within the Territory would "only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same." *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (noting venue change "unsuitable" in small jurisdictions like Puerto Rico). The statute defendants cite—*A.S.C.A. § 46.0601*—was enacted long before jury trials were constitutionally mandated in American Samoa and "simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought." It applies only to "physical" relocation for logistical convenience, not to address alleged prejudice.  

Finally, the remedy defendants seek is fundamentally misplaced. Due process does not require venue change—it requires a *fair trial*, which this Court can ensure through rigorous voir dire, as it has ordered. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (jury trials "have proven to be both practical and not anomalous in a relatively small island community"). Defendants’ request for venue change is an attempt to evade proper judicial process—not a valid legal claim.  

For these reasons, the prosecution submits that defendants have not—and cannot—meet the high bar for changing venue. This Court’s denial was legally sound and fully supported by the record. We urge the appellate court to affirm.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE APPELLATE COURT OF AMERICAN SAMOA

---

**DEFENDANTS-APPELLANTS' OPENING BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF APPEAL FROM DENIAL OF MOTION FOR CHANGE OF VENUE**

---

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in denying the motion for change of venue. While the prosecution correctly notes that the standard for venue change is stringent, the defense contends that the trial court applied an incorrectly high threshold, failed to properly weigh the cumulative effect of inflammatory publicity, and misinterpreted the statutory authority available for relief. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order and remand with instructions to grant the change of venue.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT PRESUMED PREJUDICE WAS NOT ESTABLISHED

The trial court correctly articulated the legal standard that presumed prejudice requires a showing that "the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). However, the trial court applied this standard in a manner that underweighted the cumulative effect of the publicity and the inherently prejudicial nature of the content published.

### A. The Published Material Went Beyond Objective Reporting

The prosecution characterizes the coverage as limited to "six newspaper articles and editorials" that were not "inflammatory." This characterization is misleading. The trial court acknowledged that "the letters to the editor, on the other hand, are more opinionated. They do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of some are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." The publication of such material—regardless of whether it represents the views of the entire community—constitutes the dissemination of prejudicial content into the community consciousness.

In Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1491 (11th Cir. 1985), the court recognized that presumed prejudice analysis must consider not merely the quantity of coverage but the nature and character of the published material. The letters containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" represent precisely the type of prejudicially charged content that pollutes the jury pool. The fact that these letters were published at all demonstrates editorial selection that amplified their reach and impact.

### B. The Trial Court Incorrectly Required Evidence of Community-Wide Hysteria

The prosecution argues that defendants failed to show "community-wide contamination." However, the case law does not require proof of universal community bias. In Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961), the Supreme Court held that actual prejudice is established when "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." The focus is on the reasonable likelihood of bias among prospective jurors, not on measuring the sentiment of the entire community.

The defense submitted evidence that the publicity was the product of bigotry from a culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here. While the trial court characterized this as an overgeneralization, the published letters themselves provide evidentiary support for this claim. When prospective jurors are exposed to published material declaring defendants guilty and attacking them based on race, the presumption of impartiality is undermined—not because every community member holds such views, but because enough prospective jurors may have encountered such material to render fair jury selection improbable.

### C. The Small Community Context Amplifies Prejudicial Effect

American Samoa presents unique circumstances that distinguish it from the jurisdictions typically addressed in venue jurisprudence. As the trial court noted, "our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll." This reality fundamentally alters the prejudice analysis.

In United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), the First Circuit recognized that in small jurisdictions, "alternative remedies" may be more appropriate than venue change because moving the trial would not cure the prejudice. However, this recognition cuts both ways: if venue change cannot cure prejudice in a small jurisdiction, then the constitutional guarantee of a fair trial requires that greater care be taken to prevent prejudice from occurring in the first place. The trial court failed to recognize that the impossibility of effective venue change imposes a heightened obligation to grant venue change when prejudice is demonstrated.

The defense submits that in a jurisdiction where "transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue" but "the jury would be selected from the same pool," the threshold for demonstrating presumed prejudice should be lower. When no effective alternative exists, the Constitution requires that the root cause—prejudicial publicity tainting the community—be addressed by removing the case from the contaminated venue entirely.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ITS INTERPRETATION OF A.S.C.A. § 46.0601

The trial court held that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 "simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants." The defense contends this interpretation is overly restrictive and contrary to the statute's plain language and purpose.

The statute provides: "In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa."

The trial court reasoned that because the statute was enacted in 1969, "long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were found to be neither 'impractical nor anomalous,'" it must have been intended solely for logistical purposes. However, statutory interpretation requires giving effect to the plain language. The statute explicitly authorizes relocation of court proceedings when "the interest of justice" requires. If prejudice in the community has tainted the jury pool such that a fair trial is impossible in any location within American Samoa, then the interest of justice clearly requires relocation outside the territory.

The trial court noted that it "cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times." This historical non-use does not limit the statute's applicability. The statute exists as a tool for ensuring justice, and it should be available when circumstances warrant its use.

Furthermore, the trial court's comparison to Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) is instructive. That rule provides that a court "shall transfer the proceeding" to another district "[w]henever the court finds that the fair and impartial trial cannot be held" in the district where the case is pending. The defense submits that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, properly interpreted, provides analogous authority within American Samoa's unique jurisdictional framework.

## III. THE TRIAL COURT'S PROPOSED REMEDY OF VOIR DIRE IS INADEQUATE

The trial court offered that defendants may submit a voir dire questionnaire and that "based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary."

While voir dire is a valuable tool for uncovering actual prejudice, it is insufficient to address presumed prejudice. In Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966), the Supreme Court recognized that due process requires a tribunal "free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power." The trial court's proposed remedy assumes that voir dire can identify and eliminate all biased jurors. This assumption is flawed for several reasons.

First, prospective jurors may be influenced by publicity without consciously realizing or admitting such influence. The human mind does not always accurately self-report the extent to which external information has shaped impressions. In Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d at 1543, the court noted that presumed prejudice exists precisely because the atmosphere created "belies any claim of impartiality"—meaning that even jurors who believe themselves impartial may in fact be biased.

Second, the trial court's proposed questionnaire is constrained by limitations: it "may include any inquiries about media exposure" but "shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets." These limitations may prevent the deep inquiry necessary to uncover subtle biases.

Third, in a small community where "the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest corners of the Territory," voir dire becomes an exercise in identifying which potential jurors have been exposed to prejudicial material rather than ensuring a truly impartial jury. Even if biased jurors are excused, the remaining pool may be contaminated.

## IV. THE CULTURAL BIAS ARGUMENT WAS PROPERLY RAISED AND DESERVES CONSIDERATION

The defense raised the argument that the publicity is the product of bigotry from a culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here. The trial court characterized this as an attempt to prove that the community is "scornful of outsiders," which it declined to find.

However, the defense does not need to prove that an entire culture harbors bias. The defense need only demonstrate that the publicity created a reasonable likelihood that an impartial jury cannot be obtained. The published letters containing race-based attacks on defendants provide direct evidence that the community was exposed to prejudicial, ethnicity-based content. This is not an abstract cultural argument—it is evidence of published material that any prospective juror could have read.

The trial court's refusal to "find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society" conflates two distinct inquiries. The question is not whether the letters represent the community's beliefs; the question is whether prospective jurors were exposed to prejudicial material that could influence their judgment. That question must be answered affirmatively based on the trial court's own findings that letters containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and "embarrassingly slanted" race-based remarks were published and circulated.

## V. THE REMEDY SOUGHT IS APPROPRIATE

The prosecution argues that "due process does not require venue change—it requires a fair trial." This is true as a general matter. However, when a fair trial cannot be secured in the original venue due to pervasive pretrial publicity, venue change is the constitutional remedy. In Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. at 719-21, the Supreme Court recognized venue change as the appropriate remedy when pretrial publicity has tainted the community.

The prosecution cites United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987) for the proposition that venue change is unsuitable in small jurisdictions. However, in Moreno, the court was addressing the practical difficulties of the remedy, not holding that venue change is never required. The court noted that "alternative remedies" may be appropriate. Here, no effective alternative remedy exists within American Samoa. The trial court acknowledged this difficulty but offered only voir dire as a solution—a remedy that fails to address the systemic problem.

The defense submits that the appropriate remedy is transfer to a jurisdiction outside American Samoa where the publicity has not reached and where an impartial jury can be empaneled. While this may be logistically challenging, it is the remedy required by the Constitution when prejudice has infected the community.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order denying the motion for change of venue and remand with instructions to grant the change of venue to a location outside American Samoa where the defendants can receive a fair trial by an impartial jury.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Defendants-Appellants**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Upon consideration of the Defendants-Appellants’ Opening Brief in Support of Appeal from Denial of Motion for Change of Venue and the Prosecution’s response, this Court renders the following preliminary ruling:**

The trial court’s Order denying the motions for change of venue is affirmed. The Defendants have not met the heavy burden required to demonstrate presumed prejudice, nor have they shown that the statutory remedies available within the Territory are insufficient to ensure a fair trial.

**I. Standard for Presumed Prejudice**

The right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is a fundamental requirement of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To justify a change of venue based on pretrial publicity, a defendant must show either actual prejudice or presumed prejudice. Presumed prejudice occurs only when the publicity is so pervasive and inflammatory that the trial atmosphere is inherently corrupted. *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963). This is an "extraordinary" remedy reserved for "exceptional cases." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The Supreme Court has reversed a conviction on this ground only once, in *Rideau*, where a televised confession was broadcast multiple times to tens of thousands of viewers. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272.

**II. Analysis of Publicity**

The Defendants argue that the trial court erred by focusing on the quantity of publicity rather than its inflammatory nature, specifically pointing to letters to the editor containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and racial slant. While the Court acknowledges that such content is prejudicial, the legal standard requires more than isolated instances of bias; it requires a showing that the community has been saturated with inflammatory material to the point where a fair trial is impossible. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985).

The record before the trial court consisted of only six newspaper articles and editorials. The trial court characterized the news coverage as factual and the prejudicial letters as "rogue" opinions that do not represent the beliefs of the entire society. This factual finding is supported by the record. Unlike the "sensationalized" coverage required to establish presumed prejudice, the limited number of publications here does not demonstrate the "oversaturation" necessary to overcome the presumption of juror impartiality. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272; *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The Defendants’ argument that the threshold for presumed prejudice should be lowered in a small community is without precedent; due process requires a fair trial, but it does not mandate that a court presume prejudice based on a handful of opinionated letters in a small jurisdiction.

**III. Remedy and Statutory Authority**

The Defendants contend that the trial court misinterpreted A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, which allows the court to adjourn to "any appropriate place in American Samoa" for the "interest of justice." The Defendants argue this should authorize a transfer outside the Territory. This argument is unpersuasive. The plain language of the statute limits the court’s movement to places "in American Samoa." It does not grant authority to transfer proceedings to a foreign jurisdiction or another U.S. district.

Furthermore, as noted by the trial court and supported by precedent in similar small jurisdictions, a change of venue within American Samoa would be futile. The Territory maintains a single jury roll of all registered voters. *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (finding change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico due to similar constraints). Transferring the trial to a different village would result only in a physical change of location, not a change in the jury pool. Therefore, the statutory remedy sought by the Defendants is legally unavailable and practically ineffective.

**IV. Adequacy of Voir Dire**

The trial court offered a robust alternative remedy: the use of a detailed voir dire questionnaire and individual voir dire if necessary. This is the standard mechanism for discerning actual prejudice. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 351 (1966); *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The Defendants argue that voir dire is inadequate because jurors may not admit bias. However, absent a showing of presumed prejudice, the legal system relies on the voir dire process to identify and exclude biased individuals. The trial court’s proactive measures, including specific inquiries into media exposure, are sufficient to protect the Defendants’ right to a fair trial. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977).

**Conclusion**

The Defendants have failed to demonstrate that the pretrial publicity rose to the level of inherent presumed prejudice required to warrant a change of venue. The trial court correctly applied the law, recognized the logistical limitations of the Territory, and provided adequate procedural safeguards to ensure the selection of an impartial jury.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s denial of the motion for change of venue is affirmed. The parties are directed to proceed with the preparation for trial in accordance with the trial court’s instructions regarding jury selection.

While the Court acknowledges the unique dynamics of a small island community, the legal standard for presumed prejudice is constitutional and does not fluctuate based on geography or population size.

Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963). This is an "extraordinary" remedy reserved for "exceptional cases." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The Defendants correctly note that the inquiry is qualitative as well as quantitative, *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985), but the record here does not support the finding of inherent prejudice required to override the presumption of juror impartiality.

The trial court characterized the news coverage as factual and the prejudicial letters as "rogue opinions" that do not represent the beliefs of the entire society. This factual finding is not clearly erroneous. While the letters contained "embarrassingly slanted" racial remarks, isolated expressions of bigotry, however deplorable, do not equate to the "sensationalized" news coverage required to establish presumed prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The Defendants’ argument that the threshold for presumed prejudice should be effectively lowered in small jurisdictions is without precedent. The Supreme Court has reversed a conviction on this ground only once—in *Rideau*, where a televised confession was broadcast to tens of thousands. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272. The present case, involving six articles and editorials, falls far short of that benchmark.

**II. The "Moreno" Inversion Argument Does Not Establish Prejudice**

The Defendants contend that the reasoning in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987)—which notes that venue change is unsuitable in small territories because the jury pool is the same—should be inverted to argue that the fixed nature of the pool makes the finding of prejudice *more* likely. This argument misunderstands the burden of proof.

*Moreno* stands for the proposition that a change of venue is often futile in small jurisdictions, not that the difficulty of securing a neutral venue creates a presumption of prejudice. The Defendants carry the heavy burden of showing that the publicity has "pervasively" tainted the *entire* jury pool. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The fact that the jury pool is fixed does not relieve the Defendants of the obligation to demonstrate that the *entire* pool is prejudiced. The trial court found that the publicity did not taint the community at large, and the Defendants have not provided sufficient evidence to overturn this finding. The logistical constraints identified in *Moreno* make the remedy of venue change impractical, but they do not lower the constitutional threshold for finding that a fair trial is impossible.

**III. Statutory Interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601**

The Defendants argue that the trial court erred by construing A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 too narrowly, asserting that the phrase "any appropriate place in American Samoa" is broad enough to encompass relocation for jury selection purposes. The Defendants further argue that the Prosecution’s claim that such a move would be "futile" is circular because it assumes the very issue in dispute—that the entire territory is tainted.

This argument fails because the trial court’s denial of the motion was based on the *lack of prejudice*, not the futility of the remedy. The trial court stated, "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives." The court analyzed A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to determine if it could provide a remedy *if* prejudice were found. It concluded that the statute, which limits the court to places "in American Samoa," would not result in a change of jury pool. This analysis was correct. The statute’s plain language confines the court’s movement to the Territory. A.S.C.A. § 46.0601. The "circularity" argument is therefore moot; because the Defendants failed to prove prejudice in the first instance, the court never reached the stage of determining whether a transfer under the statute would be futile or effective.

**IV. Adequacy of Voir Dire**

The Defendants argue that voir dire is inadequate where publicity has been inflammatory, citing the potential for unconscious bias. While the Court recognizes that voir dire is not a perfect cure for pretrial publicity, it remains the "primary means" of ensuring juror impartiality in the absence of presumed prejudice. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 351 (1966).

The Defendants’ argument that voir dire cannot detect unconscious bias asks the Court to presume prejudice based on the possibility of hidden bias. However, the law requires a showing that it is "reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured" before a trial can be halted or venue changed. *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). The trial court took significant steps to mitigate this risk by authorizing a detailed voir dire questionnaire and individual questioning. These measures are consistent with due process requirements. *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The Defendants have not demonstrated that these safeguards are insufficient to identify and exclude biased individuals.

**Conclusion**

The Defendants have failed to meet the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice. The pretrial publicity, while containing some offensive and opinionated content, did not rise to the level of "oversaturation" or "sensationalism" required to overcome the presumption of juror impartiality. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court correctly interpreted the statutory limitations of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and provided adequate alternative remedies to ensure a fair trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s denial of the motion for change of venue is affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

In a jurisdiction where all registered voters constitute the jury pool and where media outlets are described as "virtual monopolies," the impact of even limited publicity is magnified exponentially.

The Prosecution's reliance on Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. In Coleman, the court conducted an exhaustive analysis of the extensive publicity in that case, which included over 150 articles, radio broadcasts, and extensive community discussion. The defense here does not claim that six articles in a vacuum would constitute oversaturation. Rather, the unique circumstances of this small island community—where the same readers consume limited publications, where social networks are tightly interwoven, and where the jury pool is finite and known—create conditions where the same quantity of publicity has far greater impact.

The Prosecution argues that Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), represents the only instance where the Supreme Court reversed on grounds of presumed prejudice, implying that only comparable coverage suffices. The qualitative inquiry asks whether the publicity was "sensationalized" rather than factual. The trial court found that the letters to the editor contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt"—this is precisely the sensationalized, inflammatory content that Coleman identifies as problematic.

The Prosecution misreads United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), when it argues that the small-community context does not lower the threshold for presumed prejudice. The Defense does not argue that small size automatically satisfies the Rideau standard. Rather, the Defense argues that the Moreno court recognized a principle that cuts in favor of the Defendants: in small jurisdictions, the remedy of venue change is impractical because the jury pool cannot be changed. The Defense's argument does not "invert" Moreno but rather applies its reasoning consistently. The Prosecution argues that the Constitution requires "a fair trial, not a presumption of prejudice based on geography." This argument, while superficially appealing, ignores the specific factual finding in this case that the publicity did contain racially inflammatory content directed at foreign-born defendants. The Defense is not asking this Court to presume prejudice based on geography alone. Rather, the Defense asks the Court to recognize that when pretrial publicity contains inflammatory material targeting the defendants' national origin, and when that publicity occurs in a small, interconnected community where everyone knows everyone, the constitutional threshold is met.

The trial court acknowledged that "if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest corners of the Territory." This finding supports rather than undermines the Defense's position. The court should have drawn the logical conclusion: in a community where prejudice, once created, cannot be contained, the Defendants are entitled to a remedy before trial rather than after conviction.

## III. THE CULTURAL BIAS EVIDENCED IN THE PUBLICITY IS RELEVANT AND SIGNIFICANT

The Prosecution argues that "isolated expressions of bigotry" in letters to the editor do not establish presumed prejudice. This argument fails to appreciate the specific nature of the cultural bias at issue in this case.

The Defense presented evidence that the defendants—two Chinese nationals and one native of Western Samoa—face prejudice from a "culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here." The trial court characterized this as an overgeneralization, stating that "a few rogue letters to the editor" do not represent the beliefs of an entire society. However, the court failed to consider the specific context: these letters were published in a community with limited media options, and the defendants presented evidence of cultural animosity toward outsiders.

In Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333 (1966), the Supreme Court recognized that trial courts must take affirmative steps to ensure fair trials when publicity threatens the defendants' rights. The Court stated that the trial court has a "constitutional duty" to "protect the accused from evils which may destroy the integrity of the trial process." The cultural bias expressed in the letters is not merely "isolated" when considered in light of the defense counsel's proffered evidence regarding community attitudes toward foreigners.

The Prosecution argues that the Defense offered "no data on community sentiment, no evidence of systemic discrimination." This misstates the record. The trial court explicitly considered the letters to the editor as evidence of community sentiment. While the court characterized these letters as representing only a "limited section of society," it did not require the Defense to prove that a majority of the community held prejudicial views. The constitutional standard requires only that the publicity be sufficiently pervasive that "any subsequent court proceeding could be but a hollow formality." Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727.

## IV. VOIR DIRE IS INADEQUATE TO CURE THE PREJUDICE IN THIS CASE

The Prosecution argues that voir dire is the "primary means" of ensuring juror impartiality and that the trial court's authorization of detailed questionnaires satisfies due process. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the prejudice at issue.

In Sheppard, the Supreme Court recognized that voir dire, while important, is not a cure-all for pretrial publicity. The Court stated that "the trial court might well have proceeded with the examination of jurors on the issue of exposure to the mass news media," but also noted that "the trial court should have made an order closing the courtroom to the public during the hearing." The Court recognized that some prejudices cannot be cured through questioning alone.

The Prosecution cites Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405 (E.D. Tex. 1987), for the proposition that the Defense must show "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." However, the Bell court applied this standard in the context of actual prejudice, which requires juror testimony. The standard for presumed prejudice is different and does not require such a showing. The Defense has argued that the nature and extent of the publicity, combined with the small community context, creates a presumption of prejudice. If the presumption applies, the burden shifts to the prosecution to demonstrate that voir dire can cure the specific prejudice.

The Defense does not dispute that voir dire is the primary means of ensuring impartiality in ordinary cases. However, the circumstances here are not ordinary. The cultural bias expressed in the letters targets the defendants specifically because of their national origin. This is not the type of general publicity about a crime that might be cured through questioning. Jurors may honestly believe they can be fair while harboring unconscious biases that affect their judgment. The trial court's questionnaires, while thorough, cannot detect unconscious bias any more than the general questioning approved in other cases.

The Prosecution cites American Samoa Government v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78 (Trial Div. 1994), for the proposition that such safeguards are "consistent with due process." However, Snow involved less inflammatory publicity and did not present the same concerns about cultural bias against foreign-born defendants. The precedent does not stand for the proposition that voir dire is always sufficient, regardless of the nature of the prejudice.

## V. A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 DOES PROVIDE FOR VENUE CHANGE

The Prosecution argues that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 does not authorize venue transfers for jury selection purposes. The Defense contends that the trial court erred in its interpretation of the statute.

The statute provides that "the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa." The trial court interpreted this as applying only to situations where "the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo." This interpretation is unreasonably narrow.

The statute's plain language is broad: "any appropriate place in American Samoa" is not limited by the examples given in the court's opinion. The Defense does not ask the court to transfer the trial to a location outside American Samoa—that would indeed exceed the statute's authority. Rather, the Defense asks the court to transfer the proceedings to a different location within the territory where a different jury pool might be available.

The Prosecution argues that "transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue" because "the jury would be selected from the same pool." This argument assumes that all of American Samoa is a single, undifferentiated media market. However, Manu'a and other outer islands may have different media exposure and different community attitudes. The trial court should have permitted the Defense to present evidence on this point rather than concluding as a matter of law that no different jury pool exists.

The Prosecution's argument that the "circularity" of the venue argument renders it invalid fails because it puts the cart before the horse. The Defense does not argue that venue change is futile and therefore prejudice should be presumed. Rather, the Defense argues that the court should interpret the statute to provide a meaningful remedy if prejudice is found. The trial court's refusal to interpret the statute broadly enough to provide any remedy renders the constitutional protection hollow.

Individually, each element of the Defense's argument might be insufficient to meet the heavy burden for presumed prejudice. However, the cumulative effect of all factors together satisfies that burden.

We have: (1) pretrial publicity containing inflammatory material targeting the defendants' national origin; (2) a small, interconnected community where the jury pool is fixed and known; (3) media outlets described as "virtual monopolies" ensuring wide dissemination of prejudicial material; (4) published expressions of racial bias that, even if representing only a "limited section of society," nevertheless reach potential jurors; and (5) cultural dynamics that the defense proffered evidence suggest are hostile to foreign-born defendants.

The Prosecution asks this Court to apply the Rideau standard as if this case involved a large metropolitan community with multiple media outlets and an inexhaustible jury pool. This application is incorrect. The constitutional standard is qualitative as well as quantitative, Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490, and the qualitative analysis must account for the unique circumstances of American Samoa.

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order denying the motion for change of venue and remand with instructions to grant the motion. The pretrial publicity, considered in light of the small, interconnected community of American Samoa, the cultural dynamics at play, and the nature of the inflammatory material directed at foreign-born defendants, satisfies the constitutional standard for presumed prejudice.

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the Defendants-Appellants’ Closing Argument and the Prosecution’s Closing Argument, and upon review of the record and the applicable law, this Court affirms the trial court’s Order denying the motions for change of venue.

**I. The Defendants Have Failed to Meet the Heavy Burden of Showing Presumed Prejudice**

The Defendants argue that the combination of six newspaper articles and a series of inflammatory letters to the editor, when viewed through the lens of American Samoa’s small, interconnected community, satisfies the constitutional standard for presumed prejudice. We disagree.

The standard for presumed prejudice is exacting. It requires a showing that the publicity was so "pervasively and inflamed the community" that "any subsequent court proceeding could be but a hollow formality." *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963). This is an "extraordinary" remedy reserved for "exceptional cases" involving "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The Supreme Court has reversed a conviction on this ground only once—in *Rideau*, where a televised confession was broadcast to tens of thousands of people. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272.

The Defendants contend that the "qualitative" nature of the publicity—specifically the racial slurs and "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" found in the letters to the editor—meets this threshold. While we agree with the trial court that these letters are "embarrassingly slanted," isolated expressions of bigotry, however deplorable, do not equate to the "sensationalized" news coverage required to establish presumed prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court characterized these letters as "rogue opinions" that do not represent the beliefs of the entire society. This factual finding is not clearly erroneous.

The Defendants’ argument that the "small community" context magnifies the impact of this publicity, effectively lowering the constitutional threshold, is unpersuasive. The legal standard for presumed prejudice is constitutional and does not fluctuate based on geography or population size. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The Defendants have not provided sufficient evidence to overcome the presumption of juror impartiality or to demonstrate that the community at large has been infected by prejudice to the degree required by *Rideau*.

**II. The "Moreno" Argument Does Not Establish Prejudice**

The Defendants urge this Court to adopt an inversion of the reasoning in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), arguing that because the jury pool in American Samoa is fixed, any contamination of the community necessarily taints the entire pool. This argument misunderstands the burden of proof.

*Moreno* stands for the proposition that a change of venue is often futile in small jurisdictions because the jury pool is the same throughout the territory. *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. It does not stand for the proposition that the difficulty of securing a neutral venue creates a *presumption* of prejudice. The Defendants still carry the "heavy burden" of showing that the publicity has "pervasively" tainted the entire jury pool. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The fact that the jury pool is fixed does not relieve the Defendants of the obligation to demonstrate that the *entire* pool is prejudiced. The trial court found that the publicity did not taint the community at large, and the Defendants have not provided sufficient evidence to overturn this finding.

**III. Voir Dire Remains an Adequate Remedy**

The Defendants argue that voir dire is inadequate to cure the prejudice in this case because it cannot detect "unconscious bias" stemming from cultural animosity toward foreign-born defendants. While we acknowledge that voir dire is not a perfect cure, it remains the "primary means" of ensuring juror impartiality in the absence of presumed prejudice. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 351 (1966).

The law requires a showing that it is "reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured" before a trial can be halted or venue changed. *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). The Defendants have not met this burden. The trial court took significant steps to mitigate the risk of bias by authorizing a detailed voir dire questionnaire in both English and Samoan and individual questioning if necessary. These measures are consistent with due process requirements. *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The Defendants’ speculation regarding "unconscious bias" does not render the trial court’s procedural safeguards constitutionally insufficient.

**IV. A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 Does Not Authorize a Change of Venue**

The Defendants argue that the trial court erred by construing A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 too narrowly, asserting that the phrase "any appropriate place in American Samoa" is broad enough to encompass relocation for jury selection purposes to areas like Manu'a.

We find the trial court’s interpretation of the statute to be correct. The statute authorizes the court to adjourn to a different location for the convenience of the court, parties, or witnesses. A.S.C.A. § 46.0601. It does not authorize a change of venue for the purpose of securing a different jury pool. As the trial court noted, all registered voters on the island are part of a single jury roll. Transferring the case to Manu'a would not change the jury pool; it would only result in a physical change of location. The Defendants’ argument that Manu'a might have a different media exposure or community attitude is speculative and unsupported by the record. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants.

**Conclusion**

The Defendants have failed to meet the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice. The pretrial publicity, while containing some offensive and opinionated content, did not rise to the level of "oversaturation" or "sensationalism" required to overcome the presumption of juror impartiality. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court correctly interpreted the statutory limitations of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and provided adequate alternative remedies to ensure a fair trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s denial of the motion for change of venue is affirmed.
