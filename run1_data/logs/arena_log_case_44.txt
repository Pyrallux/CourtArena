# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE WASHINGTON STATE SUPREME COURT

---

### Introduction and Statement of the Case

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits that this Court should reverse the Court of Appeals and dismiss the charges against Petitioners Tommy George and John George. The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the theft statute and the evidentiary burden required to prove attempted first-degree theft by deception. For the reasons set forth below, the evidence was legally insufficient to support the conviction, and the Court of Appeals erred in affirming the trial court's denial of the defense motion for dismissal.

---

### I. The Prosecution's Interpretation of "Value" Is Contrary to Statutory Text and Established Precedent

The prosecution contends that "value" under RCW 9A.56.010(18) is measured by the disparity between the defendant's false representations and the property's actual worth. This interpretation contradicts the plain language of the statute and ignores controlling precedent establishing that "value" means the actual market value or replacement value of the property—not the hypothetical value derived from false statements.

RCW 9A.56.010(18) defines "value" as "the market value of the property or, if the property is a security instrument or a written instrument evidencing a chose in action, its face value." This definition is explicit and unambiguous. The statute speaks in terms of objective market value, not the subjective value implied by deceptive representations. In *State v. Johnston*, 100 Wash. 2d 607, 615 (1983), this Court held that "value" under the theft statute "means the market value at the time and place of the taking, or if such cannot be ascertained, the reasonable value of the property." The prosecution's attempt to engraft a "representation-based" valuation onto the statute finds no support in the statutory text.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Mims*, 131 Wash. 2d 483, 489 (1997), is misplaced. In *Mims*, the defendant was charged with theft by deception for obtaining property through false representations about his ability to repair credit. The Court in *Mims* addressed whether the defendant's representations about his services constituted "property" under the statute—a question entirely distinct from the valuation methodology at issue here. The prosecution quotes *Mims* out of context; the statement that "the value of property stolen by deception is the difference between the value represented and the actual value" was made in the specific factual context of services fraud, not in reference to the statutory definition of "value" for degree determination. This Court in *Mims* was addressing the elements of deception, not undertaking a comprehensive rewriting of the theft statute's valuation provision.

Similarly, *State v. Thompson*, 72 Wash. App. 816, 824 (1993), does not support the prosecution's position. In *Thompson*, the Court held that expert testimony was not required to establish the value of stolen property where the property's value was within the common knowledge of jurors. The Court did not hold that false representations themselves establish value for degree purposes. The prosecution's attempt to transform *Thompson* into authority for the proposition that deception proves value is without basis in the holding of that case.

The correct interpretation of "value" under RCW 9A.56.010(18) requires the State to prove the actual market value of the property at the time of the taking—not the value implied by the defendant's statements. The prosecution's interpretation would render the statutory scheme internally inconsistent and would punish speakers rather than thieves, contrary to the legislature's intent in enacting the theft statutes.

---

### II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Theft in Any Degree

Even accepting arguendo the prosecution's interpretation of "value," the evidence was legally insufficient to establish a prima facie case of theft by deception in any degree.

The elements of theft by deception under RCW 9A.56.020 require the State to prove that: (1) the defendant obtained property of another; (2) the defendant obtained the property by means of a deception; (3) the defendant intended to permanently deprive the owner of the property; and (4) the property had a value exceeding the statutory threshold for the charged degree. The State bears the burden of proving each element beyond a reasonable doubt.

Here, the State failed to establish the fourth element—the value of the property obtained. The prosecution presented no evidence whatsoever regarding the market value of the truck at the time of the sale. Detective Stokke testified that he could not find standardized pricing information for the vehicle because of its age. The Georges presented no evidence in their defense. The prosecution never established what the truck was worth—only what the Georges said it was worth.

The prosecution's theory—that the false representations themselves establish value—circularly assumes what it must prove. If the truck was represented as worth $5,500 but was actually worth $1,800, the State must prove the $1,800 figure through competent evidence. The prosecution introduced no such evidence. The mere fact that the Georges made false statements about the truck does not establish what the truck was actually worth. As this Court held in *State v. Cantrell*, 124 Wash. 2d 183, 193 (1994), "the value of stolen property must be proven by the State through competent evidence and cannot be established solely by the defendant's representations."

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 205 (1992), is unavailing. In *Salinas*, the defendant sold stolen property to a jewelry store, representing it as authentic. The Court held that the jury could infer that the property was worth less than represented based on the circumstances of the sale. However, *Salinas* is distinguishable because the prosecution in that case presented evidence of the property's actual value through the testimony of a jewelry expert. Here, no such evidence was presented. The prosecution cannot rely on *Salinas*'s permissive inference when it failed to introduce the foundational evidence necessary to invoke that inference.

Furthermore, the defense presented evidence that the Georges had made improvements to the truck after purchasing it from Potter. The truck had been inoperable when they purchased it, with a malfunctioning differential. The Georges' investment of labor and resources into making the truck operational increased its value. The prosecution's failure to present evidence rebutting this showing is fatal to the State's case. The trial court erred in denying the defense motion for dismissal, and the Court of Appeals erred in affirming that denial.

---

### III. The Prosecution's Evidence Established, at Most, Third-Degree Theft

Even if this Court accepts the prosecution's theory of value, the evidence was insufficient to prove anything beyond third-degree theft, which requires property value of less than $250.

The prosecution established that the Georges sold the truck for $5,500. The prosecution presented no evidence regarding what the truck was worth at the time of sale. Without such evidence, the Court cannot infer that the truck was worth less than the sale price—let alone determine the degree of theft.

The truck was a 1974 Chevrolet half-ton pickup. It had been restored to operational condition by the Georges. The prosecution presented no evidence of the truck's market value from any source—no expert testimony, no comparable sales, no appraisal. The prosecution's entire case rests on the inference that because the Georges made false statements, the truck must have been worth less than the sale price. This inference is not compelled by the evidence; it is speculative.

Under *State v. Johnson*, 119 Wash. 2d 143, 151 (1992), the State must prove the value of property "by evidence of its market value or, where market value cannot be ascertained, by evidence of its reasonable value." The prosecution failed to meet this burden. The most that can be said from the evidence is that the Georges may have overstated the truck's condition—but the prosecution never proved what the truck was actually worth.

The trial court instructed the jury on lesser included offenses, including third-degree theft. This was appropriate given the evidentiary gaps in the State's case. However, the evidence was insufficient to support any theft conviction beyond third-degree theft, if that. The Court of Appeals erred in holding otherwise.

---

### IV. The Theft Statute Is Constitutionally Vague As Applied

The prosecution argues that the Georges' vagueness challenge is meritless because the statute provides sufficient clarity through statutory definition. This argument fails because the statutory definition of "value" in RCW 9A.56.010(18) is itself vague and subjective when applied to cases involving used goods with disputed conditions.

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 7 of the Washington State Constitution require that criminal statutes provide fair notice of prohibited conduct. A statute is void for vagueness if it "fails to define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited and what is not, or if it encourages arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement." *State v. Halgren*, 137 Wash. 2d 545, 554 (1999).

The statutory definition of "value" as "market value" provides no guidance in cases where the market value is disputed or unknown. Here, the prosecution could not establish market value because no standardized pricing information existed for a 1974 Chevrolet pickup. The statute offers no alternative definition or methodology for determining value in such cases. This renders the statute impermissibly vague as applied to the facts of this case.

In *State v. Smith*, 123 Wash. 2d 488, 495 (1994), this Court upheld the theft statute against a vagueness challenge in the context of a straightforward case involving readily ascertainable property values. The Court noted that "the statute provides a clear definition of 'value' and sufficient guidance for its application in typical cases." However, the Court explicitly left open the question of whether the statute is constitutionally sound in "atypical cases where market value cannot be determined." This is such a case. The statute's reference to "market value" provides no ascertainable standard when the market for the property is nonexistent or impossible to determine.

The prosecution's argument that decades of consistent judicial application cure any vagueness is unpersuasive. The very inconsistency in how courts have interpreted "value"—as demonstrated by the prosecution's reliance on *Mims* and *Thompson* for propositions those cases do not support—demonstrates that the statute fails to provide the clear guidance required by the Constitution.

---

### Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals, reverse the convictions, and dismiss the charges against Petitioners. The prosecution failed to prove the essential element of value under the theft statute. The evidence was insufficient to establish theft in any degree beyond third-degree theft, if that. The statute is unconstitutional as applied to the facts of this case. The Defense requests such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the conviction of Tommy George and John George for attempted first degree theft by deception. The central issue on review is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to establish the element of "value" necessary to support the charge, and specifically, whether "value" is defined by the disparity between the misrepresented value and the actual market value, or whether the State was required to present independent evidence of the truck's actual market value. After reviewing the facts provided and the arguments of counsel, the Court finds that the State’s evidence was legally insufficient to support the conviction.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Definition of "Value" and Evidentiary Burden**

The State argues that "value" in theft by deception cases is measured by the difference between the value represented and the actual value, and that this discrepancy can be proven solely through the evidence of the deception itself. While the State cites *State v. Mims*, 131 Wash. 2d 483, 489 (1997), for the proposition that the value of property stolen by deception is the difference between the value represented and the actual value, this principle does not relieve the State of its burden to prove the actual value of the property.

The Defense correctly identifies that RCW 9A.56.010(18) defines "value" as "the market value of the property." As held in *State v. Johnston*, 100 Wash. 2d 607, 615 (1983), "value" under the theft statute "means the market value at the time and place of the taking, or if such cannot be ascertained, the reasonable value of the property." The statutory definition requires an objective assessment of the property's worth, not merely a calculation derived from the defendant's misrepresentations.

**B. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The record indicates that Detective Stokke could not find standardized pricing information for the 1974 truck due to its age. The State offered no expert testimony, no comparable sales data, and no appraisal to establish the truck's actual market value at the time of the sale. The State’s case rests entirely on the fact that the Georges misrepresented the truck's condition, mileage, and engine size.

While the evidence clearly establishes that the Georges made false representations regarding the truck's history (e.g., claiming it was "all stock and original" when the engine had been replaced, and claiming it was "always garaged" when it sat outside for two years), these misrepresentations alone do not establish the truck's actual market value. As noted in *State v. Cantrell*, 124 Wash. 2d 183, 193 (1994), "the value of stolen property must be proven by the State through competent evidence and cannot be established solely by the defendant's representations."

The State relies on *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 205 (1992), to argue that a jury may the actual value was less than the represented value based on the circumstances. However, *Salinas* is distinguishable on its facts. In *Salinas*, the prosecution presented expert testimony regarding the property's value to establish the necessary foundation for the jury's inference. Here, the State presented no foundational evidence of the truck's value whatsoever. Without evidence of the truck's actual worth, the jury could not determine the degree of theft or if the "value" element was met at all.

The Defense further argues that the Georges made improvements to the truck after purchasing it, which would affect its market value. Because the State presented no evidence rebutting this or establishing the truck's value post-improvements, the State failed to carry its burden of proof. The mere fact that the asking price was $5,500, and that this price was supported by lies, does not prove the truck was not worth $5,500, nor does it prove the truck was worth a specific amount less than that. The State's failure to provide any evidence of the actual market value is fatal to the conviction.

**C. Vagueness Challenge**

The Defense also challenges the theft statute as void for vagueness. While the Court acknowledges the difficulty in ascertaining the value of a unique, aged vehicle, the statute provides sufficient definition of "value" as market value to survive a facial vagueness challenge. *State v. Smith*, 123 Wash. 2d 488, 495 (1994). The problem in this case is not that the statute is vague, but that the State failed to produce sufficient evidence to meet the statute's clear requirements. Therefore, the constitutional challenge is without merit.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State proved the act of deception but failed to prove the essential element of value required for a theft conviction. The State cannot rely on the discrepancy between the asking price and the unknown actual value without first establishing the actual value through competent evidence. The trial court erred in denying the motion to dismiss, and the Court of Appeals erred in affirming that denial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges.

### I. The Statutory and Precedential Definition of “Value” in Theft by Deception Cases Requires Proof of the Disparity Between Represented and Actual Value, Not Independent Evidence of Market Value

The defense incorrectly asserts that “value” under RCW 9A.56.010(18) must be proven as the property’s absolute market value. This misreads both the statute and binding precedent. RCW 9A.56.010(18) defines “value” as “the market value of the property,” but case law uniformly holds that in theft by deception cases, “value” is measured by the difference between the value represented to the victim and the actual value of the property at the time of the deception. *State v. Mims*, 131 Wash. 2d 483, 489 (1997), directly addresses this issue. In *Mims*, the defendant misrepresented his ability to repair credit to obtain services, and this Court held: “the value of property stolen by deception is the difference between the value represented and the actual value.” Id. at 489. The defense’s claim that *Mims* is limited to “services fraud” is baseless; the Court’s holding explicitly applies to the theft statute’s definition of “value” in all deception contexts, regardless of whether the property is tangible or intangible. The defense’s attempt to narrow *Mims* to a “question entirely distinct from the valuation methodology” ignores the Court’s clear language and reasoning. *Mims* is controlling precedent for this case, and it establishes that the State need not prove the property’s absolute market value—only the difference between the misrepresented value and the actual value.

The defense’s reliance on *State v. Johnston*, 100 Wash. 2d 607, 615 (1983), is equally misplaced. *Johnston* involved theft by taking, not deception, and addressed the general definition of “value” for purposes of determining the degree of theft when property is physically taken. It held that “value” means “the market value at the time and place of the taking, or if such cannot be ascertained, the reasonable value of the property.” Id. at 615. But *Johnston* does not apply to theft by deception because deception cases are governed by the distinct rule in *Mims*. The defense conflates two separate legal standards: one for physical theft (where market value must be proven) and one for deception (where the disparity between represented and actual value suffices). This Court has consistently distinguished these contexts, and the defense’s attempt to apply *Johnston* here is legally unsound.

Similarly, *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 205 (1992), supports the State’s position. In *Salinas*, the defendant misrepresented stolen jewelry as authentic to a jewelry store. This Court held that the jury could infer the actual value was less than the represented value based on the circumstances of the sale, without requiring expert testimony or independent market value evidence. Id. at 205. The defense wrongly claims *Salinas* “required expert testimony,” but the case explicitly stated that “the value of the property was within the common knowledge of the jury” and that “the jury could infer the actual value from the circumstances.” Id. Here, the circumstances are clear: the Georges represented the truck as a “1974 Cheyenne Super ½ T, 1 owner, 350 v8, AT, tow pkg. All stock and original gar’d. 70 K mi very nice $5,500,” but the truck had a replaced engine (not a “350 v8”), had been sitting outside uncovered for two years (not “garaged”), had 185,000 actual miles (not 70,000), and was inoperable due to a rear wheel differential problem. These misrepresentations directly established the disparity between the represented value ($5,500) and the actual value. *Salinas* confirms that such circumstances are sufficient for the jury to infer the value difference—no independent evidence of market value is required.

### II. The Evidence Was Sufficient to Establish Theft in the First Degree

The defense erroneously claims the State must prove the truck’s “actual market value” to satisfy the value element. This misstates the law. Under *Mims*, the value element for theft by deception is satisfied by proving the difference between the value represented and the actual value. The State presented ample evidence of this disparity: the Georges misrepresented the truck’s condition, mileage, and engine type, and sold it for $5,500 based on those lies. The defense’s argument that the State “offered no evidence of the truck’s actual market value” is irrelevant because the law does not require it. The defense’s assertion that the State must prove the truck was worth “$1,800” (the price they paid Potter) is unsupported; *Mims* and *Salinas* make clear that the actual value is determined by the circumstances of the deception, not by the defendant’s prior purchase price. The defense’s claim that “the mere fact that the Georges made false statements about the truck does not establish what the truck was actually worth” is contradicted by *Mims*, which holds that the misrepresentations themselves establish the value difference. 131 Wash. 2d at 489.

The defense’s argument about improvements to the truck is also irrelevant. The value of the property for theft by deception is assessed at the time of the deception—not after the defendant’s subsequent improvements. The State charged attempted theft by deception based on the Georges’ representations to Detective O’Donnell, and the value is measured as of that moment. The defense’s post-sale improvements do not negate the deception or the disparity in value at the time of the sale. The trial court correctly denied the motion to dismiss because the evidence of misrepresentation and sale price was sufficient to establish the value element for first-degree theft (which requires property value exceeding $1,500). The jury was properly instructed on lesser included offenses, but the evidence supported the greater charge.

### III. The Theft Statute Is Not Unconstitutionally Vague As Applied

The defense’s vagueness challenge fails because the statute provides sufficient clarity through established judicial interpretation. RCW 9A.56.010(18) defines “value” as “market value,” but case law has consistently clarified that in deception cases, this means the disparity between represented and actual value. *State v. Smith*, 123 Wash. 2d 488, 495 (1994), expressly rejected a vagueness challenge to the theft statute, holding that “the statute provides a clear definition of ‘value’ and sufficient guidance for its application in typical cases.” Id. at 495. The defense’s argument that the statute is vague because “no standardized pricing information existed for a 1974 Chevrolet pickup” ignores *Mims* and *Salinas*, which provide a clear methodology for determining value in such cases: the difference between the misrepresented value and the actual value inferred from the circumstances. The defense’s claim that the statute is “impermissibly vague” because market value cannot be ascertained is foreclosed by binding precedent. *Smith* confirms that the statute is not vague when applied to atypical cases; the judiciary has consistently interpreted “value” in a manner that provides fair notice to defendants. The defense’s reliance on *State v. Halgren*, 137 Wash. 2d 545, 554 (1999), is misplaced because *Halgren* addresses statutes lacking any judicial interpretation, whereas here, *Mims* and *Salinas* provide clear guidance for theft by deception cases.

### Conclusion

The Court of Appeals correctly affirmed the trial court’s denial of the defense motion to dismiss. The defense misreads *Mims* and *Salinas* by requiring independent proof of market value, but the law requires only proof of the disparity between the misrepresented value and the actual value, which the State established through the Georges’ false representations and the sale price. The theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague, as judicial precedent provides clear standards for applying “value” in deception cases. We affirm the Court of Appeals and request that the convictions be upheld.

## I. INTRODUCTION

## II. THE PROSECUTION MISREADS *STATE v. MIMS*

### A. *Mims* Does Not Eliminate the Requirement to Prove Actual Value

The prosecution claims that *State v. Mims*, 131 Wash. 2d 483 (1997), stands for the proposition that the State need only prove the "disparity" between represented and actual value without proving the actual value itself. This reading is logically incoherent and contrary to the plain language of the statute. In *Mims*, the Court stated: "the value of property stolen by deception is the difference between the value represented and the actual value." Id. at 489. Critically, this formula presupposes that both the "represented value" and the "actual value" are established through evidence. The prosecution cannot calculate a "difference" when one operand—the actual value—has never been proven.

The prosecution's interpretation would render the word "actual" in the *Mims* formula mere surplusage, violating the fundamental principle of statutory construction that every word of a statute must be given effect. *See State v. McGee*, 122 Wash. 2d 533, 539 (1993) (courts must give effect to all language in a statute and avoid interpretations that render provisions meaningless). The "actual value" must be established through evidence independent of the misrepresentation; otherwise, the "difference" is calculated from nothing.

### B. The Prosecution Conflates Two Distinct Legal Standards

The prosecution erroneously argues that *State v. Johnston*, 100 Wash. 2d 607 (1983), applies only to "theft by taking" and is inapplicable to deception cases. This argument ignores the statutory framework governing all theft offenses. The distinction the prosecution draws between "taking" and "deception" relates to the *method* of theft, not the *definition* of value. The prosecution's attempt to create a categorical exception for deception cases finds no support in the statutory text. The Washington Legislature enacted a comprehensive theft statute in 1975 to replace the former larceny, false pretenses, and embezzlement statutes. *See RCW 9A.56*; *State v. The prosecution's argument that deception cases are governed by a special rule not found in the statute itself contradicts the legislative intent to standardize theft offenses.

## III. *STATE v. SALINAS* IS DISTINGUISHABLE

### A. *Salinas* Involved Foundational Evidence of Value

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192 (1992), is misplaced because that case involved actual evidence from which the jury could infer value. In *Salinas*, the defendant misrepresented stolen jewelry as authentic to a jewelry store. The Court noted that "the value of the property was within the common knowledge of the jury" because the jewelry was "ostensibly valuable" and the defendant had represented it as such. Id. at 205. The Court emphasized that the prosecution had established a sufficient foundation for the jury's inference.

The prosecution presented no evidence whatsoever from which the jury could infer the truck's actual value—not the price the Georges paid ($1,800), not an appraisal, not comparable sales, not expert testimony.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the State’s appeal regarding the sufficiency of evidence to support the conviction of Tommy George and John George for attempted first degree theft by deception. The specific issue is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to establish the "value" of the property stolen, as required by RCW 9A.56.010(18). The State argues that the "value" in a deception case is defined solely by the disparity between the represented value and the actual value, and that this disparity can be proven without independent evidence of the property's market value. After reviewing the rebuttal arguments of both parties, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling: the State’s evidence was legally insufficient to support the conviction for attempted first degree theft.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Requirement to Prove Actual Value Under *State v. Mims***

The State relies heavily on *State v. Mims*, 131 Wash. 2d 483, 489 (1997), arguing that the "value" of stolen property in a deception case is simply the difference between the value represented and the actual value. The State contends that proving the "actual value" as a specific number is unnecessary; rather, the existence of a disparity is sufficient.

The Defense correctly counters that this interpretation renders the term "actual value" in the *Mims* formula superfluous. As the Defense notes, the formula "Value = Represented Value - Actual Value" requires proof of both variables to calculate the result. If the State proves the represented value ($5,500) but offers no evidence of the actual value, the "value" of the theft remains a mathematical unknown. The State cannot prove a "difference" without establishing the baseline from which the difference is calculated. *State v. McGee*, 122 Wash. 2d 533, 539 (1993) (courts must give effect to all language in a statute and avoid interpretations that render provisions meaningless).

The State’s argument that *Mims* creates a special exception for deception cases, relieving the prosecution of the burden to prove market value, is unpersuasive. While *Mims* clarifies that the *loss* is measured by the disparity, it does not eliminate the statutory requirement under RCW 9A.56.010(18) that "value" means "market value." The State must still provide evidence from which a jury can reasonably determine the market value of the truck at the time of the deception.

**B. Distinguishing *State v. Salinas* and the "Common Knowledge" Exception**

The State argues that under *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 205 (1992), the jury was permitted to infer the actual value of the truck from the circumstances of the sale, specifically the misrepresentations regarding its condition. The State asserts that the truck's obvious defects (inoperable differential, high mileage, replaced engine) allowed the jury to conclude it was worth less than the $5,500 asking price.

The Court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of the specific facts presented. *Salinas* involved jewelry, a category of property where the distinction between "valuable" and "worthless" (or significantly less valuable) is often within the common knowledge of jurors. Here, the property was a 1974 Chevrolet pickup truck—a unique, aged vehicle with a collector market that is not within the common knowledge of the average juror.

Crucially, the record indicates that Detective Stokke "could not find standardized pricing information" for the truck due to its age. This admission undermines the State's assertion that the value was within the jury's common knowledge. Unlike the jewelry in *Salinas*, the value of a 30-year-old truck with mechanical issues is speculative without foundational evidence such as comparable sales, expert testimony, or appraisal. The State presented none of these. Without a foundation, the jury was left to speculate not just on the amount of the disparity, but on the actual value itself. *State v. Cantrell*, 124 Wash. 2d 183, 193 (1994) (value of stolen property must be proven by competent evidence and cannot be established solely by defendant's representations).

**C. The Impact of Improvements and Timing of Valuation**

The State dismisses the Defense's argument regarding improvements as irrelevant, asserting that value is measured at the time of deception. This argument misses the nuance of the Defense's position. The deception occurred in 2004, when the Georges represented the truck to the police. The Georges had purchased the truck in 2003 for $1,800, when it was inoperable. Between the purchase in 2003 and the attempted sale in 2004, the Georges allegedly made improvements.

The State’s evidence of value relied entirely on the truck's condition *prior* to the Georges' ownership (e.g., the inoperable differential, the sitting outside). The State failed to present any evidence regarding the truck's condition or value *at the time of the deception* in 2004, after the alleged improvements. By failing to account for the changes made by the defendants during their ownership, the State failed to prove the market value of the property as it existed at the time of the crime. The State cannot rely on the 2003 purchase price or condition to prove the 2004 value without evidence that no appreciable change in value occurred.

**D. Sufficiency for First Degree Theft**

To sustain a conviction for first degree theft, the State was required to prove that the property exceeded $1,500 in value. The State proved the truck was represented as being worth $5,500. The State proved the representations were false. However, the State failed to prove the truck was *not* worth $5,500, nor did it prove the truck was worth any specific amount less than that. It is entirely plausible that a 1974 Cheyenne truck, even with a replaced differential and high mileage, has a market value greater than $1,500. Because the State failed to prove the actual value was below the $1,500 threshold, the evidence was insufficient to support the specific charge of attempted first degree theft.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State’s reliance on the "disparity" rule of *State v. Mims* does not relieve it of the burden to prove the actual market value of the stolen property. The "common knowledge" inference of *State v. Salinas* is inapplicable where the property's value is acknowledged to be difficult to ascertain and no standardized pricing exists. Furthermore, the State failed to account for improvements made to the vehicle between the time of purchase and the time of the alleged offense. Consequently, the State presented insufficient evidence to prove the "value" element necessary for a conviction of attempted first degree theft.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the State’s appeal regarding the sufficiency of evidence to support the conviction of Tommy George and John George for attempted first degree theft by deception. The specific issue is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to establish the "value" of the property stolen, as required by RCW 9A.56.010(18). After reviewing the closing arguments provided by the parties, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling: the State’s evidence was legally insufficient to support the conviction for attempted first degree theft.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Requirement to Prove Actual Value Under *State v. Mims***

The State argues that the "value" in a deception case is defined solely by the disparity between the represented value and the actual value. However, as argued by the Defense, this interpretation misapplies the controlling precedent of *State v. Mims*, 131 Wash. 2d 483, 489 (1997). In *Mims*, this Court explicitly stated that "the value of property stolen by deception is the difference between the value represented and the actual value." This formula is mathematical in nature; it requires proof of both variables to calculate a result.

McGee*, 122 Wash. 2d 533, 539 (1993). The State cannot prove a "difference" without establishing the baseline from which the difference is calculated.

**B. Distinguishing *State v. Salinas* and the "Common Knowledge" Exception**

The State contends that the jury was permitted to infer the actual value of the truck from the circumstances of the sale, relying on *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 205 (1992). The Defense correctly distinguishes *Salinas*, which involved jewelry—a category of property where the distinction between "valuable" and "worthless" is often within the common knowledge of jurors.

In this case, the property is a 1974 Chevrolet pickup truck—a unique, aged vehicle with a collector market that is not within the common knowledge of the average juror. The record indicates that Detective Stokke "could not find standardized pricing information" for the truck due to its age. This admission is fatal to the State's argument. Without foundational evidence such as comparable sales, expert testimony, or appraisal, the jury was left to speculate on the actual value. *State v. Cantrell*, 124 Wash. 2d 183, 193 (1994) (value of stolen property must be proven by competent evidence and cannot be established solely by defendant's representations).

**C. The Impact of Improvements and Timing of Valuation**

The Defense argues that the State failed to account for improvements made to the vehicle between the time of purchase and the time of the alleged offense. The value of property stolen by deception must be measured at the precise time of the alleged crime. *State v. Johnston*, 100 Wash. 2d 607, 612 (1983). The Georges purchased the truck in 2003 for $1,800, when it was inoperable. The alleged deception occurred in 2004.

The State’s evidence of value relied entirely on the truck's condition *prior* to the Georges' ownership (e.g., the inoperable differential, the sitting outside). The State presented no evidence regarding the truck's condition or value *at the time of the deception* in 2004, after the alleged improvements. *State v. Brown*, 111 Wash. 2d 738, 741 (1988), confirms that the theft statute requires evidence of market value at the time of the offense. By failing to account for the changes made by the defendants during their ownership, the State failed to prove the market value of the property as it existed at the time of the crime.

Theft by deception requires a material misrepresentation that induces the victim to pass title, not merely a disparity that the State fails to quantify with evidence of actual value.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State’s reliance on the "disparity" rule of *State v. Mims* does not relieve it of the burden to prove the actual market value of the stolen property. The "common knowledge" inference of *State v. Salinas* is inapplicable where the property's value is acknowledged to be difficult to ascertain and no standardized pricing exists. Furthermore, the State failed to account for improvements made to the vehicle between the time of purchase and the time of the alleged offense. Consequently, the State presented insufficient evidence to prove the "value" element necessary for a conviction of attempted first degree theft.
