FACTS

Tina Marie Plotts died on February 27, 2004, as a result of a fire which started in the kitchen of the premises where she was a tenant. According to the landlords and owners of the premises, defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, defendant did maintenance for them in exchange for the payment of rent to reside there. On the evening of the fire, defendant had fallen asleep on a couch in the first floor living room after consuming a six-pack of beer. Ms. Plotts was in her designated second floor bedroom when a fire originated in the kitchen of the first floor of the residence. The cause and/or ignition source was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove.

Defendant escaped the burning residence, but Ms. Plotts was trapped inside the dwelling and could not flee; her bedroom windows were sealed shut and the stairs were impassible. She was found dead by fire personnel.

Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, filed a motion pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 against defendant’s estate for defendant’s failure to obey two court orders requiring his attendance at a videotape discovery deposition along with related discovery violations which impacted plaintiff’s *463ability to proceed with the lawsuit. In this motion, plaintiff outlines a long imcontro verted history of defendant’s noncompliance with scheduled depositions.

Plaintiff’s counsel first requested deposition dates for defendant by letter to defendant’s counsel dated June 17, 2007. Defendant was then scheduled for a deposition on August 13,2007, at 12:30 p.m. which was mutually agreeable to the parties. Defendant did not appear for the deposition, and his counsel stated that he could not locate him.

On August 27, 2008, defendant’s present counsel entered his appearance for defendant. Plaintiff again requested deposition dates for defendant. Eventually, defendant’s counsel provided several deposition dates; plaintiff selected October 15,2008, with no objection from defendant, for a videotaped deposition. Again, defendant failed to appear. A new date of November 8, 2008, was selected for defendant’s deposition. On October 27, 2008, defendant’s counsel contacted plaintiff’s counsel and stated that he was unable to locate defendant about the rescheduled deposition. OnNovember 7,2008, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that because his office was unable to locate defendant, defendant’s scheduled deposition of November 8, 2008, was cancelled.

On January 7, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel wrote to defendant’s counsel to request defendant’s last known address and Social Security number so he could hire a private investigator to find defendant. After receiving the requested information, plaintiff hired a private investigator who found defendant. Plaintiff then served defendant with a subpoena to take his deposition on April 27, 2009. On April 23,2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to confirm that defendant would appear for his deposition. By letter dated April 23, 2009, *464plaintiff’s counsel issued an amended notice of videotaped deposition in accordance with Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1. By letter dated April 26, 2009, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped and would therefore not appear for his deposition.

On April 28, 2009, April 29, 2009, and April 30, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to reschedule defendant’s deposition. Ultimately, defendant’s counsel informed plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped during his deposition.

On May 18,2009, plaintiff filed a motion for contempt and for sanctions. 
Plaintiff filed a second motion for contempt and for sanctions on May 10, 2010.Defendant was also entitled to receive a copy of the videotape.

The parties mutually agreed on the date of November 10, 2010, for a videotaped deposition. By letter dated November 9,2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for the deposition on November 10, 2010.

The only statement defendant ever gave concerning the fire was an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire. At that time he denied that he was the cause of the fire. Defendant died in January 2012.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPERIOR COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

---

**Defendant-Appellant Michael J. D'Aniello, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, v. Plaintiff-Joseph Michael Angelichio**

---

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Defendant-Appellant respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to Plaintiff's request to affirm the trial court's entry of default judgment. The trial court committed a manifest abuse of discretion in entering judgment against the Defendant pursuant to Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019. The record demonstrates that: (1) lesser sanctions would have adequately addressed any alleged prejudice; (2) Plaintiff bears significant responsibility for the failure to obtain Defendant's deposition through his own lack of diligence; (3) there exists no evidence in the record establishing that Defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire; and (4) the entry of default judgment was fundamentally unjust under the circumstances. Accordingly, this Court should reverse the trial court's orders and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ENTERING DEFAULT JUDGMENT AS A DISCOVERY SANCTION

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

The entry of default judgment as a discovery sanction under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019 is an extreme remedy that requires meticulous judicial analysis. As this Court held in *Werner v. Werner*, 267 Pa. Super. 292, 406 A.2d 780 (1979), the trial court must carefully balance multiple factors before imposing the sanction of dismissal or default judgment, including: (1) the nature and severity of the discovery violation; (2) whether the violation was willful or in bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence to the case.

The burden rests upon the moving party to demonstrate that dismissal or default judgment is warranted, and the trial court must explore lesser sanctions before imposing this most severe remedy. See *Bobilya v. Bobilya*, 277 Pa. Super. 162, 419 A.2d 736 (1980) (holding that dismissal with prejudice is appropriate only where the party's conduct has been so egregious as to amount to a refusal to participate in the judicial process).

### B. Application of the Balancing Test to This Case

**Nature and Severity of the Violation**

While Defendant acknowledges that he did not appear for scheduled depositions, the record establishes that his failures to appear were not the product of willful defiance or bad faith. Rather, as the facts demonstrate, Defendant's counsel repeatedly represented that he could not locate his client. The record shows that Defendant's counsel entered his appearance in August 2007 and subsequently informed Plaintiff's counsel on multiple occasions that he was unable to locate Defendant regarding scheduled depositions. This is distinguishable from a situation where a party deliberately flouts court orders with full knowledge of their obligations.

Moreover, the record reflects that Defendant did provide an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire, thereby providing some account of the incident. The violation, while real, was not of the most severe character contemplated by the case law.

**Willfulness or Bad Faith**

The record does not support a finding of willfulness or bad faith. Defendant's counsel consistently represented that he could not locate his client. There is no evidence that Defendant personally received notice of the depositions and deliberately chose not to appear. The situation appears to be one of difficulty in locating a client—which, while frustrating for the opposing party—does not rise to the level of bad faith warranting the extreme sanction of default judgment.

**Prejudice to the Plaintiff**

The Plaintiff claims prejudice, but the record does not support the magnitude of prejudice asserted. The Plaintiff has not established that Defendant possessed unique, irreplaceable evidence essential to proving his case. While Defendant was described as an "eyewitness," the facts indicate that he was asleep on the couch when the fire originated in the kitchen. His ability to provide testimony regarding the actual cause and origin of the fire is, at best, speculative.

Critically, the trial court's own findings acknowledge that "the cause and/or ignition source was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove" and that "the cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove." These determinations were made through investigation by fire personnel—not through Defendant's testimony. The evidence regarding the cause of the fire was independently developed through physical evidence, not through Defendant's conjectural observations.

**Possibility of Curing Prejudice Through Lesser Sanctions**

The most significant error in the trial court's analysis was its failure to properly consider lesser sanctions. As this Court emphasized in *Koch v. Board of Directors of West Shore School District*, 336 Pa. Super. 183, 485 A.2d 482 (1984), the trial court must impose the least severe sanction necessary to cure the prejudice.

In this case, lesser sanctions were readily available and appropriate:

1. **Monetary sanctions**: The court could have imposed monetary penalties for each missed deposition, thereby compensating Plaintiff for costs incurred.

2. **Adverse inference**: The court could have instructed the jury that an adverse inference could be drawn from Defendant's failure to testify, thereby addressing any prejudice without entering default judgment.

3. **Continuing jurisdiction**: The court retained jurisdiction to enforce its orders and could have escalated sanctions progressively if Defendant continued to violate discovery orders.

4. **Preclusion of defenses**: The court could have precluded specific defenses rather than entering a complete default judgment.

The trial court's failure to consider and reject these lesser alternatives constitutes a manifest abuse of discretion.

**Importance of the Precluded Evidence**

Finally, Plaintiff overstates the importance of Defendant's testimony. Defendant was asleep when the fire started and was in no better position than any other witness to explain how an unattended pot on an electric stove led to the fire. The physical evidence—the pot on the burner and the defective kerosene container—speaks for itself. Defendant's testimony was not "indispensable" as Plaintiff suggests; it was, at most, cumulative of other evidence regarding the circumstances of the fire.

---

## II. PLAINTIFF BEARS SIGNIFICANT RESPONSIBILITY FOR THE ALLEGED PREJUDICE

### A. Plaintiff's Diligence (or Lack Thereof) in Pursuing Discovery

The record demonstrates that Plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing Defendant's deposition. This failure weighs heavily against his claim of prejudice.

From June 2007 through Defendant's death in January 2012—over four years—Plaintiff failed to take meaningful steps to compel Defendant's attendance at a deposition. The facts establish:

1. **Initial failure**: After Defendant failed to appear on August 13, 2007, Plaintiff took no action for nearly a year until new counsel entered an appearance in August 2008.

2. **Acceptance of cancellations**: When Defendant's counsel cancelled the October 2008 and November 2008 depositions, Plaintiff accepted these cancellations without seeking court intervention.

3. **Delay in hiring investigator**: Although Plaintiff eventually hired a private investigator, this occurred only after significant delay, and the investigator was able to locate Defendant.

4. **Failure to seek court enforcement**: At no point prior to Defendant's death did Plaintiff seek court-ordered enforcement of his discovery requests through contempt proceedings or motions to compel.

In *Shalongo v. Shamash*, 305 Pa. Super. 486, 451 A.2d 1005 (1982), this Court held that a party's failure to diligently pursue discovery weighs against a claim of prejudice sufficient to justify dismissal. The Plaintiff here had ample opportunity to seek court enforcement but chose not to do so.

### B. The Plaintiff's Own Conduct Contributed to the Result

Plaintiff cannot complain of prejudice when his own choices contributed to the situation. By failing to seek timely court intervention and by accepting repeated cancellations without objection, Plaintiff enabled the very circumstances he now cites as prejudice. A party who fails to avail himself of available legal remedies cannot later claim that the resulting prejudice warrants the extreme sanction of default judgment.

---

## III. THE RECORD LACKS EVIDENCE THAT DEFENDANT WAS NEGLIGENT OR THAT HIS CONDUCT CAUSED THE FIRE

### A. The Trial Court's Error in Assuming Negligence

The trial court's reasoning appears to have assumed that Defendant was negligent and that his conduct caused the fire. However, the record contains no evidence establishing these essential elements of Plaintiff's claim.

The facts clearly establish:

1. **The fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove**—not from any conduct of Defendant.

2. **The rapid spread of the fire was caused by kerosene** leaking from a defective container stored near the stove—a condition for which Defendant bears no apparent responsibility.

3. **Defendant was asleep on the couch** when the fire started and was awakened by the fire.

4. **Defendant escaped the residence** while Ms. Plotts remained trapped inside.

There is no evidence in the record that Defendant caused the fire, negligently or otherwise. The cause was an unattended cooking vessel and the rapid spread was attributable to improperly stored kerosene. These conditions existed independent of Defendant's presence or conduct.

### B. The Prejudicial Assumption of Liability

The trial court's entry of default judgment effectively presumed that Defendant was liable for the fire—a finding that has no support in the evidence. Default judgment is appropriate only where the moving party establishes a prima facie case, not where the court assumes facts not in evidence. See *Mithrush v. Rubault*, 503 Pa. 110, 468 A.2d 1071 (1983).

Here, even if Defendant had provided deposition testimony, there is no reason to believe he would have admitted to negligence. His statement to the state trooper on the night of the fire expressly denied that he was the cause of the fire. Without additional evidence, Plaintiff's claim rests on speculation, not on the precluded testimony of Defendant.

---

## IV. THE ENTRY OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT WAS MANIFESTLY UNJUST

### A. The Proportionality Principle

The entry of default judgment must be proportionate to the underlying violation. In *Bostic v. Schwan's Home Serv.*, 157 A.3d 25 (Pa. Super. 2017), this Court emphasized that dismissal is appropriate only where the violation is so severe that no lesser sanction can address the prejudice. That standard has not been met here.

The discovery violations, while real, did not involve the wholesale refusal to participate in litigation. Defendant did not hide assets, destroy evidence, or engage in other conduct that would justify the most severe sanction. His failures were attributable to his counsel's inability to locate him—a situation that, while regrettable, does not warrant the death penalty of a default judgment.

### B. The Harshness of the Remedy

Default judgment effectively adjudicates the merits of the case against a party who has not had the opportunity to present a defense. Here, Defendant died in January 2012, and the default judgment was entered in 2013-2014. The entry of judgment against a deceased person's estate, based on discovery violations that occurred during his lifetime, is extraordinarily harsh and warrants careful scrutiny.

The prejudice to Plaintiff, while real, was not irremediable at the time of the violations. Plaintiff could have sought court enforcement, moved for sanctions short of default judgment, or taken other action. His failure to do so cannot now be transformed into a basis for the most severe sanction available.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **Reverse** the trial court's orders dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013;

2. **Vacate** the entry of default judgment against Defendant;

3. **Remand** this matter for further proceedings consistent with this opinion; and

4. **Grant** such other and further relief as this Court deems just and proper.

The trial court's entry of default judgment was a manifest abuse of discretion. The record demonstrates that lesser sanctions were available, that Plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery, that there was no evidence of Defendant's negligence or causation, and that the extreme remedy of default judgment was disproportionate to the violations alleged. Justice requires reversal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s imposition of sanctions for discovery violations is whether the court abused its discretion. As established in *In re Estate of Wernick*, 545 Pa. 533, 681 A.2d 1295 (1996), the entry of default judgment is an extreme remedy that requires a meticulous balancing of five factors: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the degree of willfulness or bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence. Similarly, *Werner v. Werner*, 267 Pa. Super. 292, 406 A.2d 780 (1979), mandates that a trial court carefully balance these factors before imposing such a severe sanction.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The record reflects a history of non-compliance spanning several years, including missed depositions in 2007, 2008, and 2010, and a refusal to be videotaped in 2009. While the prosecution characterizes this as a "dismal record," the defense correctly notes that a significant portion of this non-compliance was attributed to counsel’s inability to locate the defendant, rather than the defendant’s deliberate flouting of court orders. The specific refusal to be videotaped in April 2009 is a point of contention; however, the defense argues this does not rise to the level of "bad faith" necessary to justify the "death penalty" of litigation, as defined in *Bobilya v. Bobilya*, 277 Pa. Super. 162, 419 A.2d 736 (1980). The defense’s distinction between evasion and inability to be found is legally relevant to the "willfulness" factor.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The prosecution argues that the defendant’s death in January 2012 rendered the prejudice irremediable, as he was the sole eyewitness. However, the defense presents a compelling argument regarding the plaintiff’s lack of diligence. As cited in *Shalongo v. Shamash*, 305 Pa. Super. 486, 451 A.2d 1005 (1982), a party’s failure to diligently pursue discovery weighs against a claim of prejudice sufficient to justify dismissal. The timeline shows significant gaps in enforcement by the plaintiff; for instance, after the defendant failed to appear in August 2007, the plaintiff did not file a motion for contempt until May 2009. The defense argues that the plaintiff’s inaction contributed to the inability to secure the testimony before the defendant’s death, thereby mitigating the claim of irremediable prejudice.

**C. Importance of the Precluded Evidence**

The prosecution asserts the defendant’s testimony was indispensable. However, the defense effectively counters this by referencing the factual findings that the cause of the fire was an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container. The defense argues that because the defendant was asleep on the couch when the fire started, his ability to testify to the origin of the fire was speculative, and the physical evidence already established the cause. This challenges the prosecution's assertion that the precluded evidence was of paramount importance to the core issues.

**D. Availability of Lesser Sanctions**

The most significant argument raised by the defense concerns the trial court’s failure to consider lesser sanctions. Under *Koch v. Board of Directors of West Shore School District*, 336 Pa. Super. 183, 485 A.2d 482 (1984), a trial court must impose the least severe sanction necessary to cure the prejudice. The defense identifies several alternatives, such as monetary sanctions, adverse inference jury instructions, or the preclusion of specific defenses, which could have addressed the prejudice without adjudicating the case on the merits. The prosecution’s reliance on *Bostic v. Schwan's Home Serv.*, 157 A.3d 25 (Pa. Super. 2017), is less persuasive here because the defense has demonstrated that the violation, while serious, may not have been so egregious as to preclude all other remedies.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the arguments presented, the defense has demonstrated a strong likelihood that the trial court abused its discretion. The trial court failed to adequately consider the plaintiff’s lack of diligence in pursuing the deposition and did not fully explore lesser sanctions that would have remedied the prejudice without resorting to the extreme remedy of default judgment. Furthermore, the defense has raised valid questions regarding the indispensability of the defendant’s testimony given the physical evidence of the fire's cause.

**THEREFORE, it is hereby ORDERED that the preliminary ruling finds in favor of the Defendant-Appellant.** The trial court’s orders entering default judgment are likely to be reversed, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the imposition of lesser sanctions.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s imposition of sanctions for discovery violations is whether the court abused its discretion. As established in *In re Estate of Wernick*, 545 Pa. 533, 681 A.2d 1295 (1996), the entry of default judgment is an extreme remedy that requires a meticulous balancing of five factors: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the degree of willfulness or bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence. Similarly, *Werner v. Werner*, 267 Pa. Super. 292, 406 A.2d 780 (1979), mandates that a trial court carefully balance these factors before imposing such a severe sanction.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The prosecution argues that the defendant’s refusal to be videotaped in April 2009 constituted "egregious conduct" under *Bobilya v. Bobilya*, 277 Pa. Super. 162, 419 A.2d 736 (1980). However, the defense correctly distinguishes *Bobilya*, which involved the intentional destruction of financial records, from the present case involving a dispute over the method of discovery. While the defendant’s refusal to be videotaped was a violation, the record shows that significant periods of non-compliance (2007–2008) were due to counsel’s inability to locate the defendant, rather than a deliberate flouting of court orders. The defense successfully argues that "inability to locate" is distinct from "willful evasion." Therefore, the conduct, while problematic, does not rise to the level of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct required to justify the "death penalty" of litigation without first considering lesser remedies.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The prosecution contends that the defendant’s death rendered the prejudice irremediable, making default judgment the only option. However, the defense’s argument regarding the plaintiff’s lack of diligence is dispositive. Under *Shalongo v. Shamash*, 305 Pa. Super. 486, 451 A.2d 1005 (1982), a party’s failure to diligently pursue discovery weighs against a claim of prejudice sufficient to justify dismissal. The record reveals that after the defendant failed to appear in August 2007, the plaintiff waited nearly two years until May 2009 to file a motion for contempt. This significant delay contributed directly to the inability to secure the testimony before the defendant’s death. Because the plaintiff shares responsibility for the loss of the testimony due to this lack of diligence, the claim of irremediable prejudice solely attributable to the defendant is weakened.

Furthermore, the prosecution’s assertion that lesser sanctions are "meaningless" is unpersuasive. As noted in *Koch v. Board of Directors of West Shore School District*, 336 Pa. Super. 183, 485 A.2d 482 (1984), a trial court must impose the least severe sanction necessary to cure the prejudice. An adverse inference jury instruction is a standard and meaningful remedy for the loss of testimony, allowing the jury to infer that the missing testimony would have been unfavorable to the defendant. This would have addressed the prejudice without adjudicating the case on the merits.

**C. Importance of the Precluded Evidence**

The prosecution argues the defendant’s testimony was indispensable as the sole eyewitness. The defense effectively counters this by pointing to the physical evidence establishing the cause of the fire: an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container. The defense notes that the defendant was asleep when the fire started, meaning his testimony regarding the *origin* would have been speculative. While his testimony regarding his own conduct (e.g., handling the kerosene) was relevant, the physical evidence provided a substantial basis for the plaintiff’s case independent of the defendant’s account. The trial court failed to adequately weigh the sufficiency of this physical evidence against the need for the defendant’s testimony.

**D. Availability of Lesser Sanctions**

The trial court abused its discretion by failing to consider lesser sanctions as mandated by *Koch*. The defense identifies several viable alternatives, including monetary sanctions for the delay and adverse inference instructions for the missing testimony. The prosecution’s reliance on *Bostic v. Schwan's Home Serv.*, 157 A.3d 25 (Pa. Super. 2017), is misplaced because the defendant’s conduct here, involving a dispute over videotaping and periods of being unlocatable, does not match the "outrageous" obstruction and evidence destruction present in *Bostic*.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion. The trial court failed to properly account for the plaintiff’s lack of diligence in pursuing the discovery, which contributed to the loss of testimony. **FINAL RULING**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s imposition of sanctions for discovery violations is whether the court abused its discretion. As established in *In re Estate of Wernick*, 545 Pa. 533, 681 A.2d 1295 (1996), the entry of default judgment is an extreme remedy that requires a meticulous balancing of five factors: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the degree of willfulness or bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence. The defendant’s conduct here, while non-compliant, does not demonstrate the same level of bad faith or intent to destroy the judicial process found in those cases. Therefore, the trial court abused its discretion in treating the violation as so severe as to mandate default judgment without first exhausting lesser remedies.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The prosecution contends that the defendant’s death rendered the prejudice irremediable, making default judgment the only option. The defense, however, successfully demonstrates that the plaintiff’s own lack of diligence contributed significantly to this prejudice.
