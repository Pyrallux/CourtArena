# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the plaintiff's suppression motion. The plaintiff contends that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional because it allegedly stemmed from an unlawful pat-down of the defendant's person. However, the plaintiff's argument mischaracterizes the facts and ignores critical legal distinctions that fatalize their position. As demonstrated below, the search of the vehicle was supported by independent, valid consent given by the defendant at a time when he was free to leave, and no unlawful pat-down of his person occurred that would taint the evidence discovered. Accordingly, this Court should deny the suppression motion.

## II. FACTUAL SUMMARY FAVORABLE TO THE DEFENSE

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Applicable Legal Standards

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. A search conducted pursuant to valid consent is one of the well-established exceptions to the warrant requirement. See Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The voluntariness of consent is a question of fact to be determined from the totality of the circumstances. Id. at 248-49.

Additionally, under the independent source doctrine, even if evidence were derived from an unlawful search, evidence obtained through an independent, lawful source may still be admissible. See Murray v. United States, 487 U.S. 533 (1988). Similarly, the attenuation doctrine holds that evidence may be admissible if the connection between the unlawful conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

### B. The Plaintiff's Characterization of the Facts Is Incorrect

The plaintiff argues that "the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional because it stemmed directly from an unlawful pat-down of his person." This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the plaintiff conflates two distinct interactions. The officer's testimony establishes that after the defendant was told he was free to go, and after the officer began walking back to his vehicle, the defendant re-engaged the officer. It was only at that point that the officer asked if he "could ask him a question" and then requested consent to search the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25.) This was not a continuation of the detention; it was a separate, consensual encounter initiated after the defendant was explicitly told he was free to leave.

Second, the plaintiff has not established that a pat-down of the defendant's person actually occurred. The officer's testimony indicates that the defendant placed his hands on the side of the vehicle and stated "I can search him"—apparently referring to the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The transcript does not reflect that the officer actually conducted a pat-down search of the defendant's person. The court itself noted it "did not necessarily accept, or even follow, the officer's reasoning as to why defendant's saying he was lost" constituted suspicious activity. The court found only that "subjectively, the officer was suspicious of further criminal behavior."

Third, even assuming arguendo that some form of limited search of the defendant occurred, the vehicle search was independently supported by the defendant's voluntary consent. The consent was given at a time when the defendant was free to leave, when the traffic matter had been resolved, and when no detention existed. Under the independent source doctrine, the valid consent purges any taint from any prior unlawful conduct. See Murray v. United States, 487 U.S. 533 (1988).

### C. The Consent Was Valid Under the Totality of the Circumstances

Applying the totality of the circumstances test from Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the consent was clearly voluntary:

1. **The defendant was not in custody.** He was told repeatedly that he was free to go. He had a ride coming. He was not handcuffed or detained in any manner.

2. **The officer used no coercion or force.** The officer's weapon was not drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5.) The officer had no safety concerns. (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4.) The encounter was calm and conversational.

3. **The defendant was cooperative and agreeable.** The officer described the defendant as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.) The defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative." (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19.)

4. **The consent was explicit.** The defendant placed his hands on the vehicle and stated he could search it. This was not a ambiguous or equivocal response.

5. **The timing confirms voluntariness.** The consent was given after the officer began walking away, after the defendant was told he was free to go, and after the defendant knew the officer had completed his official business. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would have felt free to decline.

### D. The Plaintiff Cannot Invoke Fruit of the Poisonous Tree Because There Was No Initial Unlawful Search

The plaintiff relies on the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine from Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). However, that doctrine applies only when the initial search is unlawful. Here, there was no unlawful search of the defendant's person. The officer testified he had no safety concerns (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), but this does not mean he conducted an unlawful pat-down. The facts do not establish that a pat-down of the defendant's person occurred at all, let alone that it was unlawful.

Moreover, even if the plaintiff could establish an unlawful pat-down, the vehicle search was supported by independent, valid consent. The consent was not the product of the unlawful pat-down; it was a separate, free-will act occurring after the defendant was told he was free to go. Under the attenuation doctrine, the connection between any alleged unlawful pat-down and the consent is so attenuated that the consent purges the taint. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

### E. Arizona v. Johnson Is Inapplicable

The plaintiff cites Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323 (2009), for the proposition that a pat-down requires reasonable suspicion of danger. That case is inapposite because it addressed the standards for a Terry stop, not the validity of a consensual search occurring after a lawful encounter has concluded. Here, the vehicle search was not based on any pat-down or seizure—it was based on the defendant's voluntary consent given at a time when he was free to leave.

## IV. CONCLUSION

The plaintiff has failed to meet its burden of establishing that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional. The evidence demonstrates that:

1. The defendant was told he was free to go before any search request was made;
2. The defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle;
3. The consent was given under circumstances indicating voluntariness;
4. No unlawful pat-down of the defendant's person occurred that would taint the evidence; and
5. Even if any prior unlawful conduct occurred, the valid consent was an independent source that purges any taint.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the plaintiff's suppression motion and admit the evidence discovered during the search of the defendant's vehicle.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the record, the factual findings, and the opening arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The issue before the Court is whether the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional and whether the evidence discovered during that search should be suppressed. The prosecution argues the search was unconstitutional because it stemmed from an unlawful pat-down of the defendant's person. The defense argues the search was valid based on the defendant's voluntary consent, which was given after the traffic stop had concluded and the defendant was told he was free to leave.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down of the Defendant**

The prosecution correctly identifies the legal standard governing pat-downs. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a police officer may conduct a brief, investigatory stop only if they have reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. Furthermore, an officer may frisk a suspect for weapons only if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009).

The facts provided support the prosecution's contention that the pat-down was unlawful. The officer testified explicitly that he had "no safety concerns" during the encounter. (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). He further testified that the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative," and that there were "no indicia of intoxication" and "no furtive or suspicious movements." (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 31:5-11). Without reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous, the officer's pat-down of the defendant's person violated the Fourth Amendment. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

**B. The Search of the Vehicle**

While the prosecution succeeds in establishing the unlawfulness of the pat-down, they fail to demonstrate that the evidence discovered in the vehicle must be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree." *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The defense correctly argues that the search of the vehicle was supported by the defendant's voluntary consent, which was obtained during a distinct interaction following the conclusion of the traffic stop.

The Fourth Amendment recognizes that a search conducted pursuant to voluntary consent is valid. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The voluntariness of consent is determined by the totality of the circumstances. *Id.* at 248-49.

The record indicates that the traffic matter was resolved before the request to search the vehicle was made. The officer completed the paperwork for the suspended license citation and explained the license seizure procedures to the defendant. (Suppression Tr. 18:3-25). Critically, the officer testified that he told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). When asked if the defendant was free to drive the car, the officer confirmed that the defendant stated his girlfriend was coming to pick him up, and the officer reiterated, "I told him he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:11-18). The officer again stated, "I told him he was free to go... After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

It was only after the officer began walking away and the defendant re-engaged him in conversation that the officer asked for consent to search the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The defendant then placed his hands on the side of the vehicle and stated, "I can search him," which the officer described as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18, 23:18-24:2).

Under the "independent source" doctrine, evidence obtained through a source independent of the unconstitutional conduct is admissible. *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988). Even if the initial pat-down was unlawful, the defendant's consent to search the vehicle was obtained after the seizure had ended, as evidenced by the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" and his attempt to terminate the encounter. The defendant's re-engagement of the officer and subsequent voluntary consent constitute an intervening act of free will sufficient to break the causal chain between the unlawful pat-down and the discovery of the evidence in the vehicle. See *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has established that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to pat-down the defendant, rendering that specific action unconstitutional. However, the defense has shown that the search of the vehicle was based on the defendant's voluntary consent, which was given after the traffic stop had concluded and the defendant was informed he was free to leave. Therefore, the evidence discovered in the vehicle is not the fruit of the unlawful pat-down.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress the evidence obtained from the vehicle search is **DENIED**.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal misapplies the controlling precedent and ignores critical factual distinctions that separate this case from those upon which they rely. While the defense acknowledges that the initial pat-down presents a closer question, the prosecution fails to carry its burden under *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), to demonstrate that the consent to search the vehicle was the fruit of the unlawful pat-down rather than an independent act of free will. The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the temporal and causal sequence of events, conflating the initial traffic encounter with the subsequent consensual search. This Court should maintain its preliminary ruling denying the motion to suppress because: (1) the prosecution has failed to establish that the pat-down was anything more than a de minimis technical violation that did not taint the subsequent consent; (2) the attenuation analysis under *Brown* weighs decisively in favor of the defense; and (3) the consent to search the vehicle was voluntarily given under the totality of circumstances following a valid termination of the traffic encounter.

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S ATTENUATION ARGUMENT FAILS TO ACCOUNT FOR THE DISTINCT NATURE OF THE ENCOUNTER

The prosecution erroneously treats the entire interaction as a single continuous seizure, ignoring the clear factual record demonstrating that the traffic matter was fully resolved before any request for a vehicle search was made. The prosecution's reliance on *Brown v. Illinois* is misplaced because the facts here are readily distinguishable from those in *Brown*, where the defendant was arrested in his apartment, taken to the police station, and questioned for two hours before giving consent. 422 U.S. at 592-93. Here, by contrast, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 19:11-18, 21:5-12), the traffic matter was fully resolved, and the defendant was awaiting his girlfriend's arrival to pick him up. The prosecution's assertion that "the consent request followed the pat-down within seconds" ignores the substantial period between the pat-down and the consent request—a period during which the traffic matter was concluded, the defendant was told he was free to leave, and the officer began walking away before the defendant re-engaged him in conversation.

The prosecution's characterization of the encounter as a continuous "detention" from beginning to end contradicts the officer's own testimony that the defendant was "free to go" and that "our time was pretty — we were finished" before the request for consent to search was made. This Court found as a fact that "at some point during the encounter that night he formed a determination to search defendant['s] car," but the record demonstrates this determination was formed after the traffic stop concluded. The prosecution cannot escape the fact that the consent was obtained during a distinct, voluntary interaction initiated by the defendant's re-engagement of the officer, not during the traffic stop itself.

## III. THE BROWN FACTORS WEIGH IN FAVOR OF ADMITTANCE

Even assuming arguendo that the pat-down was unconstitutional, the prosecution cannot satisfy the attenuation doctrine under *Brown v. Illinois*, which requires the government to prove that the consent was sufficiently attenuated from the constitutional violation. The *Brown* Court identified three factors to consider: (1) the time elapsed between the illegality and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. 422 U.S. at 603-04. Each factor weighs in favor of the defense.

**First**, substantially more than "seconds" elapsed between the pat-down and the consent. The officer testified that approximately fifteen minutes elapsed from when he pulled in behind the defendant's vehicle "from then going back to my car[,] writing the citation, writing the vehicle seizure [sic] form, and running the NCIC check and license check" (Suppression Tr. 19:19-20:6), and that approximately twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop till when the evidence was found (Suppression Tr. 24-25). The pat-down occurred after the traffic matter was concluded, and the consent request came after the officer began walking away following his explanation of the license seizure procedures. This is not a case where consent was obtained immediately upon the heels of an unlawful seizure.

**Second**, significant intervening circumstances distinguish this case. The defendant was told he was "free to go," the traffic matter was fully resolved, the officer began walking away, and the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation. The defendant was not in custody; he was awaiting a ride from his girlfriend. These intervening circumstances break the causal chain between any potential constitutional violation and the subsequent consent. The prosecution's argument that no "new information" emerged ignores the critical fact that the entire context of the interaction changed—from a traffic stop to a consensual conversation between two individuals who had completed their official business.

**Third**, the prosecution cannot establish "flagrant misconduct" warranting suppression. The officer's conduct, even if erroneous, was not egregious or deliberate. The officer had fifteen years of experience and was investigating a potential license suspension. The pat-down, if improper, was a brief investigative action motivated by officer safety concerns, however minimal. There is no evidence of egregious misconduct, repeated violations, or deliberate disregard for constitutional rights. The prosecution's characterization of the officer's conduct as "flagrant misconduct" is unsupported by the record and hyperbolic.

## IV. THE CONSENT WAS VOLUNTARY UNDER THE TOTALITY OF CIRCUMSTANCES

The prosecution misapplies *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), by arguing that *Brown* somehow supersedes or negates the voluntariness analysis. To the contrary, *Brown* explicitly preserved the *Schneckloth* framework, holding that even voluntary consent may be tainted by an illegal seizure—but only if the government fails to prove attenuation. 422 U.S. at 602-03. The prosecution conflates two distinct inquiries: whether consent was voluntarily given (the *Schneckloth* question) and whether that voluntary consent was sufficiently attenuated from the constitutional violation (the *Brown* question). Even if this Court were to find that the consent was voluntarily given—which the defense submits it was—the prosecution still must prove attenuation under *Brown*, which it has failed to do.

The totality of circumstances demonstrates voluntary consent. The defendant was not in custody; he was told he was free to go and was awaiting a ride. The defendant was fully informed of the nature of the request—the officer asked if he "could ask him a question" before making any request to search (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The defendant was polite, cooperative, and "agreeable," and he affirmatively placed his hands on the vehicle to facilitate the search without prompting. The officer's weapon was not drawn, and there was no display of force or coercion. The defendant had every incentive to consent: he was not under arrest, he was about to be picked up by his girlfriend, and he had no reason to believe that declining would result in any adverse consequence.

The prosecution's argument that the defendant "automatically" placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2) somehow demonstrates coercion is unpersuasive. The officer testified that he found this behavior "unusual" and that he had "seen in the past when people are trying to be overly cooperative" (Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9). This suggests the defendant was eager to demonstrate his cooperativeness, not that he was coerced. The defendant's willingness to consent, while perhaps unusual, does not render the consent involuntary.

The prosecution's reliance on the absence of a warning regarding the right to refuse consent is unavailing. While such warnings are relevant to the voluntariness inquiry, they are not required under *Schneckloth*, and their absence does not automatically render consent involuntary. The prosecution has not cited, and this Court has not found, any authority holding that the absence of such a warning alone renders consent invalid under these circumstances.

## V. THE INDEPENDENT SOURCE DOCTRINE PROVIDES AN ALTERNATIVE BASIS FOR ADMISSION

Although not necessary to this Court's ruling, the prosecution's blanket rejection of the independent source doctrine is erroneous. The prosecution argues that the vehicle search "was not an independent source" because it "was the direct continuation of the unlawful pat-down." This argument conflates the temporal proximity of the events with the causal connection between them. The independent source doctrine, as articulated in *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988), allows admission of evidence obtained through an independent source even if the evidence would not have been discovered but for the illegal police conduct. The doctrine reflects the view that "the interest in protecting the integrity of the independent source doctrine... outweighs the incremental deterrence value that suppression would provide." 487 U.S. at 542.

Here, the officer's decision to request consent to search the vehicle was based on his suspicions regarding the defendant's story about being lost and his questions about the license seizure procedures—information obtained through the consensual encounter, not through the pat-down. The officer testified that he "kind of made a statement. I said, we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have" (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25). This statement, and the defendant's subsequent consent, were based on the officer's observations during the consensual portion of the encounter, not on information obtained during the pat-down. The prosecution has not established that the officer would not have requested consent to search absent the pat-down, and the officer's testimony suggests he formed the intent to search based on his interactions with the defendant after the traffic matter was concluded.

## VI. CONCLUSION

The prosecution has failed to carry its burden of establishing that the consent to search the vehicle was tainted by the unlawful pat-down. The attenuation analysis under *Brown v. Illinois* weighs decisively in favor of the defense, given the substantial time elapsed between the events, the significant intervening circumstances, and the absence of flagrant misconduct. The consent was voluntarily given under the totality of circumstances, as the defendant was told he was free to go, the traffic matter was resolved, and the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation. The prosecution's attempt to conflate the traffic encounter with the subsequent consensual search contradicts the record and misapplies the governing precedent.

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons stated in this Court's preliminary ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court maintain its ruling denying the motion to suppress and admit the evidence discovered during the search of the vehicle.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue remains whether the evidence discovered in the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional pat-down. The prosecution argues that the pat-down was a flagrant violation of the Fourth Amendment that directly tainted the subsequent consent to search the vehicle. The defense argues that the consent was voluntarily given after the traffic stop had concluded and was sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Illegality of the Pat-Down**

The Court reaffirms its finding that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a frisk for weapons only if they have reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The officer testified unequivocally that he had "no safety concerns" during the encounter and that the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" with "no furtive or suspicious movements." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4; 33:3-19). Without specific facts indicating a threat to officer safety, the pat-down violated the Fourth Amendment. *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009).

**B. The Vehicle Search and Attenuation of the Taint**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was the fruit of this unlawful pat-down. To determine whether evidence obtained after a constitutional violation must be suppressed, courts apply the "attenuation doctrine" articulated in *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975). The prosecution bears the burden of showing that the evidence was obtained by means sufficiently distinguishable from the illegality to be purged of the primary taint. *Id.* at 598-99.

The *Brown* Court identified three factors relevant to this inquiry: (1) the temporal proximity of the illegality and the evidence; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Id.* at 603-04. Upon review of the record and the arguments presented, the Court finds that these factors weigh decisively in favor of the defense.

**1. Temporal Proximity and the Termination of the Seizure**

The prosecution characterizes the encounter as a continuous detention lasting twenty-five minutes. However, the record clearly establishes that the traffic seizure ended before the request for consent to search the vehicle was made. The officer testified that after completing the paperwork and explaining the license forfeiture procedures, he told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer reiterated, "I told him he was free to go... After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

This statement is critical. Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), a police encounter does not become a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment protection unless a reasonable person would believe they are not free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly dispelled that notion. The defendant was told he was free to leave, and the officer began to terminate the encounter. Any subsequent interaction was consensual, not a product of the initial traffic stop or the pat-down.

**2. Intervening Circumstances**

The presence of intervening circumstances further breaks the causal chain between the unlawful pat-down and the vehicle search. After being told he was free to go, the defendant did not leave. Instead, he "engaged [the officer] again in conversation" to ask about the license seizure procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). The officer explained the procedures a second time, and only then did he ask if he could ask the defendant a question. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

The defendant's voluntary re-engagement of the officer constitutes a significant intervening act of free will. The defendant was not under arrest, was not being detained, and was awaiting a ride from his girlfriend. His decision to continue the conversation and subsequently consent to the search was a voluntary choice made in an environment where the seizure had legally ended. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444 (1980).

**3. Purpose and Flagrancy of the Misconduct**

While the pat-down was unlawful, the prosecution's characterization of it as "flagrant misconduct" is not supported by the record. The officer's conduct, while erroneous in light of his admitted lack of safety concerns, was brief and did not involve the use of force, threats, or a prolonged detention. The officer had a valid basis for the initial traffic stop (the suspended license), and the pat-down, though unjustified, yielded no results. The absence of egregious conduct diminishes the need for the deterrent effect of suppression under the attenuation analysis. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 603-04.

**4. The Independent Source of Consent**

Finally, the record indicates that the officer's request for consent was based on his observations during the consensual portion of the encounter, not on the results of the pat-down. The officer testified that he found the defendant's repeated questions about the ID procedures to be a "ruse" and that he found the defendant's offer to be searched "unusual." (Suppression Tr. 51:17; 56:21-57:9). These observations, made after the defendant was "free to go," formed the basis for the officer's reasonable suspicion to ask for consent. The pat-down, which found nothing, did not provide the probable cause or independent justification for the vehicle search. Therefore, the consent was not the product of the illegality. See *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has established that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional. However, they have failed to demonstrate that the consent to search the vehicle was the fruit of that illegality. The traffic stop had concluded, the defendant was told he was free to go, and the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation. Under the totality of the circumstances, the consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior unlawful conduct.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress the evidence obtained from the vehicle search is **DENIED**.

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the evidence was obtained by means "sufficiently distinguishable from the illegality to be purged of the primary taint." *Id.* at 598-99. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has carefully considered the closing arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The issue for determination is whether the evidence discovered in the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional pat-down. The prosecution argues that the pat-down constituted a flagrant violation of the Fourth Amendment that tainted the subsequent consent to search. The defense contends that the consent was voluntarily given after the seizure had terminated and was sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality. For the reasons that follow, the Court agrees with the defense and denies the motion to suppress.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Illegality of the Pat-Down**

The Court reaffirms its conclusion that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a frisk for weapons only if they have reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The officer testified unequivocally that he had "no safety concerns" during the encounter and that the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" with "no furtive or suspicious movements." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4; 33:3-19). Without specific facts indicating a threat to officer safety, the pat-down violated the Fourth Amendment. *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009). However, the illegality of the pat-down does not automatically require the suppression of all evidence obtained thereafter.

**B. Attenuation of the Taint**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was the fruit of this unlawful pat-down. To determine whether evidence obtained after a constitutional violation must be suppressed, courts apply the "attenuation doctrine" articulated in *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975). The prosecution bears the burden of showing that the evidence was obtained by means sufficiently distinguishable from the illegality to be purged of the primary taint. *Id.* at 598-99.

The *Brown* Court identified three factors relevant to this inquiry: (1) the temporal proximity of the illegality and the evidence; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Id.* at 603-04. Upon review of the record and the arguments presented, the Court finds that these factors weigh decisively in favor of the defense.

**1. Termination of the Seizure and Temporal Proximity**

The prosecution characterizes the encounter as a continuous detention lasting twenty-five minutes, emphasizing the short time between the pat-down and the request for consent. However, this analysis ignores the critical dispositive fact: the traffic seizure ended before the request for consent was made.

The officer testified that after completing the paperwork and explaining the license forfeiture procedures, he told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer reiterated, "I told him he was free to go... After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), a police encounter does not constitute a seizure unless a reasonable person would believe they are not free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly dispelled that notion. While the prosecution correctly notes that the officer's lights were still activated and a backup officer was present, these facts do not negate the officer's clear verbal statement that the defendant was free to leave and his physical act of disengaging by walking away. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 554 (1980) (stating that a seizure occurs only if, in view of all the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave).

Because the seizure had ended, the temporal proximity between the pat-down and the consent is less significant. The consent was not obtained during the coercive environment of a detention, but rather during a consensual encounter initiated by the defendant.

**2. Intervening Circumstances**

The presence of intervening circumstances further breaks the causal chain between the unlawful pat-down and the vehicle search. After being told he was free to go, the defendant did not leave. Instead, he "engaged [the officer] again in conversation" to ask about the license seizure procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). The officer explained the procedures a second time, and only then did he ask if he could ask the defendant a question. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

The defendant's voluntary re-engagement of the officer constitutes a significant intervening act of free will. The defendant was not under arrest, was not being detained, and was awaiting a ride from his girlfriend. His decision to continue the conversation and subsequently consent to the search was a voluntary choice made in an environment where the seizure had legally ended. The prosecution's argument that the defendant felt coerced by the presence of police lights is undermined by the defendant's own initiative to restart the dialogue with the officer.

**3. Purpose and Flagrancy of the Misconduct**

While the pat-down was unlawful, the prosecution's characterization of it as "flagrant misconduct" is not supported by the record. The officer's conduct, while erroneous in light of his admitted lack of safety concerns, was brief and did not involve the use of force, threats, or a prolonged detention. The officer had a valid basis for the initial traffic stop (the suspended license), and the pat-down, though unjustified, yielded no results. The absence of egregious conduct diminishes the need for the deterrent effect of suppression under the attenuation analysis. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 603-04.

**C. Voluntariness of Consent**

The prosecution argues that the consent was involuntary because the defendant was not advised he could refuse and was subjected to a coercive environment. However, under the totality of the circumstances test set forth in *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the consent was voluntary.

The record indicates that the defendant "offered [the officer] to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" before the officer even asked. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). The defendant was not threatened, the officer's weapon was not drawn, and the defendant had been told he was free to leave. In this context, the defendant's offer to be searched and his subsequent consent to search the vehicle were acts of free will, not the product of the prior unlawful pat-down.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has established that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional. However, they have failed to demonstrate that the consent to search the vehicle was the fruit of that illegality. The traffic stop had concluded, the defendant was told he was free to go, and the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation. Under the totality of the circumstances, the consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior unlawful conduct.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress the evidence obtained from the vehicle search is **DENIED**.
