COURT ARENA LOG - Case 109
FACTS:
Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. Judgment of conviction was entered upon his plea of guilty after the charge against him was reduced from aggravated battery, as origi*380nally alleged. He later filed a motion under I.C.R. 35, seeking reduction of the sentence so he could be released for psychotherapy that he claimed was unavailable in confinement. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

Here, the victim of the crime was Galbraith’s seven-month-old daughter. Two incidents of abuse occurred. In the first, Galbraith kicked the child off a bed, cracking her ribs. In proceedings under the Child Protective Act, the girl was placed with her maternal grandparents and Galbraith was enrolled in a counseling program. However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

WALTERS, C.J., and SWANSTROM, J., concur.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Idaho Court of Appeals

### State of Idaho v. Preston Galbraith, Appellant

---

## INTRODUCTION

Mr. Preston Galbraith, through counsel, respectfully submits this opening response in support of his appeal from the district court's order denying his Idaho Criminal Rule 35 motion for reduction of sentence. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the district court's order and remand with directions to reduce the sentence, or alternatively, to conduct further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The prosecution characterizes the Rule 35 motion as a "pure plea for leniency" warranting deference to the district court's discretion. However, this characterization, while accurate as a matter of law, obscures the critical issue: whether the district court *abused* that discretion by failing to give adequate consideration to material evidence presented regarding rehabilitation prospects and the defendant's constitutional right to necessary medical treatment. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). We contend it did.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS RELEVANT TO DEFENSE

The following facts, drawn from the record, are essential to our analysis:

Mr. Galbraith was eighteen years old at the time of the offense. He had no prior criminal record of consequence. He graduated from high school and was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve at the time of the offense, though unemployed. He married his wife while both were in high school because she was pregnant.

The psychological evaluation conducted after his arrest revealed that Mr. Galbraith had been abused as a child. The evaluation concluded that he had "poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize." Significantly, the psychologist who prepared this evaluation characterized Mr. Galbraith as a "typically poor" candidate for therapy because he would have "difficulty owning responsibility for his anger and hostility." However, this pessimistic assessment came from a psychologist who did not specialize in treatment.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Mr. Galbraith introduced testimony from a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This expert was *more optimistic* about therapeutic prospects than the prior psychologist. He recommended that Mr. Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible, noting that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of successful outcome. This expert explicitly testified that the therapy Mr. Galbraith needed was *unavailable* at the Idaho State Correctional Institution.

Critically, however, this same expert *also* testified that if treatment were delayed until Mr. Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—there would *not* be a "substantial diminution in the probability of success."

The district court, in denying the motion, found that the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Mr. Galbraith remained in confinement was outweighed by the risk to society if he were released early. The court noted that the sentence would require approximately twenty months' total incarceration before parole eligibility.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO ADEQUATELY WEIGH THE PSYCHOLOGICAL EVIDENCE REGARDING REHABILITATION

A motion to reduce a sentence under Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); *State v. Russell*, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); *State v. Araiza*, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App.1985). However, this discretion is not unbounded. The court must give reasoned consideration to the evidence presented. *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984).

The prosecution argues that the district court properly weighed the factors and that this Court should defer to that balancing. We submit that the court's analysis was flawed in two critical respects.

**First**, the district court failed to give appropriate weight to the testimony of the specialized psychologist presented at the Rule 35 hearing. While the prosecution is correct that the original presentence psychologist characterized Mr. Galbraith as a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child," this opinion must be read in context. That same psychologist acknowledged that Mr. Galbraith was *not considered a danger to the general public*—only to the specific victim. The district court found that Mr. Galbraith presented a "continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry." However, the record does not support a finding of risk to "other defenseless persons." The psychological evaluation specifically limited the danger assessment to access to his child.

Moreover, the specialized psychologist who testified at the Rule 35 hearing offered a more optimistic prognosis. While the prosecution dismisses this as merely "more optimistic," the testimony came from an expert who *specialized* in the precise type of behavioral problems at issue—child and adolescent behavioral problems. The district court gave insufficient weight to this expert testimony.

**Second**, the district court misconstrued the evidence regarding the unavailability of therapy. The prosecution argues that because the expert "admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole... there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success," the court's conclusion was "rationally" supported. This reasoning is flawed.

The expert's testimony established two distinct propositions: (1) therapy was unavailable at the correctional institution, and (2) delaying treatment for approximately one year would not substantially diminish success probability. The logical conclusion from these propositions is NOT that the sentence should be maintained, but rather that the *incremental* benefit of immediate release is marginal. However, the court failed to consider that maintaining the sentence *entirely eliminates* the possibility of therapy during the entire period of incarceration, while the "delay until parole" scenario at least preserves the possibility of therapy after approximately twenty months.

The court's reasoning effectively punished Mr. Galbraith for the offense of being unable to access therapy *immediately* by denying him therapy *altogether* for the duration of his sentence. This does not represent a rational balancing of interests.

### II. THE SENTENCE, AS APPLIED, VIOLATES THE PROHIBITION ON CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT

Mr. Galbraith's appellate brief raised the issue of cruel and unusual punishment under both the state and federal constitutions. The prosecution argues this claim is untimely because he did not appeal the original judgment.

We acknowledge that under *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984), and *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983), this Court will not review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. However, the prosecution's characterization overstates the preclusive effect of these authorities.

The question presented is not whether the original sentence was constitutional when imposed, but whether the *denial of the Rule 35 motion* perpetuates a constitutional violation. A Rule 35 motion creates an "expanded record" available when the motion is entertained. The district court had before it evidence—that therapy was unavailable in confinement—that was not part of the original sentencing record. This new evidence is properly considered in evaluating whether continued enforcement of the sentence violates constitutional prohibitions.

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 9 of the Idaho Constitution prohibit cruel and unusual punishments. While the prosecution is correct that the primary objective of sentencing is protecting society, this objective is not achieved by denying all possibility of rehabilitation. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

Here, the sentence effectively denies Mr. Galbraith access to necessary psychological treatment while in confinement, yet the expert testimony established that treatment is unavailable at the correctional institution. A sentence that denies all access to rehabilitation resources, when such resources are specifically needed and are available in the community but denied by incarceration, may violate the proportionality principles underlying the cruel and unusual punishment prohibition.

The district court noted that the sentence "offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation." However, this finding ignores the expert testimony that therapy is *unavailable* at the correctional institution. The "possibility" of therapy is illusory if the institution lacks the resources to provide it. The court's assumption that therapy will be available upon parole does not cure the constitutional deficiency.

### III. THE DISTRICT COURT FAILED TO GIVE ADEQUATE CONSIDERATION TO MITIGATING FACTORS

The prosecution emphasizes the severity of the offense—and we do not minimize the harm to the seven-month-old victim. However, the court failed to give adequate consideration to mitigating factors that distinguish this case from others where full sentences were upheld.

Mr. Galbraith was eighteen years old at the time of the offense. He had no prior criminal record. He was employed (though unemployed at the precise moment of the offense, he was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve, demonstrating stability and commitment). He graduated from high school. He had been abused as a child himself—a mitigating factor that the court acknowledged but failed to weigh adequately in determining whether to reduce the sentence.

The prosecution notes that the offense occurred "while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident." This fact cuts both ways. On one hand, it demonstrates a pattern. On the other hand, it demonstrates that Mr. Galbraith was *already seeking help* and that the existing counseling was inadequate. The need for more intensive therapy, as testified to by the specialized psychologist, is directly related to this history.

The court found that Mr. Galbraith presented a "continuing risk." However, the psychological evaluation specifically stated he was not a danger to the *general public*—only to the victim if he had access to her. The sentence could be structured to permit rehabilitation while still protecting the victim—for example, by conditions of parole prohibiting contact with the child. The court's binary choice between full incarceration and unrestricted release failed to consider intermediate options.

### IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON THE DISCRETIONARY NATURE OF RULE 35 MOTIONS IS MISPLACED

The prosecution is correct that Rule 35 motions are discretionary and that appellate intervention is appropriate only upon a showing of abuse of discretion. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). However, the existence of discretion does not insulate the exercise of that discretion from review.

An abuse of discretion occurs when the court "failed to consider the proper legal standard" or "the facts presented do not meet the standard." Here, the district court applied the correct standard—weighing the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society—but *applied that standard incorrectly* by failing to give appropriate weight to the evidence presented.

The prosecution argues that the court gave "informed and sensitive consideration" to the issues. We submit that the record does not support this characterization. The court acknowledged the expert testimony that therapy was unavailable in confinement but failed to explain how this fact factored into its balancing analysis. The court assumed that therapy would be available upon parole but ignored the expert testimony directly contradicting this assumption.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Galbraith respectfully requests that this Court reverse the order of the district court denying his Rule 35 motion and remand with directions to reduce his sentence to time served with appropriate conditions of parole, including mandatory psychotherapy, or alternatively, to conduct further proceedings to obtain additional evidence regarding rehabilitation resources available in the community versus in confinement.

Alternatively, this Court should remand for a new hearing on the Rule 35 motion at which the district court is directed to give proper weight to the specialized psychologist's testimony and to make specific findings regarding the availability of therapy in the correctional institution versus in the community.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Appellant Preston Galbraith**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** [Not Provided]
**Appeal from the District Court of the State of Idaho.**

**Preliminary Ruling**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s motion for reduction of sentence under Idaho Criminal Rule 35. Galbraith appeals the denial of his motion, arguing that the district court abused its discretion and that the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. After reviewing the record, the arguments presented by counsel, and the applicable law, the Court affirms the district court’s order.

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985). Such a motion is essentially a plea for leniency, and appellate intervention is warranted only if the district court abused its discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). When reviewing an indeterminate sentence, this Court considers the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply equally to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying the Rule 35 Motion**

Galbraith argues that the district court abused its discretion by failing to give adequate weight to the psychological evidence regarding his rehabilitation and by misconstruing the unavailability of therapy in confinement. We disagree.

The district court expressly balanced the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society. The record supports the court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the benefit of early release. The victim was Galbraith’s seven-month-old daughter, who suffered severe injuries, including a complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma. Notably, the second assault occurred while Galbraith was already enrolled in a counseling program following a prior incident of abuse. This history supports the court’s finding that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to defenseless persons.

Regarding rehabilitation, Galbraith presented testimony from a clinical psychologist who recommended immediate psychotherapy. However, the district court was entitled to rely on the contradictory evidence in the record. The presentence report described Galbraith as having a “poor social conscience” and a “pronounced lack of ability to empathize,” with a “typically poor” prognosis for therapy. Furthermore, the defense expert admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility—approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—would not result in a “substantial diminution in the probability of success.” Given this admission, the district court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that the State’s interest in protecting society justified the approximately twenty months of incarceration required before parole eligibility.

The defense contends the court failed to consider mitigating factors, such as Galbraith’s age and lack of criminal record. While these factors are relevant, they are not dispositive. The severity of the offense, the vulnerability of the victim, and the fact that the violence occurred during a prior counseling attempt provide a sufficient factual basis for the court’s decision to maintain the sentence. The court’s consideration of these factors is evident from its findings, and its decision to prioritize public safety over immediate leniency falls squarely within the bounds of its sound discretion. See State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**B. The Cruel and Unusual Punishment Claim is Unavailing**

Galbraith also argues that the denial of his Rule 35 motion perpetuates a violation of the constitutional prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment because the therapy he needs is unavailable in confinement. As noted by the prosecution, Galbraith did not appeal the original judgment of conviction. Therefore, any constitutional attack on the sentence as originally pronounced is untimely. See State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983).

To the extent Galbraith seeks to challenge the sentence based on the "expanded record" of the Rule 35 proceeding, the claim fails on the merits. The Eighth Amendment and the Idaho Constitution do not require the State to provide a specific form of rehabilitation in lieu of incarceration, particularly where the defendant poses a demonstrated risk to the victim. The district court found that the sentence offered the possibilities of parole and therapy for eventual rehabilitation. Because the expert testified that a one-year delay in treatment would not substantially diminish the likelihood of success, the denial of the motion does not render the sentence cruel or unusual. The district court’s refusal to reduce the sentence was based on a reasoned consideration of the evidence and the statutory goals of sentencing, including protection of society. See State v. **STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** [Not Provided]
**Appeal from the District Court of the State of Idaho.**

**FINAL RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s motion for reduction of sentence under Idaho Criminal Rule 35. Galbraith appeals the denial of his motion, arguing that the district court abused its discretion and that the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court’s order.

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985). Appellate intervention is warranted only if the district court abused its discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). When reviewing an indeterminate sentence, this Court considers the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply equally to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Weighing Rehabilitation Evidence Against Risk to Society**

Galbraith argues that the district court abused its discretion by giving disproportionate weight to speculative future risk and minimizing the immediate need for rehabilitation. Specifically, the defense contends that the court misinterpreted the expert testimony regarding the delay of treatment and improperly expanded the risk assessment to include "defenseless persons" rather than limiting it to the specific victim.

The district court was presented with conflicting psychological evaluations. The presentence report indicated Galbraith had a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize," concluding he was a "typically poor" candidate for therapy. In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith presented a licensed clinical psychologist who was more optimistic and recommended immediate psychotherapy. However, this expert admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility—approximately one year after the hearing—would not result in a "substantial diminution in the probability of success."

The defense argues that "not substantial" does not mean "no impact," and that time is of the essence for a young offender. While this is a plausible policy argument, it does not demonstrate an abuse of discretion. The district court was entitled to rely on the expert's specific concession that the delay would not be substantially detrimental. The court expressly balanced the "risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985) (holding that a district court may credit contradictory evidence and is not bound by a single expert’s opinion). Given the severity of the injuries inflicted upon a seven-month-old infant, the court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate release was within its sound discretion.

Regarding the risk assessment, the defense argues the psychologist only identified a risk to the child, not the "general public," and that the court erred by finding a risk to "other defenseless persons." The record indicates the psychologist opined Galbraith represented a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child." However, the court also had before it the presentence report noting Galbraith’s "poor social conscience" and "lack of ability to empathize." The violent nature of the offense—striking a crying infant with a fist—supports the inference that Galbraith poses a risk to any similarly defenseless person, not solely his daughter. Galbraith argues that his constitutional claim is timely because it relies on the "expanded record" of the Rule 35 proceeding, specifically the unavailability of therapy in confinement. We are not persuaded.

Therefore, his constitutional attack on the sentence as originally pronounced is untimely. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. To the extent Galbraith challenges the denial of the motion itself based on the expanded record, the claim fails on the merits. The Eighth Amendment and the Idaho Constitution do not require the State to provide a specific form of rehabilitation in lieu of incarceration, particularly where the defendant poses a demonstrated risk to the victim. The defense contends the district court failed to explicitly weigh mitigating factors such as Galbraith’s age, lack of criminal record, military service, and history of childhood abuse. The court is not required to explicitly articulate the weight given to every mitigating factor so long as the record indicates the factors were considered. State v. Here, the court’s finding that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to defenseless persons, coupled with the egregious nature of the injuries (complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma), provides a sufficient factual basis to conclude that the mitigating factors did not warrant a reduction of the sentence. The court’s decision to prioritize public safety over leniency was an appropriate exercise of discretion.

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. The court properly balanced the severity of the offense and the risk to society against Galbraith’s rehabilitation prospects. The court’s reliance on the expert’s admission that a delay in treatment would not be substantially harmful, and its finding that Galbraith posed a risk to defenseless persons generally, were supported by the record and within the court’s discretion. Because the court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion, and because the sentence does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment, the order of the district court is affirmed.

Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985). Appellate intervention is warranted only if the district court abused its discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). When reviewing a sentence, this Court considers the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply equally to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Assessing Risk to Society**

Galbraith argues the district court abused its discretion by expanding the risk assessment to include "other defenseless persons" when the psychologist’s testimony specifically limited the danger to Galbraith’s own child and stated he was "not considered a danger to the general public." We find this argument unpersuasive.

While the psychologist opined that Galbraith represented a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child," the record also contains the psychologist’s observations that Galbraith had a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize." The violent nature of the offense—striking a seven-month-old infant in the head with a fist, causing a complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma—supports the inference that Galbraith poses a risk to any similarly defenseless person who might provoke his anger. The district court’s finding that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to "defenseless persons" was a reasonable inference drawn from the psychological profile and the facts of the case. It was not an abuse of discretion for the court to conclude that the risk extended beyond the specific victim to other vulnerable individuals. See State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985) (holding that a district court may credit contradictory evidence and draw reasonable inferences therefrom).

**B. The District Court Properly Weighed Rehabilitation Evidence Against Public Safety**

Galbraith contends the district court misconstrued the defense expert’s testimony by interpreting "not substantial" to mean "no impact," thereby minimizing the need for immediate therapy. The record reflects that the defense expert recommended psychotherapy "as soon as possible" but admitted that if treatment were delayed until parole eligibility—approximately one year after the hearing—there would not be a "substantial diminution in the probability of success."

The district court expressly balanced the "risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." The court was entitled to rely on the expert’s specific concession that the delay would not be substantially detrimental. The defense’s argument that "not substantial" implies some meaningful impact is a policy disagreement, but it does not demonstrate an abuse of discretion. Given the severity of the injuries and the court’s determination that Galbraith posed a continuing risk, the court’s decision to prioritize public safety over the marginal benefit of immediate release was within its sound discretion. See State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982) (recognizing protection of society as the primary objective of sentencing).

**C. The Cruel and Unusual Punishment Claim is Without Merit**

Galbraith argues that his constitutional claim is timely because it relies on the "expanded record" of the Rule 35 proceeding, specifically the unavailability of therapy in confinement. We are not persuaded.

As a general rule, a defendant must appeal the judgment of conviction to challenge the original sentence. Galbraith did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence; he appealed only the order denying his Rule 35 motion. Therefore, his constitutional attack on the sentence as originally pronounced is untimely. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983).

To the extent Galbraith challenges the denial of the motion itself based on the expanded record, the claim fails on the merits. The Eighth Amendment and the Idaho Constitution do not require the State to provide a specific form of rehabilitation in lieu of incarceration, particularly where the defendant poses a demonstrated risk to the victim. The district court noted that the sentence offered the possibilities of parole and therapy for eventual rehabilitation. Because the defense expert testified that a one-year delay in treatment would not substantially diminish the likelihood of success, the denial of the motion does not render the sentence cruel or unusual. The district court’s refusal to reduce the sentence was based on a reasoned consideration of the evidence and the statutory goals of sentencing. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

**D. Mitigating Factors Were Properly Considered**

Galbraith argues the district court failed to explicitly weigh mitigating factors such as his age, lack of criminal record, military service, and history of childhood abuse. The record shows the district court was aware of these factors, as they were present in the presentence report and arguments. However, the court found that the severity of the offense and the fact that it occurred while Galbraith was already enrolled in counseling outweighed these mitigating circumstances.

The court is not required to explicitly articulate the weight given to every mitigating factor so long as the record indicates the factors were considered. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). Here, the court’s finding that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to defenseless persons, coupled with the egregious nature of the injuries, provides a sufficient factual basis to conclude that the mitigating factors did not warrant a reduction of the sentence. The court’s decision to prioritize public safety over leniency was an appropriate exercise of discretion.

**III. Conclusion**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. The court properly balanced the severity of the offense and the risk to society against Galbraith’s rehabilitation prospects. The court’s reliance on the expert’s admission that a delay in treatment would not be substantially harmful, and its finding that Galbraith posed a risk to defenseless persons generally, were supported by the record and within the court’s discretion. Because the court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion, and because the sentence does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment, the order of the district court is affirmed.

**ORDER**

The final ruling is in favor of the State/Prosecution. The district court’s order denying the Rule 35 motion is affirmed.
