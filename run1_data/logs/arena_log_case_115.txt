COURT ARENA LOG - Case 115
FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1

The letters to the editor, on the other hand, are more opinionated. They do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt. Some are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race. We refuse, however, to find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society. But see Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. These few letters do not represent a society scornful of outsiders, as counsel Reardon would have us believe. Instead, by attempting to influence the public, these letters affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence.
One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

May it please this Court, the prosecution submits that the defendants have categorically failed to establish that a change of venue was warranted under any applicable legal standard. The trial court’s denial of the motion was correct, consistent with binding precedent, and grounded in the record—which reveals no basis for the extraordinary remedy sought.  

To warrant a change of venue based on pretrial publicity, defendants must meet an exceptionally high burden. Presumed prejudice—a remedy reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom"—requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The Supreme Court has reversed a conviction on this ground only once: in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where a defendant’s televised confession—airing "several times to tens of thousands of people" in the community—created an "atmosphere" so "pervasively exposed to such a spectacle" that "any subsequent court proceeding could be but a hollow formality." Id. at 727. Here, by contrast, defendants cited only six newspaper articles and editorials as evidence of prejudicial publicity, none of which the trial court deemed inflammatory. This de minimis coverage falls far short of the "massive, pervasive" standard required for presumed prejudice. See *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993) (stressing that "a showing of presumed prejudice is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases"); *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002) (requiring "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage" to establish presumed prejudice).  

Actual prejudice—requiring proof that "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured"—likewise cannot be established without voir dire. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999). As the trial court properly noted, "such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire." The defendants have offered no juror responses or evidence of community sentiment beyond their unsupported assertions. Cf. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725–28 (1961) (actual prejudice "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire").  

The defendants’ claim of "cultural bias against foreign-born defendants" is equally without merit. The trial court correctly rejected the notion that "a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society." These isolated opinions—described as "embarrassingly slanted" but "not inflammatory"—do not evidence systemic prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537 (finding that "a few letters to the editor" do not establish societal bias). Indeed, the court observed that such letters "affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence," and their publication reflects protected speech that "exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views." No evidence supports the defendants’ bald assertion of a "culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here."  

Moreover, a change of venue is not a feasible remedy in American Samoa. The trial court rightly explained that "we have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same." *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (confirming jury trials are "practical and not anomalous" in small island communities). A.S.C.A. § 46.0601—the statute cited by defendants—was enacted for "physical change[s] of venue" for logistical convenience, such as holding court in Manu’a for witness access, not to circumvent alleged prejudice. As the court noted, this statute "does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants." Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 21—which permits venue transfers to avoid prejudice—is inapplicable; American Samoa’s unique demographics render such transfers "a pipe dream."  

In sum, the defendants have not met their "heavy burden" to show either presumed or actual prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court’s denial was legally sound, factually grounded, and consistent with precedent. This Court should affirm.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE HIGH COURT OF AMERICAN SAMOA

---

**DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S OPPOSITION TO CHANGE OF VENUE**

---

May it please this Court, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's characterization of the record and the applicable law distorts the analysis this Court must undertake. While the prosecution correctly states that defendants bear a heavy burden, the defense contends that the trial court applied an overly restrictive interpretation of what constitutes presumptive prejudice, failed to properly weigh the unique circumstances of this small island community, and erred in its remedy analysis. The defense requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the change of venue motion.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT PRESUMED PREJUDICE WAS NOT ESTABLISHED

The prosecution argues that defendants cited only "six newspaper articles and editorials" as evidence of prejudicial publicity, characterizing this as *de minimis* coverage. This characterization ignores critical aspects of the record that the trial court itself acknowledged.

The trial court acknowledged that the letters to the editor "contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and are "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at *108. The court further acknowledged that "some are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." *Id.* at *108. This acknowledgment alone distinguishes this case from the *de minimis* scenario the prosecution portrays.

In *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), the Eleventh Circuit recognized that presumed prejudice analysis requires examining the "cumulative effect" of publicity, not merely counting articles. The court in *Coleman* emphasized that "the community atmosphere" must be assessed holistically. *Id.* at 1490. Here, the trial court acknowledged the existence of inflammatory letters expressing racial bias, yet failed to consider their cumulative effect alongside the news coverage.

The prosecution's reliance on *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), is misplaced. While *Rideau* involved a televised confession, the Supreme Court has since recognized that presumed prejudice can arise from various forms of pervasive publicity. In *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), the First Circuit noted that presumed prejudice analysis must account for the "totality of circumstances." *Id.* at 731. The unique characteristics of American Samoa—its small population, limited media outlets, and close-knit community—create conditions where even limited publicity may have disproportionately severe effects.

The prosecution argues that "no court has yet decided" that such coverage creates presumed prejudice. However, the standard is not whether identical facts have produced a finding of presumed prejudice, but whether the totality of circumstances here creates that presumption. The trial court found that the letters "do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and some are "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." This finding, unchallenged by the prosecution, establishes the kind of "atmosphere" that *Rideau* cautioned against.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING ACTUAL PREJUDICE ANALYSIS PREMATURE

The prosecution argues that actual prejudice cannot be established without voir dire, citing *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352 (6th Cir. 1999). However, this argument ignores the defense's ability to present other evidence of actual prejudice beyond juror responses.

In *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), the Supreme Court recognized that actual prejudice may be demonstrated through evidence of "preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors." *Id.* at 722. While voir dire is one means of demonstrating such prejudice, it is not the exclusive means. The defense presented evidence of pervasive publicity, including inflammatory letters expressing racial bias against the defendants. This evidence, combined with the unique circumstances of this small island community, supports an inference of actual prejudice.

The prosecution's argument that "defendants have offered no juror responses or evidence of community sentiment beyond their unsupported assertions" ignores the nature of the burden at this stage. The defendants need not prove actual prejudice with certainty; they need only demonstrate that "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). The combination of inflammatory publicity, expressions of racial bias in published letters, and the unique circumstances of this small community satisfies this standard.

Furthermore, the trial court's acknowledgment that "if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors" suggests that the court recognizes the difficulty defendants will face even after voir dire. This recognition supports granting the motion now rather than requiring defendants to await voir dire.

## III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING THE CULTURAL BIAS ARGUMENT

The prosecution argues that the trial court "correctly rejected the notion that 'a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society.'" However, this characterization misstates the defense's argument.

The defense did not argue that the letters represent the beliefs of an entire society. Rather, the defense argued that the publication of such letters—particularly in a small community with limited media outlets—creates an atmosphere where potential jurors may feel pressure to align with published sentiments. The trial court acknowledged that "the island's media outlets are virtual monopolies." *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at *109 n.2. This monopoly status amplifies the impact of any published content.

In *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966), the Supreme Court recognized that the trial court has an affirmative duty to protect the jury from outside influences. The Court stated that "the requirement that the jury's verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources" is fundamental. *Id.* at 351. Here, the trial court acknowledged the existence of "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" published in the only media available in American Samoa. This creates precisely the kind of outside influence *Sheppard* cautioned against.

The prosecution's argument that the letters "affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence" turns the analysis on its head. The proper response to inflammatory speech is not to publish it and then claim credit for exposing its absurdity. The damage to defendants' reputation occurs regardless of whether some readers recognize the letters as bigoted. The question is whether potential jurors have been exposed to these pronouncements, not whether they agree with them.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ITS REMEDY ANALYSIS

The prosecution argues that "a change of venue is not a feasible remedy in American Samoa." This argument, while addressed by the trial court, deserves closer examination.

The trial court stated that "transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same." *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at *109. This analysis fails to recognize that the remedy need not completely eliminate all potential prejudice; it need only reduce it to an acceptable level.

In *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269 (D. Vt. 1993), the court acknowledged that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives." *Id.* at 273. The court noted that "a change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial." *Id.* The defense does not dispute that alternative remedies may be appropriate. However, the trial court failed to seriously consider what those alternatives might be or whether they would adequately address the prejudice.

The prosecution cites *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11 (D.D.C. 1977), for the proposition that "jury trials, in serious criminal cases, have proven to be both practical and not anomalous in a relatively small island community." However, *King* addressed only the threshold question of whether jury trials were constitutionally required in American Samoa. It did not address the question of whether prejudice in particular cases might require alternative remedies.

The trial court's reliance on A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 is similarly unavailing. The statute authorizes the Chief Justice to "order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa." The prosecution argues this statute was enacted for logistical convenience, not to address prejudice. However, nothing in the statute's text limits its application to logistical concerns. The statute's broad language—"any appropriate place" and "the interest of justice"—encompasses situations where prejudice requires relocating proceedings.

Furthermore, even if the statute does not explicitly authorize transfer to another jurisdiction, nothing prevents this Court from exercising its inherent authority to ensure a fair trial. The prosecution's argument that "a change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere" describes the ideal remedy. However, the Constitution requires only a fair trial, not a perfect one. Transfer within American Samoa, while not eliminating all prejudice, may reduce it sufficiently to satisfy constitutional requirements.

## V. THE DEFENSE IS ENTITLE TO RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the change of venue motion. The combination of inflammatory publicity, expressions of racial bias in published letters, the monopoly status of island media, and the unique circumstances of American Samoa's small community creates both presumed and actual prejudice that cannot be adequately addressed through voir dire alone.

The defense does not seek a perfect remedy, only one sufficient to satisfy constitutional requirements. At minimum, this Court should order a transfer of proceedings to a different location within American Samoa, require expanded voir dire procedures, or consider other alternatives that would ensure a fair trial.

Respectfully submitted,

**DEFENSE COUNSEL**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court** is the consolidated appeal of Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, seeking reversal of the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants argue that the trial court erred in finding neither presumed nor actual prejudice necessitated a change of venue, and that the court failed to consider adequate remedies for the alleged bias. After reviewing the record, the prosecution’s opening argument, and the defense’s response, the Court affirms the lower court’s order.

**I. Standard of Review**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The burden rests heavily on the defendant to demonstrate that the locale is so prejudiced that a fair trial cannot be had. See *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). Due process requires a trial free from prejudice, passion, and excitement, but it does not guarantee a trial in a location entirely free of pretrial publicity. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961); *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966).

**II. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court erred by failing to find presumed prejudice based on the "cumulative effect" of the publicity and the "totality of circumstances," specifically highlighting the racially charged letters to the editor. To establish presumed prejudice, defendants must show that the publicity was so "massive" and "pervasive" that the entire community was infected with bias, rendering the trial a "hollow formality." See *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963); *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). This standard is reserved for "exceptional cases" and requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490; *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).

The record indicates that the publicity cited by the defense consisted of six newspaper articles and several letters to the editor. While the trial court acknowledged that the letters contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," it correctly characterized these as the views of a "very limited section of society." The defense relies on *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), to argue for a totality-of-the-circumstances approach. However, even under a holistic review, the volume and nature of the publicity here—factual news articles and a handful of opinionated letters—pales in comparison to the "televised confession" aired repeatedly to tens of thousands in *Rideau*. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the defendants failed to meet the "heavy burden" of showing presumed prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490.

**III. Actual Prejudice**

The defense contends that the trial court erred in finding the inquiry into actual prejudice premature. Actual prejudice is established by showing that it is "reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). This determination is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999) (citing *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28).

The defense argues that voir dire is not the exclusive method of proving actual prejudice, citing *Irvin*. However, *Irvin* involved a trial where the jurors had already been selected and demonstrated prejudice. In the present case, no jury selection has occurred. Without the benefit of juror questioning, any finding of actual prejudice would be purely speculative. The trial court properly noted that the ability to gauge public sentiment is best achieved "after voir dire." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273. Therefore, the trial court’s determination that the inquiry was premature was correct.

**IV. Remedy and Feasibility of Change of Venue**

Finally, the defense argues that the trial court erred in its remedy analysis, specifically regarding the interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and the feasibility of a change of venue in American Samoa. The defense contends that the statute’s language regarding the "interest of justice" permits a transfer to mitigate prejudice.

The trial court found that a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in American Samoa because the territory has "but one jury roll" comprising all registered voters. Moving the trial to a different physical location, such as Manu'a, would not change the jury pool. See *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (noting jury trials are practical in small communities). The defense argues that a transfer might reduce prejudice, even if not eliminate it. However, the Constitution requires a fair trial, not a change of venue where such a change would be futile. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273. Because the jury pool would remain identical, a physical transfer offers no remedy for the alleged prejudice.

The trial court’s interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 as a logistical tool, rather than a mechanism to circumvent prejudice, is reasonable given the statutory context and the "one jury roll" reality. Furthermore, the trial court offered an alternative remedy by allowing the submission of a voir dire questionnaire to screen for potential bias, thereby addressing the defendants' due process concerns without resorting to an impractical venue change.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants have failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion. The publicity, while containing some objectionable content, did not rise to the level of presumed prejudice required to overturn the venue decision, and the inquiry into actual prejudice was appropriately deferred until voir dire. The proposed remedy of a change of venue is legally and practically unavailable in this jurisdiction.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed. The defendants shall proceed with the preparation of the voir dire questionnaire as directed by the trial court.

The prosecution's argument that "media coverage of any bias" does not create constitutional prejudice misreads *Sheppard*. The Supreme Court in *Sheppard* emphasized the trial judge's obligation to protect the trial from "prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power." Here, the trial court acknowledged the existence of prejudice in the community but declined to take prophylactic action. This was an abuse of discretion.

## V. The Trial Court Erred in Its Remedy Analysis and Failed to Consider Adequate Alternatives

The prosecution argues that a change of venue is "legally and practically unavailable" in American Samoa, but this argument conflates the remedy with the underlying constitutional violation. The trial court correctly noted that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 was enacted for logistical convenience and does not contemplate a change of venue in the traditional sense. This is correct, but the Constitution does require a fair trial, and the trial court failed to adequately explore what remedies might ensure fairness.

The trial court should have considered additional remedies, including: (1) expanded voir dire with individual questioning of each prospective juror; (2) a change of venue to a location outside American Samoa, as permitted under federal rules; (3) transfer of the case to a different judge; or (4) dismissal of the charges with prejudice if a fair trial cannot be ensured. **PRELIMINARY RULING (UPDATED)**

**Before the Court** is the consolidated appeal of Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, seeking reversal of the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants argue that the trial court abused its discretion by failing to find presumed prejudice, by prematurely deferring the inquiry into actual prejudice, and by erroneously limiting the available remedies. Having considered the rebuttals of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the lower court’s order but clarifies the scope of available remedies should the voir dire process reveal incurable prejudice.

**I. Standard of Review**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The burden rests heavily on the defendant to demonstrate that the locale is so prejudiced that a fair trial cannot be had. See *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). Due process requires a trial free from prejudice, passion, and excitement, but it does not guarantee a trial in a location entirely free of pretrial publicity. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961); *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966).

**II. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court erred by failing to find presumed prejudice based on the "cumulative effect" of the publicity and the "totality of circumstances," specifically highlighting the racially charged letters to the editor and the "virtual monopoly" of media in American Samoa. To establish presumed prejudice, defendants must show that the publicity was so "massive" and "pervasive" that the entire community was infected with bias, rendering the trial a "hollow formality." See *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963); *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). This standard is reserved for "exceptional cases" and requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490; *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).

The defense contends that the combination of factual news articles and opinionated letters creates an environment of bias equivalent to the saturation in *Rideau*. However, while the trial court acknowledged that the letters contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," it correctly characterized these as the views of a "very limited section of society." The defense’s reliance on *Coleman* for a "totality of the circumstances" approach does not lower the threshold for presumed prejudice. Even considering the small size of the community and the media monopoly, the volume and nature of the publicity here—six articles and a handful of letters—pales in comparison to the "televised confession" aired repeatedly to tens of thousands in *Rideau*. The prosecution correctly notes that isolated expressions of bias, however offensive, do not equate to the community-wide prejudice required to overturn a venue decision. See *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the defendants failed to meet the "heavy burden" of showing presumed prejudice.

**III. Actual Prejudice**

The defense contends that the trial court erred in finding the inquiry into actual prejudice premature, arguing that the language in *Nevers v. Killinger* allows for an inference of prejudice based solely on the nature of the publicity. Actual prejudice is established by showing that it is "reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). This determination is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999) (citing *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28).

The defense argues that the word "both" in *Nevers* implies that the nature of publicity alone can support a finding of actual prejudice. The Court finds this interpretation unpersuasive. The *Nevers* court emphasized juror responses as a critical component of the inquiry. Without the benefit of juror questioning, any finding of actual prejudice would be purely speculative. The prosecution correctly asserts that *Irvin* itself involved a trial where jurors had already been selected and demonstrated prejudice; here, no jury selection has occurred. The trial court properly noted that the ability to gauge public sentiment is best achieved "after voir dire." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273. Therefore, the trial court’s determination that the inquiry was premature was correct.

**IV. Remedy and Feasibility of Change of Venue**

Finally, the defense argues that the trial court erred in its remedy analysis by categorically stating that a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in American Samoa and by failing to consider transfer to a location outside the territory under Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a). The defense contends that the court’s reliance on A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 was too narrow and that the Constitution requires the court to explore all alternatives to ensure a fair trial.

The trial court found that a change of venue within American Samoa is futile because the territory has "but one jury roll" comprising all registered voters. Moving the trial to a different physical location, such as Manu'a, would not change the jury pool. See *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). The Court agrees with the trial court’s assessment of internal transfers. However, the defense is correct that the trial court’s analysis should not be read to foreclose the possibility of transfer to a federal district court outside the territory if the local jury pool is found to be hopelessly tainted. See *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (noting that a change of venue is not constitutionally required, but a fair trial is).

Nevertheless, because the defendants have not yet demonstrated actual prejudice, the drastic remedy of transfer to a jurisdiction outside American Samoa is not warranted at this juncture. The trial court’s alternative remedy—allowing the submission of a voir dire questionnaire to screen for potential bias—is a prudent and necessary first step to ascertain whether the local jury pool can yield impartial jurors. If the voir dire reveals that prejudice is so pervasive that a fair trial cannot be had in American Samoa, the trial court must then revisit the issue of remedy, including the feasibility of transfer under federal rules. At this stage, however, the denial of the motion is proper.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants have failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion. The publicity, while containing some objectionable content, did not rise to the level of presumed prejudice required to overturn the venue decision, and the inquiry into actual prejudice was appropriately deferred until voir dire. The proposed remedy of a change of venue is not warranted absent a showing that the local jury pool is incapable of producing a fair verdict.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed. The defendants shall proceed with the preparation of the voir dire questionnaire as directed by the trial court. The trial court is instructed to keep open the possibility of extra-territorial remedies should the voir dire process reveal that a fair trial cannot be conducted within American Samoa.

## II. REBUTTAL: THE PROSECUTION MISCHARACTERIZES THE STANDARD FOR PRESUMED PREJUDICE

### A. The "Exceptional Cases" Standard Is Not a Categorical Bar

The prosecution correctly notes that presumed prejudice is reserved for "exceptional cases." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The defense is not required to produce evidence of "oversaturation" measured by article count or broadcast frequency alone. ### B. The Cumulative Effect of Publicity and Community Characteristics

The prosecution dismisses the media monopoly as irrelevant, but this factor is constitutionally significant. In a jurisdiction where all registered voters comprise the jury pool—a fact explicitly acknowledged by this Court in *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. The prosecution's comparison to *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), is instructive but not controlling. The *Nevers* court used the word "both" deliberately—indicating that the **nature** of the publicity is itself a component of the analysis that can support an inference of prejudice. The purpose of the change of venue motion is **preventive**—to ensure a fair trial rather than to remediate a tainted trial after the fact.

The appellate court's ruling keeps open the possibility of extra-territorial remedies "should the voir dire process reveal that a fair trial cannot be conducted within American Samoa." This suggests that the appellate court recognizes the potential for prejudice that voir dire might confirm. The defense submits that the better approach is to **presume** that prejudice may exist in such circumstances and to provide protective mechanisms proactively, rather than waiting to confirm prejudice after it has infected the proceedings.

The defense submits that the trial court should not have categorically dismissed the possibility of extra-territorial transfer. While the defense acknowledges that no prejudice has yet been demonstrated through voir dire, the prosecution's argument that the defense must "prove" prejudice before any remedy is considered turns the constitutional analysis on its head. **FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court** is the consolidated appeal of Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, seeking reversal of the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants challenge the trial court’s findings regarding presumed prejudice, the timing of the actual prejudice inquiry, and the scope of available remedies. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue but modifies the order to ensure the trial court adequately addresses the specific risks of bias identified by the defense.

**I. Standard of Review**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The burden rests heavily on the defendant to demonstrate that the community is so prejudiced that a fair trial cannot be had. See *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). Due process requires a trial free from prejudice, passion, and excitement, but it does not guarantee a trial in a location entirely free from pretrial publicity. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961); *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966).

**II. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court erred by failing to find presumed prejudice based on the "totality of circumstances," specifically the "cumulative effect" of the publicity, the "virtual monopoly" of the media, and the racially charged nature of the letters to the editor. They contend that in a small community with a single jury roll, the qualitative impact of the publicity is magnified, warranting a finding of presumed prejudice even if the volume of coverage is modest compared to mainland cases.

To establish presumed prejudice, defendants must show that the publicity was so "massive" and "pervasive" that the entire community was infected with bias, rendering the trial a "hollow formality." See *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963); *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). This standard is reserved for "exceptional cases" and requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490; *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).

The defense correctly notes that the inquiry is qualitative as well as quantitative. However, even considering the unique characteristics of American Samoa—a small population, a single jury roll, and a media monopoly—the nature of the publicity here does not rise to the level of inherent prejudice required to overcome the high bar set by *Rideau*. In *Rideau*, the Supreme Court found presumed prejudice where a televised confession was broadcast repeatedly to tens of thousands of people. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726-27. Here, the record reflects six news articles and a handful of letters to the editor. While the trial court acknowledged that the letters contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," it characterized these as the views of a "very limited section of society."

The defense’s argument that the "effective exposure" in a small community is proportionally higher is noted, but it does not transform opinionated letters into the type of judicially condemned evidence that creates a presumption of guilt. The prosecution correctly argues that isolated expressions of bias, however offensive, do not equate to the community-wide prejudice required to overturn a venue decision. See *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the defendants failed to meet the "heavy burden" of showing presumed prejudice.

**III. Actual Prejudice**

The defense contends that the trial court erred in finding the inquiry into actual prejudice premature, arguing that the language in *Nevers v. Killinger* allows for an inference of prejudice based solely on the nature of the publicity. Actual prejudice is established by showing that it is "reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). This determination is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999) (citing *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28).

The defense argues that the word "both" in *Nevers* implies that the nature of publicity alone can support a finding of actual prejudice, rendering the wait for voir dire unnecessary. The Court finds this interpretation unpersuasive in the context of a pre-trial motion. While the nature of the publicity is a critical component of the analysis, the *Nevers* court emphasized juror responses as the necessary mechanism to discern whether that publicity has actually tainted the jury pool. Without the benefit of juror questioning, any finding of actual prejudice would be purely speculative. The prosecution correctly asserts that *Irvin* itself involved a trial where jurors had already been selected and demonstrated prejudice; here, no jury selection has occurred. The trial court properly noted that the ability to gauge public sentiment is best achieved "after voir dire." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273. Therefore, the trial court’s determination that the inquiry was premature was correct.

**IV. Remedy and Scope of Voir Dire**

Finally, the defense argues that the trial court erred in its remedy analysis by categorically stating that a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in American Samoa and by failing to explicitly consider transfer to a location outside the territory. The defense requests that this Court direct the trial court to consider extra-territorial transfer if prejudice is found and to expand the scope of the voir dire questionnaire to include specific inquiries about national origin bias.

The trial court found that a change of venue within American Samoa is futile because the territory has "but one jury roll" comprising all registered voters. Moving the trial to a different physical location would not change the jury pool. See *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). The Court agrees with the trial court’s assessment of internal transfers. However, the defense is correct that the trial court’s analysis should not be read to foreclose the possibility of transfer to a federal district court outside the territory if the local jury pool is found to be hopelessly tainted. See *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (noting that a change of venue is not constitutionally required, but a fair trial is).

The trial court’s order allowing the submission of a voir dire questionnaire is a prudent first step. However, given the trial court’s own acknowledgment that the letters were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," the Court finds that the defense’s request for specific inquiries into potential bias based on national origin is reasonable and necessary to ensure the "appropriate steps" are taken to establish impartiality. Therefore, the trial court is directed to include questions in the voir dire process that specifically address potential bias relating to the defendants' race or national origin.

Furthermore, if the voir dire reveals that prejudice is so pervasive that a fair trial cannot be had in American Samoa, the trial court must revisit the issue of remedy. While internal transfer is futile, the trial court must then actively consider the feasibility of transfer to a federal district court outside the territory under Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) or other applicable authority, rather than dismissing the possibility as a "pipe dream."

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants have failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion for change of venue based on presumed or actual prejudice. The publicity, while containing some objectionable content, did not rise to the level of inherent prejudice required to overturn the venue decision at this stage. The inquiry into actual prejudice was appropriately deferred until voir dire.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed. The trial court is directed to modify the voir dire questionnaire to include specific inquiries regarding potential bias based on the defendants' national origin or ethnicity. The trial court is further instructed to keep open the possibility of extra-territorial remedies, including transfer to a federal district court outside American Samoa, should the voir dire process reveal that a fair trial cannot be conducted within the territory.
