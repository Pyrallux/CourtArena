## DEFENSE OPENING BRIEF

### I. INTRODUCTION

Appellant Gerald Lalor respectfully submits this opening brief in response to the State's petition to affirm his commitment under Wisconsin Statute Chapter 980. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the trial court's commitment order because: (1) the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person; (2) the trial court erroneously admitted the results of pl-ethysmograph testing; and (3) the commitment order violates substantive due process because the trial court failed to make a finding that Lalor is unable to control his behavior.

---

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establishing the basis for this appeal are drawn from the record below.

On February 8, 1999, the State petitioned for Lalor's detention under Wisconsin's Sexually Violent Person Act, alleging that Lalor was a "sexually violent person" within the meaning of Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The State predicated its petition on Lalor's 1992 conviction for first-degree sexual assault of a child and the assertion that he was within ninety days of discharge from his sentence.

Dr. Hugh Moore, a licensed psychologist, examined Lalor in September 1998 and diagnosed him with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. Dr. Moore administered the Rapid Risk Assessment for Sexual Offense Recidivism (RRASOR), which yielded scores suggesting a 49.8% chance of reoffending within five years and a 73.1% chance within ten years. Dr. Moore also administered the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R).

Dr. Sheila Fields conducted a court-ordered evaluation and concluded that Lalor's "mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act." Dr. Fields relied on multiple actuarial instruments including the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG.

The trial court appointed Dr. Lynn Maskel as a defense expert. Dr. Maskel's testimony was "limited to exposing the problems associated with the testing instruments employed by Moore and Fields."

On February 8, 1999, the State filed its petition. Following a probable cause hearing on February 15, 1999, the trial court found probable cause and ordered Lalor remain in custody for evaluation. The matter proceeded to trial in February 2000.

Following the State's case, Lalor moved for a directed verdict, arguing the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court denied this motion.

On March 1, 2000, Lalor filed a motion for reconsideration and a new trial, challenging the admission of pl-ethysmograph test results and the sufficiency of evidence. The trial court denied this motion on April 19, 2000. Lalor now appeals.

---

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the trial court's factual findings under the clearly erroneous standard. However, the application of statutory and constitutional standards to those facts presents questions of law subject to de novo review. The question of whether the evidence was sufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person is a question of law this Court reviews independently.

#### B. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR IS A SEXUALLY VIOLENT PERSON

The State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the respondent is a "sexually violent person" under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). This requires proof that: (1) the person suffers from a mental disorder; and (2) the person's mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence. Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7).

The prosecution's opening argument mischaracterizes the nature of Dr. Maskel's testimony. While the State characterizes her testimony as merely "criticizing methodologies" without "contradicting the underlying diagnosis," this Court should recognize that attacking the reliability of actuarial instruments goes to the very heart of whether the State proved its case.

In **State v. Post**, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995), this Court defined "substantial probability" as requiring "more than a mere possibility but less than a certainty." However, this standard cannot be satisfied by actuarial instruments that carry significant margins of error and have not been validated for the purpose to which they were applied here.

Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed fundamental problems with the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG instruments. These instruments were developed using populations and methodologies that may not translate reliably to individual commitment proceedings. As this Court recognized in **State v. Dodd**, 2000 WI 61, 235 Wis. 2d 374, 611 N.W.2d 432 (Wis. 2000), the reliability of actuarial instruments in predicting future dangerousness is a critical issue that affects whether the State has met its burden.

The prosecution argues that "uncontradicted" evidence supports the commitment. However, Dr. Maskel's testimony did contradict the State's evidence—not by offering an alternative diagnosis, but by demonstrating that the instruments relied upon by the State's experts were scientifically unreliable for the purpose of predicting future sexual violence in an individual case. This constitutes competent evidence creating reasonable doubt.

The RRASOR, for example, yields probability estimates based on group data applied to individuals. The 49.8% and 73.1% figures represent population-based recidivism rates, not individualized predictions. As Dr. Maskel testified, these instruments cannot account for individual characteristics, treatment progress, or other mitigating factors specific to Lalor.

This Court should hold that when a defense expert successfully challenges the reliability of actuarial instruments, and no other sufficient evidence supports the commitment, the State has failed to meet its burden beyond a reasonable doubt.

#### C. THE TRIAL COURT ERRONEOUSLY ADMITTED PL-ETHYSMOGRAPH TEST RESULTS

The record indicates that the trial court admitted results from pl-ethysmograph testing. Lalor raised this issue in his motion for reconsideration.

Pl-ethysmograph testing measures physiological responses to sexual stimuli. This testing has been widely criticized as unreliable and lacking scientific foundation. In **State v. Taylor**, 2000 WI 37, 233 Wis. 2d 85, 608 N.W.2d 17 (Wis. 2000), this Court addressed the admissibility of scientific evidence and held that such evidence must satisfy the requirements of Wis. Stat. § 907.02 and the standards articulated in **Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals**, 509 U.S. 579 (1993).

Pl-ethysmograph testing has not been validated for predicting future sexual behavior. The test measures physiological arousal, not the capacity to control behavior or the likelihood of committing future acts. Its admission violated evidentiary standards and prejudiced Lalor by allowing the jury to consider unreliable evidence bearing on his alleged dangerousness.

This Court should reverse and remand for a new trial excluding pl-ethysmograph evidence.

#### D. THE COMMITMENT ORDER VIOLATES SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS

The prosecution argues that no separate "unable to control" finding is required because this element is "inherent" in the definition of mental disorder. This argument contradicts established constitutional requirements.

In **Kansas v. Hendricks**, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), the United States Supreme Court upheld Kansas's sexually violent person statute but explicitly recognized that constitutional validity depends on the statute requiring proof of "mental abnormality" that "predisposes" the person to commit sexually violent acts. The Court emphasized that civil commitment for dangerousness requires proof that the person's control over his behavior is substantially impaired.

The prosecution cites **State v. Laxton**, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002), for the proposition that no separate finding is required. However, this Court in **Laxton** merely held that a trial court need not use specific "magic words" when the record demonstrates that the required findings were made. The Laxton Court did not hold that evidence of mental disorder alone suffices without any finding regarding lack of volitional control.

Here, the trial court found that Lalor suffers from pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and that these disorders create a substantial probability he will reoffend. However, the trial court made no finding—and the State presented no evidence—establishing that Lalor is unable to control his behavior.

Pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder are serious conditions, but their presence does not automatically establish lack of volitional control. Many individuals with these diagnoses can and do control their behavior. The Constitution requires individualized proof that the person's mental disorder creates a situation where he cannot control his dangerous impulses.

In **State v. Curiel**, 2000 WI 73, 236 Wis. 2d 189, 612 N.W.2d 631 (Wis. 2000), this Court recognized that the "lack of volitional control" requirement is a constitutionally necessary element of Chapter 980 commitments. The trial court's failure to make this finding constitutes reversible error.

The prosecution's reliance on Hendricks is misplaced. In Hendricks, the Supreme Court emphasized that the Kansas statute required proof of "the inability to control behavior." 521 U.S. at 358. The Court stated that "the Constitution does not readily permit confinement of dangerous persons who are capable of exercising control over their behavior." Id.

Here, the State presented no evidence that Lalor cannot control his behavior. The experts testified about statistical probabilities of recidivism, but probability of reoffense is not equivalent to inability to control. These are distinct concepts. A person may have a high statistical likelihood of reoffending yet still possess the capacity to choose not to reoffend.

This Court should reverse the commitment order because the trial court failed to make—and the State failed to prove—the constitutionally required finding that Lalor is unable to control his behavior.

2. Order that Lalor be released from custody;

3. In the alternative, remand for a new trial excluding pl-ethysmograph evidence; and

4. Grant such other and further relief as this Court deems just and proper.

**OPINION**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews the trial court's findings of fact under the clearly erroneous standard. *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000). The trial court's determination regarding the credibility of witnesses and the weight of the evidence is entitled to deference and will not be overturned unless the trial court was clearly erroneous or a witness is incredible as a matter of law. *Id.* Questions of law, including the interpretation of statutory requirements and constitutional due process challenges, are reviewed de novo.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Substantive Due Process and Volitional Control**

Lalor argues that the commitment order violates substantive due process because the trial court failed to make an explicit finding that he is "unable to control his behavior." The defense relies on *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), and *State v. Curiel*, 2000 WI 73, 236 Wis. 2d 189, 612 N.W.2d 631 (Wis. 2000), to assert that a diagnosis of mental disorder alone is insufficient to prove a lack of volitional control.

The State counters that a separate finding regarding volitional control is not required when the mental disorder diagnosed inherently involves such a lack of control, citing *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002). In *Laxton*, the court held that the "inability to control" element is satisfied if the record supports the finding, even if the trial court does not use specific "magic words." *Id.*

The trial court found that Lalor suffers from pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and that these disorders create a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence. The State presented expert testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields linking these specific disorders to a high risk of recidivism. Under *Laxton*, the trial court's finding that Lalor is a "sexually violent person" under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) necessarily encompasses a determination that his mental disorder results in a lack of volitional control. The defense's argument that the State failed to present specific evidence on "inability to control" is unpersuasive because the diagnoses of pedophilia and psychopathy, as presented by the State's experts, are conditions recognized under *Hendricks* and *Laxton* as bearing on the individual's ability to control behavior. The trial court's findings are sufficient to satisfy due process.

**B. Sufficiency of Evidence and Actuarial Risk Assessment**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt because the actuarial instruments used (RRASOR, MnSOST, V-RAG) are unreliable for individual prediction. The defense cites *State v. Dodd*, 2000 WI 61, 235 Wis. 2d 374, 611 N.W.2d 432 (Wis. 2000), and argues that Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed fundamental flaws in these tools, creating reasonable doubt.

The State argues that the evidence was sufficient and that the trial court properly credited the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over the methodological criticisms of Dr. Maskel. The standard for commitment is "substantial probability," defined as "more than a mere possibility but less than a certainty." *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995).

The record reflects that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields utilized multiple actuarial instruments, all of which indicated a high risk of recidivism. Dr. Moore testified that Lalor had a 49.8% chance of reoffending within five years and a 73.1% chance within ten years. Dr. Fields reached a similar conclusion using a battery of tests. While Dr. Maskel challenged the reliability of these instruments, her testimony was limited to the problems associated with the testing tools and did not offer an alternative diagnosis or risk assessment. The trial court was in the best position to weigh the conflicting expert testimony regarding the reliability of the actuarial data. As held in *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000), appellate courts must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations. The trial court's finding that the State proved the elements of Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) beyond a reasonable doubt is not clearly erroneous.

**C. Admission of Pl-ethysmograph Test Results**

Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of pl-ethysmograph testing, claiming the evidence is unreliable and inadmissible under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and *State v. Taylor*, 2000 WI 37, 233 Wis. 2d 85, 608 N.W.2d 17 (Wis. 2000).

The record indicates that the trial court denied Lalor's motion for reconsideration regarding this issue. Even assuming arguendo that the admission of the pl-ethysmograph results was erroneous, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The State's case for commitment was supported by the uncontradicted diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and the compelling actuarial data from the RRASOR, PCL-R, and other instruments. The pl-ethysmograph evidence was cumulative of the other evidence regarding Lalor's risk to the community. Therefore, any error in its admission did not affect the substantial rights of the appellant.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court's commitment order is affirmed. The State met its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person, the trial court's findings are not clearly erroneous, and the commitment order does not violate substantive due process.

Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002).

**B. Sufficiency of Evidence and Actuarial Risk Assessment**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt because the actuarial instruments used (RRASOR, MnSOST, V-RAG) are unreliable for individual prediction. He argues that the statistical probabilities presented (49.8% in five years, 73.1% in ten years) represent only group data and cannot establish the "substantial probability" required for individual commitment with the certainty of "beyond a reasonable doubt." The defense asserts that the trial court erred by weighing the credibility of the experts rather than assessing the legal sufficiency of the evidence.

The State responds that the trial court properly credited the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over the methodological criticisms of Dr. Maskel. The State argues that *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000), requires this court to defer to the trial court's credibility determinations. The State further maintains that the definition of "substantial probability" found in *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995)—"more than a mere possibility but less than a certainty"—was met by the evidence.

We find the State's arguments persuasive. The standard of review for sufficiency of the evidence asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to convince a rational trier of fact of the defendant's guilt—or in this case, the elements of commitment—beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000). The defense conflates the burden of proof ("beyond a reasonable doubt") with the statutory threshold for commitment ("substantial probability"). The State must prove the elements of the statute beyond a reasonable doubt, but the element of dangerousness is defined as a "substantial probability" of reoffense. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995).

The record shows that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields utilized multiple actuarial instruments, all indicating a high risk of recidivism. Dr. Maskel's testimony was limited to exposing problems associated with the testing instruments; she did not offer an alternative diagnosis or risk assessment. The trial court was entitled to credit the testimony of the State's experts and to find that the actuarial data, corroborated by clinical judgment, established that Lalor's mental disorder created a substantial probability of reoffense. *State v. Dodd*, 2000 WI 61, 235 Wis. 2d 374, 611 N.W.2d 432 (Wis. 2000), does not hold that actuarial instruments are inadmissible or insufficient per se; rather, it emphasizes that trial courts must carefully evaluate the evidence. The trial court did so here. The defense's argument that 49.8% is statistically insufficient to prove "substantial probability" is a challenge to the weight of the evidence, which we review for clear error. The trial court's finding that the State met its burden is not clearly erroneous.

**C. Admission of Pl-ethysmograph Test Results**

Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of pl-ethysmograph testing and that this error was not harmless. He contends the evidence was uniquely probative of his sexual deviancy and highly prejudicial, making it impossible to conclude that the error did not affect the verdict under *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000), and *State v. Taylor*, 2000 WI 37, 233 Wis. 2d 85, 608 N.W.2d 17 (Wis. 2000).

The State maintains that even if the admission was erroneous, it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt because the commitment was supported by uncontradicted diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and compelling actuarial data.

We agree with the State. An error is harmless if an appellate court can conclude with confidence that the error did not affect the verdict. *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000). The State presented overwhelming evidence of Lalor's dangerousness, including the diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder, a PCL-R score consistent with psychopathy, and RRASOR scores indicating a 49.8% chance of reoffending in five years and a 73.1% chance in ten years. The pl-ethysmograph evidence was cumulative of the other evidence regarding Lalor's risk to the community. Given the strength of the other evidence, we conclude that the admission of the pl-ethysmograph results did not affect Lalor's substantial rights.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court's commitment order is affirmed. The State met its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person, the trial court's findings are not clearly erroneous, and the commitment order does not violate substantive due process.

He contends that *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002), does not control this case because the record lacks specific evidence linking his diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder to an inability to control behavior. The defense argues that a diagnosis alone is insufficient and that the State's evidence of high recidivism risk does not equate to a finding of volitional impairment, citing *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), and *State v. Curiel*, 2000 WI 73, 236 Wis. 2d 189, 612 N.W.2d 631 (Wis. 2000).

The State counters that *Laxton* permits the inference of lack of control when the mental disorder inherently involves such impairment. The State argues that pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder are universally recognized as involving volitional impairment and that the expert testimony regarding high recidivism risks supports the trial court's finding.

We agree with the State. In *Laxton*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "the trial court's finding that the respondent is a sexually violent person necessarily encompasses a determination that the respondent has a lack of volitional control when the mental disorder is such that it inherently involves lack of control." *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002). The trial court found that Lalor suffers from pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. While the defense correctly notes that *Hendricks* requires proof of a "mental abnormality" that makes it difficult for a person to control his or her behavior, *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 357-58 (1997), the trial court's acceptance of the State's expert testimony regarding the nature and consequences of Lalor's disorders satisfies this constitutional requirement.

The defense's argument that the record lacks specific evidence of "inability to control" mischaracterizes the nature of the expert testimony. Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields testified that Lalor's mental disorders create a substantial probability of reoffense. This testimony, linking the disorders to a high likelihood of future dangerous conduct, supports the inference that the disorders affect Lalor's volitional control. The defense's reliance on *Curiel* for the proposition that "predisposition" is distinct from "inability to control" does not require reversal where, as here, the trial court's findings are supported by expert testimony connecting the two. The trial court was not required to make an explicit, separate finding on volitional control where the finding of "sexually violent person" is supported by the record. *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002).

**B. Sufficiency of Evidence and Actuarial Risk Assessment**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt because the actuarial instruments used (RRASOR, MnSOST, V-RAG) are unreliable for individual prediction. He specifically argues that the RRASOR score of 49.8% represents a probability of reoffense that is statistically insufficient to constitute a "substantial probability," noting that this figure implies a 50.2% chance that he will not reoffend. The defense asserts that the trial court erred by weighing the credibility of the experts rather than assessing the legal sufficiency of the evidence, citing *State v. Dodd*, 2000 WI 61, 235 Wis. 2d 374, 611 N.W.2d 432 (Wis. 2000), for the proposition that actuarial tools must be carefully evaluated.

The State responds that the trial court properly credited the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over the methodological criticisms of Dr. Maskel. The State argues that *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000), requires this court to defer to the trial court's credibility determinations. The State further maintains that the definition of "substantial probability" found in *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995)—"more than a mere possibility but less than a certainty"—was met by the evidence, particularly the 73.1% chance of reoffending within ten years.

We find the State's arguments persuasive. The standard of review for sufficiency of the evidence asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to convince a rational trier of fact of the defendant's guilt—or in this case, the elements of commitment—beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000). The defense conflates the burden of proof ("beyond a reasonable doubt") with the statutory threshold for commitment ("substantial probability"). The State must prove the elements of the statute beyond a reasonable doubt, but the element of dangerousness is defined as a "substantial probability" of reoffense. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995).

The record shows that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields utilized multiple actuarial instruments, all indicating a high risk of recidivism. While the defense focuses on the 49.8% five-year risk, Dr. Moore also testified to a 73.1% chance of reoffending within ten years. This figure, coupled with the other actuarial data and clinical judgment, satisfies the *Post* standard. Dr. Maskel's testimony was limited to exposing problems associated with the testing instruments; she did not offer an alternative diagnosis or risk assessment. The trial court was entitled to credit the testimony of the State's experts and to find that the actuarial data, corroborated by clinical judgment, established that Lalor's mental disorder created a substantial probability of reoffense. *State v. Dodd*, 2000 WI 61, 235 Wis. 2d 374, 611 N.W.2d 432 (Wis. 2000), does not hold that actuarial instruments are inadmissible or insufficient per se; rather, it emphasizes that trial courts must carefully evaluate the evidence. The trial court did so here. The defense's argument that the evidence is legally insufficient because it relies on group data is a challenge to the weight of the evidence, which we review for clear error. The trial court's finding that the State met its burden is not clearly erroneous.

**C. Admission of Pl-ethysmograph Test Results**

Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of pl-ethysmograph testing and that this error was not harmless. He contends the evidence was uniquely probative of his sexual deviancy and highly prejudicial, distinguishing it from the other evidence in the case. He argues that under *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000), and *State v. Taylor*, 2000 WI 37, 233 Wis. 2d 85, 608 N.W.2d 17 (Wis. 2000), the error affected the verdict because it provided direct physiological evidence of dangerousness.

The State maintains that even if the admission was erroneous, it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt because the commitment was supported by uncontradicted diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and compelling actuarial data.

We agree with the State. An error is harmless if an appellate court can conclude with confidence that the error did not affect the verdict. *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 77, 236 Wis. 2d 320, 612 N.W.2d 533 (Wis. 2000). The State presented overwhelming evidence of Lalor's dangerousness, including the diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder, a PCL-R score consistent with psychopathy, and RRASOR scores indicating a 49.8% chance of reoffending in five years and a 73.1% chance in ten years. The pl-ethysmograph evidence was cumulative of the other evidence regarding Lalor's risk to the community. While the defense argues that the pl-ethysmograph provided "uniquely damning" physiological evidence, the trial court's finding of "sexually violent person" was supported by the confluence of the expert diagnoses and the high actuarial risk scores. Given the strength of the other evidence, we conclude that the admission of the pl-ethysmograph results did not affect Lalor's substantial rights.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court's commitment order is affirmed. The State met its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person, the trial court's findings are not clearly erroneous, and the commitment order does not violate substantive due process.
