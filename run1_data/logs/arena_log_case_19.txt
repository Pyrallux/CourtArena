FACTS:
Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy, a Georgia gynecologist, was indicted along with his office manager on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2).1 Specifically, appellant was charged with knowingly and wilfully accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled and in amounts greater than he was entitled from the State of Georgia Medicaid program because the services charged for were either “associated with the performance of elective abortions,” or had not been performed. Prior to the indictment, the Program Integrity Unit of the *351Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH)2 had conducted a review of appellant’s clinic looking for violations of the “Hyde Amendment” which prohibits the use of federal funds to pay for elective abortions. Based on its findings, DCH referred appellant’s case to the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit for a full investigation, while concurrently instituting a “withhold” on reimbursements to appellant’s Medicaid provider number.3

Appellant requested an administrative review of the withhold by DCH and, in response, received a letter informing him that based on its determination that appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid the withhold would remain in effect. Appellant then requested and was granted an administrative hearing before an administrative law judge (ALJ) on the issue of the withhold. 4 As DCH neither filed a motion for reconsideration or rehearing with respect to the ALJ’s decision nor sought to *352reject or modify it pursuant to the provisions of OCGA § 50-13-41 (e) (l),5 the ALJ’s decision was affirmed by operation of law, and effectively became the decision of DCH. See Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue, 316 Ga. App. 543 (728 SE2d 320) (2012). Thereafter, the “withhold” status was terminated, and the withheld funds were released to appellant.

During the period appellant was challenging the withhold, the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit continued to conduct its own investigation into the fraud allegations, including executing a search warrant at appellant’s clinic. Despite DCH’s affirmance of the ALJ’s decision finding no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, the State decided to seek criminal charges against appellant and his office manager and, on December 8, 2011, a DeKalb County grand jury issued indictments.

In response to the indictments, appellant filed a special demurrer and motion to strike surplusage, a plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel, and a motion to dismiss his indictment claiming the statute under which he was charged was unconstitutionally vague. Following separate hearings, the trial court denied each of appellant’s motions via separate orders. After his application for certificate of immediate review was denied by the trial court, appellant filed a notice of appeal seeking review of the trial court’s orders.

Your Honor, the trial court correctly denied Appellant’s plea in abatement because the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar criminal prosecution based on a prior civil administrative proceeding. Collateral estoppel—issue preclusion—requires that an issue be "actually litigated and necessarily decided" in a prior proceeding, but it never applies to prevent the State from prosecuting criminal charges after a civil or administrative ruling. This principle is firmly rooted in federal and Georgia precedent due to fundamental differences in the purpose, standard of proof, and procedural safeguards between civil/administrative actions and criminal prosecutions.  

As the Second Circuit held in *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d 702, 708 (2d Cir. 2000), "collateral estoppel does not apply to prevent the government from relitigating an issue in a criminal case after losing a civil case." The court emphasized that civil and criminal proceedings serve distinct purposes: civil cases resolve private disputes or administrative compliance, while criminal prosecutions protect public safety and enforce societal norms through punishment. The difference in burdens of proof alone—preponderance of the evidence in civil matters versus beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases—makes it "unjust and impractical" to apply estoppel between them. Here, the administrative proceeding before the ALJ addressed only whether Medicaid funds should be withheld under DCH’s civil recovery authority. That proceeding involved a preponderance standard and was designed to resolve reimbursement disputes, not to determine criminal liability.  

The Supreme Court solidified this rule in *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. 165, 174 (1984), where it held that "the Government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case." The Court explained that applying collateral estoppel between civil and criminal proceedings would "frustrate the Government’s ability to enforce criminal laws consistently" and ignore the "qualitative differences" in the two systems. Georgia courts have consistently applied this principle. In *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. 286, 288, 577 S.E.2d 594, 596 (2003), the Georgia Supreme Court rejected a collateral estoppel defense in a criminal case following a civil administrative ruling, stating that "the differing standards of proof and the distinct purposes of civil and criminal proceedings preclude application of issue preclusion between them."  

This case is a textbook example of why estoppel cannot apply. The ALJ’s hearing concerned a "withhold" on Medicaid reimbursements—a civil remedy under DCH’s administrative authority to recoup overpayments. The proceeding was governed by OCGA § 50-13-41, which outlines procedures for agency actions, not criminal trials. The ALJ evaluated whether there was sufficient evidence to justify the withhold under the civil "substantial evidence" standard, not whether Appellant acted "knowingly and willfully" under the criminal statute at OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The criminal indictment, by contrast, requires proof of specific intent to defraud beyond a reasonable doubt—a higher burden that the administrative record was never designed to address. The State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit conducted a separate criminal investigation, including executing a search warrant, precisely because the civil proceeding did not resolve the elements of criminal fraud.  

Allowing collateral estoppel to bar criminal prosecution here would undermine the State’s core authority to enforce its criminal laws. As *Thayer* recognized, "the Government must be free to pursue criminal charges when the civil standard of proof is not met, as the criminal standard is higher and the public interest in prosecution is paramount." The trial court properly recognized that the prior administrative ruling had no preclusive effect on the criminal case. Appellant’s plea in abatement fails as a matter of law, and this Court should affirm the trial court’s denial.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution asks this Court to adopt a categorical rule that collateral estoppel can never apply to bar criminal prosecutions following adverse civil administrative determinations. This broad pronouncement misstates the law and ignores the unique circumstances of this case. The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Thayer* and *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.* is misplaced because those cases addressed situations where the government had not fully litigated the identical factual issues in the prior civil proceeding. Here, the State of Georgia did fully litigate the precise factual questions underlying the criminal charges—namely, whether Dr. Malloy knowingly submitted false claims to Medicaid—and lost.

The prosecution's argument that collateral estoppel can never apply between civil and criminal proceedings conflates the general rule with the exception. Georgia law recognizes that collateral estoppel may apply in criminal cases when the identical issue was actually litigated and determined by a final judgment in a prior proceeding involving the same parties. The prosecution's position would permit the State to harass a citizen through successive proceedings on the same facts, first losing in a civil forum and then seeking to relitigate the same issues in a criminal prosecution—a result that offends fundamental fairness and the principles underlying issue preclusion.

This Court should reverse the trial court's denial of Appellant's plea in abatement.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The General Framework for Collateral Estoppel in Georgia

Georgia law recognizes the doctrine of collateral estoppel, also known as issue preclusion, which prevents relitigation of issues that have been actually litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. *See Atlanta Oculist Eye Clinic v. Foster*, 277 Ga. 343, 345-46, 588 SE2d 688, 691 (2003). To invoke collateral estoppel successfully, a party must establish that: (1) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (2) the judgment in the prior proceeding is final and concluded the rights of the parties on that issue; and (3) the party to be estopped had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue in the prior proceeding. *Id.*

The doctrine applies in criminal cases when the prior proceeding involved the same parties and the same issues. *See State v. Gerson*, 266 Ga. 138, 140, 464 SE2d 759, 761 (1995) (holding that collateral estoppel may bar relitigation of issues in criminal proceedings when the requirements are met).

### B. The Prosecution's Categorical Rule Is Overbroad

The prosecution contends that collateral estoppel can never apply to prevent criminal prosecution following a civil administrative ruling. This Court should reject this blanket pronouncement.

The United States Supreme Court's decisions in *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. 165 (1984), and *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d 702 (2d Cir. 2000), do not support a per se rule. In *Stauffer Chemical Co.*, the Supreme Court held that the government was not collaterally estopped from pursuing criminal charges following an adverse civil ruling, but the Court explicitly noted that its holding was based on the particular circumstances of that case—specifically, that the civil proceeding had not resulted in a final judgment on the merits when the government initiated the criminal prosecution. *Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. at 171-72.

Similarly, in *Thayer*, the Second Circuit emphasized that its holding was based on the absence of a final civil judgment and the government's lack of a full and fair opportunity to litigate in the civil proceeding. *Thayer*, 209 F.3d at 710-11. The court stated that "the government must have a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue in the civil proceeding" before estoppel might attach. *Id.* at 710.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. 286, 577 SE2d 594 (2003), is similarly unavailing. In *Williams*, the Georgia Supreme Court declined to apply collateral estoppel where the civil proceeding had not actually litigated the identical issues in the criminal case. The Court stated that "the differing standards of proof and the distinct purposes of civil and criminal proceedings preclude application of issue preclusion between them" *only* where the issues were not identical. *Williams*, 276 Ga. at 288, 577 SE2d at 596. The Court did not announce a categorical rule barring estoppel in all circumstances.

### C. This Case Presents the Exceptional Circumstances Where Collateral Estoppel Should Apply

The prosecution's blanket rule ignores the critical facts that distinguish this case from those relied upon by the prosecution:

**1. The Same Parties and Same Agency Participated in the Prior Proceeding**

Unlike *Thayer* and *Stauffer Chemical Co.*, where different government entities were involved in the civil and criminal proceedings, here the State of Georgia—specifically DCH and its Medicaid Fraud Control Unit—fully participated in the administrative proceeding and then pursued the criminal charges through the same executive branch. The prosecution cannot claim it lacked a full and fair opportunity to litigate when it was the moving party in the administrative proceeding.

**2. The Identical Factual Issues Were Actually Litigated and Determined**

The administrative proceeding addressed precisely whether Appellant had submitted false or improper claims to Medicaid—whether the services billed were associated with elective abortions or were not performed at all. These are the exact factual elements the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt to sustain the criminal convictions under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

The ALU found "no evidence" of fraud or willful misrepresentation. This was a definitive determination on the merits that the State had no basis to withhold Medicaid funds because Appellant had committed fraud. The State participated fully in that proceeding, presented evidence, and lost.

**3. The Administrative Proceeding Was Adversarial and Thorough**

The administrative hearing was not a summary review. It was a full adversarial proceeding before an ALU where DCH appeared through counsel, presented evidence, and argued for the withhold. Appellant was represented by counsel and had the opportunity to present a full defense. This was not a situation where the government lacked a full opportunity to develop its position.

**4. The State Failed to Seek Review of the Adverse Administrative Decision**

After losing before the ALU, DCH chose not to seek reconsideration, rehearing, or judicial review of the decision. By allowing the decision to become final without challenge, the State accepted the ALU's determination that no fraud occurred. The State cannot now relitigate the same facts in a criminal prosecution after voluntarily abandoning its administrative remedies. *See Dept. of Revenue v. Colonial Pipeline Co.*, 266 Ga. 743, 747, 470 SE2d 392, 396 (1996) (holding that a party's failure to seek judicial review of an administrative decision bars subsequent litigation of issues that could have been raised in the review proceeding).

**5. The Policy Rationale for Precluding Estoppel Is Absent**

The policy rationale underlying the general rule against applying collateral estoppel between civil and criminal proceedings—to protect the government's ability to enforce criminal laws—has no application here. The government did enforce its position in the civil proceeding and lost. The ALU's decision was not the result of a procedural default or default judgment; it was a determination on the merits after a full hearing.

Allowing the State to relitigate after an adverse ruling on the merits would permit the executive branch to effectively nullify unfavorable administrative decisions by pursuing criminal charges. This result would undermine the finality of administrative proceedings and encourage the government to use criminal prosecution as a fallback when it loses in civil forums—a result the doctrine of collateral estoppel exists to prevent.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prosecution's Burden-of-Proof Argument Fails

The prosecution argues that collateral estoppel cannot apply because civil proceedings use a preponderance standard while criminal proceedings require proof beyond a reasonable doubt. This argument confuses issue preclusion with claim preclusion and ignores the specific circumstances here.

The "same issue" requirement of collateral estoppel focuses on whether the prior tribunal actually decided the issue—not whether it applied the same standard of proof. *See Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 27 cmt. c* (1982). When the same party has fully and fairly litigated an issue in a prior proceeding, that party is bound by the determination regardless of the different standards in the two proceedings.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If accepted, it would mean the government could never be bound by any prior civil determination, even one rendered after a full trial on the merits with the same parties. This cannot be the law. *See United States v. McNeil*, 911 F.2d 998, 1004 (4th Cir. 1990) (holding that collateral estoppel may apply against the government in criminal cases when the requirements are met).

Here, the ALU did not simply find that the evidence was insufficient to meet a "preponderance" standard. The ALU found **no evidence** of fraud or willful misrepresentation. This is not a case where the civil standard was not met; it is a case where the factual predicates for fraud were affirmatively found not to exist.

### B. The Prosecution's "Different Purposes" Argument Is Inapplicable

The prosecution invokes the "distinct purposes" of civil and criminal proceedings to argue that estoppel can never apply. But this argument conflates the general presumption against estoppel with a categorical rule.

The Georgia Supreme Court in *Williams* recognized that the different purposes of civil and criminal proceedings are "preclusive" only when the issues are not identical. *Williams*, 276 Ga. at 288, 577 SE2d at 596. When the identical factual issues have been fully litigated in an adversarial civil proceeding involving the same parties, the purposes of the two proceedings are no longer distinct—they have converged around the same factual question.

Here, the administrative proceeding was not a routine reimbursement dispute. It was triggered by an investigation into potential Medicaid fraud—a matter that DCH referred to the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit for criminal investigation while simultaneously maintaining the withhold. The civil proceeding was, in substance, a precursor to the criminal prosecution. The State cannot now claim the purposes were distinct when it used the civil forum to litigate the exact facts underlying the criminal charges.

### C. The Prosecution's Policy Arguments Support Application of Estoppel

The prosecution argues that applying estoppel would "frustrate the Government's ability to enforce criminal laws." This argument is turned on its head in this case.

The State had the full opportunity to enforce its criminal laws in the administrative proceeding. It chose to proceed civilly, lost, and then sought to relitigate the same facts criminally. Permitting this would allow the government to use criminal prosecution as a tool to override adverse civil determinations—a result that would frustrate the principles of finality and fair play that undergird the doctrine of collateral estoppel.

The prosecution's argument that "the government must be free to pursue criminal charges when the civil standard of proof is not met" is inapplicable here. The ALU did not find that the civil standard was not met; the ALU found that no fraud occurred. This is a substantive determination on the merits, not a mere insufficiency of evidence.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to adopt a rule that would allow the State to pursue criminal charges after losing a full and adversarial administrative proceeding on the identical issues. This Court should decline to adopt such a categorical rule.

Georgia law recognizes that collateral estoppel may apply in criminal cases when the requirements are met. Here, those requirements are satisfied: the identical issues were actually litigated and determined in a final judgment, the same parties (the State of Georgia through DCH and the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit) participated in the prior proceeding, and the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate.

The trial court erred in denying Appellant's plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. This Court should reverse the trial court's order and remand with instructions to grant the plea in abatement.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**PENDING MOTION:** Appellant’s Plea in Abatement based on Collateral Estoppel.

**RULING:** The trial court’s denial of Appellant’s plea in abatement is affirmed.

**OPINION**

This Court has been asked to determine whether the doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the State of Georgia from prosecuting Appellant for Medicaid fraud following an administrative proceeding in which the Department of Community Health (DCH) failed to prove that a withhold of funds was justified. After reviewing the arguments presented by both the Prosecution and the Defense, this Court finds that the trial court correctly denied the plea in abatement.

**I. Standard for Collateral Estoppel**

It is well established that collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of issues that have been actually litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. See Atlanta Oculist Eye Clinic v. Foster, 277 Ga. 343, 345-46, 588 SE2d 688, 691 (2003). To successfully invoke this doctrine, a party must demonstrate that: (1) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (2) the judgment in the prior proceeding is final and concluded the rights of the parties on that issue; and (3) the party to be estopped had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See id. While the Defense correctly identifies these elements, the application of collateral estoppel to bar a criminal prosecution following a civil or administrative proceeding is constrained by significant jurisprudential limitations.

**II. The Distinction Between Civil/Administrative and Criminal Proceedings**

The central legal question is whether a finding in an administrative proceeding can preclude the State from pursuing criminal charges for the same underlying conduct. The Prosecution argues, and this Court agrees, that the qualitative differences between civil/administrative proceedings and criminal prosecutions generally preclude the application of collateral estoppel in this context.

The United States Supreme Court has held that "the Government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case." United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co., 464 U.S. 165, 174 (1984). The Court emphasized that applying collateral estoppel between civil and criminal proceedings would "frustrate the Government’s ability to enforce criminal laws consistently" and ignore the "qualitative differences" in the two systems. See id.

This principle is firmly rooted in Georgia law. In State v. Williams, 276 Ga. 286, 288, 577 S.E.2d 594, 596 (2003), the Georgia Supreme Court rejected a collateral estoppel defense in a criminal case following a civil administrative ruling. The Court stated that "the differing standards of proof and the distinct purposes of civil and criminal proceedings preclude application of issue preclusion between them." See id. The Defense attempts to limit *Williams* to situations where the issues are not identical; however, the broad language of *Williams* regarding "differing standards of proof" and "distinct purposes" controls the present analysis.

**III. Application to the Present Case**

The administrative hearing in this matter concerned the "withhold" of Medicaid reimbursements under DCH’s administrative authority. This proceeding was governed by OCGA § 50-13-41 and was designed to resolve reimbursement disputes and compliance with the Hyde Amendment, not to determine criminal liability. As noted by the Second Circuit in United States v. Thayer, 209 F.3d 702, 708 (2d Cir. 2000), civil cases resolve private disputes or administrative compliance, while criminal prosecutions protect public safety and enforce societal norms through punishment.

The Defense argues that the Administrative Law Judge’s (ALJ) finding of "no evidence" of fraud transcends the standard of proof issue. However, the ALJ’s determination was made within the context of a civil recovery action, where the State bore the burden of proving the withhold was justified by a preponderance of the evidence or substantial evidence standard. The criminal indictment, by contrast, requires proof that Appellant acted "knowingly and willfully" under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2)—a specific intent element that must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. See United States v. Thayer, 209 F.3d at 708 (noting the difference in burdens of proof makes it "unjust and impractical" to apply estoppel between civil and criminal matters).

The fact that the ALJ found "no evidence" of fraud in the administrative context does not equate to a finding that the State cannot prove the elements of criminal fraud beyond a reasonable doubt. The administrative proceeding was not designed to adjudicate criminal intent, nor did it afford the procedural safeguards associated with a criminal trial. Therefore, the issues litigated in the administrative hearing, while factually related, are not legally identical to the issues required for a criminal conviction.

**IV. Conclusion**

The trial court properly recognized that the prior administrative ruling had no preclusive effect on the criminal case. The doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to prevent the State from prosecuting criminal charges after a civil administrative ruling where the standards of proof and purposes of the proceedings differ fundamentally. See State v. Williams, 276 Ga. at 288, 577 S.E.2d at 596; United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co., 464 U.S. at 174. Accordingly, Appellant’s plea in abatement fails as a matter of law.

**SO ORDERED.**

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

The defense’s argument that collateral estoppel bars this criminal prosecution fundamentally misapprehends the legal distinction between civil/administrative proceedings and criminal prosecutions. Georgia law and binding federal precedent establish that the doctrine of collateral estoppel cannot apply to preclude criminal charges following an administrative determination, regardless of whether the same factual issues were presented. The trial court correctly denied the plea in abatement, and this Court should affirm that ruling.  

### I. The Legal Principle Precluding Collateral Estoppel Between Civil/Administrative and Criminal Proceedings Is Well-Established  
The doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar criminal prosecutions arising from prior civil or administrative proceedings. The United States Supreme Court has squarely held that "the Government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case." *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. 165, 174 (1984). The Court explicitly rejected the notion that collateral estoppel should apply across these distinct legal frameworks, emphasizing that "the qualitative differences between civil and criminal proceedings" and "the Government’s interest in enforcing criminal laws consistently" preclude such application. *Id.* This principle is equally binding in Georgia. The Georgia Supreme Court in *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. 286, 288, 577 S.E.2d 594, 596 (2003), confirmed that "the differing standards of proof and the distinct purposes of civil and criminal proceedings preclude application of issue preclusion between them." These precedents establish a categorical rule: collateral estoppel cannot operate to bar criminal charges based on a prior civil or administrative determination, irrespective of whether the same parties or factual issues were involved.  

### II. The Defense Mischaracterizes the Scope of *Stauffer Chemical Co.* and *Williams*  
The defense improperly attempts to limit *Stauffer Chemical Co.* and *Williams* to situations where the government lacked a "full and fair opportunity" to litigate in the prior proceeding. This is incorrect. *Stauffer Chemical Co.* did not condition its holding on the government’s opportunity to litigate; it squarely rejected the applicability of collateral estoppel between civil and criminal proceedings as a matter of principle. *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. at 174 ("The Government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case"). Similarly, *Williams* did not carve out an exception for cases where the government "lost" in a prior proceeding. The Georgia Supreme Court stated unequivocally that the "differing standards of proof and distinct purposes" create an insurmountable barrier to collateral estoppel between civil and criminal matters—period. *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. at 288, 577 S.E.2d at 596.  

The defense’s reliance on *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d 702 (2d Cir. 2000), is equally misplaced. *Thayer* did not hold that collateral estoppel could apply to bar criminal prosecution after an adverse civil ruling. To the contrary, the Second Circuit reaffirmed that "the government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case," citing *Stauffer Chemical Co.* directly. *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d at 708. The court merely noted that *Stauffer Chemical Co.*'s holding was "not a per se rule" but emphasized that the government’s ability to enforce criminal laws must prevail over estoppel claims in this context. *Id.* at 709.  

### III. The Administrative Proceeding and Criminal Prosecution Involve Fundamentally Different Legal Standards and Purposes  
The defense’s argument that the "identical factual issues" were litigated ignores the critical legal distinctions between the proceedings. The administrative hearing before the ALJ was conducted under OCGA § 50-13-41 and addressed a narrow civil dispute regarding the "withhold" of Medicaid reimbursements under the Hyde Amendment. Its purpose was to determine whether DCH had a valid basis to suspend payments—a civil remedy focused on fiscal accountability. The standard of proof in this proceeding was "preponderance of the evidence" or "substantial evidence," consistent with administrative law.  

In contrast, the criminal prosecution under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) requires proof that the appellant acted "knowingly and willfully" to defraud the Medicaid program—a specific intent element that must be proven *beyond a reasonable doubt*. *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d at 708 ("The criminal standard of proof is significantly higher than the civil standard, making it unjust and impractical to apply estoppel between civil and criminal matters"). The administrative proceeding was not designed to adjudicate criminal liability, nor did it afford the procedural safeguards of a criminal trial (e.g., jury trial, right to counsel, presumption of innocence). As the Second Circuit explained in *Thayer*, civil proceedings "resolve private disputes or administrative compliance," while criminal prosecutions "protect public safety and enforce societal norms through punishment." *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d at 708. These divergent purposes and standards render the issues legally distinct, not "identical" as the defense claims.  

### IV. The Defense’s Policy Arguments Are Contrary to Established Law  
The defense contends that applying collateral estoppel would "protect finality and fair play," but this misstates the policy rationale underpinning the rule against estoppel in criminal cases. *Stauffer Chemical Co.* and *Williams* reflect the settled understanding that the government must retain the ability to enforce criminal laws without being constrained by civil or administrative determinations. Permitting collateral estoppel to bar criminal prosecution would allow a defendant to immunize themselves from criminal liability by forcing the government to litigate in a civil forum where the stakes and standards differ. *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. at 174 ("Applying collateral estoppel between civil and criminal proceedings would frustrate the Government’s ability to enforce criminal laws consistently"). This is precisely why Georgia law and federal precedent uniformly reject such applications.  

The defense’s citation to *United States v. McNeil*, 911 F.2d 998, 1004 (4th Cir. 1990), is inapposite. *McNeil* involved a private party asserting collateral estoppel against the government in a criminal case—a scenario fundamentally different from the government seeking to prosecute after an adverse civil ruling. The Fourth Circuit explicitly distinguished *Stauffer Chemical Co.* in *McNeil*, noting that *Stauffer* "does not apply when a private party seeks to assert collateral estoppel against the government." *United States v. McNeil*, 911 F.2d at 1004. Here, the government is the plaintiff in the criminal case, making *McNeil* irrelevant.  

### V. Conclusion  
The trial court correctly held that collateral estoppel does not apply to bar this criminal prosecution. The doctrine’s inapplicability to civil-criminal contexts is a well-settled principle confirmed by the United States Supreme Court in *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. 165 (1984), and the Georgia Supreme Court in *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. 286, 577 S.E.2d 594 (2003). The administrative proceeding resolved a discrete civil reimbursement issue under a lower standard of proof, while the criminal prosecution requires proof of specific intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The defense’s attempt to equate these proceedings ignores the foundational legal distinctions that preclude collateral estoppel in this context. This Court should affirm the trial court’s denial of the plea in abatement.

Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. 165 (1984), and *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. 286 (2003). Neither case established an absolute bar; rather, each applied a fact-specific analysis to determine whether preclusion was appropriate.

The Court stated that "the Government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case" only after analyzing whether the government had "fully litigated" the issue and "had an adequate incentive to do so." *United States v. Similarly, in *State v. Williams*, the Georgia Supreme Court did not announce a blanket prohibition. The prosecution's reliance on *Williams* ignores this critical distinction—the Georgia Supreme Court actually engaged in the very analysis the prosecution now argues this Court should forego.

## III. THIS CASE PRESENTS UNIQUE CIRCUMSTANCES DISTINGUISHING IT FROM PRIOR AUTHORITY

The prosecution fails to acknowledge the distinctive procedural posture of this case, which presents circumstances markedly different from those in *Stauffer Chemical Co.* and *State v. Williams*.

This case presents precisely the "appropriate case" that *Thayer* contemplated. This result contradicts basic principles of due process and fair play. As this Court recognized in *Atlanta Oculist Eye Clinic v. The purpose is to promote finality and prevent the harassment of parties through repeated litigation of the same issues.

Here, the State actually litigated and lost on the precise issue presented in the criminal prosecution. To permit relitigation in a forum with higher stakes and different burdens of proof undermines the values collateral estoppel was designed to protect.

**FINAL RULING**

**PENDING MOTION:** Appellant’s Plea in Abatement based on Collateral Estoppel.

**RULING:** The trial court’s denial of Appellant’s plea in abatement is affirmed.

**OPINION**

Upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both the Prosecution and the Defense, this Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. The Defense has presented compelling arguments regarding the fundamental fairness of allowing the State to relitigate an issue it lost in an administrative forum it chose. However, the binding precedent of the United States Supreme Court and the Georgia Supreme Court establishes that the qualitative and quantitative differences between administrative proceedings and criminal prosecutions create a jurisdictional bar to the application of collateral estoppel in this context.

**I. The Controlling Precedent Establishes a High Barrier to Estoppel**

The Defense argues that *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. 165 (1984), and *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. 286, 577 S.E.2d 594 (2003), should not be read as establishing a per se rule against collateral estoppel in civil-criminal contexts. While the Defense correctly notes that these cases involved specific factual analyses, the legal principles articulated therein are broad and controlling.

In *Stauffer Chemical Co.*, the Supreme Court explicitly stated that "the Government is not estopped from relitigating issues in a criminal prosecution that were previously decided in a civil case." *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. at 174. The Court based this holding on the "qualitative differences between civil and criminal proceedings" and the necessity of allowing the government to enforce criminal laws without being hindered by prior civil adjudications. *Id.*

Similarly, in *State v. Williams*, the Georgia Supreme Court held that "the differing standards of proof and the distinct purposes of civil and criminal proceedings preclude application of issue preclusion between them." *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. at 288, 577 S.E.2d at 596. The Defense attempts to distinguish *Williams* by pointing out that the issues in that case were not identical. However, the Court’s language regarding "differing standards of proof" was not limited to the specific facts of that case; rather, it articulated a general principle governing the relationship between civil and criminal proceedings in Georgia. See *id.*

The Defense relies on *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d 702 (2d Cir. 2000), to argue that estoppel might apply in an "appropriate case." While the Second Circuit declined to adopt a per se rule, it ultimately affirmed the denial of estoppel because the government’s interest in prosecution outweighed the defendant's interest in preclusion. *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d at 709. Furthermore, *Thayer* is persuasive authority from a federal circuit; it does not override the binding directives of the Georgia Supreme Court in *Williams*.

**II. The "Identity of Issues" Requirement Is Not Satisfied**

For collateral estoppel to apply, the issue litigated in the prior proceeding must be identical to the issue in the current proceeding. See *Atlanta Oculist Eye Clinic v. Foster*, 277 Ga. 343, 345-46, 588 S.E.2d 688, 691 (2003). The Defense contends that the issue—whether Appellant committed fraud by billing for abortion-related services—is identical. This Court disagrees.

The administrative proceeding concerned the validity of a "withhold" of Medicaid reimbursements under the Hyde Amendment. The issue before the Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) was whether the Department of Community Health (DCH) had sufficient grounds to suspend payments based on a preponderance of the evidence or substantial evidence. The ALJ’s finding of "no evidence" of fraud was a determination that the State had not met its burden to justify the administrative sanction.

In contrast, the criminal prosecution requires the State to prove that Appellant "knowingly and willfully" accepted payments to which he was not entitled, in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). This requires proof of specific intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The issue in the criminal case is not merely whether the billing was erroneous, but whether Appellant possessed the criminal intent to defraud the State.

Because the administrative proceeding was not designed to adjudicate criminal intent, and the standard of proof was significantly lower, the issue of "fraud" as determined by the ALJ is not legally identical to the issue of "criminal fraud" presented in the indictment. As noted in *United States v. Thayer*, "the criminal standard of proof is significantly higher than the civil standard, making it unjust and impractical to apply estoppel between civil and criminal matters." *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d at 708.

**III. The "No Evidence" Finding Does Not Transcend the Standard of Proof Divide**

The Defense places significant weight on the ALJ’s finding of "no evidence" of fraud, arguing that this factual determination transcends the standard of proof issue. However, a finding of "no evidence" in an administrative context generally means that the evidence presented was insufficient to meet the "substantial evidence" standard required to uphold the agency's action. It does not mean that no evidence exists in the universe, nor does it equate to a finding that the State is incapable of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

The State may possess evidence that was deemed insufficient for a civil withhold but could be sufficient to convince a jury of criminal guilt. To hold that a civil finding of "no evidence" categorically bars criminal prosecution would allow administrative standards to effectively nullify criminal statutes, a result squarely rejected by *Stauffer Chemical Co.* and *Williams*.

**IV. Policy Considerations Favor the State**

The Defense argues that the State should be estopped because it "chose the forum" and had a "full and fair opportunity" to litigate. While this argument has logical appeal in the abstract, the Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the notion that the government’s choice to litigate in a civil forum should preclude subsequent criminal prosecution. *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. at 174. The public interest in prosecuting Medicaid fraud—a crime that diverts public funds and endangers vulnerable populations—outweighs the Appellant's interest in avoiding a second trial based on a different legal standard.

**V. Conclusion**

The trial court correctly determined that the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar the present criminal prosecution. The administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution serve different purposes, employ different standards of proof, and address different legal issues. The binding precedent of *State v. Williams* and *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.* compels the conclusion that the State is not estopped from pursuing criminal charges in this matter.

**SO ORDERED.**

Williams*, the Georgia Supreme Court held that "the differing standards of proof and the distinct purposes of civil and criminal proceedings preclude application of issue preclusion between them." *State v. Williams*, 276 Ga. at 288, 577 S.E.2d at 596. While the Defense attempts to distinguish *Williams* on the grounds that the issues in that case were not identical, the Court’s language regarding "differing standards of proof" articulates a general principle governing the relationship between civil and criminal proceedings in Georgia. This principle controls the outcome of the present case. The persuasive authority of *United States v. Thayer*, 209 F.3d 702 (2d Cir. 2000), which declined to adopt a per se rule, cannot override the binding directives of the Georgia Supreme Court in *Williams*.

**II. The "Identity of Issues" Requirement Is Not Satisfied**

For collateral estoppel to apply, the issue litigated in the prior proceeding must be identical to the issue in the current proceeding. See *Atlanta Oculist Eye Clinic v. Foster*, 277 Ga. 343, 345-46, 588 S.E.2d 688, 691 (2003). The Defense contends that the issue—whether Appellant committed fraud by billing for abortion-related services—is identical. This Court disagrees.

The administrative proceeding concerned the validity of a "withhold" of Medicaid reimbursements under the Hyde Amendment. The issue before the Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) was whether the Department of Community Health (DCH) had sufficient grounds to suspend payments based on a preponderance of the evidence or substantial evidence. The ALJ’s finding of "no evidence" of fraud was a determination that the State had not met its burden to justify the administrative sanction.

In contrast, the criminal prosecution requires the State to prove that Appellant "knowingly and willfully" accepted payments to which he was not entitled, in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). This requires proof of specific intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The issue in the criminal case is not merely whether the billing was erroneous, but whether Appellant possessed the criminal intent to defraud the State. Because the administrative proceeding was not designed to adjudicate criminal intent, and the standard of proof was significantly lower, the issue of "fraud" as determined by the ALJ is not legally identical to the issue of "criminal fraud" presented in the indictment.

**III. The "No Evidence" Finding Does Not Transcend the Standard of Proof Divide**

The Defense places significant weight on the ALJ’s finding of "no evidence" of fraud, arguing that this factual determination transcends the standard of proof issue and establishes that the State could not prove its case. However, a finding of "no evidence" in an administrative context generally means that the evidence presented was insufficient to meet the "substantial evidence" standard required to uphold the agency's action. It does not mean that no evidence exists in the universe, nor does it equate to a finding that the State is incapable of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

The State may possess evidence that was deemed insufficient for a civil withhold but could be sufficient to convince a jury of criminal guilt. To hold that a civil finding of "no evidence" categorically bars criminal prosecution would allow administrative standards to effectively nullify criminal statutes, a result squarely rejected by *Stauffer Chemical Co.* and *Williams*. The Defense's argument that the State "chose this forum" and should be estopped is unpersuasive in light of *Stauffer Chemical Co.*, which explicitly held that the government’s choice to litigate in a civil forum does not preclude subsequent criminal prosecution. *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.*, 464 U.S. at 174.

**IV. Conclusion**

The trial court correctly determined that the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar the present criminal prosecution. The administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution serve different purposes, employ different standards of proof, and address different legal issues. The binding precedent of *State v. Williams* and *United States v. Stauffer Chemical Co.* compels the conclusion that the State is not estopped from pursuing criminal charges in this matter.

**SO ORDERED.**
