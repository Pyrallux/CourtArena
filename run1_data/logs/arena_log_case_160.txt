Your Honor, the evidence seized from the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed because it was obtained through an unconstitutional search. The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures, and this case presents a clear violation of that fundamental right. Specifically, the officer conducted a pat down of the defendant's person without reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous, rendering that initial search unlawful. As a direct consequence, any subsequent consent to search the vehicle was tainted by this illegality and cannot validate the discovery of contraband.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a limited pat down for weapons only when there are specific and articulable facts indicating that the individual is armed and poses a threat to officer safety. This standard requires more than a hunch or generalized suspicion; it demands objective circumstances that would lead a reasonable officer to believe the person is armed and dangerous. Here, the officer admitted there were no such facts. He testified unequivocally that he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), his weapon was never drawn (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5), and the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" with "no fear for his safety or suspicion of any illegality" based on the defendant's demeanor (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 34:8-35:13, 44:21-45:24). There were no furtive movements, no signs of intoxication, no odor of drugs or alcohol, and no indication of weapons whatsoever (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11, 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3). The officer's subjective belief that the defendant was "suspicious" due to his route explanation or repeated questions about identification procedures does not satisfy *Terry*'s objective standard for a pat down. As this Court recognized, the officer's reasoning lacked "objective grounds for ... reasonably suspicious of criminal behavior above and beyond defendant’s driving under suspension" (Suppression Tr. 54:14). A pat down based on nothing more than a vague, unparticularized suspicion of general criminal activity violates the Fourth Amendment.

This unlawful pat down directly infected the officer's later request to search the vehicle. Once the officer concluded the traffic stop by issuing a citation for driving with a suspended license and explaining the license forfeiture procedures, the defendant was free to leave. The officer himself stated that the defendant "was free to go" and "had a ride coming" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 19:12-18). Any further detention or questioning after that point required new reasonable suspicion, but the officer had none. Instead, he reinitiated contact without justification, and the pat down occurred during this unlawful detention. As held in *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), "when an officer has completed the purpose of the stop, he must allow the detainee to leave unless he has reasonable suspicion to believe that criminal activity is afoot." Here, the officer had no such suspicion, making the entire sequence of events—including the pat down—unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has long held that evidence obtained as a fruit of an illegal seizure must be excluded, as recognized in *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The consent to search the vehicle was not voluntary because it was obtained during an unlawful seizure; it was the direct result of the officer's initial Fourth Amendment violation.

Therefore, the search of the vehicle was unconstitutional, and the drug evidence discovered must be suppressed. This Court has a duty to uphold the Fourth Amendment by excluding evidence tainted by unlawful police conduct. I respectfully request that the motion to suppress be granted.

## II. FACTUAL BACKGROUND

The vehicle then made an improper lane change, moving from the right-turn-only lane into the straight lane, and then immediately pulled over to the shoulder of the road.

The officer activated his lights and approached the vehicle. The defendant, who was alone, stated he was lost and handed the officer a cell phone so the officer could speak with his girlfriend, who was at a nearby 7-Eleven. The officer asked for the defendant's license and registration, which were provided. When asked where he was coming from, the defendant said Philadelphia. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Pat-Down Search Was Justified Under Terry v. Ohio

The prosecution's central argument is that the pat-down search of the defendant's person was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. This argument fails because it ignores the totality of the circumstances that existed at the time of the search.

In Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), the Supreme Court held that a police officer may stop and briefly detain a person based on reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot, and may frisk that person for weapons if the officer has reasonable suspicion to believe the person is armed and dangerous. The standard is not a bright-line rule but requires examination of the totality of the circumstances.

Here, the prosecution selectively quotes the officer's testimony to create a misleading picture. While the officer testified that he had "no safety concerns" at the initial stage of the encounter, this testimony must be understood in context. The officer's concern about officer safety developed as the encounter progressed and as suspicious circumstances accumulated. The prosecution cannot cherry-pick portions of testimony while ignoring other portions that provide the necessary context.

The circumstances that developed during the encounter provided the officer with reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant might be armed:

First, the officer observed the defendant operating a vehicle while his license was suspended—a criminal offense in Pennsylvania. This fact alone gave the officer reason to investigate further and to be concerned about what other criminal activity might be afoot.

Second, the defendant provided an inconsistent and implausible story about his whereabouts. He claimed to be lost, yet he had been to Lansdale before and should have known how to navigate there. When asked how to get to Lansdale from Philadelphia, he incorrectly identified Route 309 as the route, despite that route being inappropriate for that journey. The officer testified, "I just believed that based on — that his girlfriend lives in Lansdale, he is lost, does not know where he got to, why he was coming out of Upper Gwynedd, I felt that would be somewhat suspicious."

Third, and most significantly, the defendant engaged in what the officer reasonably perceived as deceptive behavior. After the officer thoroughly explained the license seizure procedures once, the defendant asked for the same explanation again. The officer reasonably interpreted this repeated questioning as a "ruse"—an attempt to delay or confuse the officer. As the trial court noted, the officer testified that "I believe it was [a ruse]. Like I said, I explained to other people and they have the understanding on the first time."

Fourth, the encounter occurred at approximately 3:42 a.m., a time when criminal activity is more likely and when officers must be more vigilant. The officer testified that part of his patrol was to look for things like drunk driving, and that he might be "a little more heightened to such things especially on a weekend at that time of night."

Fifth, the defendant was coming from Philadelphia, and the officer stated that "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have." While this alone would not justify a search, it provided context for the officer's suspicions.

The prosecution argues that mere suspicion of general criminal activity is insufficient under Terry. However, the prosecution misapplies this standard. The question is not whether the officer had probable cause to believe the defendant had committed a specific crime, but whether the officer had reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant might be armed and dangerous. Given the combination of factors—the defendant's suspended license, his implausible story, his deceptive behavior during the encounter, the late hour, and the officer's experience in law enforcement—the officer's concern for his safety was reasonable.

The prosecution relies heavily on the officer's statement that he had "no safety concerns" at the initial stage of the encounter. However, this statement was made in response to questions about whether the officer drew his weapon. The officer explained that his weapon was not drawn "because the officer had no safety concerns" at that particular moment. But the officer's concerns evolved as the encounter progressed and as additional suspicious circumstances came to light. The officer's subjective belief at the beginning of the encounter is not controlling; what matters is whether a reasonable officer in the same position would have had concerns at the time of the pat-down.

### B. The Defendant Was Not "Free to Go" at the Time of the Search

The prosecution's second major argument is that the traffic stop concluded when the officer issued the citation and explained the license forfeiture procedures, making the defendant "free to go" at that point. Any subsequent detention, including the pat-down and the request to search the vehicle, was therefore an unlawful seizure. This argument mischaracterizes the timeline of events and the nature of the encounter.

The prosecution relies on Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), for the proposition that "when an officer has completed the purpose of the stop, he must allow the detainee to leave unless he has reasonable suspicion to believe that criminal activity is afoot." However, the prosecution ignores the fact that the purpose of the stop had not been fully completed at the time of the search.

The officer had just written a citation for driving while license is suspended and had just explained the license forfeiture procedures. While these tasks were substantially complete, the defendant had not yet left the scene. More importantly, the defendant had explicitly stated that his girlfriend was coming to pick him up and that he was waiting for her to arrive. The officer was still engaged in the official business of the traffic stop when the defendant asked for clarification about the license seizure procedures.

The prosecution argues that the officer stated the defendant "was free to go." However, this statement must be understood in context. The officer testified that he told the defendant "he was free to go" after explaining the procedures, but the defendant responded that his girlfriend was coming to pick him up. At that point, the defendant was effectively in a holding pattern—neither free to leave in his own vehicle (because his license was suspended) nor actually leaving (because he was waiting for his ride). The officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" was not a definitive termination of the encounter; it was an acknowledgment that the defendant was not under arrest and was free to wait for his ride.

Furthermore, the defendant's own actions demonstrate that he did not consider himself free to leave. After being told he was free to go, the defendant asked the officer to explain the license seizure procedures again. This request indicates that the defendant understood the encounter was not yet fully concluded and that he still needed clarification from the officer.

The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer is misplaced. In Royer, the defendant was detained at an airport based on police suspicion that he was transporting drugs. The Court held that the detention was unlawful because the officers had not obtained a warrant and had exceeded the scope of a lawful investigative detention. Here, by contrast, the initial stop was lawful (the defendant was driving on a suspended license), and the officer's continued interaction with the defendant was a legitimate extension of the traffic stop, not a new seizure.

Even if this Court were to find that the traffic stop had technically concluded, the encounter between the officer and the defendant was not a "seizure" within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. The defendant was not handcuffed, not arrested, and not physically restrained. He was standing behind his car, engaged in a conversation with the officer. The officer asked if he could "ask a question," and the defendant agreed. This is a textbook example of a consensual encounter, which does not require reasonable suspicion or probable cause. As the Supreme Court has held, "a police officer is free to approach a person and ask questions without having any basis for suspecting the person of a crime." United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1, 4 (1989).

### C. The Consent Was Voluntary and Attenuated from Any Alleged Illegality

Even assuming arguendo that the pat-down was technically unlawful, the defendant's consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated from any alleged illegality to be considered voluntary.

The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, as established in Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), requires that evidence obtained through an illegal search be suppressed. However, this doctrine is not absolute. The Supreme Court has recognized an "attenuation doctrine," which holds that evidence may be admissible if the connection between the illegal police conduct and the evidence is so attenuated as to purge the taint of the original illegality. As the Court held in Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975), the question is "whether, granting establishment of the primary illegality, the evidence to which instant objection is made has been come at by exploitation of that illegality or instead by means sufficiently distinguishable to be purged of the primary taint."

Here, even if the pat-down was unlawful, the defendant's consent was not the product of that pat-down. The officer asked the defendant if he "could ask him a question," and the defendant agreed. The officer then made a statement about problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia and asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him. At that point, the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle."

The defendant's consent was an act of free will. He was not coerced, threatened, or pressured into consenting. He was not promised anything in exchange for his consent. He simply agreed to the officer's request. The officer described the defendant's demeanor in giving consent as "agreeable."

The prosecution argues that the consent was "tainted" by the alleged illegality of the pat-down. However, the pat-down occurred after the officer asked about weapons, not before. The officer testified that he asked the defendant "if he has been to Lansdale since his girlfriend obviously is in Lansdale," and the defendant responded that he had been there before. The officer then asked how to get to Lansdale from Philadelphia, and the defendant gave the incorrect answer about Route 309. Only after these conversations did the officer form the suspicion that led to the pat-down.

In any event, the pat-down of the defendant's person was a minimal intrusion that produced no evidence. The defendant was not injured, not handcuffed, and not detained as a result of the pat-down. The subsequent consent to search the vehicle was not the product of the pat-down but rather the defendant's own decision to allow the search. Under these circumstances, any alleged taint from the pat-down was sufficiently attenuated to allow the consent to be considered voluntary.

### D. The Search Was Justified by Voluntary Consent

Independent of the pat-down issue, the search of the vehicle was justified by the defendant's voluntary consent. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches, but "one of the exceptions to the requirement of a warrant is voluntary consent." Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). When a defendant voluntarily consents to a search, that consent validates the search regardless of whether the police had a warrant or probable cause.

The prosecution does not dispute that the defendant consented to the search. Instead, the prosecution argues that the consent was not voluntary because it was obtained during an unlawful seizure. This argument fails for the reasons stated above—the defendant was not seized at the time he gave consent, and even if he was, his consent was sufficiently attenuated to be considered voluntary.

The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer is again misplaced. In Royer, the Court found that the defendant's consent was not voluntary because it was obtained while the defendant was being detained in a small room surrounded by police officers. Here, by contrast, the defendant was standing behind his car in an open area, was not handcuffed or restrained, and had been told he was free to go. The officer asked if he could "ask a question," and the defendant agreed. This is the paradigm of voluntary consent.

The prosecution argues that the officer did not advise the defendant that he could decline to consent. However, the Supreme Court has held that "the Fourth Amendment has never been held to require proof that the subject of a search knew that he had a right to refuse to grant permission to the police to search." Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. at 234. While the better practice is to inform a defendant of his right to refuse, the failure to do so does not automatically render consent involuntary.

### E. The Prosecution's Argument Ignores the Legitimate Purpose of the Traffic Stop

The prosecution's argument, taken to its logical conclusion, would hamstring law enforcement's ability to protect public safety. The officer stopped the defendant for a legitimate traffic violation—driving while his license was suspended. During the stop, the officer discovered information that raised his suspicions. Rather than simply issuing a citation and walking away, the officer appropriately investigated further. This is exactly what police officers are supposed to do.

The prosecution asks this Court to hold that an officer who stops a driver for a traffic violation must immediately issue a citation and walk away, without any further investigation, even if suspicious circumstances develop. This is not the law. The Fourth Amendment allows for investigative detentions based on reasonable suspicion, and the officer's conduct here was well within those bounds.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the evidence seized from the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional search and seizure. The prosecution argues that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down and that the defendant's consent to search the vehicle was tainted by an unlawful detention. The defense contends that the encounter was consensual, initiated by the defendant after the traffic stop had concluded, and that the consent was voluntary. Based on the facts provided and the applicable legal standards, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Traffic Stop Concluded Prior to the Search**

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. A traffic stop constitutes a seizure limited to its purpose, which is to address the traffic violation that justified the stop. See *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983). Once the purpose of the stop is fulfilled, the officer must allow the detainee to leave unless there is reasonable suspicion to believe criminal activity is afoot. Id.

Here, the undisputed facts establish that the traffic stop concluded before the search of the vehicle occurred. The officer issued a citation for driving with a suspended license and completed the license forfeiture paperwork. The officer testified that he explained the documents to the defendant and told him "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). Critically, the officer stated, "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

This verbal statement and physical action—walking away from the defendant and back to the patrol car—objectively communicated that the detention was over. Under *Royer*, the seizure ended at that moment. The prosecution's argument that the defendant was not free to go because the officer's lights were on and a backup officer was present is unpersuasive in light of the officer's explicit statement that the defendant was free to leave and the officer's physical disengagement.

**B. The Subsequent Encounter Was Consensual**

Because the seizure ended when the officer walked away, the interaction that followed must be analyzed as a consensual encounter rather than a Fourth Amendment detention. A person has been seized only if, "in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 554 (1980) (Stewart, J., concurring in judgment). Conversely, a police-citizen encounter is consensual if the citizen is free to leave at any time and voluntarily cooperates with the officer. See *United States v. Dray*, 717 F.3d 770, 777 (6th Cir. 2013).

The facts show that the defendant was not seized during the interaction that led to the search. After the officer walked away, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" by asking about the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15). The officer answered, and the defendant "sort of walked back to his car and I started walking back towards my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). Only then did the officer ask, "if I could ask him a question," to which the defendant turned around, came back, and agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

This sequence of events demonstrates that the defendant was not restrained; rather, he voluntarily re-initiated contact and agreed to answer questions. The officer's request to ask a question and the defendant's compliance constitute a classic consensual encounter. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (stating that police may ask questions without probable cause or reasonable suspicion as long as the person is free to leave).

**C. The Consent to Search Was Voluntary**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was tainted by the prior illegality. However, because the encounter was consensual, the "taint" of any prior unlawful detention is purged. See *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975) (evidence obtained after the termination of an illegal seizure is admissible if the connection between the illegal police conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated).

Furthermore, the consent itself was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. The Fourth Amendment recognizes a voluntary consent exception to the warrant requirement. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The prosecution does not dispute that the defendant consented, but argues it was involuntary because the officer did not advise him of his right to refuse. However, the Supreme Court has held that "the Fourth Amendment has never been held to require proof that the subject of a search knew that he had a right to refuse to grant permission to the police to search." *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 234.

The facts indicate the defendant's consent was unequivocal. After the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). There is no evidence of coercion, threats, or physical restraint. The defendant was standing behind his car, free to leave, and voluntarily offered to be searched.

**D. The Pat-Down Issue**

The prosecution argues that the officer conducted an unlawful pat-down in violation of *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). Even assuming arguendo that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to frisk the defendant—given the officer's testimony that he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4)—this does not require suppression of the evidence found in the car.

The search of the vehicle was not the fruit of the pat-down. The search occurred after the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer and offered to be searched. The defendant's offer to be searched was an independent act of free will that broke any causal chain between an alleged improper pat-down and the discovery of the contraband. See *United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411, 418-19 (1976) (consent given after an illegal arrest is not per se involuntary). Moreover, the officer testified that the defendant "patted himself down" (Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9), suggesting the defendant initiated the physical contact to demonstrate he had nothing to hide, further supporting the voluntary nature of the encounter.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go," coupled with his act of walking away, objectively ended the seizure. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement with the officer constituted a consensual encounter during which he voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. The consent was not tainted by any prior illegality, nor was it rendered involuntary by the lack of a Miranda-style warning. Therefore, the search was constitutional, and the evidence discovered is admissible.

For the foregoing reasons, the prosecution's motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

However, the Supreme Court has expressly held that "the Fourth Amendment has never been held to require proof that the subject of a search knew that he had a right to refuse to grant permission to the police to search." *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. at 234. The prosecution's reliance on the absence of a *Miranda*-style warning is misplaced; no such warning is required for Fourth Amendment consent. *Id.*

The totality of the circumstances demonstrates voluntary consent. The defendant was standing behind his car, "with nothing impeding him from the driver's door of his car." Suppression Tr. 19:2-7. He had been told he was "free to go" and had a ride en route. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18, 21:5-12. The officer's weapon was not drawn, and the officer testified he had "no safety concerns." Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4. The defendant was described as "agreeable" in demeanor. Suppression Tr. 23:4-18. There was no evidence of coercion, threats, or physical restraint.

Critically, the defendant was not seized when he offered consent. After the officer told him he was free to go, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" and later "sort of walked back to his car." Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. Only then did the defendant turn around and voluntarily agree to answer questions and allow the search. This sequence demonstrates a person who was free to leave and chose to remain and cooperate. *See Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (police may ask questions without probable cause as long as the person is free to leave).

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S FOCUS ON THE PAT-DOWN IGNORES THAT THE VEHICLE SEARCH WAS INDEPENDENTLY JUSTIFIED

The prosecution attempts to conflate the pat-down with the vehicle search, but these were distinct events. The pat-down, if it occurred, was of the defendant's person. The vehicle search was of a separate location—the defendant's car. The defendant offered to allow the search of his vehicle without any prompting. This was not a situation where the officer discovered something during an unlawful pat-down and then used that information to justify searching the car. The officer had no information from any pat-down that would have justified searching the vehicle; his "reasonable suspicion" (Suppression Tr. 56:15-16) was based on his conversation with the defendant, not on any physical search.

Even if the pat-down was unlawful, the vehicle search was not predicated on anything discovered during that pat-down. The officer testified he searched "first in the compartment of the vehicle, then moved into the trunk" and discovered "drug evidence." Suppression Tr. 24:10-15. There is no causal connection between an alleged unlawful pat-down of the person and the search of the vehicle's interior and trunk. The prosecution cannot establish the required nexus.

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S *YBARRA* ARGUMENT IS INAPPLICABLE

The prosecution cites *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979), for the proposition that generalized suspicion cannot justify a frisk. This argument misses the mark. In *Ybarra*, the police had a warrant to search a bar and its bartender, and the Court held that the police could not frisk every patron absent individualized suspicion. *Id.* at 92-93. Here, by contrast, the defendant was the sole occupant of the vehicle and the sole focus of the encounter. The prosecution's argument assumes the pat-down was the basis for the vehicle search—it was not.

Moreover, the prosecution's reliance on *Ybarra* implicitly concedes that the vehicle search was not justified by the pat-down. If the pat-down was unlawful and cannot justify the vehicle search, then the vehicle search must be evaluated independently. And evaluated independently, it was the product of voluntary consent.

## VI. THE ATTENUATION DOCTRINE FURTHER SUPPORTS ADMISSIBILITY

Even if this Court were to find that the pat-down was unlawful and that some taint existed, the attenuation doctrine requires suppression only when the causal connection is not too attenuated. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. at 603-04. The factors include: (1) the time elapsed between the illegality and the evidence; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *See United States v. Terrios-Gonzalez*, 712 F.3d 371, 377 (6th Cir. 2013).

Here, approximately twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop till when the evidence was found. Suppression Tr. 24-25. During this time, the defendant was told he was free to go, received his citation, obtained a ride, and voluntarily re-engaged the officer. The officer walked back to his patrol car, creating a physical separation. These intervening circumstances break any causal chain. The purpose of the alleged misconduct (the pat-down) was officer safety, not evidence gathering, and there is no evidence of flagrant misconduct. Under these factors, any taint was sufficiently attenuated.

## VII. CONCLUSION

The prosecution's rebuttal fails to address the core holding below: the defendant was free to go, voluntarily re-engaged the officer, and offered consent to search his vehicle. The vehicle search was the product of this voluntary consent, not the fruit of any unlawful pat-down. Even assuming the pat-down was improper—which we contest—the independent source doctrine and attenuation analysis render the evidence admissible.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the evidence seized from the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional search and seizure. The prosecution argues that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down and that the defendant's consent to search the vehicle was tainted by this unlawful detention and search. The defense contends that the encounter was consensual, initiated by the defendant after the traffic stop had concluded, and that the consent was voluntary and independent of any prior illegality. Based on the facts provided and the applicable legal standards, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down and Reasonable Suspicion**

The prosecution correctly argues that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down for weapons under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). *Terry* requires that an officer have specific, articulable facts suggesting that a suspect is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 27. The officer's testimony in this case reveals that his suspicion was predicated solely on potential drug activity—specifically, his belief that the defendant was bringing contraband from Philadelphia—and his subjective view that the defendant's story about being lost was a "ruse." (Suppression Tr. 16:12-16; 51:17). The officer explicitly stated he had "no safety concerns" and that his weapon was not drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5; 32:2-4).

In *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049-50 (1983), the Supreme Court held that a *Terry* frisk must be predicated on reasonable suspicion that the suspect is "armed and dangerous." Mere suspicion of general criminal activity, such as drug possession, is insufficient to justify a weapons frisk. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 92-93 (1979). Because the officer articulated no facts suggesting the defendant was armed, any pat-down conducted without the defendant's consent would have violated the Fourth Amendment.

**B. The Termination of the Seizure**

However, the illegality of a potential pat-down does not automatically require suppression of the evidence obtained from the subsequent vehicle search. The critical inquiry is whether the vehicle search occurred during a continued illegal seizure or during a new, consensual encounter.

The undisputed facts establish that the initial seizure for the suspended license violation concluded before the search of the vehicle occurred. The officer completed the necessary paperwork, explained the documents to the defendant, and explicitly told him "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer testified that he "started walking back to my car" immediately thereafter. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

Under *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983), once the purpose of the stop is fulfilled, the officer must allow the detainee to leave unless there is reasonable suspicion to believe criminal activity is afoot. Here, the officer's statement that the defendant was free to go, coupled with his physical act of walking away, objectively communicated that the detention was over. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 554 (1980) (Stewart, J., concurring in judgment) (a person has been seized only if a reasonable person would believe he is not free to leave).

**C. The Consensual Nature of the Subsequent Encounter**

Because the seizure ended when the officer walked away, the interaction that followed must be analyzed as a consensual encounter. The facts show that the defendant was not restrained during this interaction. After the officer walked away, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" by asking about the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15). The officer answered, and the defendant "sort of walked back to his car." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). Only then did the officer ask if he could ask a question, to which the defendant turned around, came back, and agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

This sequence of events demonstrates that the defendant voluntarily re-initiated contact. A police-citizen encounter is consensual if the citizen is free to leave at any time and voluntarily cooperates with the officer. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991). The defendant's decision to return to the officer and answer questions constitutes a classic consensual encounter.

**D. The Voluntariness of the Consent and Attenuation of Taint**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was tainted by the prior illegality of the pat-down. However, the "taint" of any prior unlawful detention or search is purged if the connection between the illegal conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975).

The consent to search the vehicle was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. The Fourth Amendment recognizes a voluntary consent exception to the warrant requirement. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The prosecution does not dispute that the defendant consented, but argues it was involuntary because the officer did not advise him of his right to refuse. The Supreme Court has held that "the Fourth Amendment has never been held to require proof that the subject of a search knew that he had a right to refuse to grant permission to the police to search." *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 234.

The facts indicate the defendant's consent was unequivocal and unsolicited. After the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). There is no evidence of coercion, threats, or physical restraint.

Furthermore, the consent was an independent act of free will that broke any causal chain between the alleged improper pat-down and the discovery of the contraband. *United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411, 418-19 (1976) (consent given after an illegal arrest is not per se involuntary). The defendant initiated the search by offering to be searched, rather than responding to a coercive show of authority. The intervening circumstance of the defendant's own voluntary actions—re-engaging the officer and offering the search—serves to attenuate any potential taint from the prior interaction. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 603-04.

**III. CONCLUSION**

While the prosecution correctly identifies that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk for weapons, this error does not require suppression of the evidence found in the vehicle. The officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go," coupled with his act of walking away, objectively ended the seizure. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement with the officer constituted a consensual encounter during which he voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. The consent was not tainted by any prior illegality, nor was it rendered involuntary by the lack of a Miranda-style warning. Therefore, the search was constitutional, and the evidence discovered is admissible.

For the foregoing reasons, the prosecution's motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

The Fourth Amendment recognizes that voluntary consent to a search is a valid exception to the warrant requirement. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The prosecution does not dispute that the defendant consented to the search; rather, it argues the consent was involuntary. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly held that "the Fourth Amendment has never been held to require proof that the subject of a search knew that he had a right to refuse to grant permission to the police to search." Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 234.

The facts overwhelmingly demonstrate voluntary consent. After the officer asked "if he had anything that would concern me," the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2. The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18. There was no evidence of coercion, threats, physical restraint, or commands. The defendant unsolicitedly offered to be searched—a textbook example of voluntary consent.

The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as an involuntary consent fails because it conflates a consensual encounter with an illegal seizure. The defendant was not detained. He was not under arrest. He had been told he was free to go, and he had a ride coming. His decision to allow the search was entirely his own.

### B. The Attenuation Doctrine Breaks Any Causal Chain

Even assuming arguendo that some prior illegality existed, the consent was sufficiently attenuated to purge any taint. In Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975), the Supreme Court held that evidence obtained following an unconstitutional search or seizure is not automatically suppressible; rather, the connection between the illegality and the evidence must be sufficiently attenuated.

This Court correctly applied the attenuation doctrine. The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer, voluntarily agreed to answer questions, and voluntarily offered to be searched. These intervening acts of free will broke any causal chain between the prior encounter and the discovery of evidence. As this Court found, "The defendant initiated the search by offering to be searched, rather than responding to a coercive show of authority."

In United States v. Watson, 423 U.S. 411, 418-19 (1976), the Supreme Court held that consent given after an illegal arrest is not per se involuntary. Here, there was not even an illegal arrest—there was merely a consensual encounter following a valid traffic stop that had concluded. The attenuation is even stronger where, as here, the defendant initiates the contact and offers the search without prompting.

---

## THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON WONG SUN IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), for the proposition that "the prosecution cannot take advantage of evidence obtained by an illegal search or seizure, even if the defendant consents to it." This argument fundamentally misreads Wong Sun and ignores the critical distinction between consent given under coercion and voluntary consent.

In Wong Sun, the defendants made statements after being unlawfully arrested, and the Court addressed whether those statements were sufficiently attenuated from the illegal arrest. The Court did not hold that voluntary consent is invalid merely because it follows some prior police contact. Rather, the Court applied the same attenuation analysis that this Court correctly applied here.

The prosecution's argument would lead to the absurd result that any prior police contact—however lawful—would invalidate a subsequent voluntary consent. This is not the law. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches, and a voluntary consent makes a search reasonable. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 219.

---

## THE OFFICER'S SUBJECTIVE SUSPICIONS DO NOT INVALIDATE THE CONSENSUAL ENCOUNTER

The prosecution emphasizes the officer's subjective belief that the defendant was engaged in criminal activity. However, the Fourth Amendment is an objective standard, and subjective suspicions do not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure.

The officer may have been suspicious—this is clear from the testimony. But suspicion alone does not create a seizure. As this Court found, "subjectively, the officer was suspicious of further criminal behavior" does not equate to an objectively reasonable seizure. The officer had no reasonable suspicion of criminal activity at the time the defendant voluntarily re-engaged him. The defendant was compliant, polite, and had done nothing wrong except drive with a suspended license, which had been addressed.

The officer's subsequent questions about "people bringing things out of Philadelphia" were asked during a consensual encounter, not during a seizure. The defendant voluntarily agreed to answer and then voluntarily offered to be searched. The officer's subjective motivations do not invalidate the objectively consensual nature of the encounter.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution's motion to suppress rests on a faulty foundation. It asks this Court to find that the evidence must be suppressed because of an unlawful pat-down, but the facts establish that:

1. The seizure for the suspended license concluded when the officer told the defendant he was "free to go" and walked away;
2. The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after the seizure ended;
3. The defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle;
4. This consent was an independent act of free will that attenuated any potential taint from prior interactions.

The prosecution has not established that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional. To the contrary, the evidence shows that the search was conducted pursuant to a valid, voluntary consent given during a consensual encounter.

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons set forth in this Court's thorough ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court's ruling be affirmed and the prosecution's motion to suppress be denied.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the prosecution’s appeal of the order denying the motion to suppress evidence. The prosecution argues that the evidence seized from the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional search and seizure, specifically an unlawful *Terry* pat-down that tainted the subsequent consent to search the vehicle. The defense maintains that the encounter was consensual, initiated by the defendant after the lawful seizure for the suspended license had concluded, and that the consent was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality. After a thorough review of the record, the arguments presented, and the applicable precedent, the Court affirms the denial of the motion to suppress.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Illegality of the *Terry* Frisk and Its Scope**

The Court agrees with the prosecution that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down for weapons under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). *Terry* requires that an officer have specific, articulable facts suggesting that a suspect is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 27. The officer testified that his suspicion was predicated solely on potential drug activity—specifically, his belief that the defendant was bringing contraband from Philadelphia—and his subjective view that the defendant's story about being lost was a "ruse." (Suppression Tr. 16:12-16; 51:17). The officer explicitly stated he had "no safety concerns" and that his weapon was not drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5; 32:2-4).

In *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049-50 (1983), the Supreme Court held that a *Terry* frisk must be predicated on reasonable suspicion that the suspect is "armed and dangerous." Mere suspicion of general criminal activity, such as drug possession, is insufficient to justify a weapons frisk. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 92-93 (1979). Therefore, the officer's subjective suspicion of drug activity did not justify a non-consensual pat-down.

However, the prosecution’s argument that this error requires suppression of the evidence found in the vehicle misapprehends the scope of the illegality and the causal chain between the officer's suspicion and the discovery of the contraband. The critical question is not whether the officer's suspicion was legally sufficient for a frisk, but whether the vehicle search was the product of a continued illegal seizure or a new, consensual encounter.

**B. Termination of the Seizure and the Consensual Encounter**

The undisputed facts establish that the initial seizure for the suspended license violation concluded before the search of the vehicle occurred. The officer completed the necessary paperwork, explained the documents to the defendant, and explicitly told him "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer testified that he "started walking back to my car" immediately thereafter. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

Under *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983), once the purpose of the stop is fulfilled, the officer must allow the detainee to leave unless there is reasonable suspicion to believe criminal activity is afoot. Here, the purpose of the stop—issuing a citation for driving while license suspended—was completed. The officer's statement that the defendant was free to go, coupled with his physical act of walking away, objectively communicated that the detention was over. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 554 (1980) (Stewart, J., concurring in judgment) (a person has been seized only if a reasonable person would believe he is not free to leave).

The prosecution argues that the "free to go" statement was a mere formality and that the seizure continued because the officer remained suspicious. This argument is unpersuasive. The Fourth Amendment is governed by objective standards. The officer's subjective suspicions cannot transform an objective release into a continued seizure. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996). Once the officer walked away, the defendant was free to leave. The fact that the defendant chose not to leave, but rather to re-engage the officer, confirms the consensual nature of the subsequent interaction.

**C. Voluntariness of Consent and Attenuation of Taint**

The prosecution contends that the consent to search the vehicle was tainted by the prior illegality of the officer's suspicion. However, the "taint" of any prior unlawful detention or search is purged if the connection between the illegal conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975).

The consent to search the vehicle was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. The Fourth Amendment recognizes a voluntary consent exception to the warrant requirement. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The Supreme Court has held that "the Fourth Amendment has never been held to require proof that the subject of a search knew that he had a right to refuse to grant permission to the police to search." *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 234.

The facts indicate the defendant's consent was unequivocal and unsolicited. After the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). There is no evidence of coercion, threats, or physical restraint.

Furthermore, the consent was an independent act of free will that broke any causal chain between the officer's prior suspicion and the discovery of the contraband. *United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411, 418-19 (1976) (consent given after an illegal arrest is not per se involuntary). The defendant initiated the search by offering to be searched, rather than responding to a coercive show of authority. The intervening circumstance of the defendant's own voluntary actions—re-engaging the officer and offering the search—serves to attenuate any potential taint from the prior interaction. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 603-04.

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), is misplaced. *Wong Sun* does not stand for the proposition that any consent following an illegal police encounter is per se invalid. Rather, it requires a case-by-case analysis to determine if the consent was sufficiently an act of free will to purge the primary taint. Here, the temporal proximity between the end of the seizure and the consent, the intervening act of the defendant re-engaging the officer, and the absence of coercive police tactics all support the conclusion that the consent was voluntary and attenuated.

**III. CONCLUSION**

While the prosecution correctly identifies that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk for weapons, this error does not require suppression of the evidence found in the vehicle. The officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go," coupled with his act of walking away, objectively ended the seizure. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement with the officer constituted a consensual encounter during which he voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. The consent was not tainted by any prior illegality, nor was it rendered involuntary by the lack of a Miranda-style warning. Therefore, the search was constitutional, and the evidence discovered is admissible.

For the foregoing reasons, the prosecution's motion to suppress is **DENIED**.
