Your Honor, the search of the defendant’s vehicle was unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment because it stemmed directly from an unlawful pat-down conducted without reasonable suspicion of danger. The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures, and a pat-down of a person’s outer clothing—commonly known as a Terry frisk—requires specific, articulable facts that reasonably justify the officer’s belief that the individual is armed and dangerous. Here, the officer had no such facts, rendering the pat-down illegal and tainting all evidence derived from it.  

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), the Supreme Court established that a pat-down is permissible only when an officer has reasonable suspicion "that the person stopped is armed and dangerous." This standard demands more than a mere hunch; it requires "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant the intrusion." Id. at 21. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that reasonable suspicion must be based on objective circumstances, not subjective beliefs or generalized concerns. As clarified in *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266 (2002), the totality of circumstances must "point toward criminal activity," but the inquiry must be "fact-specific" and grounded in "objective manifestations of wrongdoing," not "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion." Id. at 273.  

In this case, the officer’s pat-down occurred after the traffic stop had concluded. When the officer issued the citation for driving with a suspended license, explained the license forfeiture procedures, and explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go" because his girlfriend was en route to pick him up, the initial purpose of the stop was complete. At that point, any further detention required new, independent reasonable suspicion. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983) (holding that "the temporary seizure of a driver must be temporary and last no longer than is necessary to effectuate the purpose of the stop," and "once the purpose of the stop is completed, the detention must cease unless there is new reasonable suspicion"). Here, the officer had no new reasonable suspicion to justify reinitiating contact.  

Crucially, the officer himself admitted there were no objective grounds for suspicion beyond his subjective disbelief of the defendant’s explanation for being lost. On cross-examination, when asked whether "aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there’s no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity," the officer unequivocally responded, "agreed." Suppression Tr. 33:16-19. This admission is fatal to the state’s position. The defendant’s behavior—being polite, cooperative, compliant, and showing no signs of intoxication, drug use, or furtive movements—was entirely consistent with lawful conduct. The officer observed no weapons, smelled no alcohol or marijuana, and had no safety concerns whatsoever, as he explicitly testified: "His weapon was not drawn during the interaction... because the officer had no safety concerns." Suppression Tr. 32:2-4.  

The officer’s claim that defendant’s re-asking about ID procedures was a "ruse" or "deceptive" lacks objective support. Such a minor procedural inquiry, especially after the officer had already explained the process, does not rise to reasonable suspicion of danger. As the Supreme Court stated in *Dunaway v. New York*, 442 U.S. 200, 217 (1979), "police officers may not seize an individual for the purpose of investigation without reasonable suspicion of criminal activity," and "a mere hunch" cannot justify a seizure. Here, the officer’s subjective suspicion—based solely on his disagreement with the defendant’s account of being lost in a familiar town—amounts to nothing more than a "hunch," which is insufficient under *Terry*.  

Moreover, the pat-down was not justified by any safety concern. *Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. 408, 415 (1997) (requiring reasonable suspicion of danger for a pat-down during a traffic stop), and *Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 111 (1977) (holding that an officer may order a driver out of a vehicle for safety reasons but only if there is a "reasonable belief that the suspect is armed and dangerous"). But the officer had no such belief—he never drew his weapon, and the record shows no indication of threat. Indeed, the court below found no objective basis for suspicion beyond the officer’s unsupported disbelief of the defendant’s story, which cannot substitute for the required specific facts.  

Because the pat-down was conducted without reasonable suspicion of danger, it violated the Fourth Amendment. Any evidence obtained as a direct result of that illegal seizure—here, the subsequent vehicle search—must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963) (holding that evidence obtained after an unlawful seizure is inadmissible unless the connection between the illegality and the evidence is "so attenuated as to dissipate the taint"). The search of the vehicle was not an independent act; it flowed directly from the unlawful pat-down and the officer’s continued detention of the defendant without justification.  

Your Honor, the Fourth Amendment demands more than convenience or intuition from law enforcement. It requires concrete facts to justify intrusions on personal liberty. Here, the officer had none. The search must be suppressed as unconstitutional.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's argument mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter and ignores controlling precedent establishing that the search in question was conducted pursuant to valid consent. The evidence obtained from the vehicle search should not be suppressed because: (1) the defendant voluntarily consented to both the pat-down and the vehicle search after the purpose of the traffic stop had concluded; (2) the officer's request to search was supported by reasonable suspicion that justified further inquiry; and (3) even assuming arguendo that the pat-down was improper, the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated from any alleged illegality to purge the taint. The prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and its progeny is misplaced where, as here, the defendant freely and voluntarily consented to the search.

---

## I. THE DEFENDANT VOLUNTARILY CONSENTED TO THE SEARCH

The central fact overlooked by the prosecution is that the defendant explicitly, voluntarily, and proactively consented to both the pat-down and the vehicle search. The record establishes that after the officer asked if he could ask a question, "defendant turned around and came back to me. And he said, yes, I can — he would answer my questions." Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. The officer then made a statement about problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia and asked if defendant "had anything that would concern me." *Id.* At that point, the defendant "basically patted himself down from top to bottom, starting here, down a couple of — you know, down to his legs. After he did that, he placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." *Id.* The officer further testified that "he — without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.

This was not a situation where the officer physically restrained the defendant or demanded consent. Rather, the defendant proactively placed his hands on his vehicle and invited the search. The officer described the defendant's demeanor in giving consent as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18. Critically, the officer "did not advise defendant he could decline to consent to a search of his car." Suppression Tr. 23:2-3. While failure to advise of the right to refuse may be relevant to the voluntariness inquiry, it is not dispositive. See *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 249 (1973) (holding that voluntariness is a question of fact to be determined from the totality of circumstances).

The prosecution argues that the stop had concluded and therefore any further detention required new reasonable suspicion. However, this argument conflates two distinct concepts: detention and consent. Even if the defendant was technically "free to go" after the traffic stop concluded, a police officer may nonetheless approach a person and request consent to search. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991) ("Even when officers have no basis for suspecting a particular individual, they may generally ask the person questions, ask to see identification, and request consent to search."). The key inquiry is not whether the stop has concluded, but whether the consent was voluntary. *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 248-49.

Here, the defendant was not being detained when he consented. He had been told he was "free to go" and his girlfriend was en route to pick him up. He voluntarily chose to remain, to engage the officer in further conversation, and to consent to the search. The prosecution cannot convert a consensual encounter into an illegal seizure simply because the defendant chose to cooperate. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 553-54 (1980) (holding that a person is "seized" only when a reasonable person would not believe they are free to leave).

---

## II. REASONABLE SUSPICION SUPPORTED THE OFFICER'S FURTHER INQUIRY

Even assuming the court finds that some form of justification was required before the officer could ask for consent to search, the totality of circumstances provided reasonable suspicion sufficient to permit further investigation. The prosecution incorrectly argues that the officer admitted there were no objective grounds for suspicion. A careful reading of the transcript reveals otherwise.

The officer testified that he found several factors suspicious: (1) defendant was coming from Philadelphia at 3:42 AM; (2) defendant claimed to be lost even though he had been to Lansdale before and knew to take Route 309; (3) defendant exited in Upper Gwynedd, which the officer apparently found unusual given his stated destination; (4) defendant asked the officer to explain the license forfeiture procedures a second time, which the officer believed was a "ruse" intended to deceive; and (5) defendant's "overly cooperative" behavior in immediately patting himself down and inviting a search was something the officer "find[s] unusual." Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9.

While the prosecution correctly notes that any single one of these factors might be insufficient, the Supreme Court has held that reasonable suspicion must be based on the totality of the circumstances, not on isolated factors. *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981). The combination of late-night travel from a source city, an implausible story about being lost in a familiar area, and unusual behavior during the encounter collectively provided a legitimate basis for the officer's suspicion. See *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 9 (1989) (holding that reasonable suspicion can arise from a "combination of innocuous facts" that together warrant further investigation).

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266 (2002), is instructive here. In *Arvizu*, the Supreme Court emphasized that the totality-of-circumstances approach "allows officers to draw on their own experience and specialized training to make inferences from and deductions about the cumulative information available to them that might well elude an untrained person." Id. at 273. The officer in this case had approximately fifteen years of law enforcement experience and testified that his patrol included looking for "things like drunk driving" and that he might be "a little more heightened to such things especially on a weekend at that time of night." Suppression Tr. 26-27. This experience-informed suspicion is precisely what the Fourth Amendment permits.

---

## III. EVEN IF THE PAT-DOWN WAS IMPROPER, THE CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS ATTENUATED

Assuming arguendo that the pat-down of the defendant was improper, the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was an independent act that sufficiently attenuated any taint from the alleged illegality. The prosecution argues that the vehicle search "flowed directly from the unlawful pat-down," but this mischaracterizes the sequence of events.

After the pat-down revealed nothing, the officer asked, "can I search your car?" Suppression Tr. 22:20-21. The defendant responded, "yes." *Id.* At that moment, the defendant made a voluntary decision to permit the search of his vehicle. This was not a automatic or reflexive continuation of the pat-down; it was a separate, discrete consent to a different object — the vehicle rather than the person.

The Supreme Court has held that consent to search is a "means of breaking the causal chain" between an illegal seizure and subsequently discovered evidence. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963). While *Wong Sun* addressed the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, its reasoning applies with equal force to the attenuation analysis. The defendant here was told he was free to go, had a ride coming, and was under no compulsion to consent. His voluntary consent — given freely and without coercion — breaks any causal connection between an allegedly improper pat-down and the vehicle search. See *United States v. Williams*, 617 F.2d 1063, 1077 (5th Cir. 1980) (en banc) (holding that voluntary consent following an illegal search purges the taint).

The prosecution argues that because the officer asked for consent immediately after the pat-down, the consent was tainted. However, the temporal proximity alone does not establish insufficient attenuation. The critical question is whether the consent was "sufficiently an act of free will to purge the primary taint." *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 486. Here, the defendant was explicitly told he was free to go, was not handcuffed or restrained, and voluntarily agreed to the search. The fact that the request came quickly does not render the consent involuntary.

---

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON FLORIDA V. ROYER IS MISPLACED

The prosecution's argument relies heavily on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), for the proposition that "once the purpose of the stop is completed, the detention must cease unless there is new reasonable suspicion." Id. at 500. However, *Royer* is distinguishable.

In *Royer*, the defendant was detained at an airport ticket counter by officers who had seized his luggage without his consent and held it for approximately fifteen minutes while awaiting a drug-sniffing dog. The Court held this detention was unlawful. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go and was in no way restrained. The officer's request for consent to search did not constitute a seizure; it was a consensual encounter that the defendant was free to decline. See *Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 434-35 (holding that a request for consent to search does not render an encounter a seizure).

Moreover, in *Royer*, the Court expressly acknowledged that "the person may refuse to cooperate" and "may leave." 460 U.S. at 498. Here, the defendant could have refused and left, but chose not to. His voluntary choice to remain and consent undermines any claim that he was illegally detained.

---

## V. THE DEFENDANT'S PROACTIVE CONDUCT DEMONSTRATES VALID CONSENT

The unique facts of this case distinguish it from typical consent cases where courts scrutinize whether consent was coerced. Here, the defendant did not merely acquiesce to a police request; he proactively invited the search. After the officer asked if there was anything that would concern him, "defendant at that point basically patted himself down from top to bottom... After he did that, he placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." Suppression Tr. 22:10-18.

This conduct demonstrates not merely consent, but enthusiastic, proactive invitation. The defendant took the initiative, presumably to demonstrate his innocence or for some other reason known only to him. Such conduct is the very antithesis of coerced consent. The prosecution's argument that the officer should have advised the defendant of his right to refuse misses the point: the defendant did not merely fail to refuse — he affirmatively invited the search.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully submits that the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional because it was conducted pursuant to the defendant's voluntary consent. Even assuming some deficiency in the earlier pat-down, the defendant's proactive invitation to search, given after he was told he was free to go, constituted a valid waiver of his Fourth Amendment rights. The prosecution has failed to establish that the search was unreasonable, and the evidence should not be suppressed.

The judgment of the trial court should be affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal misapplies the law to the facts of this case and ignores the critical distinction between a lawful seizure that has concluded and an ongoing detention. The evidence discovered during the search of defendant's vehicle was obtained pursuant to valid, voluntary consent given after the initial lawful encounter had concluded. Once the officer issued the citation and explained the license forfeiture, the purpose of the stop was fulfilled.

The prosecution's argument that the rotating lights created a coercive environment ignores the fact that defendant had already been told he was free to go before the lights became relevant to any subsequent interaction.

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement about "problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia" initiated an unlawful detention. This argument fails. A police officer may approach a citizen and ask questions without that encounter constituting a seizure. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. at 434. The officer's question was general and non-coercive. ## III. THE PAT-DOWN WAS CONSENSUAL, NOT A TERRY FRISK

The officer's lack of concern for his safety is precisely why he did not conduct a Terry frisk—he did not need to. Instead, defendant voluntarily offered to be searched. The prosecution cannot convert a consensual search into an unlawful frisk simply because the officer had no safety concerns; that fact actually supports the defense—that defendant was not subjected to a non-consensual frisk but rather consented to a search.

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the prosecution asks this Court to suppress evidence obtained during a consensual search by mischaracterizing the encounter as an ongoing unlawful seizure. ## I. THE SEIZURE LAWFULLY CONCLUDED BEFORE THE SEARCH

The prosecution contends that the defendant remained seized throughout the encounter due to the presence of rotating lights and a backup officer. This argument contravenes the record and misapplies the governing legal standard.

### A. The Officer Explicitly Terminated the Seizure

The Supreme Court in *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983), held that a temporary seizure of a driver "must last no longer than is necessary to effectuate the purpose of the stop." Here, the purpose of the stop was to address the suspended license violation. Once the officer completed the citation and explained the license seizure procedures, that purpose was fulfilled.

The officer's testimony is unequivocal: "After I explained to her where the car was located, where he was located at, she said that she was going to come pick him up and I handed the phone back to defendant. I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). The officer further confirmed that after completing the paperwork, he told the defendant "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10).

This is not a case where the officer retained control over the defendant by holding his license and registration or asking him to sit in the patrol car. Compare *United States v. Smith*, 802 F.3d 158, 165 (3d Cir. 2015) (finding ongoing seizure where officer retained documents and asked defendant to sit in patrol car). Here, the officer returned all documents except the seized license as required by PennDOT and physically disengaged by walking away.

### B. The Rotating Lights Did Not Override the Clear Indication That the Detention Ended

While the presence of rotating lights is a factor to consider under the reasonable person test articulated in *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), this factor does not create an irrebuttable presumption of ongoing seizure. The prosecution asks this Court to find that a defendant is never free to leave whenever police lights are activated, regardless of the officer's explicit statements and conduct. This reading is untenable.

The *Mendenhall* test asks whether "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." *Id.* Here, the reasonable person analysis must account for the officer's unambiguous verbal statement that the defendant was "free to go" combined with the officer's physical disengagement—walking away toward his patrol car. A reasonable person, having just been told he was free to leave and observing the officer walk away, would understand that the detention had ended. The prosecution's argument that the rotating lights alone negated the officer's clear verbal indication to the contrary would render meaningless an officer's ability to conclude an encounter.

## II. THE DEFENDANT'S RE-ENGAGEMENT WAS CONSENSUAL

The prosecution erroneously treats the defendant's post-seizure interaction with the officer as a continuation of the detention. To the contrary, the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to go.

### A. The Defendant Initiated Further Contact

After the officer began walking back to his car, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation. He asked me again what he is supposed to do with the license seizure form." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). The officer answered, and then "I asked defendant if I could ask him a question. He turned around and came back to me. And he said, yes, I can — he would answer my questions." (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25).

An encounter initiated by a police officer does not constitute a seizure if the citizen is free to leave at any time. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). Here, the defendant was not only free to leave—he had already been expressly told he was free to go and was awaiting his girlfriend's arrival. His choice to remain and converse transformed the interaction into a consensual encounter. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40 (1996).

### B. The Coercive Effect of the Rotating Lights Is Mitigated by the Defendant's Initiation

The prosecution argues that the rotating lights created a coercive environment. However, the defendant initiated the further contact while the officer was attempting to leave. This distinguishes the present case from scenarios where police approach a stationary vehicle with lights activated and demand compliance. The defendant here was not merely complying with police direction—he was proactively engaging the officer after being told he was free to go.

## III. THE PAT-DOWN WAS CONSENSUAL, NOT A TERRY FRISK

The prosecution's central argument—that the pat-down was an unconstitutional *Terry* frisk conducted without reasonable suspicion—mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter. The pat-down was not a non-consensual search for officer safety; it was a consensual search requested and authorized by the defendant.

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Pat-Down

The officer testified that after being asked "if he had anything that would concern me," the defendant "basically patted himself down from top to bottom... [and] placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 22:10-18). This is not a case where an officer unilaterally decided to conduct a pat-down for his own safety. The defendant proactively offered to be searched, demonstrating clear, voluntary consent.

The Supreme Court has held that consent to a search is valid if voluntarily given, without the need for warnings or probable cause. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248-49 (1973). The defendant's conduct—patting himself down, spreading his legs, placing hands on the vehicle, and explicitly stating "you can search me"—constitutes unambiguous voluntary consent.

### B. The Officer Lacked Safety Concerns Because This Was Not a Terry Frisk

The prosecution argues that the officer's lack of safety concerns proves the pat-down was unlawful. This reasoning is inverted. The officer's testimony that he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4) and did not draw his weapon (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5) demonstrates that this was not a *Terry* frisk, which is predicated on officer safety concerns. Rather, it was a consensual search requested by the defendant.

The prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), is misplaced because *Terry* applies to non-consensual searches conducted without the individual's permission. Here, the defendant explicitly consented. The absence of officer safety concerns does not render the defendant's consent invalid—it confirms that the search was consensual rather than a protective frisk.

---

## IV. THE VEHICLE SEARCH WAS VOLUNTARY

Following the pat-down, which revealed nothing, the officer asked for and received permission to search the vehicle. The defendant responded "yes" when asked if he could search the car. (Suppression Tr. 22:20-21).

### A. The Consent Was Given Under circumstances Demonstrating Voluntariness

The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable" (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). The defendant was not physically restrained, the officer's weapon was not drawn, and the defendant had been told he was free to go. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). The defendant was awaiting his girlfriend's arrival and had no reason to believe he was compelled to consent.

Under the totality of the circumstances, the defendant's consent was voluntary. *Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437-38. The prosecution's argument that the consent was involuntary due to the "coercive environment" of the traffic stop fails because the traffic stop had concluded. The defendant was not in custody, not under arrest, and had been explicitly informed he was free to leave.

### B. The Prosecution Cannot Rely on the Initial Seizure to Invalidate the Subsequent Consent

Even if the Court were to find that some aspect of the encounter constituted an unlawful seizure—an issue the defense disputes—the defendant's consent to search the vehicle was an independent act of free will that purged any primary taint. The defendant was told he was free to go, was not under arrest, and voluntarily agreed to the search. *United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411, 424-25 (1976).

---

## V. THE EVIDENCE IS NOT FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE

The prosecution's fruit of the poisonous tree argument fails because there was no primary illegality. The initial seizure was lawful and concluded before any search occurred. The subsequent searches were consensual. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963).

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to adopt a rule that would render any police presence—regardless of explicit verbal and physical indications to the contrary—sufficient to maintain an ongoing seizure. This Court should decline that invitation. The record establishes that:

1. The officer lawfully concluded the traffic stop by issuing the citation and informing the defendant he was free to go;
2. The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to leave;
3. The defendant proactively offered to be searched, constituting valid consent to the pat-down;
4. The defendant voluntarily consented to the vehicle search;
5. The officer found drug evidence in the trunk approximately twenty-five minutes after the initial stop, during what was by then a consensual encounter.

The evidence discovered during the search is admissible. The motion to suppress should be denied.

**FINAL RULING**

The motion to suppress is denied. The search of the defendant’s vehicle was constitutional as it was conducted pursuant to the defendant’s voluntary consent, which was not tainted by any prior unlawful seizure or detention.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Seizure Lawfully Concluded Prior to the Search**

The prosecution argues that the defendant remained seized at the time of the search due to the presence of two police vehicles with rotating lights and the presence of a backup officer. While the presence of activated emergency lights is a factor to consider under the "reasonable person" test of *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), it is not dispositive where the officer has taken clear affirmative steps to terminate the seizure.

The record establishes that the initial seizure ended before the search occurred. The officer testified that after completing the paperwork for the suspended license citation, he told the defendant he was "free to go" because his girlfriend was en route to pick him up. (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12). At that moment, the officer "started walking back to [his] car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). Under *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983), the temporary seizure of a driver must last no longer than is necessary to effectuate the purpose of the stop. Once the citation was issued and the defendant was informed he was free to leave, the purpose of the seizure was complete.

The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Mendenhall* to argue that the lights alone maintained the seizure is unpersuasive in this context. Unlike cases where the officer retains the driver's documents or orders the driver to remain in the vehicle, see *United States v. Smith*, 802 F.3d 158, 165 (3d Cir. 2015), the officer here returned the defendant's documents (aside from the seized license as required by PennDOT) and physically disengaged. The officer’s verbal statement that the defendant was free to leave, coupled with his physical act of walking away, objectively communicated that the detention had concluded.

**II. The Re-engagement Was Consensual**

The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to go. As the officer walked away, the defendant asked for further clarification regarding the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22). The officer answered, and the defendant then voluntarily returned to the officer when asked if he could answer a question. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

An encounter initiated by a police officer does not constitute a seizure if the citizen is free to leave at any time. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). Here, the defendant was not only free to leave but had already been discharged from the initial stop. His choice to remain and converse transformed the interaction into a consensual encounter, negating the need for reasonable suspicion to justify the officer’s questions. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40 (1996). The coercive effect of the rotating lights is mitigated by the fact that the defendant initiated the further contact while the officer was attempting to leave.

**III. The Pat-down Was Consensual, Not a *Terry* Frisk**

The prosecution characterizes the pat-down as an unconstitutional *Terry* frisk because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). However, the record clearly establishes that the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his person, rendering the *Terry* analysis inapplicable.

The officer testified that after he asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "basically patted himself down... placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 22:10-18). This proactive conduct by the defendant constitutes clear, voluntary consent to a pat-down, rather than a non-consensual frisk for officer safety. See *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248-49 (1973) (holding that consent to a search is valid if voluntarily given, without the need for warnings or probable cause).

The prosecution argues that the officer’s lack of safety concerns—evidenced by his testimony that his weapon was not drawn—proves the pat-down was unlawful. To the contrary, the officer’s lack of safety concerns supports the defense’s position that this was not a *Terry* frisk, which is predicated on officer safety, but rather a consensual search requested by the defendant. The officer’s subjective suspicion of criminal activity does not invalidate the defendant’s objective consent.

**IV. The Vehicle Search Was Voluntary**

Following the pat-down, which revealed nothing, the officer asked for and received permission to search the vehicle. The defendant responded "yes" when asked if the officer could search his car. (Suppression Tr. 22:20-21). The officer described the defendant’s demeanor as "agreeable," and there is no evidence of coercion or intimidation. The officer’s weapon was not drawn, and the defendant was not physically restrained. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5).

Under the totality of the circumstances, the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was voluntary. *Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437-38. The prosecution’s argument that the consent was involuntary due to the "coercive environment" of the traffic stop is unpersuasive given that the defendant had been told he was free to go, was awaiting a ride, and had initiated the conversation that led to the search request.

**V. The Evidence Is Not Fruit of the Poisonous Tree**

The prosecution argues that the evidence should be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree" because the search stemmed from an unlawful detention. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963). This argument fails because the initial seizure was not unlawful, and the subsequent searches were consensual. Even if the pat-down were viewed with suspicion, the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was an independent act of free will that purged any primary taint. The defendant was told he was free to go, was not under arrest, and voluntarily agreed to the search. See *United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411, 424-25 (1976) (holding that consent given freely and voluntarily, not coerced, is valid even without probable cause or a warrant).

**CONCLUSION**

The officer lawfully concluded the initial traffic stop by informing the defendant he was free to leave. The defendant’s subsequent re-engagement with the officer and his explicit consent to search his person and vehicle were voluntary acts that rendered the search constitutional under the Fourth Amendment. Therefore, the evidence discovered during the search is admissible.
