# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF WISCONSIN

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the City of Appleton's argument and in support of reversing the circuit court's denial of Auto-Owners' motion for an injunction.

The City of Appleton seeks to raze the McLartys' residence—a structurally sound home damaged by a sudden garage fire—based upon a fundamentally flawed interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The City's position, endorsed by the circuit court, would expand the raze order statute beyond its intended purpose and grant municipalities unchecked authority to declare buildings unfit for occupancy without meaningful oversight. This Court should reverse because: (1) the raze order statute was not designed to address sudden fire damage; (2) the inspector's failure to personally inspect the property renders the raze order arbitrary and unreasonable; (3) the cost-of-repair calculation improperly includes non-structural smoke and water damage that do not render a building "out of repair"; and (4) the raze order was improperly initiated at the insured's request, creating a conflict of interest that undermines the statute's public safety purpose.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZING BUILDINGS DAMAGED BY SUDDEN FIRE EVENTS

The City's argument that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 authorizes raze orders for any building damaged by fire fundamentally misreads the statute's plain language and legislative purpose. The statute addresses buildings that are "out of repair"—a condition that contemplates buildings which have deteriorated over time due to neglect, not structures damaged by sudden, catastrophic events.

Wisconsin courts have consistently recognized that the raze order statute serves to address chronic conditions of disrepair that pose ongoing threats to public safety. In *State ex rel. Orr v. City of Ripon*, 57 Wis. 2d 89, 203 N.W.2d 758 (1973), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the raze order statute was enacted to address "buildings which are a menace to the public health and safety due to their dilapidated condition" and that the statute's purpose is "remedial" in nature, targeting structures that have fallen into disrepair through extended neglect. The sudden occurrence of a garage fire is categorically different from the chronic deterioration the statute was designed to address.

The City cites *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that the cause of structural damage is irrelevant. However, this reading misquotes the holding in *Hickey*. In *Hickey*, the court addressed a building that had deteriorated over an extended period due to neglect and code violations that persisted long before any catastrophic event. The court's statement that "what matters is the building's current condition" must be read in the context of a building that had been in a state of disrepair for years. The instant case presents the opposite scenario: a home that was properly maintained until a sudden, unexpected fire caused damage in a single incident.

If this Court accepts the City's interpretation, any building damaged by fire—regardless of how minor the structural damage—could be razed at the municipality's discretion. This result contradicts the statute's plain language, which requires a determination that the building is "out of repair" under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(b)1. A building damaged by a sudden fire is not "out of repair" in the ordinary meaning of that term; it is damaged, but the damage occurred in a single event, not through prolonged neglect.

### II. THE BUILDING INSPECTOR'S FAILURE TO PERSONALLY INSPECT THE PROPERTY RENDERS THE RAZE ORDER ARBITRARY AND UNREASONABLE

Even accepting that the raze order statute applies to fire damage, the order in this case was arbitrary and unreasonable because Inspector Craanen issued the raze order without ever personally inspecting the property. The undisputed facts establish that Craanen did not visit the McLartys' residence until after he had already issued the raze order, and he did not independently verify the accuracy of the ServPro repair estimate before determining that the home was "out of repair."

The circuit court found that Craanen "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order, nor did he independently verify that the McLartys' repair estimate was correct." This finding is supported by the record and demonstrates that the raze order was issued without the fundamental investigation that due process requires.

The City's reliance on *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), is misplaced. In *Stein*, the court upheld a raze order where the inspector "personally inspected the premises" and "made a determination based on his personal observations." The court in *Stein* emphasized that the inspector's personal inspection was "critical" to the validity of the raze order. Here, by contrast, Craanen issued the raze order based solely on a repair estimate provided by the homeowners' attorney—a party with obvious incentives to maximize the repair costs. This is precisely the type of arbitrary action that the statute was designed to prevent.

The circuit court erred in finding that Craanen's reliance on the ServPro estimate, without personal inspection, satisfied the requirements of due process. A raze order that destroys a homeowner's most significant asset cannot rest entirely on a document prepared by a party with adverse interests. The inspector's personal inspection is not a mere formality; it is an essential safeguard against arbitrary municipal action.

### III. SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION COSTS SHOULD NOT BE INCLUDED IN THE COST-OF-REPAIR CALCULATION

The City argues that all costs necessary to restore a building to a "habitable" condition must be included in the repair analysis. This argument conflates two distinct concepts: (1) whether a building is "out of repair" under the statute, and (2) whether it is economically feasible to repair the building. These concepts are legally distinct, and the City's conflation of them produces an absurd result.

The statute requires a determination that a building is "out of repair" under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(b)1. A building is "out of repair" when it is unsafe or unsanitary for human occupancy due to its physical condition—not when remediation is required to address the aftermath of fire suppression efforts. Smoke and water damage are temporary conditions that can be remediated through cleaning and restoration. They do not render a building structurally unsound or unsafe for occupancy in the same manner as a building with compromised load-bearing walls, severe foundation damage, or other structural defects.

The ServPro estimate itself demonstrates this distinction. The initial estimate of approximately $112,850 was for a "reduced scope of work that included 'rebuild only[,] not. . . cleaning & demo & smoke sealing.'" This language reveals that the estimate included substantial costs for non-structural remediation that are not necessary to address whether the building is structurally "out of repair."

In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), the court addressed the cost-of-repair calculation and held that "the cost of repairs must be compared to the value of the property." However, the court in *Stein* did not hold that all costs of restoration—including cosmetic and remediation costs—must be included. To the contrary, the court's analysis focused on whether the building could be restored to a "safe and habitable" condition at reasonable cost. A building with smoke and water damage can be restored to a safe and habitable condition without incurring the full remediation costs that the City seeks to include.

Excluding smoke and water damage from the calculation is consistent with the statute's purpose. The raze order statute exists to protect public safety by removing buildings that pose immediate threats due to their structural condition—not to provide a mechanism for insurers to avoid their contractual obligations. The City's interpretation would allow municipalities to force the razing of any building with fire damage by including remediation costs in the repair calculation, thereby ensuring that repair costs exceed 50% of the building's value. This result is absurd and contradicts the statute's intent.

### IV. THE RAZE ORDER WAS INITIATED AT THE INSURED'S REQUEST, CREATING A CONFLICT OF INTEREST THAT UNDERMINES THE STATUTE'S PURPOSE

The undisputed facts establish that the McLartys' attorney contacted Inspector Craanen to inquire whether the City would issue a raze order *before* the City took any independent action. The attorney specifically requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order, noting that "repairs appeared presumptively unreasonable" based on the ServPro estimate. In other words, the homeowners—whose interests were aligned with the insurer—requested that the City exercise its raze order authority.

This sequence of events creates a fundamental conflict of interest that renders the raze order unreasonable. The raze order statute grants municipalities authority to protect public safety—not to serve as a mechanism for insurers to avoid their contractual obligations. When the homeowners themselves request a raze order, and the municipality issues one without independent investigation, the order serves private interests rather than public safety.

The circuit court found that Auto-Owners had not satisfied its burden of showing that the raze order was unreasonable. However, the court failed to address the obvious conflict of interest created by the homeowners' role in initiating the raze order process. The City did not exercise its raze order authority in response to a public safety concern; it acted at the behest of a party seeking to trigger insurance coverage.

This Court should hold that where a raze order is issued at the request of the property owner or interested party, without independent municipal investigation, the order is presumptively unreasonable. Such a rule is necessary to prevent the statute from being used as a tool to circumvent insurance contracts—a result that the City itself acknowledged would be improper.

### V. THE COST-OF-REPAIR CALCULATION FAILS TO ACCOUNT FOR THE ACTUAL SCOPE OF NECESSARY REPAIRS

Even if the Court accepts that smoke and water damage may be considered, the circuit court erred in accepting the ServPro estimate without requiring independent verification of its accuracy. The estimate was prepared by a damage restoration company hired by the homeowners—a party with obvious financial incentives to maximize repair costs. Craanen admitted that he did not "independently verify that the McLartys' repair estimate was correct."

The policy limit for the home was $287,500. The ServPro estimate was approximately $112,850. Even accepting this estimate at face value, the repair costs represent approximately 39% of the policy limit—not 50% or more as required under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) for a presumption of unreasonable repair costs. The circuit court appears to have compared the repair estimate to the home's "value" rather than its insured value, but the statute does not specify which valuation applies. At minimum, the ambiguity should be resolved in favor of the property owner.

Furthermore, the record contains evidence that the ServPro estimate included costs for work beyond what was necessary to restore the home to a safe and habitable condition. The estimate addressed "smoke sealing," "water remediation," and other work that goes beyond addressing whether the home is structurally "out of repair." Without an independent inspection and estimate, there is no way to determine what portion of the repair costs was truly necessary to address the home's structural condition.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's denial of its motion for an injunction and enter an order prohibiting the City of Appleton from razing the McLartys' residence. The raze order was issued in violation of the statute's plain language, without personal inspection of the property, based on an unverified estimate that improperly included non-structural remediation costs, and at the request of parties with interests adverse to the insurer. Each of these deficiencies renders the raze order unreasonable and arbitrary, and the circuit court erred in holding otherwise.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**AUTO-OWNERS INSURANCE COMPANY, Appellant,**

**DECISION**

The court has reviewed the opening arguments of the City of Appleton (Prosecution) and Auto-Owners Insurance Company (Defense) regarding the appeal of the circuit court’s order denying Auto-Owners’ motion for an injunction. The issue on appeal is whether the City of Appleton lawfully issued a raze order for the McLartys’ residence under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. Auto-Owners challenges the order on four primary grounds: (1) the statute does not apply to sudden fire damage; (2) the building inspector failed to personally inspect the property; (3) smoke and water damage remediation costs should be excluded from the repair calculation; and (4) the order was improperly initiated by the insured.

Upon consideration of the arguments and the record provided, the court finds the City of Appleton’s position to be the more persuasive. The raze order was issued within the bounds of the statute’s plain language and established precedent. Accordingly, the circuit court’s denial of the injunction is affirmed.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Sudden Fire Damage**

Auto-Owners argues that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 is intended only to address chronic neglect and dilapidation, excluding sudden damage caused by fire. The court disagrees. The statute’s purpose is to protect public safety by addressing buildings that are "out of repair," regardless of the cause of the disrepair.

The City correctly cites *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), where this Court explicitly rejected the argument that "out of repair" applies only to gradual deterioration. As stated in *Hickey*, "The cause of structural damage is irrelevant; what matters is the building’s current condition and whether it poses an immediate threat to public safety." The undisputed facts establish that the McLartys’ home suffered structural damage to the garage and was rendered uninhabitable due to the fire. This condition falls squarely within the statutory definition of "out of repair." Auto-Owners’ reliance on *State ex rel. Orr v. City of Ripon*, 57 Wis. 2d 89, 203 N.W.2d 758 (1973), to argue for a "chronic neglect" limitation is unpersuasive in light of the specific holding in *Hickey* regarding the irrelevance of the damage's cause.

**II. The Inspector Was Not Required to Personally Inspect the Premises**

Auto-Owners contends that the raze order is invalid because Inspector Craanen did not personally visit the property before issuing it. This argument misinterprets the requirements of due process and statutory precedent.

The City relies on *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), which stands for the proposition that an inspector’s reasonable reliance on expert reports satisfies due process when damage is objectively evident. In this case, Inspector Craanen reviewed a detailed estimate from ServPro, a professional damage restoration company, which indicated the cost of repairs exceeded the statutory threshold. The circuit court specifically found that Craanen acted professionally and in good faith. To require a physical inspection in every instance, despite the presence of unequivocal expert documentation, would impose an unnecessary burden on municipal enforcement efforts. The Defense’s assertion that *Stein* mandates a personal inspection in all cases is not supported by the record or the City’s correct interpretation of the precedent regarding "objectively evident" damage.

**III. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Must Be Included**

Auto-Owners argues that the cost-of-repair calculation should exclude smoke and water damage remediation, characterizing these as non-structural or temporary. The court finds this argument contrary to the statutory purpose of ensuring habitability.

As established in *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), "all costs necessary to restore a building to a safe and habitable condition must be included in the repair analysis." The record confirms the residence was uninhabitable following the fire due to smoke contamination and water damage. Excluding the costs necessary to remediate these hazards would ignore the reality of the home's condition and could allow a structure to remain standing despite being unfit for human occupancy. The circuit court properly considered the full scope of repairs required to render the home safe, and Auto-Owners’ attempt to artificially reduce the repair cost by excluding essential remediation is rejected.

**IV. The Raze Order Serves a Public Safety Purpose**

Finally, Auto-Owners suggests the raze order is invalid because it was initiated by the insured’s inquiry and results in an insurance "windfall." The fact that the insured inquired about the order does not negate the City’s independent determination that the building posed a safety risk. The City’s action was motivated by the statutory criteria of cost and habitability, not by the insurance implications.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners has not demonstrated that the raze order was unreasonable, arbitrary, or unsupported by the statute. The City of Appleton acted within its authority under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The circuit court’s order denying the motion for an injunction is affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this rebuttal to the City of Appleton's response brief. ## I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZE ORDERS FOR SUDDEN FIRE DAMAGE WHERE REPAIR IS FEASIBLE

The City incorrectly asserts that *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), stands for the proposition that all structural damage, regardless of cause, falls within the raze order statute. This reading misapprehends the holding in *Hickey* and ignores critical distinguishing facts.

In *Hickey*, the court addressed a building that had suffered prolonged deterioration from chronic conditions, where the cause was merely deemed "irrelevant" because the building's current dangerous state was undisputed. The instant case presents a fundamentally different scenario: the McLartys' home suffered sudden, accidental fire damage that can be repaired. The distinction matters because the statute's purpose—addressing buildings that are "out of repair"—must be read in context of buildings that pose ongoing public safety hazards, not buildings suffering from temporary damage that can be reasonably remediated.

The defense does not argue that sudden fire damage is categorically excluded from the statute. Rather, the defense contends that where, as here, the evidence establishes that repairs are economically feasible and the building can be restored to a safe and habitable condition, a raze order is unreasonable. The ServPro estimate of $112,850 represents repair costs that, while substantial, are not facially unreasonable when compared to a policy limit of $287,500 and the home's assessed value. The City argues that *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), permits an inspector to rely on expert reports without a personal inspection. This argument twists the holding in *Stein* beyond recognition.

In *Stein*, the inspector personally inspected the property before issuing the raze order. The court in *Stein* merely noted in dicta that an inspector could rely on expert reports in certain circumstances—but critically, that holding was predicated on the assumption that the inspector had already formed an initial judgment based on personal observation. The facts here are distinctly different: Inspector Craanen admitted that he "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order, nor did he independently verify that the McLartys' repair estimate was correct."

Due process requires that the municipal official making this momentous decision personally observe the property's condition rather than relying solely on interested party submissions. The McLartys had every incentive to maximize repair costs to trigger the raze order and collect full policy benefits. An inspector who never saw the property cannot exercise the independent judgment that the statute requires.

The circuit court found that Craanen "acted professionally and in good faith," but good faith does not substitute for compliance with statutory requirements. The statute contemplates that an inspector will make an independent determination based on personal knowledge or, at minimum, objective verification—not merely rubber-stamp an estimate provided by parties with financial incentives to see the building razed.

These are cleaning and deodorization services that address secondary effects of fire, not structural deficiencies that render a building "out of repair."

The distinction is critical: a building with smoke and water damage remains structurally intact and potentially habitable, while a building with load-bearing wall damage or compromised foundation is truly "out of repair." Including smoke and water damage remediation in the repair calculation artificially inflates the cost figure and creates an incentive for municipalities to issue raze orders for homes that are, in fact, repairable.

The City's interpretation produces an absurd result. Under the City's reasoning, a home with minor structural damage but extensive smoke contamination could be razed even though the structural damage alone would not justify such action. This cannot be the statute's intent. The raze order statute is a public safety measure, not a mechanism for circumventing insurance coverage. As this Court noted in *Hickey*, the statute "is not a tool for insurers to manipulate coverage." The defense maintains that smoke and water damage remediation costs must be excluded from the calculation under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c).

**DECISION**

The court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the City of Appleton and Auto-Owners Insurance Company. Upon further consideration of the arguments regarding the interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 and the application of due process to the issuance of raze orders, the court finds that Auto-Owners has demonstrated that the raze order in this case was unreasonable and arbitrary. While the court maintains that the statute applies to sudden fire damage and that remediation costs are generally included in repair calculations, the City's failure to conduct a personal inspection of the property constitutes a fatal procedural defect. Accordingly, the circuit court's order denying the motion for an injunction is reversed.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Sudden Fire Damage**

The court reaffirms its preliminary finding that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies to buildings damaged by sudden fire, not just those suffering from chronic neglect. The City correctly relies on *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that the cause of damage is irrelevant to the "out of repair" analysis. The Defense's attempt to distinguish *Hickey* based on the duration of the damage is unpersuasive. The statutory language focuses on the building's current condition and its threat to public safety, not the timeline of the deterioration. Therefore, the circuit court correctly determined that the fire-damaged residence fell within the purview of the statute.

**II. The Inspector Was Required to Personally Inspect the Premises**

The court reverses its preliminary ruling on the issue of personal inspection. Auto-Owners argues persuasively that the issuance of a raze order without a personal site visit violates due process and statutory requirements. The City relies on *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), to assert that an inspector may rely on expert reports when damage is "objectively evident." However, the Defense correctly notes that *Stein* involved an inspector who had personally observed the property. The City's interpretation of *Stein* to excuse a total lack of inspection is flawed.

A raze order results in the total destruction of a property owner's home. Due process requires that such a drastic measure be based on the independent judgment of a municipal official, not merely the rubber-stamping of an estimate provided by an interested party. Here, Inspector Craanen admitted he "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order, nor did he independently verify that the McLartys' repair estimate was correct." The fact that the order was initiated by the insured's attorney, using the insured's own estimate, without any verification by the City, renders the order arbitrary. The Defense's argument that *State ex rel. Orr v. City of Ripon*, 57 Wis. 2d 89, 203 N.W.2d 758 (1973), requires clear evidence of unreasonableness is well-taken; the City lacked such evidence because it never sought to verify the condition of the building firsthand.

**III. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Must Be Included**

The court upholds the inclusion of smoke and water damage remediation costs in the repair calculation. The City's argument, supported by *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, that "all costs necessary to restore a building to a safe and habitable condition must be included," is consistent with the public safety purpose of the statute. The Defense's attempt to categorize smoke remediation as distinct from "repair" is overly formalistic. A home contaminated by smoke is uninhabitable and poses a health risk; therefore, the cost to remediate that condition is a necessary cost of repair under the statute. While the Defense raises valid concerns about the potential for inflated estimates, the proper remedy for those concerns is rigorous verification by the inspector—not the exclusion of legitimate safety costs from the statutory formula.

**IV. The Raze Order Was Arbitrary**

The court agrees with Auto-Owners that the circumstances surrounding the issuance of the raze order raise serious due process concerns. The record indicates that the City acted solely at the request of the insured's attorney, relying exclusively on a ServPro estimate provided by that attorney. The City did not initiate the action based on its own independent observations of a public nuisance. While the statute is a public health measure, as noted in *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), it cannot be used as a tool for insured parties to manufacture a total loss where none would otherwise exist. The City's failure to inspect the property or independently verify the costs before issuing the order constituted an unreasonable and arbitrary exercise of municipal power.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the court finds that the City of Appleton issued the raze order in violation of due process because the building inspector failed to personally inspect the premises or independently verify the repair estimate. While the statute authorizes the inclusion of smoke and water remediation costs, the procedural deficiencies in this case require that the raze order be vacated. **AUTO-OWNERS INSURANCE COMPANY, Appellant,**
v.
**CITY OF APPLETON, Respondent.**

**CASE NO.** [REDACTED]

**DECISION**

The court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the City of Appleton and Auto-Owners Insurance Company. Upon consideration of the parties' final briefs regarding the interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 and the due process requirements for issuing a raze order, the court adheres to its determination that the raze order in this case was unreasonable and arbitrary. While the City correctly asserts that the statute applies to sudden fire damage and that remediation costs are included in repair calculations, its argument that a personal inspection is unnecessary under the precedent of *Village of Shorewood v. Stein* is unpersuasive. The City's total reliance on an estimate provided by an interested party, without any independent verification or site visit by the building inspector, constituted a fatal procedural defect. Accordingly, the circuit court's order denying the motion for an injunction is reversed.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Sudden Fire Damage**

The court reaffirms its finding that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies to buildings damaged by sudden fire. The City relies on *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), to argue that the cause of the damage is irrelevant to the "out of repair" analysis. Auto-Owners has conceded this point in its closing statement, and the court agrees. The statutory language focuses on the building's current condition and the threat it poses to public safety, not the timeline of the deterioration. Therefore, the circuit court correctly determined that the fire-damaged residence fell within the purview of the statute.

**II. The Inspector Was Required to Personally Inspect the Premises**

The central issue on appeal is whether a building inspector may issue a raze order without ever visiting the property. The City argues that *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), authorizes inspectors to rely on expert reports when damage is "objectively evident," negating the need for a personal site visit. The City contends that Inspector Craanen's review of the ServPro estimate satisfied this requirement.

Auto-Owners correctly distinguishes *Stein* by pointing out that the inspector in that case had personally observed the property before issuing the order. The City's attempt to extend *Stein* to cover a situation where the inspector conducted *no* independent observation is flawed. A raze order results in the total destruction of a property owner's home. Due process requires that such a drastic measure be based on the independent judgment of a municipal official.

Here, Inspector Craanen admitted he "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order, nor did he independently verify that the McLartys' repair estimate was correct." The City relied exclusively on a document provided by the insured's attorney—an interested party with a financial incentive to have the home razed. The City's assertion that "due process does not require a personal site visit when the evidence of danger is clear from reliable reports" fails because the reliability of the report was never verified by the City. As noted in *State ex rel. Orr v. City of Ripon*, 57 Wis. 2d 89, 203 N.W.2d 758 (1973), raze orders must be supported by clear evidence of unreasonableness. The City lacked such evidence because it never sought to verify the condition of the building firsthand.

**III. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Must Be Included**

The court upholds the inclusion of smoke and water damage remediation costs in the repair calculation. The City argues, and Auto-Owners concedes, that *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), requires the inclusion of "all costs necessary to restore a building to a safe and habitable condition." Smoke damage poses health hazards, and water damage can compromise structural integrity. Therefore, these costs are properly considered part of the "cost of repair" under the statute. Auto-Owners' concern regarding inflated estimates is valid, but the proper remedy for that concern is rigorous verification by the inspector—not the exclusion of legitimate safety costs.

**IV. The Raze Order Was Arbitrary**

The court agrees with Auto-Owners that the circumstances surrounding the issuance of the raze order render it arbitrary. The City did not initiate the action based on its own observations of a public nuisance; rather, it acted solely at the request of the insured's attorney. The City's position that the initiation of the inquiry is "irrelevant to the statutory analysis" ignores the reality that the City functioned as a rubber stamp for the insured's estimate. Without a personal inspection or independent verification of the costs, the City's action was an unreasonable and arbitrary exercise of municipal power. The statute is a public health measure, but it cannot be used as a tool for insured parties to manufacture a total loss where none would otherwise exist.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the court finds that the City of Appleton issued the raze order in violation of due process because the building inspector failed to personally inspect the premises or independently verify the repair estimate. The City's reliance on *Village of Shorewood v. Stein* to excuse this total lack of inspection is misplaced. While the statute authorizes the inclusion of smoke and water remediation costs, the procedural deficiencies in this case require that the raze order be vacated. The circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for an injunction is reversed, and the matter is remanded with instructions to grant the injunction.

BY THE COURT,

/s/ The Judge
