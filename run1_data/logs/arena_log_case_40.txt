II. Factual and Procedural Background

On October 1, 2012, Joki filed the initial complaint against the State Defendants, 114 public school districts, and one charter school.  On October 9, 2012, Joki filed an amended class action complaint, adding a second cause of action against the State Defendants. Specifically, Joki requested a declaratory judgment that Idaho’s current system of funding education is unconstitutionalIdaho Const, art. IX, § 1.

On October 30, 2012, the State Defendants filed a motion to dismiss, which the district court granted on March 19, 2013. On the same day, 53 of the 114 school districts were voluntarily dismissed by Joki. On March 27, 2013, Joki submitted a motion to alter or amend the order dismissing the State Defendants. On June 27, 2013, Joki, Meridian Joint District #2, and the State Defendants stipulated that Joki may file a second amended complaint, with the provision that Meridian Joint District #2 and the State Defendants reserved the right to seek dismissal, summary judgment, and resist class certification. The district court granted Joki’s motion for leave to file a second amended complaint. On July 16, 2013, Joki filed the second amended complaint, which sought: (1) reimbursement from Meridian Joint District #2 or the legislature of certain fees imposed by the school districts; and (2) a declaratory judgment against the State Defendants that the current system of funding education in Idaho is unconstitutional.

On July 17, 2013, the State Defendants filed a motion to dismiss the second amended complaint. In an accompanying memorandum, the State Defendants argued that the claims against them fell squarely within the terns of the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act, Idaho Code sections 6-2201-2216 (hereinafter referred to as the “CBECA”), and should be dismissed according to the provisions therein. In response, Joki argued that the CBECA did not require the dismissal of the State Defendants. Specifically, Joki asserted that according to this Court’s holding in Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (hereinafter referred to as “ISEEO IV”), the CBECA was unconstitutional as applied to his second cause of action.2

On November 27, 2013, the district court issued a memorandum decision and order regarding the State Defendants’ motion to dismiss. The district court found that the CBECA applied to Joki’s claim for fee reimbursement and required the dismissal of the State Defendants. The district court reasoned that the CBECA applied to Joki’s claim because, under the CBECA, a district court may issue any order that it determines would assist the local school district in administering constitutionally required educational services.3 Further, the district court reasoned that the State Defendants could only be added as defendants after the following occurred: (1) a bench trial; (2) a finding that a school district failed to provide constitutionally required educational services; (3) the issuance of a corresponding remedial order; and (4) continued noncompliance by a school district. Joki did not comply with the aforementioned procedural requirements; accordingly, the district court granted the State Defendants’ motion to dismiss.

Next, the district court analyzed Joki’s request for a declaratory judgment that the current system of funding education in Idaho is unconstitutional. The district court acknowledged Joki’s argument that in ISEEO V this Court found the system of funding school facilities in Idaho was unconstitutional. However, the district court held that Joki’s complaint failed to state a claim to enforce ISEEO V. The district court emphasized that ISEEO V addressed the narrow issue of funding for school facilities, not the education system as a whole. Because Joki’s complaint did not allege inadequate school facilities, the district court concluded that Joki failed to state a claim to enforce ISEEO V. Further, the district court reasoned that even if Joki were to allege facts within the purview of this *8Court’s holding in ISEEO V, the CBECA would nevertheless apply and require Joki to first exhaust the remedies under the CBECA before pursuing action against the State Defendants. In sum, the district court held that the CBECA governed all of Joki’s claims, as brought against the State Defendants, and required dismissal.

On June 27, 2014, the district court denied Joki’s motion for class certification due to “concerns over the suitability of [Joki] as representative[ ] of the proposed class.” Ultimately, on December 7, 2016, the district court entered judgment in favor of Joki against Meridian Joint District #2 in the amount of $85. Joki appealed the dismissal of the State Defendants.

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. The District Court Correctly Applied CBECA to Dismiss Joki's Claims Against the State Defendants

The district court correctly determined that CBECA applies to Joki's claims and requires dismissal of the State Defendants. CBECA establishes a comprehensive, sequential framework for resolving disputes involving constitutionally required educational services. *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (ISEEO IV).

Under CBECA, a plaintiff must exhaust remedies against local school districts before proceeding against the State. The statutory scheme requires: (1) a bench trial to determine whether a school district failed to provide constitutionally required educational services; (2) the issuance of a remedial order addressing such failure; and (3) continued noncompliance by the school district before the State may be added as a defendant. Idaho Code § 6-2203; *see also* ISEEO IV, 140 Idaho at 592-93, 97 P.3d at 459-60.

Joki failed to comply with any of these mandatory requirements. The record reflects that Joki never initiated a bench trial against any school district, never obtained a finding that a school district failed to provide constitutionally required educational services, never secured a remedial order, and never demonstrated continued noncompliance. Rather, Joki attempted to bypass the entire CBECA framework by directly suing the State Defendants in his second amended complaint.

The district court properly rejected this attempt. CBECA's procedural requirements are not mere suggestions; they represent the Legislature's determination that educational disputes should first be addressed at the local level before state liability attaches. This sequential approach ensures that remedial efforts begin with the entity closest to the educational process—the local school district—before implicating state funding mechanisms.

Joki's opening argument attempts to characterize the dismissal as a technicality, but the CBECA requirements go to the substance of the claims. By design, CBECA prevents plaintiffs from circumventing the local remediation process and directly imposing state liability without first establishing a local district's failure and giving that district an opportunity to comply with a court order. Joki's failure to follow this process renders his claims against the State Defendants premature as a matter of law.

#### B. The District Court Correctly Dismissed Joki's Attempt to Enforce ISEEO V

The district court correctly held that Joki failed to state a claim to enforce *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 530, 97 P.3d 397 (2004) (ISEEO V).

In ISEEO V, this Court addressed a narrow issue: the constitutionality of Idaho's system for funding school facilities. The Court held that the then-existing system for funding school facility construction and renovation was unconstitutional because it resulted in disparities between wealthy and poor school districts. *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 530, 97 P.3d 397 (2004).

Critically, ISEEO V did not address the general system of funding education in Idaho, nor did it address the funding of ongoing educational operations, teacher salaries, or educational programs. The decision was limited to the narrow issue of facility funding.

Joki's second amended complaint sought a declaratory judgment that "Idaho's current system of funding education is unconstitutional" under Idaho Constitution Article IX, Section 1. This broad allegation does not state a claim to enforce ISEEO V because ISEEO V addressed only facility funding, not the general education funding system. The district court correctly noted that Joki's complaint did not allege inadequate school facilities, which is the sole basis for the ISEEO V holding.

Moreover, even if Joki had alleged facts within the purview of ISEEO V, CBECA would still apply and require dismissal. CBECA applies to "any claim for educational services that are required to be provided by the constitution of the state of Idaho." Idaho Code § 6-2202(1). If ISEEO V established a constitutional requirement—which it did with respect to facility funding—then CBECA's framework applies to any claim seeking to enforce that requirement. Joki cannot circumvent CBECA by labeling his claim as one for declaratory relief; the substance of his claim determines CBECA's applicability.

The district court properly dismissed Joki's attempt to enforce ISEEO V for failure to state a claim.

#### C. CBECA Is Constitutional and Was Properly Applied

Joki's opening argument suggests that CBECA is unconstitutional as applied to his claims. This argument fails.

This Court has consistently upheld CBECA as a valid exercise of legislative authority. In ISEEO IV, this Court addressed CBECA's constitutionality and held that it does not violate principles of due process or separation of powers. 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004). CBECA is a procedural statute that establishes a framework for resolving educational claims; it does not eliminate any substantive constitutional right. Rather, it provides an orderly process for vindicating those rights.

The procedural requirements of CBECA serve legitimate legislative purposes. They ensure that local school districts have the first opportunity to address constitutional deficiencies, promote judicial efficiency by resolving disputes at the local level when possible, and provide a structured remedial process that can be tailored to the specific needs of each district.

Joki has not identified any constitutional deficiency in CBECA's application to his claims. The district court correctly applied CBECA and properly dismissed the State Defendants.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants. The Appellant, Joki, filed a complaint seeking reimbursement of fees and a declaratory judgment that Idaho’s system of funding education is unconstitutional. The district court dismissed the State Defendants, concluding that the claims were governed by the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act (CBECA), Idaho Code sections 6-2201-2216, and that Joki failed to comply with its mandatory procedural requirements. The district court further held that Joki failed to state a claim to enforce the holding of *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 530, 97 P.3d 397 (2004) (ISEEO V).

Upon review of the factual background and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the district court’s dismissal was proper.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The CBECA Establishes the Exclusive Framework for Educational Claims**

The arguments presented correctly assert that the CBECA provides the exclusive mechanism for resolving disputes regarding constitutionally required educational services. As established in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (ISEEO IV), the Legislature intended to create a specific, sequential process to address these claims. This process requires plaintiffs to seek remedies against local school districts before implicating state liability.

The record demonstrates that Joki failed to adhere to the mandatory procedural prerequisites set forth in the CBECA. Under the Act, a plaintiff must: (1) initiate a bench trial against a local school district; (2) obtain a finding that the district failed to provide constitutionally required educational services; (3) secure a remedial order; and (4) demonstrate continued noncompliance by the district after such an order. See *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004).

Here, Joki did not initiate a bench trial, nor did he obtain a finding of failure or a remedial order against any local school district. Instead, he sought to bypass these statutory requirements by suing the State Defendants directly. The arguments presented confirm that the CBECA’s procedural sequence is mandatory and not merely suggestive. Because Joki failed to exhaust the required local remedies, the district court correctly determined that the claims against the State Defendants were premature and subject to dismissal.

**B. Joki Failed to State a Claim Under ISEEO V**

The district court also correctly dismissed Joki’s request for a declaratory judgment regarding the general constitutionality of Idaho’s education funding system. The arguments distinguish the present case from *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 530, 97 P.3d 397 (2004) (ISEEO V). In ISEEO V, this Court addressed the narrow issue of funding for school facilities, specifically holding that the then-existing system for facility construction and renovation was unconstitutional.

Joki’s second amended complaint alleged that the "current system of funding education" is unconstitutional, but it did not allege inadequate school facilities. As argued, ISEEO V is limited to the specific context of facility funding and does not extend to a broad challenge of the entire education funding system. Because Joki’s complaint lacks factual allegations regarding school facility deficiencies, it fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted under ISEEO V.

Furthermore, even if Joki had alleged facts falling within the scope of ISEEO V, the CBECA would still govern his claim. The CBECA applies to "any claim for educational services that are required to be provided by the constitution of the state of Idaho." Idaho Code § 6-2202(1). Consequently, Joki would be required to exhaust the procedural remedies of the CBECA before proceeding against the State Defendants.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court properly applied the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act and correctly dismissed the State Defendants. Joki failed to satisfy the statutory prerequisites for joining the State Defendants and failed to state a claim enforceable under ISEEO V. Therefore, the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

The CBECA preserves all substantive rights while requiring plaintiffs to first seek remedies from the entities most directly responsible for providing educational services—local school districts. In that case, this Court held that the CBECA is a "valid procedural statute that does not eliminate substantive constitutional rights but merely prescribes an orderly process for their vindication." The Court recognized that the Legislature has the constitutional authority to establish procedural frameworks for the resolution of claims, provided that such frameworks do not eliminate substantive rights.

The CBECA does not eliminate any substantive constitutional right to education. Rather, it establishes an orderly process for vindicating those rights—a process that prioritizes local remediation and ensures that school districts are given the opportunity to comply with constitutional requirements before state liability is implicated.

This Court should reaffirm its holding in ISEEO IV and reject the Appellant's constitutional challenge.

For the foregoing reasons, the State Defendants respectfully request that this Court affirm the district court's order dismissing the State Defendants.

The Appellant failed to comply with the mandatory procedural prerequisites established by the CBECA for joining State Defendants in constitutionally based educational claims. His failure to exhaust local remedies renders his claims premature.

The Appellant's request for a declaratory judgment regarding Idaho's education funding system fails to state a claim because it does not fall within the narrow scope of ISEEO V, which addressed only facility funding, not the broader education system.

The Appellant's fee reimbursement claim is also governed by the CBECA and cannot proceed without compliance with its sequential requirements.

The CBECA is a constitutional and valid exercise of legislative authority that this Court should continue to uphold.

The district court correctly applied the CBECA and properly dismissed the State Defendants. This Court should affirm that ruling.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants. The Appellant, Joki, filed a complaint seeking reimbursement of fees and a declaratory judgment that Idaho’s system of funding education is unconstitutional. The district court dismissed the State Defendants, concluding that the claims were governed by the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act (CBECA), Idaho Code sections 6-2201-2216, and that Joki failed to comply with its mandatory procedural requirements. The district court further held that Joki failed to state a claim to enforce the holding of *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 530, 97 P.3d 397 (2004) (ISEEO V).

Upon review of the factual background, the initial arguments, and the closing arguments submitted by the parties, the Court finds that the district court’s dismissal was proper. The arguments presented confirm that the CBECA provides the exclusive mechanism for Joki’s claims and that ISEEO V does not support the broad challenge to the education funding system presented in this case.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The CBECA Establishes the Exclusive and Constitutional Framework for Educational Claims**

The arguments presented by the parties correctly assert that the CBECA provides the exclusive mechanism for resolving disputes regarding constitutionally required educational services. As established in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (ISEEO IV), the Legislature intended to create a specific, sequential process to address these claims. This process requires plaintiffs to seek remedies against local school districts before implicating state liability.

The record demonstrates that Joki failed to adhere to the mandatory procedural prerequisites set forth in the CBECA. Under the Act, a plaintiff must: (1) initiate a bench trial against a local school district; (2) obtain a finding that the district failed to provide constitutionally required educational services; (3) secure a remedial order; and (4) demonstrate continued noncompliance by the district after such an order. See *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004).

Here, Joki did not initiate a bench trial, nor did he obtain a finding of failure or a remedial order against any local school district. Instead, he sought to bypass these statutory requirements by suing the State Defendants directly. The arguments presented confirm that the CBECA’s procedural sequence is mandatory and not merely suggestive. Because Joki failed to exhaust the required local remedies, the district court correctly determined that the claims against the State Defendants were premature and subject to dismissal.

Furthermore, the argument that the CBECA is unconstitutional as applied is without merit. As noted in the closing arguments, this Court explicitly rejected such challenges in *ISEEO IV*, holding that the CBECA is a valid procedural statute that does not eliminate substantive rights but provides an orderly process for their vindication. The Act serves legitimate legislative interests by prioritizing local remediation and promoting judicial economy.

**B. Joki Failed to State a Claim Under ISEEO V**

The district court also correctly dismissed Joki’s request for a declaratory judgment regarding the general constitutionality of Idaho’s education funding system. The arguments presented distinguish the present case from *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 530, 97 P.3d 397 (2004) (ISEEO V). In ISEEO V, this Court addressed the narrow issue of funding for school facilities, specifically holding that the then-existing system for facility construction and renovation was unconstitutional.

Joki’s second amended complaint alleged that the "current system of funding education" is unconstitutional, but it did not allege inadequate school facilities. As argued, ISEEO V is limited to the specific context of facility funding and does not extend to a broad challenge of the entire education funding system. Because Joki’s complaint lacks factual allegations regarding school facility deficiencies, it fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted under ISEEO V.

**C. The Fee Reimbursement Claim is Governed by CBECA**

The arguments further clarify that the claim for fee reimbursement is also subject to dismissal under the CBECA. The district court found that this claim fell within the CBECA because the Act applies to "any claim for educational services that are required to be provided by the constitution of the state of Idaho." Idaho Code § 6-2202(1). Fee reimbursement claims necessarily involve questions regarding the administration of constitutionally required educational services. Under Idaho Code § 6-2204, a district court may issue orders to assist a local school district in administering these services. Therefore, Joki was required to proceed through the CBECA’s sequential process to seek fee reimbursement. Because he failed to do so, the dismissal of the State Defendants regarding this claim was proper.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court properly applied the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act and correctly dismissed the State Defendants. Joki failed to satisfy the statutory prerequisites for joining the State Defendants and failed to state a claim enforceable under ISEEO V. The arguments presented in the closing arguments confirm that the CBECA is a constitutional and mandatory procedural bar to Joki’s claims against the State Defendants at this stage. Therefore, the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
