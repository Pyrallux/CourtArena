This is not merely legally unsound—it is a direct violation of the statutory framework governing paternity and support obligations.

As *Givens* emphasized, allowing a presumed father to "circumvent the disavowal process" would "undermine the integrity of the legal system" by creating "a flood of speculative claims" against putative fathers. Here, J.M.Y. offers no evidence that R.R. is the biological father—only a bare allegation. Without judicial confirmation of R.R.’s paternity, there is no basis to impose liability on him. The child’s right to support belongs to the child—or the state—but not to the presumed father seeking reimbursement. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Correctly Granted the Exception of No Right of Action

A right of action is a legal privilege to enforce a claim. It exists only when a party has a legal interest in the subject matter of the proceeding. The exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in enforcing the claim asserted.

The appellant J.M.Y. has no right of action against R.R. for reimbursement of child support payments. Under Louisiana law, the obligation to support a child is owed *to the child*, not to other potential obligors. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides that "[t]he obligation to support a child applies equally to biological and presumed fathers." However, this article establishes *who bears the duty of support*—it does not create a private right of reimbursement for one father to recover payments from another.

The Louisiana Supreme Court in *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983), held that "a presumed father cannot recover child support payments from a biological father without first obtaining a judicial declaration that he is not the father." This holding reflects the fundamental principle that the presumed father's obligation runs to the child, and until that obligation is legally terminated through proper channels, there can be no claim for reimbursement against a third party.

J.M.Y. has never sought to disavow his own legal status as the presumed father. He has never filed a disavowal proceeding under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184. He has never obtained a judicial declaration terminating his parental obligations. Until and unless these prerequisites are satisfied, he has no legal interest in seeking reimbursement from R.R. The trial court correctly granted the exception of no right of action.

### B. The Trial Court Correctly Granted the Exception of No Cause of Action

A cause of action is a valid legal basis for asserting a claim. To survive an exception of no cause of action, the petition must state a cause of action—meaning the petition must set forth facts that would entitle the plaintiff to relief. The exception of no cause of action is a pure question of law; the court must determine whether, under the facts alleged, the law provides any remedy.

The appellant has failed to state a cause of action for reimbursement. Louisiana law does not permit a presumed father to recover child support payments from a biological father without first obtaining a judicial declaration of non-paternity. As the Louisiana Supreme Court explicitly held in *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279 (La. 1983), this two-step process is mandatory: (1) a successful disavowal of paternity to terminate the presumed father's obligation, and (2) a separate judicial determination of the biological father's liability.

J.M.Y. has completed neither step. He filed suit directly against R.R. without first initiating disavowal proceedings. He has presented no judicial determination that R.R. is the biological father. His petition contains only bare allegations—without any judicial confirmation—that R.R. is the biological father of the child.

Without a judicial declaration that J.M.Y. is not the father, his obligation to support the child remains intact. A party cannot assert a claim for reimbursement when the underlying obligation has never been judicially transferred or terminated. The appellant has failed to state a cognizable cause of action.

### C. The Policy Considerations Support the Trial Court Judgment

The Louisiana statutory framework governing paternity and support obligations reflects deliberate policy choices designed to protect children and promote stability in family relationships. The presumption of legitimacy exists to protect children from chaotic claims of paternity and to ensure stability in family relationships.

As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Givens v. Here, J.M.Y. offers no evidence that R.R. is the biological father—only a bare allegation. Without judicial confirmation of R.R.'s paternity, there is no basis to impose liability on him. The trial court properly rejected this attempt to impose retroactive financial responsibility on an unproven party.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 establishes who bears the duty of support—it does not create a mechanism for one father to recover payments from another. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**J.M.Y. v. R.R., ET AL.**
**No. 08-XXXX (La. Ct. App.)**
**Honorable Judge Presiding**

**DECISION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y., appellant, from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R., appellee, and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit with prejudice. After reviewing the record, the applicable law, and the arguments presented by counsel, the Court affirms the judgment of the trial court.

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R., alleging that R.R. was the biological father of a child born during J.M.Y.’s marriage to A.F.Y. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments previously made. J.M.Y. subsequently filed an amended petition adding A.F.Y. as a defendant, seeking reimbursement from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for past and future child support payments.

R.R. filed exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action, arguing that J.M.Y. failed to state a legally cognizable claim because he had not disavowed his own paternity. Following a hearing on May 27, 2008, the trial court granted the exceptions and dismissed the suit. J.M.Y. appealed, asserting that he is entitled to reimbursement despite his status as the presumed father.

**LAW AND ANALYSIS**

The sole issue on appeal is whether a presumed father may maintain a suit for reimbursement of child support payments against a putative biological father without first obtaining a judicial declaration of non-paternity. The exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action raise questions of law, which this Court reviews de novo. *See generally* *La. C.C.P. art. 927*.

**I. The Exception of No Right of Action**

The exception of no right of action is used to determine whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons against whom the law grants the remedy sought by the suit. *La. C.C.P. art. 927(4)*. The appellant, J.M.Y., as the presumed father of the child, asserts a right of action against R.R. for reimbursement. However, this assertion is contrary to the statutory framework governing paternity and support in Louisiana.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides that "[t]he obligation to support a child applies equally to biological and presumed fathers." While this article establishes the duty of support, it does not create a private right of action for one presumed father to seek reimbursement from a biological father. The obligation is owed to the child, not to the other potential father.

The controlling precedent on this issue is *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279 (La. 1983). In *Givens*, the Louisiana Supreme Court held that "a presumed father cannot recover child support payments from a biological father without first obtaining a judicial declaration that he is not the father." *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983). The Court emphasized that the obligation to support is a duty owed to the child, and until that duty is legally terminated, the presumed father has no legal interest in recovering funds from a third party.

Here, J.M.Y. has never filed a disavowal proceeding pursuant to Louisiana Civil Code Article 184. He has never obtained a judicial declaration that he is not the father. Consequently, his obligation to support the child remains intact under the law. Because his own legal obligation has not been extinguished, he lacks the legal interest, or right of action, to pursue R.R. for reimbursement. The trial court correctly granted the exception of no right of action.

**II. The Exception of No Cause of Action**

The exception of no cause of action tests whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged in the petition. *La. C.C.P. art. 927(3)*. For the exception to be sustained, it must appear that the plaintiff is entitled to no relief under the law, regardless of the truth of the facts alleged.

J.M.Y.’s petition alleges that R.R. is the biological father and seeks reimbursement for support paid. However, under the mandate of *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279 (La. 1983), these facts are insufficient to state a cause of action. The Supreme Court established a mandatory two-step process for a presumed father seeking such relief: (1) a successful disavowal of paternity to terminate the presumed father’s obligation, and (2) a separate judicial determination of the biological father’s liability. *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983).

J.M.Y. has completely bypassed this statutory framework. He has not initiated a disavowal action, nor has he secured a judgment declaring R.R. to be the biological father. Instead, he relies on "bare allegations" to support his claim for reimbursement. The law does not permit a direct claim for reimbursement under these circumstances. Allowing such a claim would permit a presumed father to circumvent the disavowal process, thereby undermining the integrity of the paternity laws and potentially exposing putative fathers to "a flood of speculative claims." *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983).

Because J.M.Y.’s petition fails to allege facts that satisfy the jurisdictional prerequisite of a judicial declaration of non-paternity, he has failed to state a cause of action for which relief can be granted. The trial court did not err in granting the exception of no cause of action.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y., appellant.

**JUDGMENT**

IT IS ORDERED, ADJUDGED, AND DECREED that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**J.M.Y. v. R.R., ET AL.**

**Honorable Judge Presiding**

**DECISION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y., appellant, from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R., appellee, and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit with prejudice. The Court has considered the record, the applicable law, the arguments presented in the initial briefs, and the rebuttals submitted by the appellee. For the reasons that follow, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R., alleging that R.R. was the biological father of a child born during J.M.Y.’s marriage to A.F.Y. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments previously made. J.M.Y. subsequently filed an amended petition adding A.F.Y. as a defendant, seeking reimbursement from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for past and future child support payments.

R.R. filed exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The trial court granted the exceptions and dismissed the suit. J.M.Y. appealed, arguing that he is entitled to reimbursement despite his status as the presumed father. R.R. has submitted rebuttal arguments asserting that J.M.Y.’s claims are legally insufficient because he has not disavowed his paternity, has misinterpreted the statutory duty of support, and has failed to join the child as a necessary party.

**LAW AND ANALYSIS**

The exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action raise questions of law, which this Court reviews de novo. *See generally La. C.C.P. art. 927*.

**I. The Exception of No Right of Action**

The exception of no right of action questions whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons against whom the law grants the remedy sought. *La. C.C.P. art. 927(4)*. J.M.Y., as the presumed father, asserts a right of action against R.R. for reimbursement. The appellee correctly argues that this assertion is contrary to the statutory framework governing paternity and support in Louisiana.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides that "[t]he obligation to support a child applies equally to biological and presumed fathers." However, as the appellee notes, this article establishes *who bears the duty of support*—it does not create a private right of action for one father to recover payments from another. The duty is owed exclusively to the child, not to competing claimants. *See La. Civ. Code art. 229* ("The obligation to support a child is a duty owed by the parents to the child.").

The controlling precedent on this issue remains *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279 (La. 1983). In *Givens*, the Louisiana Supreme Court held that "a presumed father cannot recover child support payments from a biological father without first obtaining a judicial declaration that he is not the father." *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983). The Court emphasized that the obligation to support is a duty owed to the child, and until that duty is legally terminated, the presumed father has no legal interest in recovering funds from a third party.

J.M.Y. has never filed a disavowal proceeding pursuant to Louisiana Civil Code Article 184. He has never obtained a judicial declaration that he is not the father. Consequently, his obligation to support the child remains intact under the law. Because his own legal obligation has not been extinguished, he lacks the legal interest, or right of action, to pursue R.R. for reimbursement. The appellee's argument that J.M.Y. attempts to transform a personal obligation into a private debt is well-taken. The trial court correctly granted the exception of no right of action.

**II. The Exception of No Cause of Action**

The exception of no cause of action tests whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged in the petition. *La. C.C.P. art. 927(3)*. J.M.Y.’s petition alleges that R.R. is the biological father and seeks reimbursement for support paid. The appellee correctly asserts that under the mandate of *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279 (La. 1983), these facts are insufficient to state a cause of action.

The Supreme Court established a mandatory two-step process for a presumed father seeking such relief: (1) a successful disavowal of paternity to terminate the presumed father’s obligation, and (2) a separate judicial determination of the biological father’s liability. *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983). The appellee's rebuttal effectively counters any attempt by J.M.Y. to characterize this requirement as a mere procedural technicality. The requirement is a substantive prerequisite rooted in the child-centered nature of support obligations.

J.M.Y. has completely bypassed this statutory framework. He has not initiated a disavowal action, nor has he secured a judgment declaring R.R. to be the biological father. Instead, he relies on "bare allegations" to support his claim for reimbursement. The law does not permit a direct claim for reimbursement under these circumstances. Allowing such a claim would permit a presumed father to circumvent the disavowal process, thereby undermining the integrity of the paternity laws and potentially exposing putative fathers to "a flood of speculative claims." *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983).

Because J.M.Y.’s petition fails to allege facts that satisfy the jurisdictional prerequisite of a judicial declaration of non-paternity, he has failed to state a cause of action for which relief can be granted. The trial court did not err in granting the exception of no cause of action.

**III. Failure to Join a Necessary Party**

In addition to the grounds addressed above, the appellee correctly identifies a fatal procedural defect in J.M.Y.’s petition: the failure to join the child as a necessary party. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 927, persons having an interest in the object of the suit must be joined.

The child is the real party in interest in any support proceeding. The obligation to support is owed to the child, and any determination of liability for past or future support necessarily affects the child's rights. By failing to join the child, J.M.Y. has deprived the court of the ability to render a complete adjudication of the rights of all interested parties. This failure provides an additional, independent basis for affirming the dismissal of the suit.

**IV. Policy Considerations**

The appellee's arguments regarding public policy are persuasive. The presumption of legitimacy serves critical public policies, including protecting children from chaotic paternity disputes and ensuring stability in family relationships. *See In re Marriage of M.*, 459 So. 2d 1315, 1318 (La. 1984). J.M.Y.’s attempt to seek reimbursement without first challenging his own legal status would undermine these policies by allowing presumed fathers to avoid the disavowal process and directly sue third parties. This would effectively turn child support into a private debt to be settled between adults, a result the Supreme Court expressly rejected in *Givens*.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y., appellant.

**Honorable Judge Presiding**

**DECISION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y., appellant, from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R., appellee, and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit with prejudice. The Court has carefully reviewed the record, the applicable law, the preliminary ruling, and the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. For the reasons that follow, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R., alleging that R.R. was the biological father of a child born during J.M.Y.’s marriage to A.F.Y. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments previously made. J.M.Y. subsequently filed an amended petition adding A.F.Y. as a defendant, seeking reimbursement from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for past and future child support payments.

R.R. filed exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The trial court granted the exceptions and dismissed the suit. J.M.Y. appealed, arguing that he is entitled to reimbursement despite his status as the presumed father. The defense argues that the mandatory disavowal process required by *Givens v. Givens* should not apply where a specific biological father is named, and that requiring disavowal prior to seeking reimbursement creates an "impossible burden."

**LAW AND ANALYSIS**

The exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action raise questions of law, which this Court reviews de novo. *See generally La. C.C.P. art. 927*.

**I. The Exception of No Right of Action**

The exception of no right of action questions whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons against whom the law grants the remedy sought. *La. C.C.P. art. 927(4)*. J.M.Y., as the presumed father, asserts a right of action against R.R. for reimbursement. The defense argues that the holding in *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279 (La. 1983), is inapplicable because J.M.Y. has identified a specific biological father, whereas the plaintiff in *Givens* had not. The defense contends that the current suit initiates the necessary judicial process to determine paternity.

This Court finds the defense's distinction unpersuasive. The controlling precedent in *Givens* establishes a substantive prerequisite for any reimbursement claim: a presumed father must first obtain a judicial declaration that he is not the father. The Supreme Court stated clearly that "a presumed father cannot recover child support payments from a biological father without first obtaining a judicial declaration that he is not the father." *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983). This rule is not limited to cases where the biological father is unknown; it is a universal requirement designed to protect the integrity of the presumption of legitimacy and the stability of the child's family environment.

J.M.Y. has not filed a disavowal proceeding pursuant to Louisiana Civil Code Article 184. He has not obtained a judgment declaring him to be not the father. Consequently, his obligation to support the child remains intact under the law. Because his own legal obligation has not been extinguished, he lacks the legal interest, or right of action, to pursue R.R. for reimbursement. The defense's attempt to frame the reimbursement suit as the equivalent of a disavowal action is legally incorrect. A suit for money damages against a third party is not a substitute for the formal disavowal process required by Article 184. The trial court correctly granted the exception of no right of action.

**II. The Exception of No Cause of Action**

The exception of no cause of action tests whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged in the petition. *La. C.C.P. art. 927(3)*. J.M.Y. argues that his petition alleges specific facts identifying R.R. as the biological father and seeks genetic testing, thereby stating a cause of action for reimbursement under theories of unjust enrichment. The defense contends that requiring a separate disavowal proceeding creates an "impossible burden" and an "absurd result."

The defense's reliance on "unjust enrichment" is misplaced where a specific statutory framework governs the remedy. The Supreme Court in *Givens* explicitly rejected the notion that a presumed father could bypass the statutory process to recover support payments. The Court established a mandatory two-step process: (1) a successful disavowal of paternity to terminate the presumed father’s obligation, and (2) a separate judicial determination of the biological father’s liability. *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983).

The defense argues that this two-step process is an "impossible burden" because it requires a presumed father to declare non-paternity before having evidentiary support. However, this burden is not impossible; it is the procedural mechanism established by the legislature to balance the rights of the father, the mother, and the child. Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 provides the specific grounds and time limits for disavowal, which include the use of genetic testing to prove non-paternity. J.M.Y. cannot circumvent this comprehensive statutory scheme by filing a direct suit for reimbursement. By failing to allege that he has successfully disavowed his paternity, J.M.Y. has failed to state a cause of action for which relief can be granted. The trial court did not err in granting the exception of no cause of action.

**III. Failure to Join a Necessary Party**

The defense argues that the child is not a necessary party because J.M.Y. is not seeking to reduce or eliminate his support obligation, and therefore the child's rights are not at stake. This argument ignores the fundamental nature of paternity and support proceedings.

Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 927, persons having an interest in the object of the suit must be joined. The child is the real party in interest in any proceeding involving paternity and support. The obligation to support is owed to the child, and any determination regarding the biological father necessarily affects the child's legal status, rights of succession, and legitimacy. *See La. Civ. Code art. 229*. Even if J.M.Y. asserts that he will continue to pay support, a judicial determination that R.R. is the biological father has profound legal consequences for the child. By failing to join the child, J.M.Y. has deprived the court of the ability to render a complete adjudication of the rights of all interested parties. This failure provides an additional, independent basis for affirming the dismissal of the suit.

**IV. Policy Considerations**

The defense urges this Court to consider the "fairness" of allowing a biological father to avoid accountability by remaining silent. While the concerns raised by the defense are not without some emotional force, they cannot override the clear statutory and jurisprudential mandates designed to protect the child.

The presumption of legitimacy serves critical public policies, including protecting children from chaotic paternity disputes and ensuring stability in family relationships. *See In re Marriage of M.*, 459 So. 2d 1315, 1318 (La. 1984). Allowing a presumed father to sue a third party for reimbursement without first disavowing the child would undermine these policies. It would effectively permit fathers to litigate paternity in piecemeal fashion, potentially subjecting the child to multiple legal battles regarding their parentage. The "flood of speculative claims" warned against in *Givens* is not avoided simply because a specific individual is named; the risk of instability remains if the disavowal process is bypassed. *Givens v. Givens*, 426 So. 2d 1279, 1281 (La. 1983).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. The defense's attempts to distinguish *Givens* or to circumvent the statutory disavowal process are rejected. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y., appellant.

IT IS ORDERED, ADJUDGED, AND DECREED that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
