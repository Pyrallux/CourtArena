FACTS:
Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. Judgment of conviction was entered upon his plea of guilty after the charge against him was reduced from aggravated battery, as origi*380nally alleged. He later filed a motion under I.C.R. 35, seeking reduction of the sentence so he could be released for psychotherapy that he claimed was unavailable in confinement. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

Here, the victim of the crime was Galbraith’s seven-month-old daughter. Two incidents of abuse occurred. In the first, Galbraith kicked the child off a bed, cracking her ribs. In proceedings under the Child Protective Act, the girl was placed with her maternal grandparents and Galbraith was enrolled in a counseling program. However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

WALTERS, C.J., and SWANSTROM, J., concur.

## In the Idaho Court of Appeals

### STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's argument, while ably presented, fails to address the fundamental equities that should govern this Court's review of the Rule 35 motion denial. While we do not contest the standard of review or the procedural posture as outlined by the prosecution, we contend that the district court failed to give adequate consideration to critical mitigating factors and the individualized circumstances of this defendant. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the district court's order and remand for further proceedings.

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO ADEQUATELY WEIGH MITIGATING FACTORS

The prosecution correctly notes that Rule 35 motions are committed to the sound discretion of the district court. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). However, this discretion is not unlimited. When exercising its discretion, the district court must consider all relevant factors, including not only the seriousness of the offense but also the defendant's personal circumstances, potential for rehabilitation, and the goals of sentencing. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

The prosecution emphasizes the horrific nature of the offense—and the defense does not minimize the suffering of the victim. However, the record contains substantial mitigating evidence that the district court appears to have given insufficient weight:

**Age and Maturity**: Galbraith was eighteen years old at the time of the offense. As this Court has recognized, youth is a factor that may be considered in assessing rehabilitation potential. The human brain continues to develop into the early twenties, and impulse control, judgment, and emotional regulation are all areas where maturation can produce meaningful change. While this does not excuse the conduct, it is a relevant sentencing consideration that bears upon the appropriate duration of confinement.

**Lack of Prior Criminal Record**: Galbraith had no criminal record of consequence prior to these incidents. This absence of prior convictions is a factor that should weigh in favor of leniency, particularly in the context of a first-time offender seeking rehabilitation.

**Military Service**: Galbraith was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve at the time of the offense. This factor demonstrates a willingness to serve society and adhere to structure and discipline—qualities that bode well for successful rehabilitation if given the opportunity.

**Background Factors**: The psychological evaluation revealed that Galbraith himself was abused as a child. While this does not excuse his conduct toward his own daughter, it provides important context for understanding the cycle of abuse and supports the argument that targeted therapeutic intervention could break this cycle.

The defense acknowledges the prosecution's emphasis on the psychological evaluation's findings regarding lack of empathy and poor prognosis. However, we note that the prosecution selectively quotes from this evaluation. The defense also presented testimony from a second psychologist who was "more optimistic" about Galbraith's therapeutic prospects and recommended psychotherapy. The district court was in the best position to assess these competing opinions, but it was obligated to give meaningful consideration to both.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT FAILED TO ADEQUATELY CONSIDER THE THERAPEUTIC NEEDS PRESENTED

The prosecution characterizes the defense psychologist's testimony as "hollow" because he admitted that delaying therapy until parole would not "substantially diminish" rehabilitation prospects. This characterization misreads the testimony and ignores its substance.

The defense psychologist testified that psychotherapy was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution and recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy "as soon as possible." The psychologist was not saying that incarceration was equally effective as therapy—he was saying that the passage of one year would not make therapy ineffective. This is a far cry from saying that incarceration is an acceptable substitute for treatment.

Furthermore, the defense psychologist testified that "age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome." This suggests that early intervention is preferable to delayed intervention, even if the delay does not catastrophically undermine prospects.

The prosecution argues that the district court correctly balanced the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society. However, the defense submits that the court's balancing was flawed because it failed to recognize that the two risks are not equivalent. The risk to society from early release can be managed through conditions of probation, monitoring, and therapeutic oversight. The risk of impaired rehabilitation from continued incarceration without treatment is that Galbraith may never receive the intervention he needs to address the underlying issues that led to the abuse.

The prosecution cites *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984), for the proposition that a Rule 35 motion is not a vehicle for re-litigating the original sentence. However, a Rule 35 motion is also not an occasion for the court to simply rubber-stamp the original sentence without meaningful consideration of new circumstances or evidence presented in support of the motion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The appeal of the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed. The district court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to reduce the sentence, nor did the denial of the motion constitute cruel and unusual punishment.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence pursuant to Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App.1985). Such a motion is essentially a plea for leniency, and on appellate review, we will not substitute our judgment for that of the trial court unless we find an abuse of that discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984).

**II. Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. However, his constitutional attack on the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence, but only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. This Court consistently holds that we will not review a sentence on direct appeal when the defendant has appealed only the denial of a Rule 35 motion. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Therefore, our review is limited to whether the district court abused its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion based on the expanded record available at that time.

**III. Merits of the Rule 35 Motion**

In reviewing the denial of a Rule 35 motion, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply equally to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

The district court’s decision was grounded in the severity of the offense and the need to protect society. Galbraith committed a violent assault against his seven-month-old daughter, resulting in a complex skull fracture and a subdural hematoma. This was the second such incident; the first occurred while the child was placed in his care, leading to counseling and placement with grandparents. The second assault occurred while Galbraith was babysitting the child and was ostensibly enrolled in a counseling program. The court correctly noted that the offense was committed while Galbraith was already under court-ordered supervision for prior abuse.

The defense argues that the court abused its discretion by failing to give adequate weight to mitigating factors such as Galbraith’s age (eighteen), lack of prior criminal record, military service, and status as a victim of childhood abuse. While these factors are relevant to sentencing, they are not dispositive. The district court acknowledged the arguments for leniency but found that the risk Galbraith posed to the victim and other defenseless persons outweighed these considerations. The presentence report and psychological evaluation supported this finding, describing Galbraith as having a “poor social conscience,” “communication problems,” and a “pronounced lack of ability to empathize.” The evaluator concluded Galbraith was a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.”

Regarding rehabilitation, the defense contends that the court failed to properly consider Galbraith’s therapeutic needs, specifically the unavailability of psychotherapy in the correctional institution. The district court explicitly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society. While the defense psychologist testified that early intervention was preferable and that therapy was unavailable in prison, he also admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility—which would occur approximately one year after the hearing—would not result in a “substantial diminution in the probability of success.” Given this admission, the district court acted within its discretion in determining that the risk to society posed by early release outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate therapy.

The defense suggests that the risk to society could be managed through probation conditions preventing contact with the child. However, the psychological evaluation indicated a risk not only to his daughter but to other defenseless persons. The district court was entitled to rely on this assessment in determining that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the public at large.

The sentence imposed—a five-year indeterminate term—allows for parole eligibility after approximately twenty months. This duration is consistent with the objective of protecting society while preserving the possibility of rehabilitation through therapy upon parole. The court’s decision to deny the Rule 35 motion reflects a balanced consideration of the relevant factors and does not constitute an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion. The sentence is consistent with the goals of protecting society and is not cruel and unusual. Therefore, the order denying relief is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

The appeal of the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed. The district court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to reduce the sentence.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence pursuant to Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App.1985). On appellate review, we will not substitute our judgment for that of the trial court unless we find an abuse of that discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). An abuse of discretion occurs only when the trial court's action is arbitrary, capricious, or unreasonable.

**II. Mitigating Factors and Weight of the Evidence**

The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to mitigating factors such as Galbraith’s youth, lack of prior criminal record, military service, and status as a victim of childhood abuse. While the defense correctly asserts that discretion is not unlimited, the record demonstrates that the district court did consider these factors but determined they were outweighed by the severity of the offense and the need to protect society.

The sentencing objectives include protecting society, deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The district court found that the nature of the offense—striking a seven-month-old infant with sufficient force to cause a complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma—was egregious. Furthermore, the offense occurred while Galbraith was already enrolled in a counseling program following a prior incident of abuse against the same child. This recidivism, occurring despite intervention efforts, supports the court's determination that Galbraith presented a continuing risk.

The defense contends the court improperly extrapolated a risk to "other defenseless persons" from a psychological evaluation that primarily identified a risk to the child. However, given the violence inflicted upon a helpless infant, the court’s inference that Galbraith might pose a similar risk to other defenseless individuals was not unreasonable. The district court was within its discretion to assign greater weight to the protection of society—specifically defenseless persons—than to Galbraith’s mitigating factors. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

**III. Rehabilitation and Risk to Society**

The defense argues that the district court misapplied the rehabilitation analysis by focusing on the "marginal benefit" of immediate therapy rather than the necessity of the sentence. The district court explicitly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

Crucially, the defense psychologist admitted that delaying treatment until Galbraith earned parole—approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—would not result in a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." This admission significantly undermines the defense's claim that immediate release was necessary for effective rehabilitation. The district court acted within its discretion in determining that the risk to society posed by early release outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate therapy. The sentence, an indeterminate five-year term with parole eligibility after approximately twenty months, allows for therapy upon release without unduly compromising public safety.

**IV. Probation and Alternative Sentencing**

The defense suggests that the district court failed to properly address probation as a viable alternative, arguing that the risk to society could be managed by conditions prohibiting contact with the child. The district court, however, found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and other defenseless persons. The psychological evaluation described Galbraith as having a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize."

Given the severity of the injury and the fact that the second assault occurred while Galbraith was under court-ordered supervision, the district court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that probation was insufficient to protect society. The court is not required to accept the defense's proposed sentencing alternatives, particularly where the record supports a finding of ongoing risk. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984).

**V. Constitutional Challenge**

Therefore, our review is limited to the propriety of the district court's denial of the Rule 35 motion.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. It balanced the mitigating factors against the severity of the offense and the risk to society, finding the latter to be paramount. The court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion. **FINAL RULING**

The appeal of the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed. The district court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to reduce the sentence.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence pursuant to Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App.1985). On appellate review, we will not substitute our judgment for that of the trial court unless we find an abuse of that discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). An abuse of discretion occurs only when the trial court's action is arbitrary, capricious, or unreasonable.

**II. Mitigating Factors and Weight of the Evidence**

The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to mitigating factors such as Galbraith’s youth, lack of prior criminal record, military service, and status as a victim of childhood abuse. While the defense correctly asserts that discretion is not unlimited, the record demonstrates that the district court did consider these factors but determined they were outweighed by the severity of the offense and the need to protect society.

The sentencing objectives include protecting society, deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The district court found that the nature of the offense—striking a seven-month-old infant with sufficient force to cause a complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma—was egregious. Furthermore, the offense occurred while Galbraith was already enrolled in a counseling program following a prior incident of abuse against the same child. This recidivism, occurring despite intervention efforts, supports the court's determination that Galbraith presented a continuing risk.

The defense contends the court improperly extrapolated a risk to "other defenseless persons" from a psychological evaluation that primarily identified a risk to the child. However, given the violence inflicted upon a helpless infant, the court’s inference that Galbraith might pose a similar risk to other defenseless individuals was not unreasonable. The psychological evaluation described Galbraith as having a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize." These characteristics support the district court's conclusion that the risk extended beyond the specific victim. The district court was within its discretion to assign greater weight to the protection of society—specifically defenseless persons—than to Galbraith’s mitigating factors. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

**III. Rehabilitation and Risk to Society**

The defense argues that the district court misapplied the rehabilitation analysis by focusing on the "marginal benefit" of immediate therapy rather than the necessity of the sentence. The district court explicitly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

Crucially, the defense psychologist admitted that delaying treatment until Galbraith earned parole—approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—would not result in a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." This admission significantly undermines the defense's claim that immediate release was necessary for effective rehabilitation. The district court acted within its discretion in determining that the risk to society posed by early release outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate therapy. The sentence, an indeterminate five-year term with parole eligibility after approximately twenty months, allows for therapy upon release without unduly compromising public safety.

**IV. Probation and Alternative Sentencing**

The defense suggests that the district court failed to properly address probation as a viable alternative, arguing that the risk to society could be managed by conditions prohibiting contact with the child. The district court, however, found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and other defenseless persons. The psychological evaluation described Galbraith as having a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize."

Given the severity of the injury and the fact that the second assault occurred while Galbraith was under court-ordered supervision (counseling), the district court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that probation was insufficient to protect society. The court is not required to accept the defense's proposed sentencing alternatives, particularly where the record supports a finding of ongoing risk. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985).

**V. Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith’s constitutional challenge to the original sentence is untimely, as he did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence but only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Therefore, our review is limited to the propriety of the district court's denial of the Rule 35 motion.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. It balanced the mitigating factors against the severity of the offense and the risk to society, finding the latter to be paramount. The court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion. Accordingly, the order denying relief is affirmed.
