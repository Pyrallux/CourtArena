Your Honor, the joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was eminently proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). This statute permits joinder when charges arise from the "same conduct" or "a series of acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (1991). Here, the charges were inextricably linked by a single, coherent pattern of intimidation and control exercised by the defendant over his victims—specifically, his systematic use of threats to silence and manipulate M.S. and V.G. to prevent reporting of his abuse. This pattern spanned both the domestic violence incident and the years-long sexual assaults, making joinder not only legally sound but necessary for judicial efficiency and truth-seeking.

The evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that the intimidation charges were part of a unified scheme. V.G. was a victim in both the sexual assault charges and one intimidation count. During the domestic violence proceedings, the defendant repeatedly threatened V.G. and M.S. via jail phone calls to alter their statements and influence the sentencing. For instance, he told M.S., "You better straighten your stinking, your fucking stinky ass. I'm so fucking sick of it. If I get out, if I get out, you are going to be sorry, my darling." He similarly pressured V.G. directly, instructing her to "change her statement and tell the sentencing court she missed him and wanted him home." These threats were not isolated incidents but integral to the defendant’s broader strategy of maintaining dominance over his household. As this court held in State v. Sorenson, 157 Wis. 2d 212, 458 N.W.2d 861 (Ct. App. 1990), "evidence of prior threats and intimidation is admissible in sexual assault cases to show the defendant’s pattern of behavior and the victim’s delay in reporting." Here, the intimidation evidence directly explains why V.G. waited two years to report the sexual assaults—because the defendant had conditioned her silence through threats like "I will take your little brother away or send her away," which echoed in the phone calls where he warned M.S. that "if you hate me so much, why don’t you let me fuck myself up?" This continuity of coercive tactics creates a logical, inseparable connection between the charges.

Moreover, the circuit court correctly determined that "there is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence." This aligns with Wisconsin law, which permits joinder when evidence from one charge would be admissible in a separate trial for the other. State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 382, 533 N.W.2d 485 (Ct. App. 1995). In a standalone sexual assault trial, the defendant’s threats during the domestic violence proceedings would be admissible to explain V.G.’s delayed reporting and to establish his motive for intimidation—namely, to prevent exposure of the sexual assaults. The circuit court’s reasoning that "one of the intimidation counts involves the same victim of the sexual assaults—V.G.—making it logical to connect those two for purposes of trial" is precisely the type of logical connection Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) requires. As Higgenbotham emphasized, "the common scheme or plan test is satisfied when the evidence of one charge would be relevant to the other charge in a separate trial," and here, the intimidation evidence is not merely relevant but essential to understanding the full context of the sexual assaults.

Joinder also served critical practical purposes. Forcing separate trials would have subjected M.S. and V.G. to the trauma of testifying twice about the same threats and coercive behavior, while duplicating judicial resources. The circuit court’s decision prevented this inefficiency and ensured the jury could evaluate the defendant’s pattern of conduct holistically. The jury instructions correctly limited the use of prior convictions to credibility purposes only, and the court ensured the jury understood that the intimidation evidence was part of the broader narrative—not a separate attack on the defendant’s character. This careful management of the trial further confirms that joinder was proper and harmless.

In reversing, the court of appeals misapplied the law by severing charges that were fundamentally interconnected. Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) was designed to promote judicial economy and truth-finding when charges arise from a cohesive scheme. Here, the defendant’s threats during the domestic violence proceedings were not a distinct, unrelated episode but a continuation of the very intimidation that enabled the sexual assaults to persist for years. The circuit court’s joinder order was legally correct, and this court should affirm it.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

The prosecution's argument that joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the charges and ignores the well-established limits on joinder in Wisconsin jurisprudence. The defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals, which correctly determined that the circuit court improperly joined these charges.

The intimidation charges at issue arose directly from Salinas's phone calls to M.S. during the pending domestic violence case—calls made with the explicit purpose of influencing the sentencing in that domestic violence matter. These charges have no factual nexus to the sexual assault allegations. The sexual assault charges, by contrast, involve years of alleged abuse against V.G. that occurred in the family home. While V.G. was nominally a victim in one intimidation count, the intimidation charge itself stemmed from Salinas's efforts to influence the domestic violence sentencing, not from any conduct related to the sexual assaults.

The prosecution's attempt to recast these as a "unified scheme" of intimidation and control fails because it ignores the temporal separation, the distinct victims (M.S. was the primary target of the intimidation, not V.G.), and—most critically—the different purposes behind Salinas's conduct. The phone calls were designed to secure a favorable sentencing in the domestic violence case; they were not designed to conceal or facilitate sexual assault, which had already ceased when Salinas was arrested on October 26, 2009.

As the Court of Appeals correctly held, the joinder was improper and not harmless error. The improper joinder allowed the jury to hear extensive evidence of Salinas's violent behavior toward M.S. and V.G. in the domestic violence context, which painted him as a dangerous individual and deprived him of a fair trial on the sexual assault charges.

## I. THE LEGAL STANDARD FOR JOINDER

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) provides that two or more crimes may be joined if they "are based on the same act or on a series of acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." The statute is designed to promote judicial economy, but joinder is not automatic whenever some tenuous connection exists between charges.

This Court has consistently held that joinder requires a "logical relationship" between the charges. In State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 60, 65, 321 N.W.2d 213 (1982), this Court emphasized that "the test for joinder is whether the charges are so connected in terms of proof that it would be difficult or impossible to separate the evidence on one charge from the evidence on the other." The burden is on the state to establish that joinder is proper.

Critically, this Court has also recognized that improper joinder constitutes reversible error when it prejudices the defendant. In State v. Williams, 70 Wis. 2d 538, 548, 235 N.W.2d 548 (1975), this Court stated that "the prejudice resulting from a joint trial of properly joined charges is outweighed by the economy achieved, but where charges are improperly joined, the prejudice is not offset by any corresponding benefit."

## II. THE INTIMIDATION CHARGES DID NOT ARISE FROM THE SAME CONDUCT AS THE SEXUAL ASSAULT CHARGES

The facts make clear that the intimidation charges arose from a completely separate criminal episode than the sexual assault charges. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls Salinas made from jail between October 2009 and May 2010, during the pendency of the domestic violence case. The purpose of these calls, as the prosecution's own evidence showed, was to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and to appear at sentencing to help Salinas receive a favorable sentence.

The record contains Salinas's recorded statements from these calls. He stated: "I thank you so much for having my son, [M.S.] But you really piss me off. You don't know what I'm able to do... Fourteen years and six years for this and that and you think you're playing with a piece of shit." He also told M.S.: "You better straighten your stinking, your fucking stinky ass... If I get out, if I get out, you are going to be sorry, my darling."

These statements, while threatening, were explicitly directed at influencing the domestic violence case. Salinas told M.S. to change her statement to say he did not try to strangle her and did not threaten to kill her. He asked her to convince V.G. to change her statement to say Salinas did not hit her. The entire context of the phone calls was the pending domestic violence sentencing.

### B. The Sexual Assaults Constitute a Distinct Criminal Episode

The sexual assault charges, by contrast, involve alleged conduct that occurred between 2007 and October 2009—before Salinas was arrested for domestic violence. According to V.G.'s testimony, Salinas began sexually assaulting her when she turned 13 years old, and the assaults occurred "6 to 12 times a month over the course of three years." The last alleged assault occurred on October 26, 2009—the very day Salinas was arrested for domestic violence.

V.G. testified that the sexual assaults stopped when Salinas was arrested: "That is a day that stopped what was happening to [V.G.] That is a day that family got help. That is the day that [V.G.] stopped being assaulted from the defendant." The prosecution's own closing argument acknowledged that "the last sexual assault occurred on the day [Salinas] went to jail for this strangulation."

There is no factual basis for connecting the intimidation charges—which arose from post-arrest phone calls—with the sexual assault charges—which alleged conduct that ceased when Salinas was arrested. The prosecution's argument that these constitute a "single scheme or plan" ignores the fundamental temporal and factual distinctions between these episodes.

### C. The Prosecution's Characterization of a "Unified Scheme" Is Incorrect

The prosecution argues that Salinas engaged in a "systematic use of threats to silence and manipulate M.S. and V.G. to prevent reporting of his abuse." This characterization is factually incorrect. The record shows that:

1. The intimidation was specifically directed at influencing the domestic violence case, not concealing sexual assault. Salinas wanted M.S. and V.G. to provide favorable statements for sentencing—not to remain silent about sexual assault, which he apparently believed had not been reported.

2. V.G. had not reported the sexual assaults at the time of the intimidation. The sexual assaults were not disclosed until May 13, 2010—two days after Salinas was sentenced in the domestic violence case. Salinas could not have been intimidating V.G. to conceal sexual assaults that he did not know she would eventually report.

3. The prosecution conceded below that the intimidation charges arose from Salinas "trying to get them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case. Salinas's own lawyer stated the intimidation was "completely separate from any sort of sexual assault allegation."

The prosecution's attempt to create a unified narrative fails because the record does not support it. The intimidation and the sexual assaults are separate criminal episodes that happened at different times, involved different conduct, and had different purposes.

## III. THE JOINDER WAS PREJUDICIAL

### A. The Jury Heard Inflammatory Evidence That Would Not Have Been Admissible in a Separate Sexual Assault Trial

Even if some marginal relevance existed between the charges, the joinder was highly prejudicial. The jury heard extensive evidence about the October 26, 2009 domestic violence incident, including:

- Salinas slapping V.G.'s face
- Salinas attempting to choke M.S.
- Salinas holding a knife while holding his four-year-old son
- Salinas threatening to kill his son and himself if V.G. called police

This evidence was inflammatory and would not have been admissible in a standalone sexual assault trial. While the prosecution argues that the intimidation evidence would have been admissible as "other acts" evidence in a separate trial, this argument conflates two distinct concepts. The question is not whether any evidence could theoretically be admissible in separate trials, but whether the specific evidence presented through joinder would have been admissible.

The domestic violence evidence—the strangulation, the knife, the threats against the child—was not relevant to the sexual assault charges. It was relevant only to show Salinas's bad character, which is precisely what Wisconsin's evidence rules prohibit. The circuit court's limiting instruction that prior convictions could be used only for credibility purposes did nothing to neutralize the prejudice from the domestic violence evidence, which was admitted not as prior convictions but as evidence of the charged intimidation offenses.

### B. The Prosecution's Opening Statement Compound the Prejudice

The prejudice was compounded by the prosecution's opening statement, which explicitly told the jury the evidence would show "a pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, repeated sexual assault of a child." The prosecutor described the October 26, 2009 incident in graphic detail, telling the jury:

"That is a day of horror but that is also a day that stopped what was happening to [V.G.] That is a day that family got help. That is the day that [V.G.] stopped being assaulted from the defendant."

This opening statement essentially told the jury that Salinas was a dangerous, violent person who needed to be stopped—a narrative that pervaded the entire trial and made it impossible for the jury to fairly evaluate the sexual assault charges.

### C. The Defense Was Unable to Effectively Contest the Sexual Assault Charges

Salinas's lawyer recognized the prejudice and attempted to address it in opening statements: "Just because he might be a bad guy is really irrelevant." He told the jury: "I hope that you can parse out the difference between Mr. Salinas having a jaded past, perhaps having done a bad thing to these two people... and whether the charges that he's here for today actually happened or not."

This request was unrealistic given the trial structure. The jury was asked to consider all five charges together, with the domestic violence context providing a constant backdrop. The defense's central argument—that V.G. had "very little recall of the sexual assaults" while having "very vivid" memories of the domestic violence incident—was undermined by the joinder, which allowed the jury to attribute any inconsistencies in V.G.'s testimony to fear caused by the domestic violence rather than to fabricate or misremember the sexual assaults.

## IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY APPLIED THE LAW

The Court of Appeals correctly reversed based on improper joinder. The charges did not arise from the same conduct or a series of acts connected together. The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's post-arrest phone calls designed to influence the domestic violence sentencing. The sexual assault charges arose from alleged conduct that occurred years earlier and ceased when Salinas was arrested.

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (1991), is misplaced. In Higgenbotham, this Court upheld joinder of charges where the defendant committed multiple acts of sexual assault against the same victim over an extended period—charges that were clearly part of a "common scheme or plan." Here, by contrast, the intimidation and sexual assault charges involve different conduct, different time periods, and different purposes.

Similarly, the prosecution's citation of State v. Sorenson, 157 Wis. 2d 212, 458 N.W.2d 861 (Ct. App. 1990), is inapposite. Sorenson addressed the admissibility of threat evidence as other-acts evidence in a sexual assault trial—not joinder of separate charges. The question of whether evidence might be admissible in a separate trial is distinct from whether charges should be joined, particularly where, as here, the prejudice from joinder substantially outweighs any judicial economy.

The circuit court's reasoning that "there is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence" was incorrect. The domestic violence evidence would not have been admissible in a standalone sexual assault trial to prove the sexual assault charges. It was relevant only to show Salinas's character or propensity for violence, which Wisconsin law prohibits.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals, which correctly held that the joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was improper and not harmless error. The charges arose from separate criminal episodes, the joinder allowed the jury to hear highly prejudicial evidence that would not have been admissible in a standalone sexual assault trial, and Salinas was denied his right to a fair trial on the sexual assault charges.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The issue before this Court is whether the circuit court improperly joined the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). After reviewing the arguments presented by the prosecution and the defense, as well as the factual record provided, this Court finds that the circuit court erred in joining the charges. The defense has demonstrated that the charges arise from separate criminal episodes with distinct purposes, and that the joinder resulted in significant prejudice that was not harmless.

**I. STANDARD FOR JOINDER**

Wisconsin Stat. § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses if they "are of the same class of offenses or crimes or are based on the same act or on a series of acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." The statute is designed to promote judicial economy but is not without limit. As stated in State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 60, 321 N.W.2d 213 (1982), the test for joinder is whether the charges are so connected in terms of proof that it would be difficult or impossible to separate the evidence on one charge from the evidence on the other. The burden rests on the State to establish that joinder is proper.

**II. ABSENCE OF A COMMON SCHEME OR PLAN**

The prosecution argues that the charges are part of a "unified scheme" of intimidation and control, citing State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (1991). However, a review of the factual timeline reveals that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges constitute distinct criminal episodes rather than a single scheme.

The sexual assault charges involve conduct that occurred between 2007 and October 26, 2009. The record indicates that the last sexual assault occurred on October 26, 2009, the same day Salinas was arrested for domestic violence. In contrast, the intimidation charges stem from phone calls made from jail between October 2009 and May 2010. The purpose of these calls, as evidenced by the transcripts, was to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the *domestic violence* incident and to secure a favorable sentencing in that specific case. Salinas explicitly told M.S. to change her statement to say he "did not try to strangle her" and "did not threaten to kill her."

Crucially, the sexual assaults were not reported to authorities until May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. Therefore, during the period of the alleged intimidation (October 2009 to May 2010), the sexual assaults were unknown to the State and had effectively ceased. The intimidation was not directed at concealing the sexual assaults, but rather at mitigating the consequences of the domestic violence conviction. Because the intimidation was aimed at a different objective (sentencing for domestic violence) than the sexual assaults (gratification and concealment of abuse), the charges do not constitute parts of a "single scheme or plan" as required by statute. The reliance on State v. Higgenbotham is misplaced, as that case involved repeated sexual assaults against the same victim as part of a continuous course of conduct, whereas here the intimidation was a discrete, post-arrest response to a separate prosecution.

**III. PREJUDICE RESULTING FROM JOINDER**

Even if a tenuous connection existed, the joinder was highly prejudicial to Salinas. Wisconsin law recognizes that while properly joined charges balance prejudice with judicial economy, improperly joined charges result in prejudice that is not offset by any benefit. State v. Williams, 70 Wis. 2d 538, 235 N.W.2d 548 (1975).

By joining the charges, the jury was exposed to extensive, inflammatory evidence regarding the domestic violence incident—specifically, that Salinas strangled M.S., slapped V.G., and held a knife while threatening to kill his four-year-old son and himself. This evidence paints Salinas as a violent and dangerous individual. While the prosecution argues this evidence was admissible under State v. Sorenson, 157 Wis. 2d 212, 458 N.W.2d 861 (Ct. App. 1990), to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting, the specific details of the domestic violence (the strangulation and the knife) are not probative of delay; only the *threats* made during the intimidation calls are relevant to that issue.

The admission of the violent domestic violence details served primarily to depict Salinas as having a violent character, thereby increasing the likelihood of a conviction on the uncorroborated sexual assault charges. The defense's attempt to distinguish the "jaded past" of domestic violence from the specific sexual assault allegations was undermined by the prosecution's opening statement, which explicitly linked the two as a "pattern of violence." The circuit court's limiting instruction, that prior convictions could only be used for credibility, failed to address the prejudice arising from the *evidence* of the charged domestic violence acts, which were presented as part of the intimidation narrative.

**IV. ADMISSIBILITY OF OTHER ACTS EVIDENCE**

The circuit court relied on the premise that the evidence would be admissible in separate trials under the "other acts" exception, citing State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 382, 533 N.W.2d 485 (Ct. App. 1995). However, this reasoning is flawed. While the *threats* made in the jail calls might have been admissible in a sexual assault trial to show motive or intent to silence a witness, the *details* of the underlying domestic violence offense (the strangulation and the knife incident) would likely have been excluded in a standalone sexual assault trial as irrelevant and unduly prejudicial under Wis. Stat. § 904.04. Joinder allowed the State to circumvent these evidentiary barriers by trying the domestic violence facts as part of the intimidation counts.

**CONCLUSION**

The intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were not based on the same act or a common scheme or plan. The intimidation was a post-arrest effort to influence the sentencing in a separate domestic violence case, distinct from the prior sexual assaults. The joinder of these charges improperly exposed the jury to highly prejudicial evidence of domestic violence that would have been inadmissible in a separate sexual assault trial. Therefore, the circuit court abused its discretion in joining the charges, and the error was not harmless.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court's judgment is affirmed.

The prosecution's reliance on V.G.'s testimony that Salinas threatened to "take her little brother away or send her away" fails to establish a connection to the intimidation charges. These threats were made during the sexual assaults themselves to maintain compliance, not during the post-arrest phone calls. The prosecution cannot bootstrap the sexual assault threats onto the distinct intimidation campaign.

## III. STATE V. HIGGENBOTHAM IS INAPPLICABLE

The prosecution cites State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Wis. 1991), for the proposition that charges are properly joined when they constitute "parts of a single scheme or plan." Higgenbotham involved repeated sexual assaults against the same victim over an extended period—the very conduct giving rise to the charges. There was no separate criminal episode; the sexual assaults themselves constituted the scheme.

Here, by contrast, the sexual assaults (2007-October 2009) and the intimidation (October 2009-May 2010) are distinct criminal episodes separated by Salinas's arrest. The intimidation was not part of the sexual assault scheme; it was a post-arrest response to a separate prosecution. The prosecution's attempt to extend Higgenbotham to join post-arrest witness intimidation with pre-arrest sexual assaults finds no support in Wisconsin law and would eviscerate the joinder statute's requirements.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S "OTHER ACTS" ARGUMENT FAILS

The prosecution argues that the domestic violence evidence would have been admissible in a separate sexual assault trial under State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 382, 533 N.W.2d 485 (Wis. Ct. App. 1995), to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, Johnson addresses admissibility of evidence at trial—not joinder of charges. The joinder analysis asks whether charges arise from the same act or series of acts connected together, not whether evidence might be admissible under exceptions. The circuit court conflated these distinct inquiries, as this Court recognized.

Second, even assuming some evidence of threats might be admissible to explain delay, the specific details of the domestic violence—the strangulation of M.S., the knife, the threat to kill the four-year-old son—are not probative of why V.G. delayed reporting. Only the threats communicated during the intimidation calls are relevant to that issue. The prosecution used the joinder to introduce inflammatory evidence far beyond what would have been admissible in a standalone trial. This Court correctly identified that the details of the domestic violence "are not probative of delay; only the threats made during the intimidation calls are relevant to that issue."

The prosecution's argument that "there is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence" is speculative and was properly rejected by this Court. The circuit court's assumption that evidence would be admissible does not transform improper joinder into proper joinder.

## V. THE LIMITING INSTRUCTIONS DID NOT CURE THE PREJUDICE

The prosecution argues that the circuit court's limiting instruction—that prior convictions could only be used for credibility—mitigated any prejudice. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the limiting instruction addressed the use of prior convictions, not the charged conduct. The domestic violence incident was not a prior conviction; it was the subject of the intimidation charges. The jury was instructed that the prior domestic violence conviction could only be used for credibility, but the jury was also presented with extensive evidence of the underlying domestic violence acts as part of the intimidation narrative. The prosecution's opening statement explicitly described the domestic violence in graphic detail—the strangulation, the knife, the threat to kill the child—and argued this showed a "pattern of violence."

Second, the limiting instruction could not cure the fundamental unfairness of trying the domestic violence case alongside the sexual assault case. As this Court recognized, "the admission of the violent domestic violence details served primarily to depict Salinas as having a violent character, thereby increasing the likelihood of a conviction on the uncorroborated sexual assault charges." No limiting instruction can prevent jurors from inferring that a person who strangles a mother in front of her children and threatens to kill his own son is capable of sexual assault.

The prosecution cites State v. Williams, 70 Wis. 2d 538, 235 N.W.2d 548 (Wis. 1975), for the proposition that prejudice is outweighed by judicial economy when charges are properly joined. But Williams holds that prejudice must be balanced against judicial economy only when joinder is proper. When joinder is improper, there is no judicial economy to weigh—the charges should not have been joined in the first place.

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution has not rebutted this Court's finding that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges arise from distinct criminal episodes. The intimidation was directed at influencing a separate domestic violence prosecution, not at concealing sexual assaults that were unknown to authorities. The joinder allowed the prosecution to present inflammatory evidence that would have been inadmissible in a standalone sexual assault trial. The limiting instructions did not cure this fundamental error.

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons stated in this Court's preliminary ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court's judgment.

**I. JOINDER STANDARD AND BURDEN OF PROOF**

Wisconsin Stat. § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they are "of the same class of offenses or crimes or are based on the same act or on a series of acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." The burden rests on the State to establish that joinder is proper. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 60, 321 N.W.2d 213 (1982). As noted in Lehman, the test is whether the charges are so connected in terms of proof that it would be difficult or impossible to separate the evidence on one charge from the evidence on the other.

**II. ABSENCE OF A SINGLE SCHEME OR PLAN**

The prosecution argues that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges constitute a "single scheme or plan" to intimidate and silence the victims regarding all forms of abuse. The defense correctly counters that this argument collapses under the weight of the factual timeline.

The record establishes that the sexual assaults occurred between 2007 and October 26, 2009, the date of Salinas's arrest for domestic violence. The intimidation was a discrete, post-arrest effort to influence the sentencing in the domestic violence case, not a continuation of the sexual assault scheme.

The prosecution relies on State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (1991), to support the "single scheme" theory. However, Higgenbotham involved repeated sexual assaults against the same victim as part of a continuous course of conduct. Here, the charges involve distinct criminal episodes: pre-arrest sexual assaults and post-arrest intimidation regarding a separate prosecution. The intimidation was not an integral component of the sexual assault plan but a separate reaction to the arrest for domestic violence. Therefore, the charges do not constitute parts of a "single scheme or plan" under § 971.12(1).

**III. PREJUDICE AND ADMISSIBILITY OF OTHER ACTS EVIDENCE**

The circuit court joined the charges, in part, based on the belief that the evidence would be admissible in separate trials as "other acts" evidence. The defense correctly argues that this reasoning conflates the standard for joinder with the standard for admissibility under Wis. Stat. § 904.04.

While evidence of threats might be admissible in a sexual assault trial to explain a victim's delay in reporting, as recognized in State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 382, 533 N.W.2d 485 (Ct. App. 1995), the graphic details of the domestic violence incident—the strangulation of M.S. and the knife incident involving the four-year-old child—are not probative of delay. These details are highly inflammatory and serve primarily to depict Salinas as having a violent character. In a standalone sexual assault trial, the specific details of the domestic violence would likely have been excluded as unduly prejudicial under Wis. Stat. § 904.04. Joinder allowed the State to circumvent these evidentiary barriers by trying the domestic violence facts as substantive elements of the intimidation counts.

The prosecution contends that any prejudice was mitigated by the circuit court's limiting instruction, which told the jury that prior convictions could be used only for credibility. This argument is unpersuasive. The prejudice arose not from the fact of the prior conviction, but from the jury hearing detailed evidence of the violent domestic violence acts—strangulation and threats with a knife—as part of the current intimidation charges. A limiting instruction regarding credibility does not cure the risk that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults based on the violent character demonstrated by the domestic violence evidence, rather than on the evidence specific to the sexual assaults.

State v. Williams, 70 Wis. 2d 538, 235 N.W.2d 548 (1975), requires a balancing of prejudice against judicial economy only when joinder is proper. Because the joinder here was improper, the judicial economy argument is moot. The prejudice to Salinas was significant and not harmless.

The State has failed to meet its burden under State v. Lehman to show that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of the same act or a single scheme or plan. The intimidation was directed at a separate prosecution for domestic violence, not at concealing the sexual assaults. The prosecution's argument that Salinas's general threats demonstrate a "unified scheme" ignores the specific language of the phone calls. Salinas told M.S.: "You don't know what I'm able to do. You don't know what I have done or what I could do." Read in context, these vague threats about "what I have done" plainly refer to the domestic violence incident for which Salinas was awaiting sentencing—not to sexual assaults that no one, including Salinas, knew would be reported. The prosecution engages in improper speculation by interpreting these statements as references to hidden sexual misconduct.

## II. THE INTIMIDATION WAS A DISCRETE RESPONSE TO THE DOMESTIC VIOLENCE PROSECUTION

The prosecution's theory collapses under the weight of the factual timeline. The sexual assaults occurred between 2007 and October 26, 2009. On October 26, 2009, Salinas was arrested for domestic violence. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls Salinas made from jail between his arrest in October 2009 and his sentencing in May 2010. The sexual assaults were first reported on May 13, 2010—two days after sentencing.

This timeline demonstrates that the intimidation was not a continuation of the sexual assault scheme but a discrete, post-arrest effort to influence the outcome of the domestic violence case. Salinas had a clear, documented motive to influence the sentencing: he faced fourteen years and six years on the domestic violence charges. His efforts to get M.S. to change her statement and to get V.G. to speak favorably at sentencing were aimed at securing a more lenient sentence—not at concealing sexual assaults that had not been reported and that Salinas had no reason to believe would be reported.

The prosecution argues that Salinas's threats during the phone calls were "identical" to threats used during the sexual assaults. This argument mischaracterizes the record. The threats during the sexual assaults—threats to "take [V.G.'s] little brother away or send her away"—were directed at preventing V.G. from disclosing the sexual assaults themselves. The threats during the jail calls were directed at getting M.S. and V.G. to influence the sentencing outcome in the domestic violence case. While both involve threats, the targets, purposes, and temporal contexts are entirely different.

The prosecution's reliance on the fact that V.G. delayed reporting the sexual assaults is misplaced. While evidence of threats may be admissible to explain delay in reporting under State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 382, 533 N.W.2d 485 (Ct. App. 1995), this does not mean that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges arise from the same "scheme or plan." The existence of a plausible evidentiary explanation for admissibility is distinct from the question of whether charges should be joined for trial.

## III. HIGGENBOTHAM DOES NOT SUPPORT JOINDER HERE

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Wis. 1991), is fundamentally misplaced. In Higgenbotham, the defendant was charged with multiple sexual assaults against the same victim occurring over a period of time. The Court held that "a series of acts committed by a defendant against the same victim over a period of time may constitute a single scheme or plan for joinder purposes." Id. at 988.

Higgenbotham involved repeated sexual assaults against the same victim as part of a continuous course of conduct. The charges at issue here involve entirely different criminal episodes: pre-arrest sexual assaults and post-arrest intimidation regarding a separate prosecution. The intimidation was not an integral component of the sexual assault plan but a separate reaction to the arrest for domestic violence.

The prosecution argues that "the domestic violence incident on October 26, 2009, was not a standalone event but the culmination of Salinas's abusive control, which included the sexual assaults." This argument finds no support in the record. The domestic violence incident involved Salinas assaulting M.S. and V.G. in the household. The sexual assaults involved Salinas sexually assaulting V.G. when M.S. was not home. These are distinct criminal acts targeting different victims (M.S. was not a victim of the sexual assaults) and occurring in different circumstances. The prosecution's attempt to characterize them as part of a unified "scheme" rewrites the factual record.

The prosecution's argument that "threats to silence victims do not cease upon arrest; they intensify to protect the perpetrator's interests" is precisely the kind of generalized characterization that the joinder statute was designed to prevent. The statute requires more than a vague theoretical connection; it requires that the charges be "based on the same act or on a series of acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The prosecution has not demonstrated that the sexual assaults and the post-arrest intimidation calls constitute parts of a single scheme.

## IV. THE PREJUDICE FROM IMPROPER JOINDER WAS NOT HARMLESS

Even if there were any doubt about the impropriety of joinder, the error was not harmless. The circuit court joined the charges, in part, based on the belief that the evidence would be admissible in separate trials as "other acts" evidence. This reasoning conflates the standard for joinder with the standard for admissibility under Wis. Stat. § 904.04.

The graphic details of the domestic violence incident—the strangulation of M.S. and the knife incident involving the four-year-old child—are not probative of the sexual assault charges. They do not establish motive, intent, or absence of mistake regarding the sexual assaults. Instead, they serve primarily to depict Salinas as having a violent character—exactly the kind of propensity evidence that Wis. Stat. § 904.04 is designed to exclude.

In a standalone sexual assault trial, the specific details of the domestic violence would likely have been excluded as unduly prejudicial under Wis. Stat. § 904.04. The prosecution's argument that the evidence would have been admissible under § 904.04(2) to show motive, intent, or lack of mistake is unpersuasive. The domestic violence incident had no logical connection to the sexual assaults—it was a separate criminal act involving different victims and different purposes. The prosecution's theory would allow any prior bad act involving the defendant to be admitted simply because the defendant also committed the charged offense—a result § 904.04 was expressly designed to prevent.

The prosecution contends that any prejudice was mitigated by the circuit court's limiting instruction regarding prior convictions. This argument is unpersuasive for two reasons. First, the prejudice arose not from the fact of prior convictions, but from the jury hearing detailed evidence of violent domestic violence acts—strangulation and threats with a knife—as part of the current charges. Second, a limiting instruction regarding credibility does not cure the risk that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults based on the violent character demonstrated by the domestic violence evidence, rather than on the evidence specific to the sexual assaults.

The prosecution cites State v. Williams, 70 Wis. 2d 538, 235 N.W.2d 548 (Wis. 1975), for the proposition that judicial economy outweighs prejudice when joinder is proper. But Williams expressly conditions this balancing on the joinder being proper in the first instance. As Williams held, "the interest of justice may require that separately charged offenses be tried separately even though they could have been joined." Id. at 545. Because the joinder here was improper, the judicial economy argument is inapplicable.

## V. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument that "the evidence demonstrated beyond doubt that these charges were part of a unified course of conduct" is directly contradicted by the factual findings in the record. The Court of Appeals correctly identified that the intimidation was "a discrete, post-arrest effort to influence the sentencing in the domestic violence case, not a continuation of the sexual assault scheme."

The prosecution's argument that "Salinas's threats during the calls were identical to those used during the sexual assaults" misrepresents the record. The threats during the calls were directed at M.S. and V.G. to influence the sentencing. The threats during the sexual assaults were directed at V.G. to prevent disclosure of the sexual assaults. The targets, purposes, and contexts are entirely different.

The prosecution's argument that "the domestic violence evidence was relevant to the intimidation counts and provided critical context for why V.G. delayed reporting" improperly conflates two distinct concepts. While evidence of threats might be admissible in a sexual assault trial to explain a victim's delay in reporting, this does not mean that the detailed facts of the domestic violence incident—the strangulation, the knife, the threats to kill the child—were admissible. V.G. delayed reporting because she was afraid of Salinas; the specific details of how Salinas strangled her mother and held a knife to his own child are not necessary to explain that fear.

The prosecution's argument that "the defense's claim that the domestic violence details would have been excluded in a separate trial is speculative" ignores the well-established principles of evidence law. The detailed domestic violence evidence had no probative value regarding the sexual assault charges—it did not establish any element of those charges. Its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The defense is not speculating; it is applying the plain language of § 904.04.

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution has failed to meet its burden under State v. Lehman to establish that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of the same act or a single scheme or plan. The intimidation was directed at a separate prosecution for domestic violence, not at concealing the sexual assaults. The joinder of these charges improperly exposed the jury to highly prejudicial evidence of domestic violence that would have been inadmissible in a separate sexual assault trial. The circuit court abused its discretion in joining the charges, and the error was not harmless.

For all the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court's judgment.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, this Court affirms its preliminary determination that the circuit court improperly joined the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges. The defense has persuasively demonstrated that the State failed to meet its burden under the governing joinder statute and that the error was not harmless.

**I. STANDARD FOR JOINDER**

Wisconsin Stat. § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they are "of the same class of offenses or crimes or are based on the same act or on a series of acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." The burden rests on the State to establish that joinder is proper. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 60, 321 N.W.2d 213 (1982). As stated in Lehman, the test is whether the charges are "so connected in terms of proof that it would be difficult or impossible to separate the evidence on one charge from the evidence on the other." Id. at 65.

**II. FAILURE TO ESTABLISH A SINGLE SCHEME OR PLAN**

The prosecution argues that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges constitute a "single scheme or plan" based on a theory of ongoing control and intimidation. While the evidence may show a pattern of abusive behavior, the legal standard for joinder requires a more specific connection between the charged offenses than a general "holistic" view of the defendant's conduct.

The record establishes a clear temporal and motivational distinction between the two sets of charges. The sexual assaults occurred between 2007 and October 26, 2009. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made between October 2009 and May 2010, while Salinas was incarcerated awaiting sentencing for the domestic violence incident. The sexual assaults were not reported to authorities until May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges.

The prosecution contends that the jail calls were part of a unified scheme to silence V.G. regarding the sexual assaults. However, the content of the calls contradicts this assertion. The transcripts show Salinas pressuring M.S. to change her statement specifically regarding the domestic violence incident—namely, to deny that he strangled her or threatened to kill her. He also pressured V.G. to tell the sentencing court that she missed him and wanted him home. At the time these calls were made, the State was unaware of the sexual assaults. Therefore, the intimidation cannot logically be viewed as a scheme to conceal the sexual assaults; rather, it was a discrete effort to influence the sentencing in the domestic violence case.

The prosecution relies on State v. Higgenbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (1991), to argue that a series of acts against the same victim over time constitutes a single scheme. However, Higgenbotham involved repeated acts of the same nature (sexual assaults) against the same victim. Here, the charges involve distinct criminal episodes: pre-arrest sexual assaults and post-arrest intimidation regarding a separate prosecution. The intimidation was not an integral component of the sexual assault plan but a separate reaction to the arrest for domestic violence. The defense correctly notes that the "single scheme" analysis requires more than similar tactics; it requires a connection between the offenses themselves. The State has not met this burden under Lehman.

**III. PREJUDICE AND ADMISSIBILITY OF EVIDENCE**

The circuit court joined the charges partly on the belief that the evidence would be admissible in separate trials as "other acts" evidence. The defense correctly argues that this reasoning conflates the standard for joinder with the standard for admissibility under Wis. Stat. § 904.04.

While evidence of threats might be admissible in a sexual assault trial to explain a victim's delay in reporting, as recognized in State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 382, 533 N.W.2d 485 (Ct. App. 1995), the graphic details of the domestic violence incident—the strangulation of M.S. and the knife incident involving the four-year-old child—are not probative of delay. These details are highly inflammatory and serve primarily to depict Salinas as having a violent character. In a standalone sexual assault trial, the specific details of the domestic violence would likely have been excluded as unduly prejudicial under Wis. Stat. § 904.04. Joinder allowed the State to circumvent these evidentiary barriers by trying the domestic violence facts as substantive elements of the intimidation counts.

The prosecution contends that any prejudice was mitigated by the circuit court's limiting instruction. This argument is unpersuasive. The prejudice arose not from the fact of the prior conviction, but from the jury hearing detailed evidence of violent domestic violence acts as part of the current charges. A limiting instruction does not cure the risk that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults based on the violent character demonstrated by the domestic violence evidence, rather than on the evidence specific to the sexual assaults.

State v. Williams, 70 Wis. 2d 538, 235 N.W.2d 548 (1975), requires a balancing of prejudice against judicial economy only when joinder is proper. Because the joinder here was improper, the judicial economy argument is moot. The prejudice to Salinas was significant and not harmless.

**CONCLUSION**

The State has failed to meet its burden under State v. Lehman to show that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of the same act or a single scheme or plan. The intimidation was directed at a separate prosecution for domestic violence, not at concealing the sexual assaults. The joinder of these charges improperly exposed the jury to highly prejudicial evidence of domestic violence that would have been inadmissible in a separate sexual assault trial. The circuit court abused its discretion in joining the charges, and the error was not harmless.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court's judgment is affirmed.
