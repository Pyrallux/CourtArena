Your Honors, the prosecution respectfully submits that this appeal must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The order denying Mr. Majhor’s motion for a change of venue is neither a "final decision" under the applicable statute nor an immediately appealable collateral order. ### I. THE ORDER IS NOT A "FINAL DECISION" UNDER STATUTORY REQUIREMENTS  
A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) permits appeals only from "final decisions" of the trial court. A "final decision" is one that terminates the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the trial court to do but execute the judgment. Here, the denial of the change-of-venue motion is purely interlocutory. The trial has not commenced, let alone concluded. The charges against Mr. Majhor remain pending, and the trial court retains full authority to manage the proceedings—including resolving evidentiary disputes, ruling on jury selection, and ultimately rendering a verdict. No legal principle or statutory provision transforms a pretrial venue ruling into a final judgment. To hold otherwise would invite endless appeals from every pretrial ruling, fracturing the judicial process and overwhelming appellate dockets with premature challenges.  

### II. THE COLLATERAL ORDER DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY  
The collateral order doctrine, as established in *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, imposes a strict three-prong test for immediate appealability:  
1. **The order must conclusively determine the disputed issue**;  
2. **It must resolve an important question completely separate from the merits of the action**; and  
3. **It must be effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment**.  

This order fails all three prongs.  

#### A. The Venue Issue Is Not Conclusively Determined  
While the trial court has denied the motion, the ruling is not final. Venue objections may be raised again at trial—during jury selection, for instance—if circumstances change or new evidence emerges. The court retains discretion to revisit the issue should prejudice manifest during proceedings. A pretrial ruling on venue is inherently provisional, subject to reconsideration based on evolving facts. It does not "conclusively determine" anything; it merely sets the stage for further proceedings.  

#### B. The Issue Is Not Separate from the Merits  
Venue is inextricably intertwined with the merits of the case. A change-of-venue motion turns on factual inquiries about community sentiment, media exposure, and juror impartiality—questions that cannot be resolved in a vacuum. These issues depend on the context of the trial itself: how evidence is presented, what testimony is adduced, and how the jury responds. The Supreme Court has consistently held that venue disputes are not "separate" from the merits but are part of the trial’s procedural framework. To treat them as collateral would disrupt the trial court’s ability to manage proceedings holistically.  

#### C. The Order Is Reviewable After Final Judgment  
Critically, any alleged error in the venue ruling can be fully reviewed on direct appeal after conviction or acquittal. If Mr. Majhor is convicted, he may challenge the venue denial as part of his appeal—where the full record, including jury selection and trial proceedings, will be before the appellate court. This is the proper time to assess whether the trial was fundamentally unfair. To allow interlocutory review now would force appellate courts to rule on hypothetical prejudice, without the benefit of a complete record. As this Court has recognized, the collateral order doctrine does not apply when the issue can be meaningfully addressed post-trial.  

### III. THE CRIMINAL CONTEXT REINFORCES THE NEED FOR FINALITY  
In criminal cases, courts apply even stricter standards to interlocutory appeals. The final judgment rule exists to prevent disruptive delays, protect the presumption of innocence, and avoid the waste of resources from piecemeal litigation. Permitting appeals from pretrial venue denials would encourage defendants to seek multiple interlocutory reviews for every contested ruling—motions to suppress, discovery disputes, and now venue challenges—before trial even begins. This would clog appellate dockets, prolong criminal proceedings, and undermine the efficient administration of justice. The Supreme Court has emphasized that such rulings are "not the type of order that warrants immediate appellate review" in criminal cases, given their provisional nature and reviewability post-trial.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honors, this Court has no jurisdiction to hear this appeal. The denial of the change-of-venue motion is a routine pretrial ruling that does not terminate the litigation, is not separate from the merits, and can be reviewed after a final judgment. The prosecution urges this Court to dismiss the appeal forthwith to preserve judicial integrity and ensure that criminal trials proceed without unnecessary interlocutory interruptions.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE APPELLATE DIVISION OF AMERICAN SAMOA**

**RICHARD MAJHOR,**
Appellant,

v.

**PROSECUTION,**
Appellee.

**No. [Interlocutory Appeal]**

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM:**

Before this Court is Appellant Richard Majhor’s interlocutory appeal seeking review of the Trial Division’s order denying his renewed motion for a change of venue. Appellant concedes that the order is not a "final decision" under A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) but argues that the order is immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine. For the reasons set forth below, we conclude that we lack jurisdiction to hear this appeal and dismiss the matter.

**I. JURISDICTIONAL STANDARD**

The jurisdiction of this Court is statutorily defined. A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) permits appeals only from "final decisions" of the trial court. A final decision is one that "ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment." See *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). Appellant correctly concedes that the order denying his change of venue motion does not meet this standard, as the criminal proceedings against him remain pending.

However, Appellant invokes the collateral order doctrine, an exception to the final judgment rule recognized in *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949). To fall within this narrow exception, an order must satisfy three prongs: (1) it must "conclusively determine the disputed issue"; (2) it must "resolve an important question completely separate from the merits"; and (3) it must be "effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment." *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 546; see also *Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc.*, 511 U.S. 863, 867 (1994).

**II. ANALYSIS**

While Appellant has presented compelling arguments regarding the first two prongs of the *Cohen* test, he cannot satisfy the third and most stringent requirement: effective unreviewability.

**A. Conclusively Determined and Separate from the Merits**

We accept, for the purposes of this preliminary ruling, that the Trial Division’s order conclusively determined the issue of venue for the present time and that the question of venue is conceptually distinct from the question of Appellant’s guilt. As Appellant argues, citing *Stack v. Boyle*, 342 U.S. 1 (1951), the fairness of the forum is a separate legal inquiry from the defendant's culpability. Furthermore, the Trial Division conducted an evidentiary hearing and issued a definitive ruling, satisfying the requirement of a conclusive determination regarding that specific motion.

**B. Effectively Unreviewable**

The dispositive issue is whether the denial of a change of venue is "effectively unreviewable" on appeal from a final judgment. The collateral order doctrine is reserved for rights that would be "irretrievably lost" if review were postponed until the end of the case. *Abney v. United States*, 431 U.S. 651, 659 (1977) (holding that double jeopardy claims are immediately appealable because the right not to be tried twice is lost if the trial proceeds).

Appellant argues that the unique nature of his claim—alleging cultural and racial bias by Samoan jurors against non-Samoans—renders the error unreviewable post-trial. He relies on *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), and *Estes v. Texas*, 381 U.S. 532 (1965), to suggest that the prejudice alleged is so pervasive that it constitutes a structural error infecting the entire proceeding.

However, the distinction between the right against double jeopardy and the right to a change of venue is critical. Unlike the double jeopardy context, where the harm of a second trial cannot be undone by a later appellate reversal, the harm of an improper venue is remediable. If Appellant is convicted, he may challenge the venue denial on direct appeal. If this Court determines that the Trial Division abused its discretion and that Appellant could not receive a fair trial in American Samoa, the remedy is a vacatur of the conviction and a new trial in a different venue. See *United States v. Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. 263, 269 (1982) (noting that the denial of a transfer motion is generally reviewable after final judgment).

The "structural error" Appellant anticipates does not render the issue unreviewable; rather, it underscores the importance of appellate review to ensure the validity of the conviction. The fact that a trial might be "wasted" or that resources might be expended on a proceeding later found to be unfair is a policy consideration that does not override the jurisdictional finality requirements. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the collateral order doctrine does not apply to pretrial orders that can be adequately corrected after final judgment. See *Will v. United States*, 389 U.S. 90, 95-96 (1967).

Appellant’s reliance on *Rideau* is misplaced. *Rideau* involved a situation where the community's prejudice was deemed so total that the defendant's due process rights were violated by the very act of conducting the trial in that environment. Even assuming Appellant’s allegations of cultural bias are true, the proper mechanism for addressing such a claim is to preserve the objection, proceed to trial, and seek reversal if convicted. The appellate court is fully capable of reviewing the record—including the voir dire, the evidence presented, and the jury composition—to determine if the alleged bias resulted in an unfair trial. The availability of this post-trial remedy means the order is not effectively unreviewable.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Because the order denying the change of venue does not present a question that is effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment, it fails the third prong of the collateral order doctrine established in *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949). The prosecution’s arguments regarding the need for finality and the piecemeal nature of interlocutory appeals are well-taken. Permitting immediate appeal in this context would undermine the efficient administration of justice by allowing defendants to delay trial proceedings based on claims of prejudice that are, as a matter of law, reviewable after conviction.

Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**SO ORDERED.**

**OPINION ON REBUTTAL**

**PER CURIAM:**

This matter comes before the Court on Appellant Richard Majhor’s rebuttal to our preliminary ruling dismissing his interlocutory appeal for want of jurisdiction. Appellant challenges our conclusion that the denial of his motion for a change of venue fails to satisfy the third prong of the collateral order doctrine—specifically, that the order is not "effectively unreviewable" on appeal from a final judgment. After careful consideration of Appellant’s arguments regarding structural error, presumptive bias, and judicial economy, we affirm our preliminary ruling.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

As established in our preliminary ruling, jurisdiction in this matter is governed by A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) and the collateral order doctrine. To invoke the collateral order doctrine as an exception to the final judgment rule, Appellant must demonstrate that the district court's order (1) conclusively determined the disputed question, (2) resolved an important question completely separate from the merits, and (3) is effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). We have already accepted, for the sake of argument, that the first two prongs are met. The sole question for determination is whether the third prong is satisfied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

Appellant argues that the denial of his change of venue motion is effectively unreviewable because the alleged cultural and racial bias constitutes a "structural error" that infects the trial process itself. He relies on *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), to posit that the trial environment is the constitutional violation, rendering post-trial review inadequate. We find this argument unpersuasive in light of binding precedent regarding the reviewability of venue orders.

**A. The Collateral Order Doctrine Does Not Extend to Venue Denials**

The Supreme Court has squarely addressed the applicability of the collateral order doctrine to venue and transfer orders. In *United States v. Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. 263 (1982), the Court held that the denial of a motion for transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) is not immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine. The Court reasoned that such orders do not involve a "right that would be irretrievably lost" if review were postponed. *Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. at 269.

Appellant attempts to distinguish *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* by characterizing his claim as one of "structural error" rather than a routine transfer dispute. He argues that because the alleged bias is "presumptive" and inherent in the Samoan jury pool, the harm of proceeding to trial cannot be remedied by a new trial. However, this characterization conflates the *merits* of the due process claim with the *jurisdictional* availability of interlocutory appeal.

Even assuming, arguendo, that the trial environment in American Samoa is constitutionally infirm due to cultural bias, the remedy for such a violation is the reversal of the conviction on direct appeal. The fact that a trial is "wasted" or that a defendant must endure a constitutionally unfair trial before obtaining relief does not render the order "effectively unreviewable." The Supreme Court has consistently held that the collateral order doctrine is not a "general catchall" for avoiding the costs of litigation, but rather a narrow exception for rights that are "lost" if not vindicated immediately. *Will v. United States*, 389 U.S. 90, 95-96 (1967). Unlike the double jeopardy right at issue in *Abney v. United States*, 431 U.S. 651 (1977), which is lost the moment a second trial commences, the right to a fair trial is preserved if the appellate court can vacate an unfair conviction.

**B. Structural Error Is Reviewable on Direct Appeal**

Appellant’s reliance on *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), is misplaced regarding the timing of appellate review. *Rideau* stands for the proposition that a trial held in an atmosphere of total and pervasive prejudice deprives the defendant of due process. It does not hold that such a claim must be reviewed before trial. Indeed, *Rideau* itself was an appeal from a final conviction.

Appellant argues that requiring him to proceed to trial renders the appellate process an "exercise in futility." However, the legal system is replete with situations where a party must endure a costly proceeding to preserve a claim for appeal. The "structural error" Appellant identifies—if proven—would result in the automatic reversal of his conviction. This is a complete and adequate remedy. The availability of this remedy defeats the claim that the issue is "effectively unreviewable." See *Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc.*, 511 U.S. 863, 878 (1994) (noting that the collateral order doctrine is inapplicable where the right can be vindicated on appeal).

**C. Judicial Economy Does Not Trump Jurisdictional Limits**

Appellant correctly notes that an evidentiary hearing has been held and that a record exists on the venue issue. He argues that reviewing the issue now promotes judicial economy by avoiding a potentially lengthy trial that may later be reversed. While we are sympathetic to the concerns of efficiency and resource conservation, these policy considerations cannot override the statutory jurisdictional limits of this Court.

Congress and the Supreme Court have balanced the interests of finality against the costs of piecemeal litigation, concluding that—with narrow exceptions—interlocutory appeals are disfavored. See 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (applicable federal analogue); A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c). The existence of a developed record does not transform a non-final order into a final one. To accept Appellant’s argument would be to allow defendants to appeal any pretrial order that might result in a reversal, effectively collapsing the final judgment rule in criminal cases.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Appellant has raised serious and novel allegations regarding the intersection of cultural bias and the right to an impartial jury. However, the gravity or novelty of a claim does not confer jurisdiction on this Court to hear it before a final judgment. The denial of a motion for change of venue remains an order that is reviewable on appeal from a final judgment. Because Appellant has not demonstrated that this right is "effectively unreviewable" post-trial, he cannot satisfy the third prong of the collateral order doctrine.

Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**SO ORDERED.**

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the Prosecution's arguments and in support of Appellant Richard Majhor's position that this Court possesses jurisdiction to hear this interlocutory appeal under the collateral order doctrine.

Mr. Majhor stands accused of serious criminal offenses, including first-degree murder, and faces the prospect of trial before a jury in American Samoa. The Trial Court denied his motion twice—first initially, and again after an evidentiary hearing. The Prosecution now asks this Court to dismiss his appeal, arguing that jurisdiction is lacking. For the reasons set forth below, we submit that the Prosecution's position misapplies controlling precedent and ignores the unique circumstances presented in this case.

---

## I. THIS CASE PRESENTS EXCEPTIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES THAT SATISFY THE THIRD PRONG OF THE COLLATERAL ORDER DOCTRINE

The Prosecution correctly notes that the collateral order doctrine requires satisfaction of three prongs: (1) the order must conclusively determine the disputed question; (2) it must resolve an important question completely separate from the merits; and (3) it must be "effectively unreviewable" on appeal from a final judgment. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). The Prosecution further acknowledges, as this Court has previously noted, that the first two prongs are satisfied here.

The dispute centers on the third prong—whether the denial of a change of venue motion is "effectively unreviewable." The Prosecution relies heavily on *United States v. Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. 263 (1982), for the proposition that venue denials are never immediately appealable. However, the defense submits that *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* does not control this case, because the circumstances here are materially distinguishable.

In *Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, the defendant sought transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) based on the convenience of witnesses and the interests of justice. The Supreme Court held that such transfer motions do not involve rights that would be "irretrievably lost" if review were postponed. 458 U.S. at 269. But the Court explicitly distinguished between routine transfer disputes and cases involving "exceptional circumstances." The defense submits that this case presents such exceptional circumstances.

---

## II. THE ALLEGED CULTURAL AND RACIAL BIAS CONSTITUTES A STRUCTURAL ERROR

The Prosecution dismisses Mr. Majhor's "structural error" argument as a misapplication of precedent. This characterization is incorrect. A "structural error" is a constitutional defect that "affects the framework within which the trial proceeds, rather than simply an error in the trial process itself." *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991). When a defendant is deprived of the right to an impartial jury—a right guaranteed by the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments—the trial itself is fundamentally compromised.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a trial conducted in an atmosphere of prejudice violates due process. In *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), the Court held that a trial in a community "so saturated with prejudice" that the defendant could not receive a fair trial violated the Due Process Clause. While *Rideau* was decided on direct appeal, the principle underlying that decision—that the trial environment itself can be constitutionally infirm—supports Mr. Majhor's position.

The Prosecution argues that structural errors are routinely reviewed after trial. This is true, but it does not answer the question before this Court. The question is not whether structural errors can be reviewed after trial, but whether they are "effectively unreviewable" if immediate appeal is denied. The defense submits that when the alleged bias is presumptive and inherent in the jury pool—as distinguished from bias that might arise from media coverage of a particular case—the harm cannot be cured by reversal after trial.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *HOLLYWOOD MOTOR CAR CO.* IS MISPLACED

The Prosecution argues that *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* controls this case and forecloses interlocutory appeal of venue denials. The defense submits this is a misreading of the Supreme Court's holding.

In *Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, the Court emphasized that the collateral order doctrine must be applied "with the utmost strictness" and should not become a "general catchall" for avoiding litigation costs. 458 U.S. at 265, 271. However, the Court did not hold that venue denials are categorically unreviewable on interlocutory appeal. Rather, the Court held that the particular transfer motion at issue there—a routine § 1404(a) transfer based on witness convenience—did not satisfy the third prong.

The circumstances in *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* bear no resemblance to those here. That case involved a civil antitrust suit in which the defendant sought to transfer from one federal district to another for convenience. The defendant did not allege that the trial forum was constitutionally incapable of providing a fair trial. Here, Mr. Majhor alleges that the entire jury pool in American Samoa is presumptively biased due to cultural factors and his status as a non-Samoan. This is not a dispute about witness convenience; it is a dispute about the fundamental fairness of the trial itself.

The Supreme Court has recognized that the collateral order doctrine applies in criminal cases where the right at stake would be "irretrievably lost" if not vindicated immediately. In *Abney v. United States*, 431 U.S. 651 (1977), the Court held that double jeopardy claims are immediately appealable because a second trial would destroy the right forever. Similarly, here, if Mr. Majhor is tried before a presumptively biased jury and convicted, the remedy of reversal and new trial does not fully remedy the harm. He would have been subjected to the trauma of a biased trial, and the appellate process would be, as he argues, an "exercise in futility."

---

## IV. THE DEFENSE HAS DEMONSTRATED EXCEPTIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES

The evidentiary hearing below established the following facts, which the Prosecution does not dispute:

1. Mr. Majhor is non-Samoan and is perceived as an "outsider" in American Samoa.
2. There has been considerable media coverage of his case.
3. Samoan cultural values regarding justice and race differ from those prevalent in the fifty states.
4. The jury pool in American Samoa is predominantly Samoan.

The Prosecution argues that these facts are insufficient to warrant interlocutory appeal. But the defense submits that they establish precisely the "exceptional circumstances" that *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* contemplated. When a defendant makes a colorable showing that the trial forum is constitutionally incapable of providing a fair trial—due not to transient media prejudice but to enduring cultural factors—the interests of justice require immediate appellate review.

The Prosecution argues that requiring Mr. Majhor to proceed to trial does not render the appellate process futile because he can raise his claims on direct appeal if convicted. This argument ignores the reality of what a criminal defendant faces. A defendant who must endure a trial before a biased jury suffers harm that cannot be fully remedied by later reversal. The trial itself is the constitutional violation. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726, "the defendant is entitled to have the trial judge's judgment upon this matter taken before any other trial begins."

The Prosecution argues that judicial economy does not trump jurisdictional limits. The defense agrees—but submits that judicial economy actually supports jurisdiction in this case.

An evidentiary hearing has already been held below. A complete record exists on the venue issue. If this Court dismisses this appeal, and Mr. Majhor is convicted, the venue issue will be litigated again on direct appeal—after a potentially lengthy trial that may later be reversed. This wastes judicial resources and subjects Mr. Majhor to unnecessary suffering.

The Prosecution argues that allowing interlocutory appeals in cases like this would "collapse the final judgment rule." This is a slippery-slope argument without merit. Every defendant who claims media bias would not qualify; only those who can demonstrate presumptive, inherent bias in the jury pool would qualify.

The defense acknowledges that the collateral order doctrine is narrow and must be applied with caution. United States*, 389 U.S. 90, 95 (1967).

Mr. Majhor has demonstrated that this case presents exceptional circumstances: presumptive bias in the jury pool due to cultural factors, his status as a non-Samoan, and the inability of post-trial review to remedy the harm. **UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE APPELLATE DIVISION OF AMERICAN SAMOA**

**RICHARD MAJHOR,**
Appellant,

v.

**PROSECUTION,**
Appellee.

**No. [Interlocutory Appeal]**

**OPINION ON FINAL RULING**

**PER CURIAM:**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Prosecution and the Defense. Appellant Richard Majhor seeks review of the Trial Court’s denial of his motion for a change of venue via interlocutory appeal. Appellant contends that this Court has jurisdiction under the collateral order doctrine because the denial of his motion constitutes a "structural error" involving "exceptional circumstances"—specifically, presumptive cultural and racial bias in the Samoan jury pool—that renders the order "effectively unreviewable" on appeal from a final judgment. For the reasons that follow, we find that Appellant has not satisfied the third prong of the collateral order doctrine. Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Jurisdiction to review an interlocutory order in a criminal case is generally governed by the final judgment rule. See A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c). The collateral order doctrine, established in *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949), provides a narrow exception to this rule. To invoke this exception, the appellant must demonstrate that the district court's order (1) conclusively determined the disputed question, (2) resolved an important question completely separate from the merits of the action, and (3) is effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment. *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 546. It is undisputed that the first two prongs are met for the purposes of this appeal. The sole issue before this Court is whether the denial of the motion for change of venue is "effectively unreviewable."

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Collateral Order Doctrine and Venue Denials**

The Supreme Court has explicitly addressed the applicability of the collateral order doctrine to orders denying venue or transfer. In *United States v. Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. 263 (1982), the Court held that the denial of a motion for transfer is not immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine. The Court reasoned that such orders do not involve a right that would be "irretrievably lost" if review were postponed. *Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. at 269.

Appellant attempts to distinguish *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* by arguing that it involved a routine civil transfer for convenience under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), whereas his case involves "exceptional circumstances" of a constitutional nature—specifically, the alleged inability of a Samoan jury to render an impartial verdict due to cultural bias. Appellant argues that this distinction transforms the venue denial into a "structural error" that requires immediate correction.

We are not persuaded. The Supreme Court in *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* emphasized that the collateral order doctrine must be applied "with the utmost strictness" and is not a "general catchall" for avoiding the costs of litigation. *Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, 458 U.S. at 265, 271. While the factual context of Appellant’s case differs from the civil antitrust dispute in *Hollywood Motor Car Co.*, the jurisdictional inquiry remains the same: whether the right at stake is effectively unreviewable later. The Court in *Hollywood Motor Car Co.* noted that the right to a change of venue is not lost if review is postponed until after final judgment. This principle applies with equal force to criminal defendants alleging constitutional bias. The fact that Appellant frames his claim as one of "structural error" does not alter the timing of review.

**B. Structural Error Is Reviewable on Direct Appeal**

Appellant relies heavily on *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279 (1991), and *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), to argue that the alleged bias infects the "framework" of the trial, rendering the appellate process an "exercise in futility" if delayed. Appellant contends that the "trauma" of a trial before a biased jury constitutes an irretrievable loss.

We acknowledge the gravity of Appellant’s allegations. However, the characterization of an error as "structural" speaks to the *consequence* of the error on appeal, not the *timing* of the appeal. As the Prosecution correctly notes, *Rideau* itself was decided on direct appeal following a conviction. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. 723. The Supreme Court has consistently held that structural errors—such as the total deprivation of counsel or a biased judge—are reviewed and remedied on direct appeal through the reversal of the conviction. See, e.g., *United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez*, 548 U.S. 140 (2006).

Appellant attempts to analogize his situation to *Abney v. United States*, 431 U.S. 651 (1977), where the Court held that double jeopardy claims are immediately appealable because the right not to be tried twice is "irretrievably lost" once trial commences. *Abney*, 431 U.S. at 659. This analogy fails. Unlike double jeopardy, which bars a second trial entirely, the right to an impartial jury is vindicated by vacating an unfair conviction and ordering a new trial. The "harm" of enduring a trial, while significant, does not equate to the jurisdictional "irretrievable loss" required by the collateral order doctrine. The legal system presumes that the remedy of reversal is sufficient to address constitutional violations that occur during the trial process. See *Digital Equipment Corp. v. Desktop Direct, Inc.*, 511 U.S. 863, 878 (1994) (holding that the collateral order doctrine does not apply where the right can be vindicated on appeal).

**C. Judicial Economy and Exceptional Circumstances**

Appellant argues that "exceptional circumstances" exist here because an evidentiary hearing has already been held and the record is fully developed. He contends that judicial economy favors immediate review to avoid the waste of resources on a trial that may be reversed.

While we appreciate the efficiency of reviewing a developed record, judicial economy is a policy consideration that cannot override the jurisdictional limits imposed by the final judgment rule. As stated in *Will v. United States*, 389 U.S. 90, 95-96 (1967), the collateral order doctrine is a "narrow exception" reserved for rights that would be irretrievably lost, not a mechanism for appellate courts to manage court dockets. If we were to accept Appellant’s argument, any defendant who alleges "presumptive bias" and holds an evidentiary hearing could bypass the final judgment rule, effectively collapsing the distinction between interlocutory and final appeals.

The existence of a "presumptive" or "cultural" bias, as alleged by Appellant, presents a serious claim on the merits. However, the proper forum for evaluating the sufficiency of the evidence regarding this bias is on direct appeal, should Appellant be convicted. The Trial Court’s denial of the motion for change of venue does not present a question that is "effectively unreviewable" post-trial.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Appellant has raised novel and serious arguments regarding the impact of Samoan cultural values on his right to a fair trial. However, the collateral order doctrine is a doctrine of jurisdiction, not of merits. The denial of a motion for change of venue, even when predicated on allegations of structural error and cultural bias, remains an order that is reviewable on appeal from a final judgment. Because Appellant has not demonstrated that his right to a fair trial will be "irretrievably lost" if review is postponed, he cannot satisfy the third prong of *Cohen*.

Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**SO ORDERED.**
