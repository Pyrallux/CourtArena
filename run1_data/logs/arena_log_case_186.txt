## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The material facts, as alleged in the Complaint and admitted by demurrer, establish the following: Plaintiff Gary Ross suffers from chronic back pain resulting from injuries sustained during his service in the United States Air Force. In September 1999, upon his physician's recommendation, Plaintiff began using marijuana for medical purposes pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Safety Code § 11362.5).

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

On review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer without leave to amend, this Court's inquiry is limited to whether the plaintiff can state a cause of action. In reviewing the complaint, the Court treats the demurrer as admitting the well-pleaded allegations of material fact, but not the contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. (*Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241].)

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights or Obligations

The Plaintiff's central contention—that the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 creates a right to use medical marijuana that employers must accommodate—rests upon a fundamental misconstruction of the statute's scope and purpose. The Compassionate Use Act was enacted by the voters in 1996 as an initiative measure adding Health and Safety Code Section 11362.5. The Act's plain language provides that persons who use marijuana for medical purposes upon a physician's recommendation "shall not be subject to criminal prosecution" under certain state criminal statutes relating to marijuana. (Health & Safety Code § 11362.5, subd. (d).)

As this Court recognized in *People v. Kelly* (2010) 47 Cal.4th 100, 124 [97 Cal.Rptr.3d 532, 222 P.3d 186], the Compassionate Use Act "was designed to address the criminal prosecution of patients who use marijuana for medicinal purposes" and was not intended to create comprehensive regulatory scheme governing all aspects of medical marijuana. The Act is, by its terms, a criminal defense mechanism—it provides an affirmative defense to certain marijuana-related criminal charges. It does not create affirmative rights to use marijuana in any and all circumstances, nor does it impose obligations upon third parties, including employers, to accommodate such use.

The Court of Appeal for the Fourth District explicitly addressed this issue in *People v. Trippet* (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1536 [154 Cal.Rptr.2d 365], holding that the Compassionate Use Act "does not provide a right to use marijuana; it merely provides a defense to criminal prosecution for certain marijuana-related offenses." This Court should adopt the same interpretation here.

The legislative history of the Compassionate Use Act confirms that the voters who approved the initiative in 1996 never contemplated that the Act would regulate employer-employee relationships. The ballot argument presented to voters in support of Proposition 215 made no mention of employment rights or employer obligations. The voters were concerned with providing relief from criminal prosecution for seriously ill patients, not with establishing workplace policies. Nothing in the text, structure, or history of the Act suggests an intent to impose duties upon employers.

Therefore, the Plaintiff cannot base a cause of action upon the Compassionate Use Act, as the Act creates no duty that Defendant allegedly violated.

### B. FEHA Does Not Require Employers to Accommodate Medical Marijuana Use

The Plaintiff's FEHA claims fail because the reasonable accommodation required by statute does not extend to conduct that violates legitimate employer policies, particularly policies relating to workplace safety and drug-free environments.

The Fair Employment and Housing Act, codified at Government Code Section 12900 et seq., prohibits employment discrimination against qualified individuals with disabilities. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (a).) FEHA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodation for the known physical or mental disabilities of qualified individuals, unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the employer's business. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (n).)

However, the accommodation obligation extends to the disability itself, not to every method of treating or managing the disability. As this Court established in *Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 349 [100 Cal.Rptr.2d 352, 8 P.3d 1089], reasonable accommodation under FEHA does not require employers to tolerate conduct that violates company policies or poses safety risks. The focus of the accommodation analysis is on enabling the employee to perform the essential functions of the job, not on requiring the employer to abandon legitimate operational policies.

Here, the Plaintiff requests as accommodation that Defendant exempt him from its drug-testing policy or tolerate his use of marijuana. This requested accommodation would require Defendant to abandon a lawful, neutral policy that applies to all employees—regardless of whether they use marijuana for medical purposes or otherwise. Such an accommodation is not "reasonable" within the meaning of FEHA.

Moreover, Defendant's drug-testing policy serves legitimate business purposes. As this Court recognized in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." Although *Loder* addressed the admissibility of drug test results in criminal proceedings, its underlying principle—that employers have substantial discretion to maintain drug-free workplaces—applies with equal force in the civil employment context.

The Plaintiff argues that because his physician recommended marijuana to treat his back pain (a qualifying disability), Defendant must accommodate this treatment. This argument proves too much. FEHA does not require employers to accommodate the specific treatment method chosen by an employee when that treatment conflicts with legitimate employer policies. An employee with diabetes who uses insulin may be accommodated, but an employee who chooses to use an illegal substance (even if legally recommended for medical purposes) does not trigger an accommodation obligation that would require the employer to tolerate violations of its policies.

The Plaintiff cites no authority supporting the proposition that FEHA requires employers to accommodate off-duty use of controlled substances, including marijuana. Indeed, federal courts interpreting analogous provisions of the Americans with Disabilities Act have consistently held that employers are not required to accommodate the use of illegal drugs. (*See, e.g.,*, *Raytheon Co. v. Hernandez* (2004) 540 U.S. 44, 53-54 [124 S. Ct. 513, 157 L.Ed.2d 357]; *Coffman v. Michigan City* (7th Cir. 2001) 253 F.3d 392, 399.) California law should be interpreted consistently with this well-established principle.

The Plaintiff's reliance on *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157] is misplaced. In *Stevenson*, this Court held that the right to privacy embodied in the California Constitution includes the right to use physician-prescribed marijuana in one's home. However, *Stevenson* did not address whether this right creates any obligation upon employers to accommodate such use in the employment context. The right to privacy is a constitutional protection against government intrusion, not a basis for imposing obligations upon private employers. The Plaintiff has cited no authority extending *Stevenson* to the employment context, and this Court should decline to do so here.

Accordingly, the Plaintiff fails to state a cause of action for failure to accommodate under FEHA.

### C. The Plaintiff Fails to State a Claim for Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy

The Plaintiff's second cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy fails for the same fundamental reason: the Compassionate Use Act does not create a public policy requiring employers to accommodate medical marijuana use.

The tort of wrongful termination in violation of public policy, as recognized in *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330], requires that the termination violate a fundamental public policy derived from constitutional, statutory, or regulatory provisions. The public policy must be substantial, well-established, and fundamental to the state's interest in protecting the health, safety, or welfare of its citizens.

The Compassionate Use Act does not establish such a public policy. As discussed above, the Act is a narrow criminal defense provision that does not create affirmative rights to use marijuana in all circumstances. The Act contains no reference to employment relationships, and nothing in its text or history suggests an intent to regulate the employer-employee relationship.

The Plaintiff has not identified any other source of public policy that Defendant allegedly violated. The Plaintiff does not allege that Defendant violated any statute, regulation, or constitutional provision relating to employment discrimination beyond the FEHA claims already addressed. The mere fact that the Plaintiff used marijuana legally under California law does not mean that his termination violated public policy.

In *Tameny*, this Court emphasized that the public policy exception to at-will employment is narrow and should be recognized only when a statutory or constitutional provision clearly establishes the policy at issue. (27 Cal.3d at 178.) The Plaintiff cannot satisfy this requirement here because no law establishes a public policy requiring employers to accommodate medical marijuana use.

Therefore, the Plaintiff fails to state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

### D. The Lower Courts Correctly Applied Established Legal Principles

The lower courts correctly applied established legal principles to the facts alleged in the Complaint. The trial court sustained Defendant's demurrer without leave to amend, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. Both courts correctly determined that the Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights and that FEHA does not require accommodation of medical marijuana use.

The Plaintiff urges this Court to create new law extending the Compassionate Use Act and FEHA to the employment context. However, the Plaintiff's proposed expansion of these statutes finds no support in the text, history, or existing case law. This Court should decline the Plaintiff's invitation to judicially legislate new employment rights that the Legislature and the voters have not seen fit to create.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. PROCEDURAL POSTURE AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This matter comes before the Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to the plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The Court of Appeal affirmed the judgment. In reviewing a ruling on a demurrer, we treat the demurrer as admitting the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations of material fact, but not its contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. (*Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241].) The sole question presented is whether the plaintiff has stated a cause of action for disability discrimination under the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

The plaintiff contends that his termination violates public policy because his use of marijuana was authorized by the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5). We disagree. The Compassionate Use Act was designed to address the criminal prosecution of patients who use marijuana for medicinal purposes. As the Court of Appeal explicitly held in *People v. Trippet* (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1536 [65 Cal.Rptr.2d 647], the Act "does not provide a right to use marijuana; it merely provides a defense to criminal prosecution for certain marijuana-related offenses." This interpretation is consistent with the California Supreme Court’s observation in *People v. Kelly* (2010) 47 Cal.4th 100, 124 [97 Cal.Rptr.3d 532, 222 P.3d 186], that the Act was not intended to create a comprehensive regulatory scheme governing all aspects of medical marijuana.

The text and history of the Compassionate Use Act are silent regarding employment rights and duties. The ballot arguments presented to voters focused on providing relief from criminal prosecution for seriously ill patients and did not mention employer obligations. Because the Act is strictly a criminal defense mechanism, it cannot be construed to create a civil right to use marijuana that supersedes an employer’s right to maintain a drug-free workplace. Consequently, the plaintiff cannot base a cause of action on the Compassionate Use Act.

**B. FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Medical Marijuana Use**

The plaintiff further alleges that the defendant violated FEHA by failing to accommodate his disability. FEHA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodation for the known physical or mental disabilities of qualified individuals, unless doing so would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the employer's business. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (n).) However, the obligation to accommodate extends to the disability itself, not to the specific method of treatment chosen by the employee if that method conflicts with legitimate employer policies.

In *Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 349 [100 Cal.Rptr.2d 352, 8 P.3d 1089], this Court clarified that reasonable accommodation under FEHA does not require employers to tolerate conduct that violates company policies or poses safety risks. Here, the plaintiff requests an exemption from the defendant’s drug-testing policy. Such an accommodation would require the employer to abandon a lawful, neutral policy applicable to all employees. This is not "reasonable" under FEHA.

Furthermore, employers retain broad discretion to maintain drug-free workplaces and to require preemployment drug tests. (*Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882–883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200].) *Loder* establishes that employers may take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. Although the plaintiff’s marijuana use was recommended by a physician, marijuana remains an illegal drug under federal law and its use is not protected under FEHA to the extent it violates neutral employment policies. The plaintiff’s reliance on *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157] is misplaced; *Stevenson* addressed a constitutional right to privacy against government intrusion, not an obligation on private employers to accommodate medical marijuana use.

**C. No Claim for Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy**

Finally, the plaintiff asserts a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.
