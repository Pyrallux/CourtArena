Your Honor, the Fourth Amendment was violated when the officer conducted a pat-down of the defendant without reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous, rendering the subsequent search of the vehicle unconstitutional. The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures, and a pat-down for weapons requires specific, articulable facts supporting a reasonable belief that the individual poses an immediate threat to officer safety. Here, the officer had no such basis, as confirmed by his own testimony.

As this Court established in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a frisk is lawful only when an officer has "reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and presently dangerous." The officer in this case admitted he had "no safety concerns" and his weapon was never drawn. He observed no furtive movements, no signs of intoxication, no smell of alcohol or drugs, and no other suspicious activity beyond the suspended license. On cross-examination, he explicitly stated: "Aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there’s no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity." This testimony confirms the absence of any objective facts justifying a pat-down for weapons.

This Court reaffirmed in *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009), that "the sole justification for a pat-down is the officer's reasonable belief that the individual is armed and dangerous." The officer’s subjective belief that defendant’s story about being "lost" was untruthful does not equate to reasonable suspicion of an armed threat. Reasonable suspicion requires specific, articulable facts indicating danger, not mere speculation about deception. As held in *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989), "reasonable suspicion requires specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences, reasonably warrant the intrusion." The officer provided no such facts here—he described no weapon, no threatening gestures, no bulges in clothing, and no other indicia of danger. His testimony about defendant’s "overly cooperative" behavior does not create reasonable suspicion; it merely reflects voluntary compliance.

Furthermore, defendant’s act of patting himself down and offering consent does not cure the constitutional violation. Consent must be voluntary and untainted by prior unlawful seizures. In *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), this Court held that "consent to search is not voluntary if it is the product of an illegal detention." Here, the pat-down was an illegal seizure because it lacked reasonable suspicion. The officer conducted the pat-down based on defendant’s cooperation, not on any objective threat. This sequence transformed the encounter into an unlawful detention, rendering any consent to search the vehicle involuntary.

The officer’s search of the vehicle—discovering drug evidence—was the direct fruit of this unconstitutional pat-down. Without reasonable suspicion to justify the initial frisk, the entire chain of events violating the Fourth Amendment must be suppressed. The evidence obtained should be excluded to uphold the constitutional guarantee against unreasonable searches and seizures.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of the Suppression of Evidence

---

Your Honor, the prosecution's argument fails for multiple reasons and ignores critical facts establishing that the defendant's constitutional rights were not violated. The defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the suppression motion.

---

## I. THE ENCOUNTER WAS NOT A SEIZURE REQUIRING REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution incorrectly frames the initial encounter as a seizure. The facts demonstrate otherwise.

The defendant pulled his vehicle over on his own initiative. The officer testified unequivocally: "Regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." The officer activated his lights only after the defendant had already stopped—not to initiate a stop, but "so the driver would know it was a police officer and as protection in case anyone would come up from behind." This constitutes a mere communication of official presence, not a seizure.

In *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983), the Supreme Court held that a seizure requires "a governmental initiation of an investigation, the display of official authority, and the submission to that authority." Here, the defendant initiated the stop himself. The officer's activation of lights was purely protective and informational. The defendant was never detained in the constitutional sense until after the license suspension was discovered—and that detention was justified by the administrative violation of driving on a suspended license.

---

## II. THE PAT-DOWN WAS CONSENSUAL AND INITIATED BY THE DEFENDANT

The prosecution's central argument rests on the assumption that an unlawful pat-down occurred. This fundamentally mischaracterizes what happened.

The officer testified that after explaining the license seizure procedures:

> "[Defendant] at that point basically patted himself down from top to bottom, starting here, down a couple of— you know, down to his legs. After he did that, he placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me."

The defendant *voluntarily* initiated the pat-down of his own person. He placed his hands on the vehicle and explicitly invited the officer to search him. The officer did not request, demand, or suggest a pat-down. This distinguishes the case from *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), where the Court addressed *police-initiated* frisks.

In *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), the Supreme Court held that a consensual encounter does not implicate the Fourth Amendment "even if the officer has no basis for suspecting individual of misconduct." The defendant here was not merely consenting to a search—he was *actively inviting* it. His conduct demonstrates unequivocal voluntary consent, not submission to governmental authority.

The prosecution argues the officer should have advised the defendant of his right to refuse. However, in *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the Court held that voluntariness is determined by the totality of the circumstances, and knowledge of the right to refuse is not a prerequisite. Here, the defendant was fully oriented, sober, cooperative, and explicitly offered to be searched—unprompted by any officer conduct.

---

## III. CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS INDEPENDENTLY VALID

Even assuming arguendo that some constitutional issue attended the pat-down of the defendant's person, the search of the vehicle rests on independent consent.

The officer testified:

> "I asked him, can I search your car? And he said, yes."

The defendant gave clear, verbal consent to search his vehicle. This consent was not the product of any alleged illegality. The defendant had already been told he was "free to go" after receiving the citation and license forfeiture form. He was waiting for his girlfriend and had no obligation to remain. His continued engagement with the officer and his ultimate consent to search the vehicle were entirely voluntary.

In *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the Supreme Court established that consent must be voluntary under the totality of circumstances, and the government bears the burden of proving voluntariness. The prosecution cannot meet this burden here, where the defendant:

1. Was fully compliant, polite, and respectful throughout
2. Was never subjected to any show of force (officer's weapon was never drawn)
3. Was explicitly told he was "free to go"
4. Initiated the pat-down and search conversation on his own
5. Verbally consented to the vehicle search

The prosecution's reliance on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In *Royer*, the defendant was detained in an airport terminal by officers with badges displayed, his luggage was taken, and he was questioned—all before any consent was given. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to leave, was not detained, and voluntarily offered both his person and vehicle for searching.

---

## IV. THE OFFICER HAD LEGITIMATE REASONS FOR THE ENCOUNTER

The prosecution minimizes the lawful bases for the officer's conduct. The officer observed a vehicle stopped at an unusual location at 3:42 a.m., followed it, and upon approaching, discovered the defendant was driving on a suspended license. This is a criminal violation in Pennsylvania.

When the officer ran the license check, he discovered the suspension and was required to seize the license. This created a lawful basis for the officer's continued interaction with the defendant. The officer wrote a citation and explained the license forfeiture procedures—legitimate ministerial duties.

The fact that the officer may have subjectively suspected other criminal activity does not invalidate the lawful bases for the encounter. The defendant was in possession of a suspended license, and the officer was engaged in a legitimate administrative function when the defendant volunteered to be searched.

---

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT IMPROPERLY SHIFTS THE BURDEN

The prosecution asks this Court to suppress evidence based on speculation about what *might* have motivated the defendant's conduct. This ignores the well-established principle that the Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches, not against voluntary cooperation.

In *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), the Supreme Court emphasized that officers may approach individuals and ask for consent to search without any articulable suspicion, and such encounters are consensual and Fourth Amendment-free. The defendant here went far beyond mere passive submission—he affirmatively invited the search.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the suppression motion. The encounter was not a seizure, the pat-down was voluntarily initiated by the defendant, and the subsequent search of the vehicle was based on the defendant's independent, voluntary consent. The evidence obtained should not be suppressed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The matter before the Court is the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The central issue is whether the search of the defendant’s person and subsequent search of his vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. The prosecution argues that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down, rendering the subsequent vehicle search unconstitutional as fruit of the poisonous tree. The defense argues that the encounter was consensual, the pat-down was initiated by the defendant, and the vehicle search was supported by independent, voluntary consent. Based on the facts provided and the applicable legal standards, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down of the Defendant’s Person**

The prosecution contends that the officer violated the defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights by conducting a pat-down without reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous. The prosecution correctly cites *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), for the proposition that a police officer may conduct a brief investigatory stop and a frisk for weapons only when the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot and that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The prosecution further relies on *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009), to emphasize that the sole justification for a pat-down is officer safety.

However, the prosecution’s analysis fails to account for the specific factual finding that the defendant voluntarily initiated the pat-down. The record indicates that after the officer explained the license forfeiture procedures, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer did not order the defendant to assume the position or demand a search; rather, the defendant performed these actions unprompted.

Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), a seizure does not occur simply because a police officer approaches an individual and asks questions; so long as a reasonable person would feel free to decline the request or terminate the encounter, the Fourth Amendment is not implicated. Here, the defendant did not merely submit to a show of authority; he affirmatively invited the search. As held in *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), police officers may engage in consensual encounters with citizens and request consent to search without any level of suspicion, provided the citizen’s cooperation is voluntary. Because the defendant voluntarily initiated the pat-down by his own actions and words, the officer was not required to possess reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous prior to conducting it. The prosecution’s argument regarding the lack of reasonable suspicion is therefore moot regarding the pat-down, as the intrusion was justified by consent rather than *Terry* doctrine.

**B. The Search of the Vehicle**

The prosecution argues that the evidence found in the vehicle must be suppressed because the search was the fruit of an unlawful pat-down, citing *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983). In *Royer*, the Court held that consent to search is not voluntary if it is the product of an illegal detention. However, this precedent is inapplicable here for two reasons: the pat-down was not unlawful, and the vehicle search was not the product of an illegal detention.

First, as established above, the pat-down was consensual and therefore lawful. Consequently, there is no "primary illegality" to taint the subsequent evidence under the fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree doctrine.

Second, the facts indicate that the detention related to the traffic violation had concluded prior to the request to search the vehicle. The officer testified that after explaining the documents, the defendant was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). Although the defendant decided to wait for his girlfriend, the officer had completed the administrative tasks associated with the suspended license citation. The interaction that followed—where the defendant initiated the pat-down and then consented to the vehicle search—occurred during what the defense characterizes as a consensual encounter, and the facts support this characterization. The officer did not brandish his weapon, used a polite tone, and the defendant was cooperative and compliant.

The voluntariness of consent is judged by the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The prosecution argues that the consent was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse. However, *Schneckloth* explicitly holds that knowledge of the right to refuse is not a prerequisite to a valid consent. The defendant’s clear, verbal response of "yes" to the officer’s question, "can I search your car?" constitutes valid consent.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the officer’s conduct violated the defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights. The pat-down was initiated by the defendant’s voluntary actions, negating the requirement for reasonable suspicion. The subsequent search of the vehicle was based on the defendant’s clear verbal consent, given after he was informed he was free to leave regarding the traffic matter. Therefore, the evidence discovered is admissible.

**IT IS ORDERED that the defendant’s motion to suppress is denied.**

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal misapplies the law to the facts of this case. The prosecution argues that the officer unlawfully prolonged the traffic stop and that any subsequent consent was tainted by this illegality. This argument fails because the facts establish that the traffic stop had concluded before the pat-down occurred.

The prosecution relies on Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348 (2015), for the proposition that a traffic stop may not be prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to address the traffic violation. However, Rodriguez is inapposite because here, the stop was not prolonged. The officer completed all traffic-related business and explicitly informed the defendant he was free to go. The subsequent conversation occurred after the stop concluded, during what is properly characterized as a consensual encounter.

The prosecution contends that the defendant remained "detained" because his license was seized, lights were activated, and a backup officer was present. However, these circumstances, standing alone, do not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure. In United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194, 201 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the presence of officers does not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure so long as a reasonable person would feel free to decline the request or leave. The defendant here was told he was free to go. He chose to remain. He then chose to engage the officer in further conversation and offer his person for a search.

The officer did not request this; the defendant offered it unprompted.

The prosecution argues that any consent given during an unlawful detention is per se involuntary under Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983). However, Royer applies when consent is obtained during an unlawful detention. Here, there was no unlawful detention. The defendant was told he was free to go. He was not handcuffed. His vehicle was not blocked. His license had been seized, but that administrative act does not constitute a continuing detention for Fourth Amendment purposes. The defendant voluntarily chose to remain and engage with the officer.

The prosecution cites United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), for the proposition that a seizure occurs when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. However, Mendenhall itself recognized that not every police encounter constitutes a seizure. Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was free to go. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would have understood that he was at liberty to leave. The defendant's choice to stay and offer himself for a search was therefore voluntary.

The prosecution emphasizes the officer's admission that "aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there's no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity." Suppression Tr. 33:16-19. This admission, however, cuts in favor of the defense. It demonstrates that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for any detention—which is precisely why the encounter must be analyzed as consensual rather than as an investigatory stop. Because there was no unlawful detention, the consent analysis under Royer is inapplicable.

The prosecution argues that the lack of warnings about the right to refuse renders the consent invalid. However, in Schneckloth v. The focus is on the voluntariness of the consent, judged by the totality of the circumstances. Here, those circumstances overwhelmingly support a finding of voluntariness: the defendant was told he was free to go, he was cooperative and polite, the officer was courteous, and the defendant explicitly said "yes" to the search request.

Furthermore, the prosecution cannot establish that the vehicle search was fruit of the poisonous tree. The vehicle search was based on the defendant's independent consent to search the car—a separate act from the pat-down. Even if the pat-down were unconstitutional (which it was not), the vehicle search would be admissible because it was supported by independent consent. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963) (holding that evidence derived from an unlawful source may nonetheless be admissible if it was obtained by an independent source).

In Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991), the Court emphasized that a seizure does not occur simply because a police officer approaches an individual and asks questions. The relevant inquiry is whether a reasonable person would feel free to decline the request or terminate the encounter. Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant was not handcuffed. The officer's weapon was not drawn. The officer used a polite and conversational tone throughout. The defendant was fully compliant, respectful, and cooperative. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue remains whether the search of the defendant’s person and vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. Specifically, the Court must determine if the encounter was consensual or if the defendant was unlawfully detained, thereby tainting his consent to search. For the reasons that follow, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Traffic Stop Concluded Prior to the Search Requests**

The prosecution argues that the officer unlawfully prolonged the traffic stop in violation of *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015). Under *Rodriguez*, a police stop may not be prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to address the traffic violation that justified the stop in the first place. The prosecution contends that because the officer waited with the defendant for his girlfriend to arrive, the stop was unlawfully extended without reasonable suspicion of additional criminal activity.

The Court finds this argument unpersuasive based on the specific factual findings. The record clearly establishes that the officer completed the "mission" of the stop prior to the search. The officer issued the citation for driving with a suspended license, completed the license forfeiture form, and ran the necessary NCIC checks. (Suppression Tr. 19:19-20:6). Critically, the officer testified that after explaining these documents, the defendant was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10).

Once the officer issued the citation and informed the defendant he was free to leave, the investigative detention associated with the traffic violation concluded. See *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983) (holding that a temporary detention terminates when the police purpose is fulfilled and the suspect is free to leave). The defendant’s subsequent decision to wait for his girlfriend was a voluntary choice on his part, not a detention imposed by the officer. The officer did not compel the defendant to stay; he merely allowed him to remain on the roadside while he waited for a ride. Therefore, the *Rodriguez* constraints regarding the prolongation of a traffic stop are not triggered, as the stop had already ended.

**B. The Encounter Was Consensual, Not a Seizure**

The prosecution argues that despite the "free to go" statement, the defendant was still seized because a reasonable person would not feel free to leave given the presence of rotating police lights, a backup officer, and the seizure of his driver's license. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980).

While these factors are relevant to the totality of the circumstances, they do not override the defendant's objective liberty. The officer’s statement that the defendant was free to go is a dispositive fact in this analysis. See *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002) (noting that police conduct does not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure unless it would communicate to a reasonable person that he is not free to leave). Here, the officer communicated clearly that the defendant was free to leave. The presence of a backup officer and activated lights are consistent with officer safety during a late-night roadside interaction and do not, in themselves, transform a consensual conversation into a Fourth Amendment seizure. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (rejecting the notion that police presence alone is inherently coercive).

Furthermore, the defendant’s behavior confirms that he felt free to leave. He did not attempt to depart, but instead, he re-engaged the officer in conversation to ask about the license forfeiture form—a procedure the officer had already explained. This voluntary re-engagement supports the conclusion that the encounter was consensual.

**C. The Pat-Down and Vehicle Search Were Consensual**

Because the traffic stop had concluded and the encounter was consensual, the Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable seizures do not require the officer to have reasonable suspicion to conduct the pat-down or search the vehicle.

The prosecution argues that the pat-down violated *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. However, *Terry* governs police-initiated frisks for weapons during an investigatory stop. Here, the defendant initiated the search himself. The record shows that the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the car, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer did not order the defendant to assume the position; the defendant offered to be searched unprompted. A citizen may voluntarily consent to a search without the need for police justification. See *Drayton*, 536 U.S. at 201-02.

The prosecution further argues that the consent to search the vehicle was involuntary because it was the product of an unlawful detention, citing *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983). Since the Court has found that the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, the *Royer* taint doctrine does not apply.

The prosecution also suggests the consent was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse. This argument is rejected under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973), which holds that knowledge of the right to refuse is not a prerequisite to a valid consent. The totality of the circumstances supports a finding of voluntariness: the officer’s weapon was not drawn, the officer’s demeanor was polite, the defendant was "agreeable," and the defendant gave clear, verbal consent ("yes") to the search of his car. (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18, 24:3-5).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has failed to establish that the officer violated the defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights. The traffic stop concluded when the officer completed the citation and informed the defendant he was free to go. The subsequent interaction was a consensual encounter during which the defendant voluntarily initiated a pat-down and verbally consented to the search of his vehicle. The evidence discovered is admissible.

**IT IS ORDERED that the defendant’s motion to suppress is denied.**

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving that the search of the defendant's vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. The record clearly establishes that the traffic stop concluded before any search requests were made, the subsequent encounter was entirely consensual, and the defendant voluntarily initiated both the pat-down and the vehicle search. The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as an illegal detention fails because it ignores the explicit testimony that the defendant was "free to go" and overlooks the defendant's own affirmative actions demonstrating voluntary cooperation. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the denial of the motion to suppress.

## I. The Traffic Stop Concluded Prior to Any Search Requests

The prosecution's central argument rests on the premise that the officer unlawfully prolonged a traffic stop. This premise is fundamentally flawed because the traffic stop had already concluded before the search requests were made.

Under *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015), a police stop may not be prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to address the traffic violation that justified the stop. However, as this Court found in its ruling, the officer completed the "mission" of the stop prior to any search requests. The officer issued the citation for driving with a suspended license, completed the license forfeiture form, and ran the necessary NCIC checks. Suppression Tr. 19:19-20:6. Critically, the officer testified that after explaining these documents, the defendant was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10.

The Supreme Court has held that a temporary detention terminates when the police purpose is fulfilled and the suspect is free to leave. *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983). Here, the officer fulfilled his purpose by issuing the citation and explaining the license forfeiture procedure. The defendant's subsequent decision to wait for his girlfriend was a voluntary choice, not a detention imposed by the officer. The officer did not compel the defendant to stay; he merely allowed him to remain on the roadside while he waited for a ride. The prosecution's reliance on *Rodriguez* is misplaced because that case applies only when a stop is unlawfully prolonged—not, as here, when a stop has already concluded.

The prosecution argues that the officer "waited with the defendant for his girlfriend to arrive," but this characterization distorts the record. The officer did not wait with the defendant; the officer completed his official duties, informed the defendant he was free to go, and the defendant independently chose to remain on the roadside to wait for his girlfriend. This distinction is legally significant because it separates official police activity from the defendant's private choices.

## II. The Encounter Was Consensual, Not a Seizure

The prosecution contends that despite the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go," the defendant remained seized because a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave given the presence of rotating police lights, a backup officer, and the seizure of his driver's license. This argument fails because it asks the Court to disregard the officer's explicit communication to the defendant in favor of inferring coercion from circumstances that, while relevant to the totality of the circumstances, are not dispositive.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that police conduct does not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure unless it would communicate to a reasonable person that he is not free to leave. *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002). In *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991), the Court rejected the notion that police presence alone is inherently coercive, holding that "a reasonable person would not feel free to leave" only when surrounded by police in a confined space—or when police conduct rises to the level of a show of authority.

Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. This clear communication is dispositive. As the Court in *Drayton* recognized, 536 U.S. at 204, the police officer's conduct must be assessed from the perspective of a reasonable person, but the officer's own statements factor prominently into that assessment. When an officer explicitly informs a suspect that he is free to leave, a reasonable person would understand that permission to depart has been granted.

The prosecution relies on *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), for the proposition that police presence can transform a consensual encounter into a seizure. However, *Mendenhall* itself recognized that not every police encounter constitutes a seizure. The Court in *Mendenhall* identified several factors relevant to the seizure inquiry, including the threatening presence of several police officers, the display of a weapon, the physical touching of the person, the use of aggressive language, and the blocking of the person's path. None of these factors are present here. The officer's weapon was never drawn. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The officer was polite and professional throughout. The defendant was not physically touched, blocked, or ordered to do anything. The presence of a backup officer and activated lights are consistent with standard officer safety protocols during a late-night roadside interaction and do not, in themselves, transform a consensual conversation into a Fourth Amendment seizure. *Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437.

Most significantly, the defendant's own behavior confirms that he felt free to leave. He did not attempt to depart. Instead, he re-engaged the officer in conversation to ask about the license forfeiture form—a procedure the officer had already explained. This voluntary re-engagement demonstrates that the defendant understood he was free to interact with the officer or to leave, and he chose to remain and ask additional questions. The prosecution cannot have it both ways: if the defendant truly felt coerced, he would not have initiated further conversation; his voluntary re-engagement affirmatively demonstrates that the encounter was consensual.

The prosecution emphasizes that the officer seized the defendant's license. However, the license seizure was a lawful consequence of the traffic violation—driving with a suspended license—and does not transform the encounter into an illegal detention. The officer completed the license forfeiture form as part of his official duties, explained the procedures to the defendant, and informed the defendant that he could resolve the suspension. Suppression Tr. 18:14-25. The defendant was not detained pending the resolution of the license issue; he was told he could address it at a later date and was free to leave in the meantime.

## III. The Pat-Down Was Voluntary and Initiated by the Defendant

The prosecution argues that the pat-down violated *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. This argument fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the pat-down.

*Terry* governs police-initiated frisks for weapons during an investigatory stop. It does not apply when, as here, the defendant himself initiates the search. The record shows unambiguously that the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. The officer did not order the defendant to assume the position; the defendant offered to be searched unprompted.

The prosecution attempts to frame this as a police-initiated search by arguing that the officer's presence coerced the defendant into consenting. However, the prosecution cannot escape the fact that the defendant took the initiative. He patted himself down. He placed his hands on the car. He spread his legs. He explicitly invited the search. These are not the actions of a coerced individual; they are the actions of someone who voluntarily offered to be searched.

The prosecution cites *Terry* for the proposition that a pat-down requires reasonable suspicion. However, *Terry* applies to police-initiated frisks. When a citizen voluntarily offers to be searched, no *Terry* justification is required. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches, and a voluntary, unsolicited offer to be searched is not unreasonable. *Drayton*, 536 U.S. at 201-02.

The prosecution argues that the officer's subjective belief that the defendant was "suspicious" demonstrates that the pat-down was coerced. However, the prosecution's own witness—the officer—testified that he had "no fear for his safety or suspicion of any illegality from the initial contact based on defendant's demeanor." Suppression Tr. 33:3-19. The officer's subjective suspicions, which the Court found lacked "objective grounds," do not negate the voluntary nature of the defendant's offer to be searched. The defendant was polite, cooperative, and fully compliant throughout the encounter. Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 34:8-35:13. His decision to offer himself for a search was his own choice, not a response to any police demand or coercion.

## IV. The Vehicle Search Was Based on Independent, Voluntary Consent

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was involuntary because it was the product of an unlawful detention. This argument fails because the detention was lawful—it had concluded—and the consent was independent.

The Supreme Court has held that consent to search obtained while a person is illegally detained is not voluntary. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 501 (1983). However, as this Court found, the detention had ended before the search requests were made. The defendant was "free to go." He chose to remain and wait for his girlfriend. He then voluntarily offered to be searched and voluntarily consented to a search of his vehicle. Under these circumstances, the *Royer* taint doctrine does not apply.

The prosecution argues that the officer asked to search the car only after the pat-down, demonstrating that the pat-down "catalyzed" the vehicle search. This argument misapprehends the law. The sequence of events does not render the consent invalid. What matters is whether the consent was voluntary, not whether it was preceded by other events. The defendant was free to refuse the search request. He did not refuse. He said "yes." Suppression Tr. 23:2-3. His consent was knowing and voluntary.

The prosecution argues that the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse. However, under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973), knowledge of the right to refuse is not a prerequisite to a valid consent. The Court in *Schneckloth* held that consent is valid if it is voluntary under the totality of the circumstances, and the prosecution bears the burden of proving voluntariness by a preponderance of the evidence.

Here, the totality of the circumstances overwhelmingly supports a finding of voluntariness. The officer's weapon was not drawn. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The officer's demeanor was polite and professional. The defendant was "agreeable" throughout the encounter. Suppression Tr. 23:4-18. The defendant gave clear, verbal consent ("yes") to the search of his car. Suppression Tr. 23:2-3. The defendant was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and was not threatened in any way. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the consent was involuntary.

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963), is misplaced. *Wong Sun* applies to evidence obtained as fruit of an illegal search. Here, there was no illegal search. The pat-down was initiated by the defendant, not the officer. The vehicle search was based on the defendant's voluntary consent. Neither search was unlawful, and thus there is no "poisonous tree" tainting the evidence.

## V. The Prosecution's Characterization of the Facts Is Incorrect

The prosecution's closing statement mischaracterizes the facts in several material respects. First, the prosecution argues that the officer "waited with the defendant for his girlfriend to arrive." This is incorrect. The officer completed his official duties and informed the defendant he was free to go. The defendant's decision to wait for his girlfriend was his own choice, not a continuation of the stop.

Second, the prosecution argues that the defendant was "surrounded by police" and could not leave. This is incorrect. There were two officers present, both in their vehicles initially, and the defendant was informed he was free to go. The defendant did not attempt to leave; he chose to remain and ask additional questions.

Third, the prosecution argues that the defendant's statement "you can search me" was "not a free choice" but "the product of coercion." This is incorrect. The defendant initiated the pat-down on his own, without prompting. He patted himself down, placed his hands on the car, spread his legs, and invited the search. This is the very definition of a free choice.

Fourth, the prosecution argues that the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" was "meaningless." This is incorrect. The statement communicated to the defendant that he was free to leave. A reasonable person would understand that communication, and the defendant's subsequent behavior—re-engaging the officer in conversation—confirms that he understood he was free to interact or depart.

## Conclusion

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the denial of the motion to suppress. The traffic stop concluded before any search requests were made. The subsequent encounter was consensual. The defendant voluntarily initiated the pat-down and voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. The prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving that the search violated the Fourth Amendment.

The evidence discovered during the search of the defendant's vehicle is admissible. The motion to suppress was properly denied.

This matter is before the Court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has carefully reviewed the record, the factual findings, and the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue for determination is whether the search of the defendant’s person and vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. Specifically, the Court must decide if the defendant was unlawfully detained during the encounter, thereby tainting his subsequent consent to search. For the reasons that follow, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Traffic Stop Concluded Prior to the Search Requests**

The prosecution argues that the officer unlawfully prolonged the traffic stop in violation of *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015). Under *Rodriguez*, a police stop may not be prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to address the traffic violation that justified the stop. The prosecution contends that the officer’s continued presence and the seizure of the defendant’s license constituted an unlawful extension of the stop.

The Court finds this argument unpersuasive based on the specific factual findings established by the record. The officer completed the "mission" of the stop prior to the search requests. The officer issued the citation for driving with a suspended license, completed the license forfeiture form, and ran the necessary NCIC checks. (Suppression Tr. 19:19-20:6). Critically, the officer testified that after explaining these documents, the defendant was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10).

Once the officer issued the citation and informed the defendant he was free to leave, the investigative detention associated with the traffic violation concluded. See *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983) (holding that a temporary detention terminates when the police purpose is fulfilled and the suspect is free to leave). The defendant’s subsequent decision to wait for his girlfriend was a voluntary choice on his part, not a detention imposed by the officer. The officer did not compel the defendant to stay; he merely allowed him to remain on the roadside while he waited for a ride. Therefore, the *Rodriguez* constraints regarding the prolongation of a traffic stop are not triggered, as the stop had already ended.

**B. The Encounter Was Consensual, Not a Seizure**

The prosecution argues that despite the "free to go" statement, the defendant was still seized because a reasonable person would not feel free to leave given the presence of rotating police lights, a backup officer, and the seizure of his driver's license. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980).

While these factors are relevant to the totality of the circumstances, they do not override the defendant's objective liberty. The officer’s statement that the defendant was free to go is a dispositive fact in this analysis. See *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002) (noting that police conduct does not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure unless it would communicate to a reasonable person that he is not free to leave). Here, the officer communicated clearly that the defendant was free to leave. The presence of a backup officer and activated lights are consistent with officer safety during a late-night roadside interaction and do not, in themselves, transform a consensual conversation into a Fourth Amendment seizure. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (rejecting the notion that police presence alone is inherently coercive).

Furthermore, the defendant’s behavior confirms that he felt free to leave. He did not attempt to depart, but instead, he re-engaged the officer in conversation to ask about the license forfeiture form—a procedure the officer had already explained. This voluntary re-engagement supports the conclusion that the encounter was consensual. The prosecution’s assertion that the "free to go" statement was meaningless ignores the defendant's agency in choosing to wait for his girlfriend and initiating further conversation.

**C. The Pat-Down Was Voluntary and Initiated by the Defendant**

The prosecution argues that the pat-down violated *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. The Court agrees that the officer lacked *Terry* grounds for a *self-initiated* pat-down, as the officer admitted no specific suspicious activity aside from the defendant's story of being lost. (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). However, *Terry* governs police-initiated frisks for weapons during an investigatory stop. Here, the defendant initiated the search himself.

The record shows that the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the car, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer did not order the defendant to assume the position; the defendant offered to be searched unprompted. A citizen may voluntarily consent to a search without the need for police justification. See *Drayton*, 536 U.S. at 201-02. The prosecution's argument that this offer was the product of coercion is unsupported by the record, given the officer's testimony that he had no fear for his safety and the defendant's polite and cooperative demeanor.

**D. The Vehicle Search Was Based on Independent, Voluntary Consent**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was involuntary because it was the product of an unlawful detention, citing *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 501 (1983). Since the Court has found that the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, the *Royer* taint doctrine does not apply.

The prosecution also suggests the consent was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse. This argument is rejected under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973), which holds that knowledge of the right to refuse is not a prerequisite to a valid consent. The totality of the circumstances supports a finding of voluntariness: the officer’s weapon was not drawn, the officer’s demeanor was polite, the defendant was "agreeable," and the defendant gave clear, verbal consent ("yes") to the search of his car. (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18, 24:3-5).

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963), is misplaced. *Wong Sun* applies to evidence obtained as fruit of an illegal search. Here, there was no illegal search. The pat-down was initiated by the defendant, not the officer. The vehicle search was based on the defendant's voluntary consent. Neither search was unlawful, and thus there is no "poisonous tree" tainting the evidence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has failed to establish that the officer violated the defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights. The traffic stop concluded when the officer completed the citation and informed the defendant he was free to go. The subsequent interaction was a consensual encounter during which the defendant voluntarily initiated a pat-down and verbally consented to the search of his vehicle. The evidence discovered is admissible.

**IT IS ORDERED that the defendant’s motion to suppress is denied.**
