## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Standard of Review

Appellate review of the sufficiency of evidence in a Chapter 980 proceeding is de novo, requiring this Court to examine whether the evidence, viewed most favorably to the State, was sufficient to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Poellinger, 153 Wis. 2d 493, 451 N.W.2d 752 (1990)*. This Court must also review de novo whether the trial court applied the proper legal standard in evaluating the evidence. The State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant is a sexually violent person. *State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 464, 596 N.W.2d 497 (1999)*.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting the Results of the Penile Plethysmograph Test

As noted in the motion for reconsideration, the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a penile plethysmograph test. This Court has recognized that the admission of such test results requires rigorous scrutiny because the reliability of plethysmographic testing has been questioned by courts and scholars alike. The use of such testing to determine sexual arousal patterns raises substantial concerns regarding scientific reliability and the potential for constitutional violation of the right against self-incrimination. The trial court's error in admitting this evidence requires reversal unless this Court can conclude that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, which it cannot be where, as here, the evidence constituted a substantial portion of the State's case.

### C. The State Failed to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That Lalor Is a Sexually Violent Person

The statutory definition of "sexually violent person" under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) requires proof that the person (1) has a mental disorder, and (2) is dangerous to others because the mental disorder creates a substantial probability that the person will engage in acts of sexual violence. The State must prove both elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

#### 1. The "Substantial Probability" Standard Requires Proof of More Than Statistical Risk

The State argues that the RRASOR scores indicating a 73.1% likelihood of reoffending within ten years satisfy the statutory standard. This argument fails as a matter of law. The "substantial probability" standard in Chapter 980 requires individualized proof that the specific person's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future violence—it cannot be satisfied by actuarial statistics derived from general populations. *State v. Post, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995)*.

In *Post*, this Court recognized that while actuarial instruments may be considered as part of the evidence, they do not alone establish the required probability. The Court emphasized that the evidence must demonstrate that the individual's mental disorder specifically creates the probability of future acts. The mere application of a risk score derived from population studies does not satisfy this requirement.

The RRASOR and similar actuarial instruments measure group-level recidivism rates and cannot, standing alone, establish that a particular individual's mental disorder creates a substantial probability that they will commit sexually violent acts. These instruments do not account for individual characteristics, treatment progress, or the specific nature of the mental disorder in the particular case.

#### 2. The State Must Prove the Causal Connection Between Mental Disorder and Dangerousness

Even accepting that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder, the State must prove that this disorder creates the substantial probability of future violence. The evidence presented by the State failed to establish this causal connection. Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied on actuarial data without adequately explaining how Lalor's specific mental disorders—pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder—create the particularized probability of future acts.

Anti-social personality disorder is characterized by a disregard for social norms and persistent criminal behavior, but it is not inherently linked to sexual violence. The State's experts failed to adequately explain how Lalor's specific combination of disorders creates a substantial probability specifically of sexually violent acts rather than general criminal conduct.

#### 3. The Defense Expert's Testimony Establishes Reasonable Doubt

The State incorrectly characterizes Dr. Maskel's testimony as merely methodological criticism that does not contradict the State's conclusions. This characterization ignores the substance of her testimony.

Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed fundamental problems with the actuarial instruments relied upon by the State's experts. Where the State's experts relied on these instruments to establish a "substantial probability" of reoffense, testimony establishing that these instruments are unreliable, unvalidated, or improperly applied directly undermines the State's case.

Specifically, Dr. Maskel testified regarding problems with the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG instruments. These problems include:

- Lack of validation studies for use with individual prediction
- Failure to account for treatment effects
- Overreliance on static factors that do not change over time
- Improper weighting of various risk factors
- Use of instruments developed for different populations

This testimony, when considered alongside the inherent limitations of actuarial prediction, creates reasonable doubt as to whether Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexual violence.

### D. The "Inability to Control Behavior" Is a Required Element

The State argues that the trial court was not required to make an express finding on "inability to control behavior" because this element is "inherently incorporated" into the "substantial probability" standard under *State v. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784*.

The defense respectfully submits that the State misreads *Laxton*. That case addressed whether the "inability to control behavior" language created a separate element, not whether it was eliminated entirely from the statutory definition. The statutory definition of "sexually violent person" explicitly includes this component, and the trial court must make a finding that the person's mental disorder renders them unable to control their behavior.

The constitutional requirements for involuntary commitment, as established in *Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346 (1997)*, require that the State prove the person is unable to control their behavior. This requirement cannot be satisfied by statistical predictions of future conduct alone.

Even under the State's interpretation of *Laxton*, the trial court must still find that the person is dangerous because their mental disorder impairs their capacity to control behavior. The evidence presented was insufficient to establish this element because the State's experts relied on actuarial instruments that measure statistical risk, not actual inability to control.

### E. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Commitment

When all evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to the State, the following remains established:

1. Lalor was convicted of first-degree sexual assault of a child in 1992.
2. Experts diagnosed him with pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder.
3. Actuarial instruments suggested elevated statistical risk of reoffense.
4. Defense expert testified that these instruments are unreliable for individual prediction.

The evidence failed to establish that Lalor's mental disorder specifically creates a substantial probability that he will commit sexually violent acts. The State's evidence amounted to statistical predictions based on group data, combined with diagnoses of mental disorders, without adequate proof of the causal connection between the disorders and future dangerousness.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF WISCONSIN, Plaintiff-Respondent,**
*v.*
**ROBERT LALOR, Defendant-Appellant.**

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of conviction and order of commitment finding Robert Lalor to be a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). Lalor argues that the trial court erred in admitting the results of a penile plethysmograph test and that the evidence was insufficient to support the commitment because the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that his mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts. Specifically, Lalor contends that the State’s reliance on actuarial data was improper because it constituted group-based statistics rather than individualized proof, and that the State failed to establish the requisite causal connection between his mental disorders and future dangerousness. After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, we affirm.

**I. Standard of Review**

Appellate review of the sufficiency of evidence in a Chapter 980 proceeding is de novo. *State v. Post, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995)*. We must examine the evidence in the light most favorable to the State to determine whether it was sufficient to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Poellinger, 153 Wis. 2d 493, 451 N.W.2d 752 (1990)*. The State bears the burden of proving that the defendant is a sexually violent person. *State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 464, 596 N.W.2d 497 (1999)*.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

Lalor argues that the State’s evidence was insufficient because it relied solely on actuarial instruments—specifically the RRASOR, MnSOST, and PCL-R—which he claims provide only group-based statistical probabilities rather than the individualized proof of future dangerousness required by *State v. Post*. He further argues that the defense expert, Dr. Maskel, created reasonable doubt by exposing the flaws in these instruments.

We are not persuaded. The record reflects that the State presented the testimony of two qualified experts, Dr. Hugh Moore and Dr. Sheila Fields. Dr. Moore diagnosed Lalor with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and utilized the RRASOR to determine a 73.1% chance of reoffending within ten years. Dr. Fields conducted a court-ordered evaluation, concluding that Lalor’s mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act. Her evaluation relied on multiple actuarial tools, including the PCL-R, RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG.

While the defense correctly notes that actuarial tools are derived from group data, the use of such instruments is permissible and recognized as reliable evidence in SVP proceedings. *State v. Richard, 179 Wis. 2d 311, 507 N.W.2d 171 (Ct. App. 1993)*. The experts did not merely present raw statistics; they applied these validated tools to Lalor’s specific history and diagnoses to form an individualized assessment. The defense expert, Dr. Maskel, limited her testimony to critiquing the methodology of the testing instruments. She did not offer an alternative opinion regarding Lalor’s risk of reoffense or contradict the conclusion that he suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of future sexual violence.

Under *State v. Post*, the trial court is entitled to weigh the credibility of the experts. Here, the trial court chose to credit the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over the methodological critiques offered by Dr. Maskel. Because the State presented uncontroverted expert testimony on the ultimate issue of future dangerousness, the evidence was sufficient to support the commitment.

**III. The "Inability to Control" Element**

Lalor argues that the trial court erred by failing to make an express finding that he is "unable to control" his behavior, a requirement he argues is mandated by *Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346 (1997)*. He contends that statistical predictions of risk cannot substitute for proof of a volitional impairment.

We reject this argument based on the controlling precedent of *State v. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784*. As the State correctly argues, and as held in *Laxton*, the "unable to control behavior" language is not a separate element that the State must prove independently. Rather, it is inherently incorporated into the statutory requirement that the person’s mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" that he will engage in acts of sexual violence. The trial court’s finding that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of reoffense satisfies the constitutional requirements outlined in *Kansas v. Hendricks*.

**IV. Admission of the Plethysmograph Test**

Finally, Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a penile plethysmograph test and that this error was not harmless. The defense characterizes this evidence as a "substantial portion" of the State's case.

The facts provided, however, do not support this characterization. The summary of Dr. Fields’ evaluation in the record indicates that she relied on the Department of Corrections records, the PCL-R, the RRASOR, the MnSOST, the MnSOST-R, the V-RAG, and consultations with other individuals. The plethysmograph is not listed among the instruments relied upon by Dr. Fields in the factual summary provided. Furthermore, the summary of the trial testimony indicates that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields testified generally to the actuarial data contained in their reports. Even assuming the test was admitted, the overwhelming nature of the other expert testimony regarding Lalor’s high RRASOR scores and diagnoses of pedophilia and psychopathy indicates that any error in admitting the plethysmograph would be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**V. Conclusion**

The State presented sufficient evidence to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The trial court properly applied the law, and the admission of the plethysmograph, if error, was harmless. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment of the trial court.

**By the Court.** The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

The State asks this Court to affirm a commitment that rests on actuarial instruments and diagnostic labels without adequate individualized proof that Lalor's specific mental disorders create a substantial probability of future sexual violence. ## II. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR'S MENTAL DISORDER CREATES A SUBSTANTIAL PROBABILITY OF FUTURE SEXUALLY VIOLENT ACTS

### A. The State's Reliance on Group-Based Actuarial Data Cannot Satisfy the Constitutional "Substantial Probability" Standard

The State correctly notes that *State v. Richard, 179 Wis. 2d 311, 507 N.W.2d 171 (Ct. App. 1993* permits the use of actuarial instruments in SVP proceedings. However, the State misreads Richard's holding. That case addressed the preliminary question of whether such instruments are *admissible*—not whether they are *sufficient* to prove the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. The distinction is critical.

The constitutional requirements for civil commitment demand individualized proof. In *Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346 (1997)*, the United States Supreme Court emphasized that commitment must be based on the individual's present mental condition and cannot rest on mere predictions of future dangerousness derived from group statistics. The Court specifically required proof that the individual has "serious difficulty in controlling behavior" which "tend[s] to show that the underlying mental disorder distorts the individual's perception of reality." *Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. at 360.*

The record here demonstrates that the State's case consisted primarily of statistical scores derived from instruments designed to predict recidivism among groups of offenders. Dr. Moore's RRASOR score indicated a "49.8% chance that he would reoffend in the next five years and a 73.1% chance that he would reoffend in the next ten years." These figures represent probability estimates derived from studies of *other* offenders—not individualized assessments of Lalor's specific volitional impairments.

The State argues that the experts "applied" these instruments to Lalor's "specific history and diagnoses." This argument conflates the application of a statistical tool with individualized proof of dangerousness. Applying a group-derived formula to an individual's background does not transform statistical probability into proof beyond a reasonable doubt that *this particular individual* will commit future acts of sexual violence. The experts did not testify about Lalor's specific behavioral patterns, his treatment progress, his actual functioning in the community, or any clinical indicators of his present ability to control his impulses. They simply applied actuarial instruments to his file and reported the resulting scores.

### B. The State Failed to Establish the Requisite Causal Connection

The State contends it established a causal connection between Lalor's diagnoses and future dangerousness. This argument fails because it assumes without proof that the diagnoses alone suffice.

First, the record contains no evidence establishing how pedophilia—a disorder characterized by sexual attraction to children—causes an inability to control behavior in any particular individual. While pedophilia may describe a sexual preference, the State presented no evidence that Lalor specifically lacks the ability to control his impulses. The diagnosis tells us what Lalor is attracted to; it does not establish that he cannot resist acting on those attractions.

Second, the State's assertion that "antisocial personality disorder—when co-occurring with pedophilia—is recognized as a risk factor for sexual violence" conflates correlation with causation. The State presented no evidence that Lalor's antisocial personality disorder specifically impairs his capacity for behavioral control with respect to sexual conduct. Antisocial personality disorder is characterized by a general disregard for social norms and impulsive behavior—but the State did not prove that this disorder causes Lalor to be unable to control his sexual impulses specifically, as opposed to merely being inclined to engage in general antisocial conduct.

The critical deficiency in the State's case is the absence of testimony connecting Lalor's specific mental disorders to his specific inability to control sexual behavior. The experts diagnosed Lalor and reported statistical scores. They did not explain with particularity how his mental disorders impair his volitional control in a manner that creates a substantial probability of future sexual violence.

## III. DR. MASKEL'S TESTIMONY CREATED REASONABLE DOUBT

The State argues that Dr. Maskel's testimony did not create reasonable doubt because she "did not offer an alternative opinion regarding Lalor's risk of reoffense." This argument reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the defense's burden and the function of expert testimony in SVP proceedings.

The State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person. The defense is not required to prove that Lalor is safe—only to raise reasonable doubt about the State's case. Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed fundamental problems with the instruments upon which the State relied:

1. The RRASOR, MnSOST, and PCL-R are actuarial tools derived from group data about recidivism rates among populations of offenders.

2. These instruments cannot account for individual variation, treatment progress, or changes in an offender's circumstances.

3. The instruments provide probabilistic estimates, not definitive predictions about any particular individual.

By exposing these limitations, Dr. Maskel's testimony demonstrated that the State's evidence—while perhaps creating a suspicion of dangerousness—failed to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor specifically poses a substantial probability of future sexual violence. The trial court was permitted to weigh credibility, as the State notes. However, the existence of competing testimony does not eliminate the reasonable doubt created by the defense's expert. When the defense expert effectively undermines the reliability of the State's primary evidence, the trier of fact must consider whether that evidence still satisfies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard.

The State's argument that Dr. Maskel "did not refute the core conclusion" misses the point. The defense need not refute the conclusion; it need only raise doubt about the State's proof. By showing that the State's actuarial tools cannot reliably predict Lalor's future behavior, Dr. Maskel created that doubt.

## IV. THE "INABILITY TO CONTROL" ELEMENT REQUIRES EXPLICIT FINDINGS

The State argues that *State v. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784* holds that "unable to control behavior" is not a separate element. The defense does not dispute this. However, the defense does contend that the trial court must make findings sufficient to establish that the person's mental disorder impairs their volitional capacity to the degree required by the Constitution.

In *Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. at 360*, the Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires proof "that the person has serious difficulty in controlling behavior." The trial court's commitment order found that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that his "mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." The State argues that the "substantial probability" standard "encompasses the necessary volitional component." This argument conflates two distinct concepts. **PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of conviction and order of commitment finding Robert Lalor to be a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). Lalor argues that the trial court erred in admitting the results of a penile plethysmograph test and that the evidence was insufficient to support the commitment because the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that his mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts. **I. Standard of Review**

Appellate review of the sufficiency of evidence in a Chapter 980 proceeding is de novo. *State v. Post, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995)*. We must examine the evidence in the light most favorable to the State to determine whether it was sufficient to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Poellinger, 153 Wis. 2d 493, 451 N.W.2d 752 (1990)*. The State bears the burden of proving that the defendant is a sexually violent person. *State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 464, 596 N.W.2d 497 (1999)*.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence and Actuarial Data**

Lalor argues that the State’s evidence was insufficient because it relied solely on actuarial instruments—specifically the RRASOR, MnSOST, and PCL-R—which he claims provide only group-based statistical probabilities rather than the individualized proof of future dangerousness required by *State v. Post*. The defense distinguishes *State v. Richard, 179 Wis. 2d 311, 507 N.W.2d 171 (Ct. App. 1993)*, arguing that while *Richard* permits the *admissibility* of actuarial tools, it does not establish that such tools are *sufficient* to prove the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

We are not persuaded. While the defense correctly notes the distinction between admissibility and sufficiency, the record reflects that the State presented the testimony of two qualified experts, Dr. Hugh Moore and Dr. Sheila Fields, who did more than merely present raw statistics. Dr. Moore diagnosed Lalor with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and utilized the RRASOR to determine a 73.1% chance of reoffending within ten years. Dr. Fields conducted a court-ordered evaluation, concluding that Lalor’s mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act. Her evaluation relied on multiple actuarial tools, including the PCL-R, RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG, applied to Lalor’s Department of Corrections records and clinical history.

The use of actuarial instruments is permissible and recognized as reliable evidence in SVP proceedings. *State v. Richard, 179 Wis. 2d 311, 507 N.W.2d 171*. The experts applied these validated tools to Lalor’s specific history and diagnoses to form an individualized assessment. The defense argument that the State failed to prove how Lalor’s specific disorders lead to an inability to control behavior attacks the weight of the evidence rather than its sufficiency. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784* does not eliminate the constitutional requirement, established in *Kansas v. The trial court’s finding that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of reoffense satisfies the constitutional requirements outlined in *Kansas v. Hendricks*. **OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of conviction and order of commitment finding Robert Lalor to be a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). Lalor argues that the trial court erred in admitting the results of a penile plethysmograph test and that the evidence was insufficient to support the commitment because the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that his mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts. Specifically, Lalor contends that the State’s reliance on actuarial data was improper because it constituted group-based statistics rather than individualized proof, and that the State failed to establish the requisite causal connection between his mental disorders and future dangerousness. After reviewing the record, the closing arguments of both parties, and the applicable law, we affirm.

**I. Standard of Review**

Appellate review of the sufficiency of evidence in a Chapter 980 proceeding is de novo. *State v. Post, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995)*. We must examine the evidence in the light most favorable to the State to determine whether it was sufficient to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Poellinger, 153 Wis. 2d 493, 451 N.W.2d 752 (1990)*. The State bears the burden of proving that the defendant is a sexually violent person. *State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 464, 596 N.W.2d 497 (1999)*.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence and Actuarial Data**

Lalor argues that the State’s evidence was insufficient because it relied solely on actuarial instruments—specifically the RRASOR, MnSOST, and PCL-R—which he claims provide only group-based statistical probabilities rather than the individualized proof of future dangerousness required by *State v. Post*. The defense distinguishes *State v. Richard, 179 Wis. 2d 311, 507 N.W.2d 171 (Ct. App. 1993)*, arguing that while *Richard* permits the *admissibility* of actuarial tools, it does not establish that such tools are *sufficient* to prove the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

We are not persuaded. While the defense correctly notes the distinction between admissibility and sufficiency, the record reflects that the State presented the testimony of two qualified experts, Dr. Hugh Moore and Dr. Sheila Fields, who did more than merely present raw statistics. Dr. Moore diagnosed Lalor with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and utilized the RRASOR to determine a 73.1% chance of reoffending within ten years. Dr. Fields conducted a court-ordered evaluation, concluding that Lalor’s mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act. Her evaluation relied on multiple actuarial tools, including the PCL-R, RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG, applied to Lalor’s Department of Corrections records and clinical history.

The use of actuarial instruments is permissible and recognized as reliable evidence in SVP proceedings. *State v. Richard, 179 Wis. 2d 311, 507 N.W.2d 171*. The experts applied these validated tools to Lalor’s specific history and diagnoses to form an individualized assessment. The defense argument that the State failed to prove how Lalor’s specific disorders lead to an inability to control behavior attacks the weight of the evidence rather than its sufficiency. The trial court, as the trier of fact, was entitled to accept the experts' linkage between the diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and the high risk of recidivism indicated by the actuarial scores.

**III. The "Inability to Control" Element**

Lalor argues that the trial court erred by failing to make an express finding that he is "unable to control" his behavior. He contends that *State v. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784* does not eliminate the constitutional requirement, established in *Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346 (1997)*, to prove "serious difficulty in controlling behavior." The defense argues that statistical predictions of risk cannot substitute for proof of a volitional impairment and that the State presented no evidence specifically addressing Lalor's present ability to control his behavior.

We reject this argument based on the controlling precedent of *State v. Laxton*. As held in *Laxton*, the "unable to control behavior" language is not a separate element that the State must prove independently. Rather, it is inherently incorporated into the statutory requirement that the person’s mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" that he will engage in acts of sexual violence. *State v. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784*. The trial court’s finding that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of reoffense satisfies the constitutional requirements outlined in *Kansas v. Hendricks*. The defense's request for a remand to make explicit findings on this element is therefore without merit.

**IV. Dr. Maskel's Testimony and Reasonable Doubt**

Lalor argues that Dr. Maskel’s testimony created reasonable doubt by exposing the flaws in the actuarial instruments. The defense contends that the State bears the burden of proof, and by undermining the reliability of the primary evidence (the actuarial tools), the defense necessarily created reasonable doubt regarding the "substantial probability" of future dangerousness.

We disagree. *State v. Post* establishes that appellate courts defer to the trial court’s credibility determinations regarding expert testimony unless a witness is incredible as a matter of law. *State v. Post, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115*. Dr. Maskel limited her testimony to critiquing the methodology of the testing instruments. She did not offer an alternative opinion regarding Lalor’s risk of reoffense or contradict the conclusion that he suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of future sexual violence. The trial court was free to credit the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over Dr. Maskel’s methodological critiques. Because the State presented uncontroverted expert testimony on the ultimate issue of future dangerousness, the evidence was sufficient to support the commitment.

**V. Admission of the Plethysmograph Test**

Finally, Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a penile plethysmograph test and that this error was not harmless. The defense characterizes this evidence as significant and argues that the State cannot dismiss its relevance simply because other evidence existed.

The facts provided, however, do not support the defense's characterization of the plethysmograph as a central pillar of the State's case. The summary of Dr. Fields’ evaluation in the record indicates that she relied on the Department of Corrections records, the PCL-R, the RRASOR, the MnSOST, the MnSOST-R, the V-RAG, and consultations with other individuals. The plethysmograph is not listed among the instruments relied upon by Dr. Fields in the factual summary provided. Furthermore, the summary of the trial testimony indicates that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields testified generally to the actuarial data contained in their reports. Even assuming the test was admitted, the overwhelming nature of the other expert testimony regarding Lalor’s high RRASOR scores and diagnoses of pedophilia and psychopathy indicates that any error in admitting the plethysmograph would be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**VI. Conclusion**

The State presented sufficient evidence to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The trial court properly applied the law, and the admission of the plethysmograph, if error, was harmless. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment of the trial court.
