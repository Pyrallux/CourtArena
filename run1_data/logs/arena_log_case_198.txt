MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:

The central issue before this Court is whether the Director correctly applied the *Flor* test to determine whether the aggravation of Mr. Van Ness’s asthma was proximately caused by his employment. The answer is unequivocally no. *Flor v. Holguin*, 113 Haw. 448, 153 P.3d 1049 (2007), explicitly limits its applicability to determining compensability for injuries by disease that "result from the nature of the employment"—a distinct and narrower standard than proximate causation. Here, the Director misapplied *Flor* to a proximate causation question, which is governed solely by the unitary test under Hawaii workers’ compensation law. This error requires reversal.

Under Hawaii law, a workers’ compensation claim must satisfy the unitary test: the injury must both "arise out of" and "in the course of" employment. *See* HRS § 386-2. The "arising out of" element requires that the employment be a proximate cause of the injury—a standard distinct from *Flor*’s "resulting from the nature of the employment" test. *Flor* itself clarifies that the latter test applies only when the disease is inherent to the job itself, such as when the occupation inherently exposes the worker to the hazard. But where the issue is proximate causation—as it is here—the unitary test controls, and the focus shifts to whether the employment was a substantial factor in bringing about the injury, regardless of whether the hazard exists elsewhere in the general environment.

In this case, the Director erred by requiring that vog exposure be unique to Lahainaluna High School to establish proximate causation. This is not the law. *Flor* does not mandate exclusivity; it only applies to a different legal question entirely. The Director’s conclusion—that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment"—is irrelevant under the unitary test. What matters is whether Mr. Van Ness’s employment subjected him to the specific exposure that aggravated his condition. The evidence is overwhelming: Dr. Mathison, a board-certified pulmonologist, diagnosed Mr. Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and explicitly stated that "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" caused "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during his work period on Maui. Dr. Mathison further noted that volcanic pollutants are a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" and that Mr. Van Ness’s symptoms "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu." Similarly, Dr. Tom documented that Mr. Van Ness’s symptoms "never completely went away" after December 2005 and identified "vog contributing" to his condition. These medical opinions directly tie the aggravation to employment-related exposure, not merely the general presence of vog on Maui.

The Director’s error is compounded by the fact that Mr. Van Ness’s work duties required him to be present in an environment where severe vog conditions were documented. Vice Principal Dennis confirmed "unusually severe vog-polluted days" at Lahainaluna, with "atmosphere [so] heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School." This testimony, coupled with the medical evidence, establishes that the employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation. The unitary test does not require that the hazard be exclusive to the workplace; it only requires that the employment was a substantial factor in causing the injury. *See, e.g., In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004) (holding that for proximate causation, "the employment need not be the sole cause, but must be a substantial factor"). Here, the employment was precisely that: Mr. Van Ness was exposed to vog during work hours at his assigned workplace, and that exposure directly triggered the exacerbation of his asthma.

By incorrectly applying *Flor* to a proximate causation question, the Director imposed a legal standard not required by Hawaii law. *Flor* is inapplicable here because the claim does not turn on whether vog is "inherent" to the nature of Mr. Van Ness’s job as a technology coordinator; it turns on whether his employment proximately caused the aggravation. The Director’s decision rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the law and must be reversed. For these reasons, the Court should find that the Director erred as a matter of law and remand for a determination under the proper unitary test.

Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:

The Defendant-Appellant Department of Education, State of Hawai'i (hereinafter "DOE" or "the Defense") respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the Director's decision denying workers' compensation benefits to Claimant-Appellee Ronald Van Ness (hereinafter "Van Ness" or "Claimant"). The Defense submits that the Director correctly applied the law and that substantial evidence supports the denial of benefits. This Court should affirm the Director's decision.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This workers' compensation appeal arises from the Director's denial of Van Ness's claim for benefits related to the alleged aggravation of his pre-existing asthma condition due to exposure to "vog" (volcanic smog) while employed as a technology coordinator at Lahainaluna High School on Maui. The Director found that although Van Ness's exposure to vog aggravated his pre-existing asthma, the vog was present throughout the entire island of Maui and was not unique to his place of employment. Accordingly, the Director concluded that Van Ness's injury did not "arise out of" his employment and denied benefits.

On appeal, Van Ness contends that the Director erroneously applied the *Flor v. Holguin* test and that the proper standard requires only that employment be a "substantial factor" in causing the injury. This contention misapprehends both the nature of Van Ness's claim and the applicable legal framework. The Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Director's decision.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The Defense accepts the facts as presented in the record and responds to the relevant factual assertions as follows:

### A. Van Ness's Pre-Existing Medical Condition

Van Ness has a long-standing history of "mild persistent asthma" dating back to at least 1989. *See* Record at 547I. During his employment as a teacher in Kona in 1989, Van Ness was exposed to vog and "had difficulty breathing and required courses of systemic corticosteroid for rescue along with regular controller medication." *Id.* In 1991, testing confirmed "allergic potentials to multiple inhalant allergens including dust mite and mold spores." *Id.* He underwent "several-year course of desensitizing immunotherapy" with "favorable outcome." *Id.*

Critically, from 1995 to 2001, Van Ness lived in Idaho, where he "had few symptoms of allergic-respiratory disease." *Id.* This demonstrates that Van Ness's respiratory condition was manageable when he was removed from environmental triggers present in Hawai'i.

### B. The Nature of Van Ness's Employment at Lahainaluna

Van Ness was employed as a technology coordinator at Lahainaluna High School from July 2005 to November 2006. *Id.* The school is located on the side of a mountain, with buildings "spread out and put up against the sides of the mountain in various locations." *Id.* Van Ness's office was in the library at the highest point of campus, and he was required to climb "fifty to sixty stairs at the base of the library, another eighty stairs to reach the next level of buildings, about 'three or four floors' down to the parking lot, and then another 'hike down' to the final section of buildings." *Id.*

Van Ness estimated he spent less than five percent of his work time in his air-conditioned office. *Id.* He worked "all over campus" in non-air conditioned environments with "louvered windows" and fans. *Id.* The record reflects that Van Ness's employment required significant physical exertion traveling between buildings on the mountainous campus.

### C. Vog Exposure on Maui

Van Ness testified that there was a significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006. *Id.* However, the record establishes that vog was a general environmental condition affecting the entire island of Maui, not merely Lahainaluna High School.

Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "we experienced some unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding that winter break." *Id.* She also stated that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived. *Id.* This is significant because Van Ness testified that while living on Maui he spent little time outside his home, stating he was "not much of an outdoors person" and "there really wasn't much to do there." *Id.* His home in Kihei was air-conditioned with HEPA filters. *Id.*

Van Ness did not have problems with volcanic ash inside his car or home. *Id.* He traveled to O'ahu on December 23, 2005, during a school holiday, and was seen by Dr. Tom for his respiratory condition. *Id.*

### D. Medical Evidence

Dr. Ajit S. Arora conducted an Independent Medical Examination on January 25, 2008. *Id.* Notably, Dr. Arora stated that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." *Id.* This opinion directly supports the Director's finding that vog exposure was not unique to the workplace.

Dr. David A. Mathison, who examined Van Ness on February 14, 2008, diagnosed him with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." *Id.* Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's symptoms improved when he relocated to O'ahu. *Id.* However, Dr. Mathison also acknowledged that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis" had "significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash." *Id.* This indicates Van Ness's susceptibility to vog was a pre-existing condition inherent to his physiology, not something created by his employment.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Applicable Legal Framework

Under Hawaii's Workers' Compensation Law, a claimant must establish that an injury "arises out of" and "in the course of" employment. *See* Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-2. These two requirements constitute the "unitary test." *See* *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 333, 104 P.3d 286 (2004).

The "arising out of" element requires a causal connection between the employment and the injury. *See* *Id.* This connection must be more than incidental; the employment must be a proximate cause of the injury. *See* *Id.* However, the employment need not be the sole cause—it must only be a "substantial factor" in bringing about the injury. *See* *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004).

### B. The *Flor v. Holguin* Framework

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 113 Haw. 448, 153 P.3d 1049 (2007), this Court addressed the compensability of diseases that "result from the nature of the employment." The *Flor* Court held that when a disease results from the nature of the employment, the claimant need only demonstrate that the employment was a "contributing cause" of the disease. *Id.* However, when the disease does not result from the nature of the employment, the claimant must demonstrate that the employment was the "proximate cause" of the disease. *Id.*

The distinction in *Flor* turns on whether the disease is inherent to the occupation itself. As this Court explained, the "nature of the employment" test applies when "the occupation itself, by its nature, predisposes the worker to the disease." *Id.* In such cases, the employment is considered a "physiological" cause of the disease. *Id.* In contrast, when the disease is not inherent to the occupation, the employment must be established as a "substantial factor" in causing the injury. *Id.*

### C. Application to This Case

#### 1. Van Ness's Claim Does Not "Result from the Nature of His Employment"

The nature of Van Ness's employment as a technology coordinator did not inherently expose him to vog. A technology coordinator at any school in Hawai'i would perform similar job functions—maintaining computers, troubleshooting technical issues, managing network systems. The job of a technology coordinator does not, by its nature, require exposure to vog or volcanic emissions.

Dr. Arora explicitly noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." *Record at 550.* This opinion supports the Director's finding that vog exposure was not inherent to Van Ness's employment. The *Flor* "nature of the employment" test therefore does not apply, and Van Ness was required to establish proximate causation under the unitary test.

#### 2. Van Ness Failed to Establish Proximate Causation

Even applying the unitary test, the Director correctly found that Van Ness failed to establish that his employment was a proximate cause of his injury. The evidence establishes that vog was present throughout the entire island of Maui and affected all residents, not merely those employed at Lahainaluna High School.

**a. Vog Was a General Environmental Condition**

Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that vog was present throughout Maui, noting that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness resided. *Record at 549.* Van Ness himself testified that he lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei and that his home was equipped with HEPA filters. *Id.* He testified that he spent little time outside his home because he was "not much of an outdoors person." *Id.* This demonstrates that Van Ness was exposed to vog both at work and at home.

**b. Van Ness's Exposure Was Not Increased by Employment**

The record does not establish that Van Ness's exposure to vog was greater at work than it would have been had he simply lived on Maui without employment. He lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted vog was "even worse" than at Lahainaluna. *Id.* His home was equipped with air conditioning and HEPA filters, which provided some protection. At work, he spent most of his time in non-air conditioned environments with open windows—a condition that existed regardless of vog levels.

Dr. Mathison's opinion that vog caused Van Ness's exacerbations does not establish that employment was a proximate cause. Dr. Mathison acknowledged that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis" made him susceptible to vog regardless of location. *Id.* The improvement in Van Ness's condition when he left Maui entirely demonstrates that his susceptibility was to the general environment, not specifically to his workplace.

**c. The Physical Exertion of Employment Was Not the Cause of Respiratory Issues**

While Van Ness testified about the physical demands of his job—climbing numerous stairs between campus buildings—this physical exertion is not alleged to have caused his respiratory injury. His claim is specifically that vog exposure aggravated his asthma. The physical demands of his job are relevant only to the extent they interacted with vog exposure, and the record does not establish that his respiratory symptoms were caused by physical exertion rather than vog exposure.

**d. Alternative Causation Strongly Suggests Non-Work-Related Injury**

The timeline of Van Ness's symptoms supports the Director's finding. Van Ness testified that his symptoms worsened during the period of severe vog from late October 2005 through April 2006. *Id.* However, he also testified that his symptoms improved when he traveled to Pennsylvania in March 2006 and when he was subsequently transferred to O'ahu in November 2006. *Id.* Dr. Mathison confirmed that since moving back to O'ahu, Van Ness "has been relatively free of respiratory symptoms." *Id.*

This pattern of improvement when away from Maui demonstrates that Van Ness's susceptibility was to the general Maui environment, not specifically to his employment at Lahainaluna. If his employment were a proximate cause, his symptoms would not have resolved simply by leaving Maui for Pennsylvania or by transferring to O'ahu.

### D. The Director's Decision Is Supported by Substantial Evidence

The Director's decision denying benefits is supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition." *Record at 551.* This finding is supported by Vice Principal Dennis's testimony that vog was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived, and by Dr. Arora's opinion that vog is not unique to schools.

The Director credited Dr. Arora's report and the employer's position. *Id.* While Van Ness points to Dr. Mathison's contrary opinion, the Director, as the finder of fact, was entitled to weigh the evidence and credit the opinions he found more persuasive. *See* *Tagupa v. Department of Education*, 103 Haw. 205, 61 P.3d 501 (2002) (holding that the Director has discretion to weigh evidence and determine credibility).

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The central issue on appeal is whether the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) erred in denying Claimant Ronald Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. Specifically, the Court must determine whether the Director correctly applied the legal standard for “arising out of” employment under Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-2 when concluding that Van Ness’s asthma aggravation was not work-related.

**I. Legal Standard**

Under Hawaii law, a claimant must prove that an injury both “arises out of” and is “in the course of” employment to be compensable. *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 333, 104 P.3d 286 (2004); Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-2. The “arising out of” element requires a causal connection between the employment and the injury, meaning the employment must be a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004).

The Supreme Court of Hawaii in *Flor v. Holguin*, 113 Haw. 448, 153 P.3d 1049 (2007), clarified that for diseases “resulting from the nature of employment,” the claimant need only show the employment was a contributing cause. However, where a disease does not result from the nature of the employment, the claimant must prove the employment was a proximate cause. *Id.* Both parties agree that Van Ness’s employment as a technology coordinator does not inherently involve exposure to volcanic smog (“vog”); thus, the “nature of employment” standard is inapplicable, and the proximate cause standard governs.

**II. Analysis**

The Director denied the claim, finding that “vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment” and that there was “insufficient evidence” that Van Ness’s exposure occurred “at this place of employment only.” The Director concluded that because the vog was an island-wide condition, the injury did not arise out of employment.

The Prosecution correctly argues that this reasoning constitutes legal error. Under the “substantial factor” test articulated in *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004), the employment need not be the sole cause of the injury, nor must the exposure be unique to the workplace. The question is whether the employment was a substantial factor in aggravating the condition.

The Director’s requirement that Van Ness prove exposure occurred “only” at the workplace imposes a higher burden than the law allows. The record indicates that Van Ness worked in a non-air conditioned environment requiring significant physical exertion (climbing stairs and hiking between buildings), which likely increased his susceptibility to the environmental vog compared to his home environment in Kihei, which was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters. Furthermore, medical evidence from Dr. Mathison supports the conclusion that the exposure at work caused a “serious flare of asthmatic symptoms.”

While the Defense argues that the vog was a general environmental condition and that exposure may have been worse at Van Ness’s home in Kihei, the Director’s decision was not based on a factual comparison of exposure levels between home and work. Rather, the Director relied on the legal premise that the presence of vog outside the workplace precludes compensability. This premise is inconsistent with *In re Estate of Kama*, which holds that an injury arises out of employment if the employment contributes to the risk of harm, even if that risk exists in the general environment.

**III. Conclusion**

The Director misapplied the law by requiring Van Ness to demonstrate that his exposure to vog was unique to his employment. The correct inquiry is whether the employment conditions were a substantial factor in causing the injury. Because the Director applied an incorrect legal standard—effectively requiring exclusivity rather than a substantial causal connection—the decision cannot stand.

**THEREFORE, IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s claim is vacated and remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must determine whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his asthma, without requiring proof that the vog exposure was unique to the workplace.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Ronald Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution (Van Ness) and the Defense (Department of Education). The central issue remains whether the Director erred as a matter of law in concluding that Van Ness’s injury did not arise out of his employment.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

To establish compensability, a claimant must demonstrate that an injury both arose out of and in the course of employment. *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 333, 104 P.3d 286 (2004); Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-2. The "arising out of" element requires a causal connection between the employment and the injury. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004).

As established in *Flor v. Holguin*, 113 Haw. 448, 153 P.3d 1049 (2007), when a disease does not result from the nature of the employment, the claimant must prove that the employment was a proximate cause of the injury. Under the "substantial factor" test derived from *Kama*, the employment need not be the sole cause of the injury, nor must the risk be unique to the workplace. Rather, the inquiry is whether the employment was a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. at 122.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" that exposure occurred "at this place of employment only." The Director concluded that because the vog was an island-wide condition, the injury did not arise out of employment.

**A. The Director Committed Legal Error by Applying an "Exclusivity" Standard**

The Defense argues that the Director’s finding was based on insufficient evidence rather than legal error, asserting that the "substantial factor" test requires employment to contribute to the risk of harm in a way distinct from the general environment. While the Defense correctly states the legal principle, the Director’s decision failed to apply it.

The Director explicitly stated: "The Director therefore did not find a nexus between the claimant’s employment and his injury of 12/23/2005" because "vog was present on the entire island of Maui." This reasoning is legally flawed under *In re Estate of Kama*. The Supreme Court of Hawaii has explicitly rejected the notion that a general environmental hazard precludes a finding of compensability. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. at 122. The law does not require that the hazard be present "only" at the workplace; it requires that the employment be a substantial factor in aggravating the condition.

The Defense’s attempt to characterize the Director’s "only" language as a mere factual finding is unpersuasive. The Director’s conclusion that no nexus existed was predicated on the legal premise that island-wide vog cannot be work-related. This premise misapplies the "substantial factor" test. The Prosecution correctly argues that the Director effectively created a new legal standard requiring exclusivity, which is inconsistent with Hawaii law.

**B. The Director Failed to Consider Whether Employment Conditions Increased the Risk**

The proper inquiry under the "substantial factor" test is whether the specific conditions of Van Ness’s employment increased his risk of harm compared to the general environment. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. at 122. The Director failed to conduct this analysis.

The Prosecution presents evidence that Van Ness’s workplace conditions differed significantly from his home environment. At work, Van Ness was required to perform significant physical exertion, climbing stairs and hiking between buildings in non-air conditioned environments with louvered windows and fans. In contrast, his home in Kihei was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters. Dr. Mathison’s report supports the assertion that Van Ness’s susceptibility to volcanic pollutants was exacerbated by his work environment, recommending that his employer avoid placing him in areas with such exposure.

The Director did not weigh these distinctions. Instead, the Director relied on Dr. Arora’s observation that vog is "not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" and the fact that vog was present island-wide. This reliance was misplaced. As noted in *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. at 338, the focus must be on the causal connection between the employment and the injury, not the uniqueness of the hazard.

The Defense argues that the evidence shows Van Ness’s symptoms improved when he left Maui entirely, suggesting the general environment, not the employment, was the cause. While this is a relevant factual argument, it does not cure the Director’s legal error. The Director did not evaluate whether the *combination* of vog exposure and the physical demands of the job (stairs, exertion, lack of filtration) constituted a substantial factor. The Director’s analysis stopped at the presence of vog.

**C. Remand is Necessary for a Proper Application of the Law**

The Defense contends that the evidence is insufficient to establish that employment was a substantial factor. However, because the Director applied the wrong legal standard—requiring proof that exposure occurred "only" at work—the factual findings are tainted. The Director did not make findings regarding whether the specific workplace conditions (physical exertion, lack of air conditioning, dust) exacerbated Van Ness’s condition in a way that his home environment did not.

On remand, the Director must consider whether the employment conditions were a substantial factor in aggravating Van Ness’s asthma. This includes weighing the evidence presented by the Prosecution regarding the lack of air filtration and physical exertion at work against the Defense’s arguments regarding the general prevalence of vog and the lack of requested accommodations.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director erred as a matter of law by requiring Van Ness to prove that his exposure to vog was unique to his workplace. The correct inquiry is whether the employment conditions were a substantial factor in causing the injury. Because the Director applied an incorrect legal standard, the decision cannot stand.

**THEREFORE, IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s claim is vacated and remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must determine whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his asthma, applying the "substantial factor" test without requiring proof that the vog exposure was unique to the workplace.

## MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:

The Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the Prosecution's arguments and in defense of the Department of Education's position before the Director. ## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a circumstance where a public employee with a pre-existing asthmatic condition experienced exacerbation of that condition due to an environmental hazard—volcanic smog, or "vog"—that was present across the entire island of Maui. The Defense does not dispute that Mr. Van Ness experienced respiratory difficulties while working at Lahainaluna High School. Nor does the Defense dispute that vog was present on Maui during the relevant time period. What the Defense does dispute is the legal conclusion that these facts are sufficient to establish compensability under Hawaii's workers' compensation framework.

The Court's ruling correctly identified that the Director applied an "exclusivity" standard that is inconsistent with *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004). However, the Defense submits that the proper remedy is not to simply reverse and remand, but rather to direct the Director to apply the correct legal standard while also considering the full body of evidence that supports the denial of benefits.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Substantial Factor Test Under Hawaii Law

The Defense agrees with the Court that the "substantial factor" test, as articulated in *In re Estate of Kama*, is the proper standard for analyzing whether Van Ness's injury arose out of his employment. Under *Kama*, the employment must be a substantial factor in bringing about the injury; it need not be the sole cause, nor must the risk be unique to the workplace. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. at 122.

However, the Defense emphasizes that the "substantial factor" test requires more than mere presence of a hazard at the workplace. The claimant must demonstrate that the employment contributed to the injury in some manner distinct from the general environment. As this Court recognized in *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 333, 104 P.3d 286 (2004), there must be a causal connection between the employment and the injury.

The Prosecution has conflated the "substantial factor" test with a mere "but-for" analysis. Under the Prosecution's logic, because Van Ness was exposed to vog at work and subsequently experienced symptoms, his employment must have been a substantial factor. This misreads *Kama*. The substantial factor test requires that the employment *increase the risk* of harm or *contribute* to the injury in a manner beyond what would exist in the general environment.

### B. The Defense Does Not Argue for an Exclusivity Standard

The Prosecution repeatedly characterizes the Defense as advocating for an "exclusivity" standard that has been rejected by Hawaii law. This characterization is inaccurate. The Defense does not argue that Van Ness must prove vog was present only at Lahainaluna High School. Rather, the Defense argues that Van Ness must prove that his employment *exacerbated* his condition beyond what would have occurred from general island-wide exposure.

This distinction is critical. Under *Kama*, a claimant may recover even when the hazard exists in the general environment, provided the employment was a substantial factor. But the claimant must still prove this element. The Director found that Van Ness failed to meet this burden, and that finding should be reviewed for evidentiary sufficiency on remand.

### C. Application of *Flor v. Holguin*

The Prosecution correctly notes that under *Flor v. Holguin*, 113 Haw. 448, 153 P.3d 1049 (2007), when a disease does not result from the nature of employment, the claimant must prove that employment was a proximate cause of the injury. The Defense agrees with this standard. Asthma is not a disease that results from the nature of Van Ness's employment as a technology coordinator; therefore, Van Ness must prove that his employment was a proximate cause of his injury.

The *Flor* standard requires proof of proximate cause, which in this context means the "substantial factor" test from *Kama*. The Prosecution has the burden of establishing this element, and the Defense retains the right to challenge the sufficiency of that evidence.

This suggests that despite his home protections, Van Ness was exposed to significant vog at his residence.

At the workplace, Van Ness's office was air-conditioned. The Defense notes that Van Ness never requested any workplace accommodations related to his asthma prior to his claim.

The Prosecution argues that the burden is on the employer to establish that the injury did not arise out of employment, citing *Ching v. Department of Education*. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Prosecution (Claimant Ronald Van Ness) and the Defense (Department of Education). The Court has previously determined that the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) committed legal error by applying an incorrect "exclusivity" standard to Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The parties have now presented their final arguments regarding whether the evidence compels a finding of compensability or whether the case should be remanded for further proceedings.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The standard for compensability in Hawaii requires that a claimant demonstrate an injury both arose out of and in the course of employment. *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 333, 104 P.3d 286 (2004). When a disease does not result from the nature of the employment, the claimant must prove that the employment was a proximate cause of the injury. *Flor v. Holguin*, 113 Haw. 448, 153 P.3d 1049 (2007). Under the "substantial factor" test, the employment need not be the sole cause of the injury, nor must the risk be unique to the workplace. Rather, the inquiry is whether the employment was a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. 114, 94 P.3d 608 (2004). The Supreme Court of Hawaii has explicitly held that "the fact that a hazard is present in the general environment does not preclude a finding that the employment was a substantial factor in causing the injury." *In re Estate of Kama*, 105 Haw. at 122.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Application of the Wrong Legal Standard Requires Remand**

The Defense concedes, and the Court has previously ruled, that the Director erred as a matter of law by requiring Van Ness to prove that his exposure to vog occurred "only" at his place of employment. The Director’s finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui" was used to negate the existence of a nexus between the employment and the injury. This reasoning is directly contrary to the "substantial factor" test articulated in *In re Estate of Kama*. Because the Director’s denial was predicated on this legal error, the factual findings supporting the denial are tainted and cannot stand.

**B. The Evidence Presents Genuine Issues of Material Fact Precluding an Award of Benefits at This Time**

The Prosecution argues that the evidence compels a finding of compensability, emphasizing the specific conditions of Van Ness’s workplace. The Prosecution correctly notes that Van Ness’s home was equipped with HEPA filters and air conditioning, whereas his workplace required him to perform physical exertion—climbing stairs and hiking between buildings—in non-air conditioned environments with louvered windows and circulating fans. The Prosecution relies on Dr. Mathison’s report, which recommends that the employer avoid placing Van Ness in areas with volcanic exposure, to argue that the employment conditions were a substantial factor in aggravating his asthma.

However, the Defense presents conflicting evidence that the Director must weigh under the correct legal standard. The Defense points to testimony that vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. The Defense also notes that Dr. Arora, the independent medical examiner, observed that vog is "not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." Furthermore, the Defense argues that Van Ness’s symptoms improved significantly upon leaving Maui entirely, suggesting that the general environment, rather than specific employment conditions, was the primary cause of the exacerbation.

These conflicting interpretations of the medical evidence and the comparative risk levels of the home versus work environments present genuine issues of material fact. It is the role of the Director, as the trier of fact, to assess the credibility of the witnesses and the weight of the medical evidence. *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. at 338. The Court cannot substitute its judgment for that of the Director regarding the factual determination of whether the employment was a substantial factor. The Director must make this determination based on the correct legal standard, considering whether the specific demands of the job—physical exertion in a non-filtered environment—increased the risk of harm beyond that of the general environment.

**C. The Burden of Proof**

The Prosecution argues that the burden is on the employer to establish that the injury did not arise out of employment. While the employer bears the burden of proof for certain defenses, the claimant retains the burden of establishing a prima facie case that the injury arose out of and in the course of employment. *Ching v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. at 338. The Director found that Van Ness failed to meet this burden because the exposure was not unique to the workplace. On remand, the Director must evaluate whether Van Ness has met his burden under the "substantial factor" test, considering the totality of the circumstances, including the physical requirements of the job and the environmental conditions at the workplace.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director committed legal error by applying an "exclusivity" standard rather than the "substantial factor" test required by *In re Estate of Kama*. While the Prosecution presents evidence suggesting the workplace conditions exacerbated Van Ness’s condition, the Defense presents evidence regarding the island-wide nature of the hazard and the lack of specific medical causation linking the injury solely to employment. Because the Director did not have the opportunity to weigh this evidence under the correct legal framework, the Court cannot find that the evidence compels a finding of compensability as a matter of law at this stage.

**THEREFORE, IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s claim is vacated. The case is remanded to the Director for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must apply the "substantial factor" test to determine whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his asthma, without requiring proof that the vog exposure was unique to the workplace. The Director shall consider all relevant evidence, including the physical demands of the job, the environmental conditions at the workplace compared to the home, and the medical opinions of Dr. Mathison and Dr. Arora.
