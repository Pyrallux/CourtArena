# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

Appellant respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the trial court's denial of his motions, though with significant objections to certain evidentiary rulings. The prosecution's case rests upon the validity of the search that yielded the cocaine and the denial of Appellant's constitutional right to discover the confidential informant's identity. As set forth below, the trial court erred in denying Appellant's motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity, as such disclosure was essential to the resolution of the motion to suppress and to Appellant's constitutional right to present a complete defense. Accordingly, this Court should reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose and remand for further proceedings.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts, drawn from the record, establish the following:

On the date in question, Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine). Appellant filed a motion to suppress the evidence on the basis that it was illegally obtained. He also filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The motion to disclose rested on two grounds: (1) that the informant's identity was needed to show that the officers who conducted the arrest and search did not have probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant, and (2) that the informant's identity was necessary because the informant was a material witness to the Appellant's guilt or innocence and upon the possible defense of entrapment.

At the hearing on the motion to suppress, defense counsel raised the issue of the confidential informant's identity, arguing it went to the reliability of the informant and thus to the probable cause determination. Defense counsel represented that new information had been received regarding the confidential informant. Defense counsel specifically argued that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day in question, that the informant's belongings were found in Appellant's car when it was released from evidence, and that this evidence was critical to Appellant's defense, particularly the defense of entrapment. Defense counsel cited *Bodin v. State* for the proposition that disclosure was required when the informant's testimony may be necessary to a fair determination of guilt.

The trial judge ultimately denied both the motion to suppress and the motion for disclosure of the confidential informant. Appellant then pled guilty pursuant to the plea agreement and was sentenced to sixteen years in prison.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Erred in Denying the Motion to Disclose the Confidential Informant's Identity

The prosecution bears the burden of demonstrating that the confidential informant's identity should remain confidential. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957). The Supreme Court in *Roviaro* established that the scope of the privilege depends upon the particular facts of each case, and that when the informant's testimony is relevant to a material issue in the case, the privilege must give way. *Id.* at 60-61. The Court held that the trial court should balance the public interest in protecting the flow of information against the individual's right to prepare his defense. *Id.*

Here, the informant's identity was directly relevant to the motion to suppress. The probable cause determination depended upon the reliability and credibility of the confidential informant. As defense counsel argued below, the informant's reliability was essential to the probable cause analysis. If the informant was unreliable, biased, or had set up the Appellant, the probable cause supporting the search and arrest would be undermined.

The prosecution argued below that the motion to suppress was "dispositional" and that disclosure of the informant's identity would be a matter for trial. This argument is fundamentally flawed. The very purpose of the motion to suppress was to challenge the existence of probable cause. Probable cause cannot be properly assessed without understanding the informant's reliability. As this Court has recognized, the identity of a confidential informant may be required when it is essential to a fair determination of a material issue. *Bodin v. State*, 767 S.W.2d 885 (Tex. App.—Beaumont 1989, no pet.).

In *Bodin*, the court held that disclosure was required where the informant was a participant in the offense and where the informant's testimony could affect the determination of guilt or innocence. Similarly, here, the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day in question, and her belongings were found in his car—evidence directly relevant to both the probable cause determination and the defense of entrapment.

The prosecution's reliance on the "dispositional" nature of the motion to suppress does not diminish Appellant's constitutional right to challenge the legality of the search. A defendant does not forfeit his right to challenge the reliability of an informant simply because he has agreed to have the suppression motion decided in a dispositive manner. The agreement to make the motion dispositive concerned the consequences of the ruling, not the scope of evidence the Court could consider.

### B. The Informant Was a Material Witness to Appellant's Defense of Entrapment

The facts presented below established that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day in question and that her belongings were found in his car. This evidence is critical to the defense of entrapment. Entrapment requires proof that the defendant was induced by the government to commit the crime. If the confidential informant induced Appellant to possess the cocaine, this would constitute a complete defense.

As the Supreme Court has recognized, the defense of entrapment requires that the defendant be permitted to present evidence showing government involvement in the creation of the crime. *Sherman v. United States*, 356 U.S. 369 (1958). Here, Appellant cannot present this defense without knowing the informant's identity. The trial court's refusal to disclose the informant's identity effectively precluded Appellant from presenting the defense of entrapment.

In *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), the Supreme Court held that a state rule that excludes evidence of third-party guilt when the prosecution has strong forensic evidence violates the defendant's constitutional right to present a complete defense. While *Holmes* addressed third-party guilt evidence, its reasoning applies with equal force here. Appellant has a constitutional right to present evidence that someone else—here, the confidential informant—may be responsible for his alleged conduct. The trial court's refusal to disclose the informant's identity violated this right.

### C. The Trial Court's Denial of the Motion to Suppress Was Error

Although the facts provided do not detail the circumstances of the search and arrest, the denial of the motion to suppress cannot be properly evaluated without disclosure of the informant's identity. The probable cause determination rests upon the informant's reliability, which cannot be assessed without knowing who the informant is and what her motivations might be.

As the Supreme Court held in *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108 (1964), and *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410 (1969), probable cause based upon an informant's tip requires showing both the informant's basis of knowledge and the informant's reliability. Without disclosure of the informant's identity, Appellant cannot challenge either prong of this analysis.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution likely argues that the trial court properly exercised its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. However, the trial court's discretion is limited by constitutional requirements. When a defendant makes a preliminary showing that an informant may be able to provide evidence relevant to a defense, disclosure is required. *Pennsylvania v. Ritchie*, 480 U.S. 39 (1987).

The prosecution may also argue that the Appellant waived his right to disclosure by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress. This argument fails because the agreement concerned the consequence of the ruling, not the evidence that could be considered. The Appellant did not agree to waive his right to challenge the reliability of the informant; he only agreed that if the motion to suppress was denied, he would plead guilty.

Finally, the prosecution may argue that the informant's identity should be protected to encourage future cooperation. However, this consideration cannot override the Appellant's constitutional right to present a complete defense. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60. When the informant's testimony is relevant to a material issue, the privilege must yield.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity and remand for further proceedings. Disclosure is required because the informant's identity is essential to the probable cause determination and to the Appellant's defense of entrapment. The trial court's refusal to disclose violated the Appellant's constitutional rights under the Sixth Amendment to present a complete defense.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s denial of Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). Appellant argues that the trial court erred in refusing to order disclosure because the informant’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the informant for purposes of the motion to suppress and to establish a defense of entrapment. After reviewing the defense’s opening arguments and the factual background provided, the Court finds that Appellant has raised substantial legal questions regarding the trial court’s ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard for Disclosure of a Confidential Informant**

The privilege to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is not absolute. The Supreme Court has held that "the fundamental privilege of the government to withhold the identity of an informant must be balanced against the defendant's right to prepare his defense." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957). Where the disclosure of an informant's identity, or the contents of their communication, "is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause, the privilege must give way." *Id.* at 60-61.

**B. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Motion to Suppress**

The prosecution argued at the hearing, and likely maintains on appeal, that the motion to suppress was "dispositive," meaning that the only issue before the court was the existence of probable cause. The prosecution contended that issues of guilt or innocence, such as entrapment, were irrelevant to the proceeding because a ruling in favor of the State would result in a guilty plea.

While the Court acknowledges the procedural context of a dispositive motion, this agreement does not strip the defendant of the right to challenge the foundational elements of the State's probable cause. The defense correctly argues that the reliability of the informant is a cornerstone of the probable cause analysis. Under the test established in *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108 (1964), and *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410 (1969), a magistrate must be informed of the underlying circumstances from which the informant concluded the crime was being committed and the underlying circumstances from which the police concluded the informant was credible.

If the informant was a participant in the offense or had a motive to fabricate the tip, this directly impacts the "veracity" or "basis of knowledge" prongs of the probable cause test. Therefore, the defense’s assertion that the informant’s reliability is "important even on a Motion to Suppress" is legally sound. The "dispositive" nature of the hearing limits the *consequences* of the ruling, but it does not limit the *scope of evidence* relevant to determining probable cause.

**C. The Informant as a Material Witness and Participant**

The defense argues that the informant was a material witness whose testimony is necessary to a fair determination of the case. The record indicates that defense counsel represented to the court that the CI was with Appellant on the day in question and that the CI's belongings were found in Appellant's car. This factual assertion, if true, aligns with the precedent in *Bodin v. State*, 767 S.W.2d 885 (Tex. App.—Beaumont 1989, no pet.), where disclosure was required because the informant was a participant in the offense.

When an informant is more than a mere tipster and is an active participant in the events leading to the arrest, their identity becomes highly relevant to the issue of probable cause. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957). The defense’s claim that the informant may have "set him up" goes to the heart of the informant's credibility and bias. If the informant planted the evidence or induced the possession, the tip lacks the reliability required to establish probable cause.

**D. The Right to Present a Defense**

Furthermore, the defense cites *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), arguing that the trial court’s ruling violated Appellant’s constitutional right to present a complete defense. While *Holmes* specifically addressed the exclusion of third-party guilt evidence, its underlying principle—that a defendant may not be precluded from presenting relevant evidence that supports his defense—is applicable here. The defense asserts that the identity of the informant is critical to the defense of entrapment. Although entrapment is typically a trial defense, the facts suggesting the informant "set up" the defendant are inextricably linked to the informant's reliability for the suppression hearing. By denying disclosure, the trial court effectively prevented the Appellant from testing the informant's credibility, which is essential to the "fair determination" of the motion to suppress. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957).

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the defense’s opening arguments and the provided facts, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity appears to be in error. The defense has demonstrated a "particularized need" for the information to challenge the reliability of the informant, which is a material issue in the probable cause determination. The "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress does not override the defendant's constitutional rights to challenge the validity of the search and seizure.

Accordingly, the Court is inclined to reverse the trial court's ruling denying the motion to disclose and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

Moreover, the prosecution's reading of *Lewis* would create a perverse incentive: the more involved an informant is in the crime, the less likely disclosure would be required. This cannot be the law. *Roviaro* specifically held that disclosure is required when the informant "may be a participant in the crime charged." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 64.

**C. The Prosecution's Efficiency Arguments Lack Merit**

The prosecution argues that requiring disclosure at suppression hearings would undermine judicial efficiency. This argument is without merit.

First, the defense is not seeking a full trial on the merits. Rather, the defense is seeking an in-camera hearing or some other mechanism to determine whether the informant's identity is relevant to the probable cause determination. This is a limited inquiry that does not require a full evidentiary hearing.

Second, the prosecution's efficiency argument proves too much. The same argument could be made against any suppression hearing—that requiring the State to prove probable cause is "inefficient." But the Fourth Amendment does not yield to considerations of convenience.

Third, the prosecution's argument ignores the cost of getting it wrong. If the search was unlawful and the evidence is suppressed, the defendant goes free. If the search was lawful and the evidence is admitted, the defendant may spend years in prison. The stakes are high, and the Constitution requires a fair determination of the probable cause question.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity.

The prosecution's arguments, while superficially appealing, fail to address the fundamental constitutional issue: the defendant has a right to challenge the reliability of the sole source of the probable cause. The "dispositive" label does not eliminate this right. The informant's status as a potential participant triggers the disclosure requirement of *Roviro*. And the defendant's right to present a defense encompasses the right to challenge the validity of the search.

The defense has made a sufficient showing that the informant may have been a participant in the offense. The informant was with the appellant on the day in question. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant argues that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause supporting the search and to present a defense of entrapment or "set-up." The State argues that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress limited the inquiry to the legality of the search, precluding the need for disclosure to litigate issues of guilt or innocence.

After a thorough review of the record, the arguments presented, and the applicable legal standards, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose. The "dispositive" label of the hearing did not waive the Appellant’s constitutional right to challenge the reliability of the informant, particularly where the defense established a prima facie case that the informant was a participant in the offense.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Dispositive" Agreement Dictates Consequence, Not Standard of Proof**

The Prosecution argues persuasively that the parties agreed to a "dispositive" motion to suppress, meaning the outcome of the probable cause determination would resolve the case without a trial. The State contends this agreement limited the scope of the hearing to the Fourth Amendment question, excluding "set-up" evidence that pertains to guilt or innocence. The State relies on *United States v. Rios*, 611 F.2d 1335, 1339 (10th Cir. 1979), for the proposition that a suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits.

While the Court agrees that a suppression hearing is distinct from a trial, the State’s argument conflates the *consequence* of the hearing with the *standard of proof* required to reach that consequence. The agreement that a ruling for the State would result in a guilty plea underscores the gravity of the probable cause determination; it does not lower the burden of proof or insulate the State’s evidence from scrutiny.

To determine if the search was lawful, the court must assess whether the police had probable cause. If the informant "set up" the Appellant—meaning the informant fabricated the crime or induced the Appellant to possess the controlled substance—then the informant’s tip lacks the reliability necessary to establish probable cause. Therefore, evidence of a "set-up" is not solely a matter of guilt or innocence; it is directly relevant to the *veracity* of the informant, which is a foundational element of the probable cause analysis under *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108 (1964), and *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410 (1969). The defense cannot be precluded from challenging the veracity of the tip simply because the parties agreed to a streamlined procedure.

**B. The Participant Exception to Informant Privilege**

The most significant error in the trial court’s ruling was its failure to recognize the application of the participant exception to the informant’s privilege. The Defense represented to the court that the CI was with the Appellant on the day of the arrest and that the CI’s belongings were found in the Appellant’s car. These facts distinguish this case from those involving a detached citizen informant.

In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), the Supreme Court held that "the government’s privilege to withhold the identity of an informant must give way to the right of a defendant to prepare his defense where the informant is a material witness on the issue of guilt or innocence, or where the informant might be a participant in the offense charged." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 64-65. The State relies on *United States v. Lewis*, 406 F.2d 540 (2d Cir. 1969), to argue that *Roviaro* does not apply to suppression hearings. However, *Lewis* involved a situation where the defendant failed to show the informant could provide helpful evidence regarding the legality of the search. *Lewis*, 406 F.2d at 544.

Here, the defense has made a specific showing that the informant was a participant. An informant who is present during the alleged crime and whose personal effects are found at the scene is more than a mere tipster; they are a potential actor in the events. If the informant participated in the offense, their motive to provide information may be to shift blame or eliminate a rival, rather than to assist law enforcement. This potential bias goes directly to the informant’s credibility and the reliability of the tip. The trial court’s reliance on the informant’s "past reliability" was insufficient to address this specific allegation of participation in the instant offense. As noted in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), while past reliability is a factor in the totality of the circumstances, it does not automatically cure a specific showing of bias or fabrication in the case at bar.

**C. The Right to a Meaningful Opportunity to Be Heard**

The Defense argues that the denial of the motion to disclose violated the Appellant’s right to present a defense. While *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), primarily addresses the exclusion of evidence at trial, its underlying principle—that a defendant has a right to present relevant evidence—is applicable to the suppression context insofar as it protects the integrity of the probable cause determination.

By denying the motion to disclose without an *in-camera* review or a hearing on the specific allegations of participation, the trial court effectively prevented the Appellant from testing the sole basis for the probable cause. The State’s assertion that the informant was reliable based on past tips did not account for the defense's specific allegations that the informant "set him up" on this occasion. The Appellant was entitled to an opportunity to demonstrate that the informant’s identity was necessary to show that the tip was fabricated or the result of entrapment, which would negate probable cause.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution’s arguments regarding procedural efficiency and the scope of the "dispositive" agreement are not without merit; however, they cannot override the Appellant’s constitutional rights. The defense presented specific facts suggesting the informant was a participant in the offense, triggering the disclosure requirements of *Roviaro*. The trial court abused its discretion by denying the motion to disclose based solely on the informant’s general history of reliability, without considering the defense's specific allegations of bias and participation.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion, including an *in-camera* hearing to determine if the informant’s identity must be disclosed to allow the Appellant to challenge the probable cause for the search and arrest.

**FINAL JUDGMENT**

The motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant is granted on remand. The trial court is directed to conduct an *in-camera* review of the informant's identity and determine if disclosure is necessary for the defense to challenge the probable cause and the informant's reliability. The judgment of the trial court is otherwise vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings.
