**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLANT SECRETARY RUMSFELD**  

Your Honor, today this Court confronts a fundamental error by the district court—one that threatens the delicate balance of separation of powers and invites judicial overreach into the heart of military operations. The district court erred in two critical respects: first, by improperly extending *Bivens* to a context of military detention in a foreign warzone, where special factors counsel hesitation; and second, by denying qualified immunity for substantive due process claims that were not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. We respectfully submit that this Court must reverse.  

### I. The District Court Committed Reversible Error by Recognizing a *Bivens* Remedy in This Novel Context  

*The Supreme Court has consistently held that *Bivens* claims are disfavored in new contexts, particularly where national security and military operations are involved.*  

* *Bivens* v. *Six Unknown Named Agents of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), established a narrow exception to sovereign immunity for constitutional violations by federal officials. But this exception is "a limited one," and the Court has repeatedly cautioned against extending it to new contexts where "special factors counsel hesitation." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). In *Ziglar*, the Court explicitly refused to extend *Bivens* to post-9/11 detention policies, emphasizing that "when the government is acting in the realm of national security, and in the context of military operations, courts should be hesitant to create new causes of action." *Id.* at 1859.  

This case presents precisely that scenario. Doe was detained by military authorities in Iraq during active hostilities—a foreign warzone where the United States was engaged in a combat operation against al Qaeda and other hostile forces. The facts alleged, though accepted as true for purposes of this motion, center on military interrogation and detention protocols conducted under the command of the Marine Corps and the Department of Defense. As *Ziglar* made clear, "the military context" is "a paradigmatic example" of a setting where "special factors" demand judicial restraint. *Id.* at 1858. The Court in *Ziglar* reasoned that "Congress is in the best position to weigh the costs and benefits of creating a damages remedy" in such sensitive areas, and that "judicial intervention in military affairs" would "undermine the chain of command" and "interfere with the military’s ability to conduct operations." *Id.* at 1859.  

The district court’s extension of *Bivens* to this context ignores these bedrock principles. By recognizing a cause of action for substantive due process violations arising from military detention in a warzone, the court disregarded the Supreme Court’s directive that *Bivens* "should not be extended to new contexts where there are special factors counseling hesitation." *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 744 (2020). Here, the "special factors" are overwhelming: the conduct occurred in a combat zone during an active war, involved military personnel and intelligence operations, and implicated national security decisions made by the Executive Branch. As the Court held in *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007), "the existence of alternative remedies or the absence of a clear legislative mandate to create a damages remedy" is itself a "special factor" precluding *Bivens*. While we do not rely on statutory remedies here, the context alone—military detention in a warzone—is sufficient to bar *Bivens*.  

Moreover, *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), confirms that courts must avoid judicial interference in military discipline. Though *Chappell* involved active-duty service members, its rationale applies with even greater force to a civilian contractor like Doe, who was embedded with a Marine Corps unit in a combat zone. The military’s need for "unquestioned obedience" and "a unified command structure" cannot be undermined by federal courts second-guessing detention and interrogation protocols in active hostilities. *Id.* at 303. The district court’s ruling—allowing a damages suit against the Secretary of Defense for policies implemented in Iraq—directly contravenes this principle.  

### II. The District Court Also Erred in Denying Qualified Immunity  

*Even if *Bivens* applied (which it does not), Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because Doe’s substantive due process right was not "clearly established" at the time of the alleged conduct.*  

Qualified immunity shields officials from personal liability unless they violated "clearly established" constitutional rights. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). For a right to be "clearly established," its contours must be "sufficiently clear that every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664 (2012) (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, no prior case law established that a civilian contractor detained in a foreign warzone had a "clearly established" right to be free from interrogation practices that "shock the conscience" under the Fifth Amendment.  

The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process is context-specific and highly fact-bound. In *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998), the Court held that only "the most egregious official conduct" violates substantive due process—conduct that is "intended to injure" or "so outrageous as to shock the contemporary conscience." But in the military context, courts have consistently recognized that operational decisions in warzones are insulated from judicial second-guessing. For example, in *Rasul v. Myers*, 563 F.3d 527 (D.C. Cir. 2009), the D.C. Circuit held that "military detention of enemy combatants in a theater of war" does not give rise to a *Bivens* claim because the "conscience-shocking" standard is too vague to apply to military operations. While *Rasul* is not binding here, it underscores the lack of clarity in this area.  

Crucially, no case at the time of Doe’s detention (2005–2006) had ever applied the "shock the conscience" standard to a civilian contractor in an active warzone. The Supreme Court had never addressed whether contractors detained by the military during combat operations had substantive due process rights, let alone defined what conduct would "shock the conscience" in that setting. As *Hope* requires, the right must be defined with "particularized" precision, but here the law was not "sufficiently clear" to put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that his policies would violate Doe’s constitutional rights. *Hope*, 536 U.S. at 741.  

### Conclusion  

The district court’s decision to extend *Bivens* to a military detention in a foreign warzone disregarded the Supreme Court’s clear instruction that such contexts demand judicial restraint. It also failed to recognize that no clearly established constitutional right existed for Doe’s claims. By allowing this suit to proceed, the court has opened the door to judicial interference in military operations—a result the Constitution and precedent forbid. We urge this Court to reverse the district court’s order and dismiss Doe’s claims in their entirety.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA CIRCUIT

---

**JOHN DOE, APPELLEE**
**v.**
**DONALD RUMSFELD, APPELLANT**

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

Appellee John Doe respectfully submits this response in opposition to Appellant Secretary Rumsfeld's appeal from the district court's order denying his motion to dismiss. Secretary Rumsfeld asks this Court to reverse the district court's determination that Doe may proceed with his Bivens claim for substantive due process violations. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the district court's decision.

The Appellant's argument rests on two flawed premises: first, that the military context categorically precludes any Bivens remedy, and second, that no clearly established constitutional right protected Doe from the alleged conduct. Both arguments fail. The Supreme Court has never held that military detention in a foreign warzone categorically bars Bivens actions, and the "shock the conscience" standard has long been recognized as applicable to egregious government conduct. The district court correctly applied binding precedent, and its decision should be affirmed.

---

### II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY IMPLIED A BIVENS REMEDY

#### A. The Military Context Does Not Categorically Preclude a Bivens Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld contends that this Court should follow *Ziglar v. Abbasi* and *Hernandez v. Mesa* to bar Doe's Bivens claim solely because it arises in a military context. This argument misreads the precedent and overstates the reach of those decisions.

In *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), the Supreme Court declined to extend Bivens to a "new context" involving high-level Executive Branch officials and national security detention policies. However, the Court explicitly recognized that its refusal was based on the particular facts of that case—including the class-action nature of the claims, the high-level policy decisions at issue, and the availability of alternative remedies. The Court did not announce a categorical rule that any military context bars Bivens relief.

Indeed, in *Ziglar*, the Court emphasized that it was not overruling prior cases extending Bivens to contexts involving individual officers and specific misconduct. The Court stated that "expanding the Bivens remedy is now a 'disfavored' judicial activity," but this language reflects the Court's general caution rather than a categorical bar. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The Court has never held that the mere involvement of military personnel in a detention automatically precludes a Bivens remedy.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized Bivens remedies in contexts where government actors operated in sensitive environments. In *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), the Court allowed a Bivens action to proceed against federal prison officials for unconstitutional prison conditions, even though prisons involve security concerns analogous to those in military detention settings. Similarly, in *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), the Court permitted a Bivens remedy for constitutional violations by a congressional official, recognizing that sensitive governmental functions do not categorically bar relief.

The Appellant relies heavily on *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), but that case is distinguishable. In *Chappell*, the Supreme Court declined to imply a Bivens remedy for active-duty military personnel who alleged constitutional violations by their superior officers. The Court emphasized that "the military is, by necessity, a specialized society separate from civilian society" and that military discipline requires special treatment. *Id.* at 300. However, the Court specifically grounded its decision in the unique nature of the military hierarchy and the availability of alternative remedies within the military justice system.

Doe is not an active-duty service member subject to military discipline. He was a civilian contractor—an American citizen employed by a private defense firm—who was detained by military authorities. The military justice system that provided alternative remedies for service members in *Chappell* does not apply to civilian contractors. Doe had no access to the military's internal grievance mechanisms, no ability to appeal through the chain of command, and no alternative forum for seeking redress for his alleged mistreatment. This distinction is critical.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's decision in *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537 (2007), does not support the Appellant's position. In *Wilkie*, the Court declined to extend Bivens where the plaintiff had alternative remedies available and where the claim involved "the Government as landowner" rather than direct constitutional violations by individual officers. *Id.* at 550. Here, Doe has no alternative remedy. He cannot bring a claim under the Detainee Treatment Act against Secretary Rumsfeld in his personal capacity, and no other forum provides a damages remedy for the constitutional violations alleged.

#### B. The Conduct Alleged Is of the Type Historically Subject to Bivens Relief

The Supreme Court has recognized that Bivens serves as a remedy for constitutional violations that are "of the kind traditionally remediable by the tort system." *Carlson*, 446 U.S. at 19. Doe alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld developed, authorized, and implemented policies that resulted in his being subjected to severe physical mistreatment, including being kicked, choked, threatened with death, kept in solitary confinement for months, exposed to extreme temperatures, and deprived of sleep. These allegations describe conduct that would constitute assault, battery, false imprisonment, and intentional infliction of emotional distress under any state's tort law.

In *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), the Supreme Court recognized a damages remedy for Fourth Amendment violations involving physical intrusion into a citizen's home. The conduct alleged here—physical assault, prolonged detention without due process, and coercive interrogation—is far more egregious than the conduct at issue in *Bivens* and falls squarely within the type of wrongdoing that Bivens was designed to address.

The district court correctly noted that Doe's claims are analogous to those in *Carlson*, where the Court allowed a Bivens action for a prisoner's death allegedly caused by inadequate medical care. If Bivens applies to prison conditions—a context involving security concerns—it must also apply to military detention, where the alleged misconduct goes far beyond conditions of confinement to include direct physical assault and torture.

---

### III. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS NOT ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

#### A. The Constitutional Right to Be Free from Conscience-Shocking Treatment Was Clearly Established

Secretary Rumsfeld contends that he is entitled to qualified immunity because no prior case had specifically held that a civilian contractor detained in a foreign warzone had a clearly established right to be free from conscience-shocking interrogation and detention practices. This argument misapplies the qualified immunity standard.

Qualified immunity shields government officials from personal liability unless their conduct violated "clearly established" constitutional rights. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). However, the "clearly established" requirement does not require a case directly on point. As the Supreme Court has held, officials are on notice that their conduct violates constitutional rights when the "contours" of the right are "sufficiently clear" that a reasonable official would understand that his actions violate the right. *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). The constitutional principle must be defined with "obvious clarity" rather than "meticulous specificity." *Hope*, 536 U.S. at 741.

The right to be free from treatment that "shocks the conscience" has been clearly established for decades. In *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), the Supreme Court held that substantive due process protects individuals from "the most egregious official conduct" that is "intended to injure in some way unjustifiable by any government interest" or is "so outrageous as to shock the contemporary conscience." *Id.* at 846. This standard applies with full force to detention and interrogation practices.

The Supreme Court applied this standard in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), holding that police conduct involving force and coercion shocks the conscience and violates due process. The Court stated that conduct "shocking the conscience" is conduct that "shocks the eye" and offends "the sense of justice." *Id.* at 174. More recently, in *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992), the Court held that the use of excessive force by prison guards violates the Eighth Amendment even when the prisoner does not suffer serious injury, because the "malicious and sadistic" use of force "violates the Constitution." *Id.* at 9.

Doe's complaint alleges conduct far more severe than that at issue in *Rochin* or *Hudson*. He was kicked in the back, threatened with death, choked repeatedly by a guard, kept in solitary confinement for twelve months, exposed to extreme temperatures, and deprived of sleep for extended periods. These allegations describe conduct that no reasonable official could believe is permissible under the Constitution.

The Appellant argues that the "shock the conscience" standard is too vague to provide clear notice in the military context. However, the Supreme Court has rejected similar arguments. In *Hope*, the Court held that the "shock the conscience" standard was sufficiently clear to provide notice to prison guards who placed inmates in painful restraints. The Court emphasized that the standard is "intentionally 'vague'" to capture "a broad range of unwitnessed misconduct" and to avoid "the temptation to set 13th-century standards." *Hope*, 536 U.S. at 740. The same reasoning applies here.

#### B. The Conduct Alleged Satisfies the Shock the Conscience Standard

The district court correctly held that Doe alleged facts sufficient to satisfy the "shock the conscience" standard. The complaint describes a pattern of treatment that any objective observer would find egregious: four hours of interrogation without counsel, physical assault, twelve months of detention without formal charges, solitary confinement, exposure to extreme temperatures, sleep deprivation, and repeated choking by a guard. This conduct was not only intentional but, according to the complaint, was part of a deliberate policy authorized at the highest levels of the Department of Defense.

In *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, the Supreme Court suggested that the "shock the conscience" standard would be met where government officials acted with "a purpose to harm" unrelated to legitimate governmental objectives. *Id.* at 835. Doe alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld developed and authorized policies specifically designed to coerce detainees through physical mistreatment—a purpose entirely unrelated to any legitimate military or intelligence objective.

The district court properly denied qualified immunity at this stage, accepting the well-pleaded facts as true and construing reasonable inferences in Doe's favor. The Appellant's arguments about the military context and the lack of direct precedent are matters for summary judgment or trial, not for dismissal on qualified immunity grounds.

---

### IV. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE PLEADING STANDARDS

The Appellant asks this Court to reweigh the factual allegations and draw inferences in his favor rather than Doe's. This request contradicts the standard of review for a motion to dismiss.

On a motion to dismiss, the court must accept all well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). The court is not permitted to evaluate the credibility of the allegations or to weigh evidence. The district court properly applied this standard, and this Court should do the same.

Doe's complaint alleges with particularity the specific acts of mistreatment he endured, the dates and locations of his detention, the officials involved, and the policies allegedly authorized by Secretary Rumsfeld. These allegations are sufficient to state a claim for substantive due process violations under *Bivens*.

---

### V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court properly held that Doe may proceed with his Bivens claim for substantive due process violations, and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation.

The Appellant has not demonstrated that the military context categorically bars a Bivens remedy, nor has he shown that no clearly established constitutional right protected Doe from the alleged conduct. To the contrary, the right to be free from conscience-shocking treatment has been clearly established for decades, and the conduct alleged in Doe's complaint satisfies that standard.

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case presents the Court with the question of whether a Bivens action may be implied against the Secretary of Defense for the alleged detention and mistreatment of a United States citizen, a civilian contractor, in a warzone. We review the district court’s denial of Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). For the reasons that follow, we agree with the Appellant that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this context. Consequently, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the Bivens remedy is now a “disfavored” judicial activity. Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). A court may not imply a cause of action under Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), if there are “special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress.” Id. at 1854 (quoting *Corr. Servs. Corp. v. Malesko*, 534 U.S. 61, 66 (2001)). The Appellant argues, and we agree, that the context of military detention in a foreign warzone presents precisely such special factors.

The Appellee attempts to distinguish the military context by noting his status as a civilian contractor rather than an active-duty service member, relying on *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983). While *Chappell* specifically addressed the unique disciplinary structure of the military, the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Ziglar* extends far beyond the internal military hierarchy. In *Ziglar*, the Court refused to extend Bivens to claims arising from the detention of illegal aliens in the wake of the September 11 attacks, emphasizing that “national security and foreign affairs” are areas where “judicial caution is appropriate.” *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. The Court noted that “the risk of intrusion on the authority and discretion of the Executive” is particularly acute in matters of military detention. Id.

The Appellee’s reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), and *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), is unpersuasive. While those cases recognized Bivens remedies in sensitive contexts, neither involved the conduct of active warfare or the detention of individuals in a theater of combat operations. As the Appellant argues, the military context is a “paradigmatic example” of a setting where special factors demand judicial restraint. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The separation-of-powers implications of subjecting military detention policies in a warzone to judicial review via damages actions are profound. See *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007) (noting that the absence of a clear legislative mandate to create a damages remedy is a special factor counseling hesitation).

Furthermore, this Court has previously recognized the inapplicability of Bivens to military detention in a warzone. In *Rasul v. Myers*, 563 F.3d 527 (D.C. Cir. 2009), the court held that the “conscience-shocking” standard was too vague to apply to military operations and that Bivens should not be extended to the detention of enemy combatants. Although the Appellee here is a United States citizen, the underlying context—military detention in Iraq during active hostilities—remains the same. The potential for judges to second-guess tactical military decisions and the risk of interfering with the chain of command are present regardless of the detainee’s citizenship. See *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 304.

Therefore, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy for the Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The special factors present in this military context counsel hesitation, and Congress has not authorized such a cause of action.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available in this context, which it is not, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability for civil damages unless the official violated a “clearly established” statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The Appellee contends that the right to be free from “conscience-shocking” treatment was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct in 2005 and 2006.

The “clearly established” standard requires that the “contours of the right [be] sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right.” *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). This standard must be defined with “particularized” precision. *Hope*, 536 U.S. at 741. The Appellee relies on general substantive due process principles, such as those articulated in *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), and *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), to argue that the alleged physical abuse was clearly unconstitutional.

However, the Appellant correctly argues that no prior case had specifically applied the “shock the conscience” standard to the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in a foreign warzone. The Supreme Court has never addressed whether such a detainee possesses substantive due process rights against military commanders that would “shock the conscience.” In the absence of controlling authority or a robust consensus of persuasive cases placing the specific constitutional question beyond debate, Secretary Rumsfeld cannot be said to have violated clearly established law. See *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664-65 (2012).

The Appellee’s reliance on *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992), which involved the Eighth Amendment rights of a convicted prisoner, is inapposite. The rights of convicted prisoners in domestic facilities are distinct from the rights of individuals detained in a combat zone. As the D.C. Circuit noted in *Rasul*, the “shock the conscience” standard is ill-suited for the battlefield context. *Rasul*, 563 F.3d at 538-39. Because the law did not clearly establish that the Secretary of Defense’s policies regarding the detention of a contractor in Iraq violated the Constitution, the district court erred in denying qualified immunity.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.

**REVERSED.**

**INTRODUCTION**  
Appellee’s arguments fundamentally misread Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit precedent, ignoring the critical distinction between domestic settings and military detention in an active foreign warzone. Myers* establish that military detention in a combat zone presents “special factors counselling hesitation” that categorically preclude Bivens relief. Appellee’s attempt to distinguish civilian contractor status from military context is legally unsound and contradicts controlling authority. This Court must reverse the district court’s error.  

---

### I. THE MILITARY CONTEXT OF FOREIGN WARZONE DETENTION CATEGORICALLY PRECLUDES A BIVENS REMEDY  

Appellee mischaracterizes *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), by claiming it does not “categorically bar” Bivens in military contexts. That assertion is directly contradicted by *Ziglar* itself. The Supreme Court explicitly recognized that “national security and foreign affairs” are “paradigmatic examples” of areas where “judicial caution is appropriate,” and that “the risk of intrusion on the authority and discretion of the Executive” is “particularly acute” in military detention operations. *Id.* at 1859. The Court did not merely “caution” against extending Bivens in such contexts—it held that “special factors” in these settings “counsel hesitation” as a matter of law. *Id.* at 1854.  

Appellee’s reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), and *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), is inapposite. *Carlson* involved domestic prison conditions in a non-combat setting, where the Court emphasized that Congress had not “foreclosed” damages remedies for constitutional violations. *Id.* at 18–19. *Davis* concerned a congressional staff member’s employment discrimination claim—far removed from military operations in an active warzone. Neither case addressed the unique separation-of-powers concerns that arise when courts second-guess tactical military decisions in a theater of combat. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007) (holding that Bivens is disfavored where “Congress has not authorized a damages remedy” and “special factors counsel hesitation”).  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Rasul v. Myers*, 563 F.3d 527 (D.C. Cir. 2009), directly controls this case. *Rasul* held that Bivens cannot be extended to claims arising from military detention in Iraq during active hostilities, even for U.S. citizens. The court reasoned that the “shock the conscience” standard is “ill-suited for the battlefield context” because it would “invite judicial second-guessing of military judgments” and “interfere with the chain of command.” *Id.* at 538–39. Appellee’s attempt to distinguish *Rasul* based on civilian contractor status fails: the court explicitly stated that the “underlying context—military detention in Iraq during active hostilities—remains the same” regardless of detainee citizenship. *Id.* at 539. The Supreme Court has never held that a civilian contractor’s status overrides the “special factors” of a foreign warzone.  

---

### II. CHAPPELL V. WALLACE AND CIVILIAN CONTRACTOR STATUS DO NOT CREATE AN EXCEPTION TO BIVENS PROHIBITION  

Appellee argues that *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), is distinguishable because Doe is a civilian contractor. That argument misreads *Chappell* and ignores the broader principles it reinforces. *Chappell* did not merely address “internal military hierarchy”; it recognized that the military’s “specialized society” requires “special treatment” to preserve discipline and operational effectiveness. *Id.* at 300. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not require the military to provide a judicial forum for all claims arising within its ranks,” and that such claims must be resolved through “military discipline” mechanisms. *Id.* at 304. Crucially, *Chappell*’s reasoning extends beyond service members to *any* individual subject to military detention in a warzone. As the D.C. Circuit held in *Rasul*, the “risk of judicial interference with military operations” exists for all detainees—citizen or non-citizen—because “tactical decisions in combat zones must remain insulated from judicial review.” *Rasul*, 563 F.3d at 538.  

Appellee’s claim that Doe lacked “alternative remedies” within the military justice system is irrelevant. *Ziglar* makes clear that the absence of an alternative remedy does not overcome “special factors.” 137 S. Ct. at 1858 (“The existence of alternative remedies is not dispositive; the question is whether there are ‘special factors counselling hesitation’”). In *Wilkie*, the Supreme Court rejected a Bivens claim even though the plaintiff had no alternative remedy, holding that the “context” of landowner-government disputes required judicial restraint. 551 U.S. at 550. Here, the context is military detention in a foreign warzone—a setting where “Congress has not authorized a damages remedy,” and where “judicial intervention would disrupt delicate foreign policy and national security considerations.” *Id.*  

---

### III. THE “SHOCK THE CONSCIENCE” STANDARD WAS NOT CLEARLY ESTABLISHED FOR MILITARY DETENTION IN A FOREIGN WARZONE  

Even if Bivens applied (which it does not), Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Appellee’s claim that the “shock the conscience” standard was “clearly established” misapplies *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635 (1987). Qualified immunity requires that the contours of the right be “sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right.” *Id.* at 640. This standard demands “particularized” precision—not general constitutional principles. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 741 (2002).  

Appellee cites *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992), but both involve domestic law enforcement or prison settings—*not* military detention in an active combat zone. *Rochin* addressed police conduct during a California search, while *Hudson* concerned Eighth Amendment claims by a convicted prisoner in a Texas jail. Neither case addressed the unique challenges of battlefield detention, where commanders must make split-second decisions under life-threatening conditions. As the D.C. Circuit held in *Rasul*, the “shock the conscience” standard “is ill-suited for the battlefield context” precisely because it fails to account for the “realities of combat.” 563 F.3d at 539.  

*County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), does not save Appellee’s argument. While it defined the “shock the conscience” standard for high-speed police chases, the Court emphasized that the standard must be applied with “careful attention to the context.” *Id.* at 846. In military operations, the Supreme Court has never held that the standard applies to commanders making tactical detention decisions. *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664–65 (2012) (qualified immunity applies “where the law is not clearly established” and “no controlling precedent” addresses the specific conduct). No prior case held that a Secretary of Defense’s policies for detaining civilian contractors in Iraq violated the Constitution—let alone that such policies “shocked the conscience.”  

---

### IV. CONCLUSION  

The district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy for Mr. Doe’s claims. *Ziglar* and *Rasul* establish that military detention in a foreign warzone presents “special factors counselling hesitation” that categorically preclude judicial creation of a damages remedy. *Chappell* reinforces that military operations require insulation from judicial second-guessing, regardless of detainee status. And no precedent clearly established that Secretary Rumsfeld’s actions violated the Constitution in this context. Appellee’s arguments rely on misreadings of precedent and ignore the separation-of-powers principles that forbid courts from inserting themselves into military decision-making. **OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case requires us to determine whether a Bivens action may be implied against the Secretary of Defense for the alleged detention and mistreatment of a United States citizen, a civilian contractor, in a foreign warzone. We have reviewed the parties’ rebuttals and the applicable law. For the reasons stated herein, we affirm our preliminary ruling: the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this context, and Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Consequently, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

We agree with the Appellant that the context of military detention in a foreign warzone presents "special factors counselling hesitation" that preclude the implication of a Bivens remedy. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the Bivens remedy is now a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). In *Ziglar*, the Court explicitly recognized that "national security and foreign affairs" are "paradigmatic examples" of areas where "judicial caution is appropriate," and that "the risk of intrusion on the authority and discretion of the Executive" is "particularly acute" in military detention operations. *Id.* at 1859. The Court held that where such special factors are present, courts must "hesitate" before extending Bivens, and the presence of these factors "counsel[s] hesitation" as a matter of law. *Id.* at 1854.

The Appellee attempts to distinguish this military context by noting his status as a civilian contractor rather than an active-duty service member. However, this distinction is insufficient to overcome the separation-of-powers concerns inherent in this case. As the D.C. Circuit held in *Rasul v. Myers*, 563 F.3d 527 (D.C. Cir. 2009), the "shock the conscience" standard is "ill-suited for the battlefield context" because it would "invite judicial second-guessing of military judgments" and "interfere with the chain of command." *Id.* at 538–39. The *Rasul* court explicitly stated that the "underlying context—military detention in Iraq during active hostilities—remains the same" regardless of detainee citizenship. *Id.* at 539. The potential for judicial interference with tactical military decisions exists regardless of whether the detainee is a service member, a civilian contractor, or an alleged enemy combatant.

The Appellee’s reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), and *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), is misplaced. *Carlson* involved domestic prison conditions in a non-combat setting, and *Davis* concerned a congressional staff member’s employment discrimination claim. Neither case addressed the unique separation-of-powers concerns that arise when courts second-guess tactical military decisions in a theater of combat. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007) (noting that the absence of a clear legislative mandate to create a damages remedy is a special factor counseling hesitation). Furthermore, *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), reinforces the principle that the military constitutes a "specialized society" requiring "special treatment" to preserve discipline and operational effectiveness. *Id.* at 300. The Appellee’s claim that he lacked "alternative remedies" within the military justice system does not overcome these special factors, as the existence of alternative remedies is not dispositive under *Ziglar*. 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

Therefore, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy for the Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The special factors present in this military context counsel hesitation, and Congress has not authorized such a cause of action.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available in this context, which it is not, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability for civil damages unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The "clearly established" standard requires that the "contours of the right [be] sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). This standard must be defined with "particularized" precision. *Hope*, 536 U.S. at 741.

The Appellee relies on general substantive due process principles, such as those articulated in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992), to argue that the alleged physical abuse was clearly unconstitutional. However, *Rochin* addressed police conduct during a California search, and *Hudson* concerned Eighth Amendment claims by a convicted prisoner in a Texas jail. Neither case addressed the unique challenges of battlefield detention, where commanders must make split-second decisions under life-threatening conditions. As the D.C. Circuit noted in *Rasul*, the "shock the conscience" standard is ill-suited for the battlefield context precisely because it fails to account for the "realities of combat." 563 F.3d at 539.

*County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), does not alter this conclusion. While it defined the "shock the conscience" standard for high-speed police chases, the Court emphasized that the standard must be applied with "careful attention to the context." *Id.* at 846. In military operations, the Supreme Court has never held that the standard applies to commanders making tactical detention decisions. *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664–65 (2012) (qualified immunity applies "where the law is not clearly established" and "no controlling precedent" addresses the specific conduct). No prior case held that a Secretary of Defense’s policies for detaining civilian contractors in Iraq violated the Constitution—let alone that such policies "shocked the conscience." Because the law did not clearly establish that the Secretary of Defense’s actions violated the Constitution in this specific context, the district court erred in denying qualified immunity.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.

**REVERSED.**

The court did not address the situation of American citizens detained as civilians in Iraq. The Appellant's reliance on *Rasul* for the proposition that all detainees in Iraq are categorically excluded from Bivens remedies is overbroad and was expressly disavowed by the *Rasul* court itself. *Id.* at 539 n.13 (noting that the court did not address "claims by U.S. citizens detained in Iraq").

### B. The Allegations Against Secretary Rumsfeld Involve Policy Decisions, Not Battlefield Tactics

Second, the Appellant conflates tactical military decisions made in the fog of war with high-level policy decisions allegedly made by the Secretary of Defense. Mr. Doe does not challenge the tactical decisions of field commanders facing immediate threats. Rather, he alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm"—policies that allegedly authorized detention practices that shock the conscience.

This distinction is critical. The Supreme Court in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1861 (2017), recognized that Bivens may be appropriate where plaintiffs challenge "the sort of executive action that is directly regulated by the Constitution"—not merely actions taken in a military context. The Court emphasized that the "special factors" analysis requires a "careful assessment" of whether judicial remedy would interfere with executive authority. *Id.* at 1859.

Where, as here, the plaintiff alleges that the Secretary of Defense himself authorized policies that violated the Constitution, judicial review does not intrude upon tactical military decisions—it vindicates the constitutional rights of a citizen who has been allegedly tortured and arbitrarily detained. The Constitution does not permit the Secretary of Defense to authorize torture with immunity.

---

## II. THE APPELLANT'S QUALIFIED IMMUNITY ARGUMENT IGNORES ALLEGATIONS THAT THE CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS WERE CLEARLY ESTABLISHED

Even if this Court were to find that a Bivens remedy is available, the Appellant is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation. The Appellant argues that no clearly established constitutional right was violated. This argument fails because the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established long before 2005, when the alleged conduct occurred.

### A. The "Shock the Conscience" Standard Was Clearly Established

The Supreme Court has long held that executive action that "shocks the conscience" violates substantive due process. *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). This standard applies to executive officials who abuse their power to the point of shocking the conscience. *Id.* at 174; *see also County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998) (holding that the "shock the conscience" standard applies to executive conduct that "violates the Decencies of civilized conduct").

The Appellant attempts to limit *Rochin* and *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992), to their specific contexts—police searches and prisoner conditions. But these cases establish the general principle that executive conduct that is brutal, arbitrary, or shocking violates the Constitution. The "shock the conscience" standard is not limited to these precise factual scenarios. As this Court held in *NAACP v. Trump*, 298 F. Supp. 3d 209, 239 (D.D.C. 2018), "the Supreme Court has made clear that the 'shock the conscience' standard is the appropriate metric for evaluating substantive due process claims against federal officers."

Mr. Doe has alleged conduct that, if proven, would shock the conscience of any reasonable person: detention for nine months without charges, solitary confinement, exposure to extreme temperatures, deprivation of sleep, physical abuse including choking, and threats to shoot him. These allegations are not "tactical military decisions"—they are alleged violations of basic human rights that have been prohibited since the founding of this Nation.

### B. The Appellant's Argument That Military Context Negates Conscience-Shocking Conduct Is Circular

The Appellant argues that the "shock the conscience" standard is "ill-suited for the battlefield context." But this argument is circular. The standard is designed precisely to address executive overreach—including in contexts where the executive claims extraordinary authority. *See Rochin*, 342 U.S. at 172 ("[O]fficious intermeddling" with private affairs by executive officials "shocks conscience").

The Appellant cites *Rasul* for the proposition that the "shock the conscience" standard cannot apply in military detention contexts. But *Rasul* involved detainees who were apprehended abroad as alleged enemy combatants—a fundamentally different situation from Mr. Doe, an American citizen detained while working as a civilian translator. The *Rasul* court explicitly noted that it was not addressing claims by U.S. citizens. *Rasul*, 563 F.3d at 539 n.13.

Moreover, the fact that Mr. Doe was detained in a military facility does not automatically exempt his detention from constitutional constraints. The Constitution follows American citizens wherever they go—including into military facilities. *See Johnson v. Eisentrager*, 339 U.S. 763, 770 (1950) (holding that constitutional protections extend to citizens abroad, subject to reasonable military regulations).

---

## III. THE APPELLANT'S SEPARATION OF POWERS ARGUMENT IGNORES THE CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS OF AMERICAN CITIZENS

The Appellant argues that allowing this suit to proceed would "violate separation of powers" by allowing courts to "second-guess" military decisions. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the role of the judiciary in vindicating constitutional rights.

The separation of powers does not immunize executive officials from liability for constitutional violations. To the contrary, the Constitution establishes a system of checks and balances precisely to ensure that executive overreach is subject to judicial review. As the Supreme Court held in *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803), "it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

The Appellant's argument, if accepted, would create a categorical exemption for all executive actions taken in a military context—a result that would render constitutional rights meaningless for American citizens detained abroad. The Constitution does not permit such an exemption. *See Hamdi*, 542 U.S. at 535 ("[E]ven the war power does not remove constitutional limitations").

Allowing Mr. Doe to proceed with his claim does not require courts to micromanage military operations. It requires only that executive officials who allegedly authorize torture and arbitrary detention be held accountable for their actions. This is precisely the function of the civil justice system.

---

## IV. THE APPELLANT'S RELIANCE ON *ZIGLAR* IS MISPLACED

The Appellant relies heavily on *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), for the proposition that Bivens should not be extended to this context. But *Ziglar* actually supports Mr. Doe's claim.

In *Ziglar*, the Supreme Court held that Bivens should not be extended to claims by detainees at the Guantanamo Bay facility, in part because of "the unique nature of the military detention of enemy combatants." *Id.* at 1861. The Court emphasized, however, that its holding was limited to "the specific context of military detention at Guantanamo." *Id.* at 1862.

The Court in *Ziglar* explicitly noted that its analysis "may be different" in cases involving "different circumstances"—including cases involving "U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents detained within the territory of the United States." *Id.* at 1862 n.7. Mr. Doe's case involves precisely these "different circumstances": he is a U.S. citizen who was allegedly detained in Iraq under circumstances that, if proven, constitute torture and arbitrary detention.

Moreover, the *Ziglar* Court emphasized that its decision did not "foreclose Bivens actions in other contexts." *Id.* at 1863. The Court specifically noted that Bivens remains available where "another thread in the Constitution" provides a basis for relief. *Id.* at 1858. Mr. Doe's claim is based on the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of due process—a thread that has been part of the Constitution since its ratification.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we respectfully urge this Court to affirm the district court's denial of the Appellant's motion to dismiss. Mr. Doe has alleged constitutional violations of the most serious nature—torture, arbitrary detention, and denial of due process. These allegations, if proven, shock the conscience and violate clearly established constitutional rights. The fact that these alleged violations occurred in a military context does not immunize the alleged perpetrators from accountability.

The Constitution does not permit the Secretary of Defense to authorize torture with immunity. American citizens who are allegedly subjected to such treatment are entitled to their day in court. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the district court's order and allow this case to proceed.

Respectfully submitted,

**[Appellee's Counsel]**

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case requires us to determine whether a Bivens action may be implied against the Secretary of Defense for the alleged detention and mistreatment of a United States citizen, a civilian contractor, in a foreign warzone. We have carefully reviewed the closing arguments of both parties. For the reasons stated herein, we affirm our preliminary ruling: the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this context, and Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Consequently, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

We agree with the Appellant that the context of military detention in a foreign warzone presents "special factors counselling hesitation" that preclude the implication of a Bivens remedy. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the Bivens remedy is now a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). In *Ziglar*, the Court explicitly recognized that "national security and foreign affairs" are "paradigmatic examples" of areas where "judicial caution is appropriate," and that "the risk of intrusion on the authority and discretion of the Executive" is "particularly acute" in military detention operations. *Id.* at 1859. The Court held that where such special factors are present, courts must "hesitate" before extending Bivens, and the presence of these factors "counsel[s] hesitation" as a matter of law. *Id.* at 1854.

The Appellee attempts to distinguish this military context by emphasizing his status as a civilian contractor and a United States citizen. He relies on *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), and *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866), to argue that his constitutional protections remain intact despite his presence in a theater of war. However, this distinction is insufficient to overcome the separation-of-powers concerns inherent in this case. As the D.C. Circuit held in *Rasul v. Myers*, 563 F.3d 527 (D.C. Cir. 2009), the "shock the conscience" standard is "ill-suited for the battlefield context" because it would "invite judicial second-guessing of military judgments" and "interfere with the chain of command." *Id.* at 538–39. The *Rasul* court explicitly stated that the "underlying context—military detention in Iraq during active hostilities—remains the same" regardless of detainee citizenship. *Id.* at 539. The potential for judicial interference with tactical military decisions exists regardless of whether the detainee is a service member, a civilian contractor, or an alleged enemy combatant.

The Appellee argues that *Ziglar* left open the possibility of Bivens actions for United States citizens. While *Ziglar* noted that its analysis "may be different" in cases involving "U.S. citizens," 137 S. Ct. at 1862 n.7, it did not diminish the force of the "special factors" analysis. The "unique nature of the military detention" and the "risk of intrusion on the authority and discretion of the Executive" are present here just as they were in *Ziglar*. *Id.* at 1861. The Appellee’s reliance on *Ex parte Milligan* is misplaced; *Milligan* involved a civilian detained in Indiana where civilian courts were open, not a civilian detained in an active warzone abroad. 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) at 121.

Furthermore, the Appellee’s claim that he is challenging "policy decisions" rather than "tactical decisions" does not alter the analysis. The policies at issue—detention and interrogation protocols in a warzone—are inextricably linked to military command and the conduct of operations. Allowing damages suits against high-level officials for such policies would inevitably require courts to sit in judgment of military necessity and discretion. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007) (noting that the absence of a clear legislative mandate to create a damages remedy is a special factor counselling hesitation). The Appellee’s argument that he lacked "alternative remedies" within the military justice system does not overcome these special factors, as the existence of alternative remedies is not dispositive under *Ziglar*. 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

Therefore, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy for the Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The special factors present in this military context counsel hesitation, and Congress has not authorized such a cause of action.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available in this context, which it is not, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability for civil damages unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The "clearly established" standard requires that the "contours of the right [be] sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). This standard must be defined with "particularized" precision. *Hope*, 536 U.S. at 741.

The Appellee relies on general substantive due process principles, such as those articulated in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), to argue that the alleged physical abuse was clearly unconstitutional. However, *Rochin* addressed police conduct during a California search, and *Lewis* defined the "shock the conscience" standard for high-speed police chases. Neither case addressed the unique challenges of battlefield detention, where commanders must make split-second decisions under life-threatening conditions. As the D.C. Circuit noted in *Rasul*, the "shock the conscience" standard is ill-suited for the battlefield context precisely because it fails to account for the "realities of combat." 563 F.3d at 539.

The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard is a general principle that applies to all executive officials. However, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that qualified immunity requires more than the mere existence of a broad standard; it requires specific notice that the conduct at issue is unlawful. *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664–65 (2012) (qualified immunity applies "where the law is not clearly established" and "no controlling precedent" addresses the specific conduct). No prior case held that a Secretary of Defense’s policies for detaining civilian contractors in Iraq violated the Constitution—let alone that such policies "shocked the conscience." Because the law did not clearly establish that the Secretary of Defense’s actions violated the Constitution in this specific context, the district court erred in denying qualified immunity.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.

**REVERSED.**
