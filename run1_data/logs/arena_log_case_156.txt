# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Summary of Argument

Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., respectfully submits this opening response in support of the judgment below. The Court should affirm the dismissal of Plaintiff's complaint because Plaintiff has failed to state viable causes of action under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

The central premise of Plaintiff's claims—that his physician-recommended marijuana use entitles him to protection from employment consequences—fails as a matter of law. Under the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution, federal law prohibiting marijuana supersedes California's Compassionate Use Act. Because Plaintiff's marijuana use remains unlawful under federal law, Defendant was permitted to maintain its drug-free workplace policy and to terminate Plaintiff for failing the preemployment drug test. The FEHA does not require employers to accommodate federally illegal conduct, and the Compassionate Use Act does not create a protected employment right. The lower courts correctly applied these principles, and this Court should affirm.

## I. The FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Federally Illegal Conduct

The California Fair Employment and Housing Act prohibits employers from discriminating against qualified individuals with disabilities and requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations that do not impose undue hardship. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subds. (a), (n).) However, the accommodation obligation extends only to lawful conduct. As this Court held in Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions."

The complaint alleges that Plaintiff used marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation and that this use was permitted under the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5). However, the Compassionate Use Act provides only a defense to certain California state criminal charges—it does not render marijuana use lawful under federal law, nor does it create any employment protection. (See Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (d) [providing a defense to state criminal possession charges only].)

Federal law unambiguously classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance and prohibits its possession. (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a).) The United States Supreme Court has confirmed that this federal prohibition "trumps state laws permitting medical marijuana." (Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195].) The Court in Gonzales v. Raich explicitly rejected the argument that state-authorized medical use creates an exception to federal law, holding that the Controlled Substances Act "reflects a federal policy that experimental drug use in general, and marijuana use in particular, is not in the best interest of the nation." (Id. at 26-27.)

Similarly, in United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], the Supreme Court held that federal law "makes no exception for medical necessity," making marijuana possession illegal "regardless of the extent of the individual patient's medical need." (Id. at 494.) These binding precedents establish that Plaintiff's marijuana use, though authorized under California state law, remains unlawful under federal law.

Because Plaintiff's requested accommodation—permission to use marijuana while employed—would require Defendant to condone conduct that violates federal law, such accommodation is not "reasonable" within the meaning of the FEHA. The FEHA's accommodation mandate does not require employers to choose between violating federal law or facing state employment liability. (See Loder, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883 [employer may consider illegal drug use in employment decisions].)

## II. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create an Employment Right

Plaintiff appears to argue that the Compassionate Use Act creates a public policy protecting medical marijuana users from employment discrimination. This argument fails because the statute contains no such provision.

The Compassionate Use Act was enacted as an initiative measure to provide a defense to certain California criminal charges for patients who use marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation. (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (d).) The statute's plain language limits its scope to criminal defense—it says nothing about employment rights or protections. The voters who approved Proposition 215 did not create a right to use marijuana in the workplace or to demand that employers accommodate medical marijuana use.

This Court has consistently held that the FEHA does not protect conduct that remains illegal under federal law. (See Loder, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883.) To hold otherwise would render the FEHA in direct conflict with the Controlled Substances Act, creating an impossible situation for California employers who wish to maintain drug-free workplaces consistent with federal law.

## III. The Wrongful Termination Claim Fails for the Same Reason

Plaintiff's second cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy rests on the same flawed premise—that the Compassionate Use Act creates a public policy protecting medical marijuana users. As explained above, the Compassionate Use Act contains no such policy. The statute provides a criminal defense only and does not address employment.

Moreover, the public policy exception to at-will employment, as articulated in Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330] and Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157], requires a clear public policy derived from constitutional or statutory provisions. No California statute creates a public policy protecting medical marijuana users from employment termination. The Compassionate Use Act's purpose was to provide criminal immunity, not employment protection, and this Court should not engraft an employment right onto a statute that contains none.

## IV. Defendant's Drug Policy Is Lawful and Non-Discriminatory

Defendant maintained a lawful drug-free workplace policy requiring preemployment drug testing for new employees. This policy is consistent with federal law, which prohibits marijuana possession, and with California law, which permits employers to consider illegal drug use in employment decisions. (See Loder, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883.)

Plaintiff does not allege that Defendant's policy was applied discriminatorily to individuals with disabilities. To the contrary, Plaintiff alleges only that he was terminated because he used marijuana—conduct that remains illegal under federal law. Defendant's decision to maintain a drug-free workplace, and to terminate an employee who tested positive for a controlled substance, does not constitute disability discrimination under the FEHA.

Plaintiff's allegations that his marijuana use did not affect his job performance are immaterial. The question is not whether Plaintiff could perform his job adequately while using marijuana, but whether Defendant was required to accommodate federally illegal conduct. The FEHA does not compel such accommodation.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeal, which affirmed the superior court's dismissal of Plaintiff's complaint. Plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy because: (1) the FEHA does not require employers to accommodate federally illegal conduct; (2) the Compassionate Use Act does not create an employment right or public policy protecting medical marijuana users; and (3) Defendant's drug-free workplace policy is lawful and non-discriminatory.

The judgment below should be affirmed.

The termination was based on the plaintiff's choice to use a substance prohibited by federal law, not on his status as a disabled individual.

### B. Wrongful Termination Claim

The plaintiff cannot state a cause of action for wrongful termination because:

1. **No Clear Public Policy**: The plaintiff has not identified any constitutional or statutory provision establishing a clear public policy that protects medical marijuana users from employment termination. The Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to *criminal prosecution*, not a shield against adverse employment actions.

2. **Federal Preemption**: Federal law prohibits marijuana use in all circumstances. Under the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution, federal law preempts any conflicting state law. No state-created public policy can override the federal prohibition.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeal. The superior court properly sustained the demurrer without leave to amendment because the plaintiff cannot state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

The defendant did not discriminate against the plaintiff based on his disability. Rather, the defendant lawfully exercised its right to maintain a drug-free workplace and to consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions. The plaintiff's use of marijuana, even with a physician's recommendation, violates federal law. The FEHA does not require employers to accommodate federally illegal conduct, and no public policy exists to shield employees who engage in such conduct from termination.

The defense acknowledges the plaintiff's difficult circumstances and his service to his country. However, the rule of law must prevail. The defendant was entitled to terminate the plaintiff's employment based on his positive drug test, and the lower courts correctly held that the plaintiff failed to state a cognizable cause of action.

**FINAL RULING**

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Court of Appeal is affirmed. The superior court’s order sustaining the demurrer without leave to amend is upheld.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Plaintiff and the Defendant. The matter comes before us on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to the Plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The central issue is whether the Plaintiff can state a cause of action for disability discrimination under the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) or wrongful termination in violation of public policy, based on his use of medical marijuana pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act.

**I. The Plaintiff’s Concession Regarding FEHA Accommodation**

The Plaintiff’s closing argument includes a critical concession: he acknowledges that he has failed to state a cause of action under the FEHA because the statute does not require an employer to accommodate conduct that is illegal under federal law. This concession is well-founded and dispositive of the first cause of action.

The Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) provides a defense to criminal prosecution under state law, but it does not authorize conduct that violates federal law. The Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)) categorizes marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance, prohibiting its possession and use. The United States Supreme Court has made clear that federal law prohibiting marijuana possession "trumps state laws permitting medical marijuana." (Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195].) Furthermore, in *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], the Court held that federal law "makes no exception for medical necessity."

Because the requested accommodation—medical marijuana use—constitutes a violation of federal law, the Defendant was under no obligation to accommodate it. As this Court held in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], an employer may lawfully "take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." The Defendant’s decision to terminate the Plaintiff based on his positive drug test for THC was therefore lawful.

**II. Absence of Public Policy Supporting Wrongful Termination**

The Plaintiff’s claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy similarly fails. To establish such a claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate a clear public policy derived from constitutional or statutory provisions. (Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330]; see also Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157].)

The Compassionate Use Act provides a limited defense to state criminal prosecution; it does not create a public policy protecting medical marijuana users from adverse employment actions. As established in *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195], state laws cannot override the federal prohibition on marijuana. Consequently, there is no clear public policy that would categorize the termination of an employee for federally illegal drug use as a wrongful termination. The Defendant correctly argues that the Act is a shield against criminal liability, not a sword to enforce employment rights.

**III. Conclusion**

The Plaintiff’s concession that the FEHA does not mandate accommodation of federally illegal conduct, combined with the Defendant’s correct application of *Loder*, *Raich*, and *Tameny*, leaves no genuine dispute of material law. The lower courts properly sustained the demurrer without leave to amend. The judgment is affirmed.
