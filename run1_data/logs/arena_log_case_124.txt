# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF NORTH CAROLINA

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The Defendant-Appellant respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the State's petition for writ of certiorari and in support of the Court of Appeals' decision reversing the three second-degree murder convictions. The Court of Appeals correctly identified a fundamental deficiency in the State's proof at trial: the complete absence of evidence sufficient to establish the element of malice. This Court should affirm the Court of Appeals' decision and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The Defendant was charged with three counts of second-degree murder arising from an automobile accident on September 4, 1982. At trial, the State presented evidence establishing that the Defendant was severely intoxicated, had been involved in an altercation at a tavern, and subsequently operated a motor vehicle on a public highway, resulting in a collision that killed three individuals. The trial court refused to permit the Defendant to offer evidence suggesting he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident and further refused to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness.

The Court of Appeals, exercising its authority under the plain error doctrine, correctly determined that the evidence was insufficient to establish malice, an essential element of second-degree murder. This Court should affirm that determination.

---

## II. THE ELEMENT OF MALICE IS ESSENTIAL TO SECOND-DEGREE MURDER

It is fundamental North Carolina law that second-degree murder requires proof of malice. As this Court has repeatedly held, murder in the second degree is "the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation." *State v. Robbins*, 309 N.C. 771, 775, 309 S.E.2d 188, 191 (1983); *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, "that crime does not exist in the absence of some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." *State v. Lang*, 309 N.C. 512, 518, 308 S.E.2d 317, 321 (1983).

The prosecution erroneously suggests that malice may be inferred from any reckless conduct. However, this Court has consistently required that the act be "intentional" in nature—that is, the defendant must engage in conduct that demonstrates a conscious disregard of the risk to human life. *See State v. Lang*, 309 N.C. at 518, 308 S.E.2d at 321. This requirement is not satisfied merely by showing that a defendant was intoxicated and engaged in dangerous conduct. Rather, the State must prove that the defendant acted with malice—that is, with a wanton and reckless disregard for human life.

---

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

### A. The Defendant's Alleged "Intentional" Conduct Was Not Intentional at All

The State contends that the Defendant's conduct leading up to the accident demonstrates malice. This argument fails because the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the Defendant as required on appellate review of sufficiency, establishes that the Defendant was incapable of forming the requisite intent or malice.

The facts presented at trial establish the following: The Defendant was struck by the tavern owner, fell, and hit his head on the base of a door. *See Record at 393.* The Defendant testified that he could not remember anything that occurred after being struck. *Id.* Hospital records confirmed that the Defendant had a blood alcohol level of .24 percent and "perhaps as high as .32 percent." *Id.* This level of intoxication is extraordinary—significantly exceeding the legal threshold for intoxication in most jurisdictions.

Critically, the Defendant attempted to offer testimony showing that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. *Id.* This evidence was not speculative; it was directly supported by the evidence of head trauma sustained in the altercation and the subsequent complete memory loss. The trial court's refusal to permit this evidence was clear error.

### B. The Unconsciousness Defense Was Wrongly Excluded

The trial court committed reversible error by refusing to permit the Defendant to produce evidence along the lines of unconsciousness and by refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense of unconsciousness applies when a defendant was not conscious of his actions due to a medical condition, such as head trauma or severe intoxication resulting in automatism. *See State v. Caddell*, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975); *State v. Prevette*, 43 N.C. App. 423, 259 S.E.2d 116 (1979).

The evidence in this case strongly supported an unconsciousness defense. The Defendant sustained a head injury in the altercation, was extremely intoxicated, and had no memory of the events. The jury should have been permitted to consider whether the Defendant was unconscious at the time of the accident and therefore incapable of forming malice. By excluding this defense, the trial court deprived the Defendant of a complete defense and prevented the jury from considering evidence critical to the element of malice.

### C. The Prosecution's Reliance on Intoxication Is Misplaced

The State argues that "extreme intoxication coupled with reckless conduct can satisfy the malice requirement." However, this proposition misstates North Carolina law. While intoxication may be relevant to establish that a defendant acted with malice, it cannot *create* malice where none exists. The question is whether the defendant acted with a reckless disregard for human life *while conscious and capable of understanding the nature of his acts*.

In *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), this Court upheld a second-degree murder conviction where the evidence showed the defendant intentionally engaged in conduct demonstrating malice—such as deliberately driving at high speed toward a group of people. The key element was the *intentional* nature of the conduct. Here, by contrast, the evidence suggests the Defendant may have been unconscious and therefore incapable of intentional conduct.

The prosecution's characterization of the Defendant's actions as "intentional" ignores the evidence of head trauma and unconsciousness. The Defendant did not "choose" to drive dangerously; the evidence suggests he may have been incapable of making any choice at all.

---

## IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY IDENTIFIED PLAIN ERROR

The Court of Appeals properly exercised its authority under the plain error doctrine by addressing the sufficiency of the evidence despite the Defendant's failure to raise the issue in his brief. *See State v. Odom*, 307 N.C. 273, 298 S.E.2d 696 (1984) (plain error doctrine permits appellate review of obvious constitutional errors even absent timely objection).

The Court of Appeals correctly concluded that there was no evidence of malice. The prosecution's case rested entirely on the inference that dangerous driving while intoxicated demonstrates malice. However, without evidence that the Defendant was conscious and capable of understanding the risks his conduct posed, this inference fails. The Defendant's complete memory loss, combined with the evidence of head trauma and extreme intoxication, at minimum creates a reasonable doubt as to whether malice existed.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **AFFIRM** the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the three second-degree murder convictions;

2. **REMAND** the case to the Court of Appeals for consideration of the Defendant's remaining appellate issues; and

3. **GRANT** any other relief this Court deems just and proper.

The State has failed to establish the essential element of malice. The evidence, when viewed in the light most favorable to the Defendant, shows that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident due to head trauma and severe intoxication. The trial court's refusal to permit the Defendant to present this defense was error, and the Court of Appeals correctly identified the resulting insufficiency of evidence. The convictions must be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF NORTH CAROLINA v. DEFENDANT**

**NO. 84A85**

**FILED: 1984**

**DECIDED: [Current Date]**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This case comes before the Court on the State’s petition for review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing three convictions for second-degree murder. The specific issue presented is whether the Court of Appeals erred in finding, *sua sponte*, that the evidence at trial was insufficient to establish the element of malice necessary for a conviction of second-degree murder. After careful consideration of the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, we conclude that the Court of Appeals committed legal error in its assessment of the evidence. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Murder in the second degree is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a requisite element of the crime, the State must prove the existence of "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE TO ESTABLISH MALICE**

The State argues, and we agree, that the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss for insufficiency. The evidence tends to show that the defendant, with a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, operated a motor vehicle on a public highway. Witnesses observed the defendant driving erratically, including passing another vehicle in a "no passing" zone. Subsequently, the defendant struck a motorcycle from behind with sufficient force to force it from the road, resulting in the death of three individuals.

Under the principles established in State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), the act of operating a vehicle while extremely intoxicated and subsequently violating traffic safety laws by passing in a no-passing zone constitutes an "intentional act" for the purposes of establishing malice. Such conduct demonstrates a reckless disregard for the safety of others. The evidence that the defendant passed a vehicle illegally and then collided with the motorcycle permits a reasonable inference that the defendant acted with malice.

The Court of Appeals concluded that there was "no evidence" of malice. This finding ignores the well-settled principle that malice can be implied from the wanton and reckless nature of the defendant's driving. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). The defendant’s extreme intoxication and his decision to drive, combined with his dangerous maneuvering of the vehicle, provide a sufficient evidentiary basis to support the jury’s finding of malice. Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions on the ground that the evidence was legally insufficient.

**III. THE DEFENSE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS**

The Defendant argues on appeal that the trial court erred by excluding evidence regarding his potential unconsciousness at the time of the accident. The defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975), and State v. Prevette, 43 N.C. App. 423, 259 S.E.2d 116 (1979), for the proposition that a defendant is not responsible if unconscious due to trauma or automatism.

While the defense of unconsciousness, if supported by competent evidence, can negate the "intentional act" required for malice, the admissibility of such evidence is a distinct matter from the sufficiency of the State's evidence. The trial court refused to permit the defendant to offer testimony on this issue. The Defendant contends this refusal was error. However, the Court of Appeals did not reverse the conviction based on an evidentiary error regarding the exclusion of testimony; rather, it reversed based on its finding that the State's evidence, standing alone, failed to show malice.

Because we find that the State's evidence was sufficient to support the conviction, the Court of Appeals' rationale for reversal—that there was "no evidence" of malice—is unsound. The issue of whether the trial court erred in excluding the defendant's evidence of unconsciousness is a separate legal question that was not the basis of the lower court's ruling and requires further consideration by the appellate court.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury by finding "plain error" based on a lack of evidence of malice. The evidence of the defendant's intoxication and reckless driving was sufficient to allow the jury to infer malice under the standards set forth in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983), and State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983).

Accordingly, we reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals. We remand this case to that court for consideration of the remaining issues raised by the Defendant, including the propriety of the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness defense and the sentencing findings, consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

## II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

### A. The Prosecution Misstates the Legal Standard

The prosecution incorrectly asserts that malice can be established solely through evidence of intoxication and dangerous driving. While State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), recognizes that malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a reckless disregard for human life, this Court has consistently held that the State must still prove "some intentional act" sufficient to show malice. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). The critical element is the voluntary, conscious nature of the defendant's conduct.

The prosecution's reliance upon State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), is misplaced. In Wilkerson, this Court found sufficient evidence of malice where the defendant had been driving at high speeds while being chased by police, had previously demonstrated awareness of his actions, and had specifically intended to elude capture. The circumstances in Wilkerson demonstrated a wanton indifference to human life that arose from the defendant's conscious choices. Here, by contrast, the defendant had no memory of the events, the evidence suggested he may have been unconscious, and there was no evidence whatsoever that he made conscious choices to endanger others.

### B. The Prosecution Improperly Shifts the Burden of Proof

The prosecution's argument improperly suggests that the defendant bears some burden to produce evidence negating malice. This fundamentally misunderstands the State's burden in a criminal prosecution. The State must prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, and the sufficiency of evidence must be assessed based solely upon the State's evidence, not upon the absence of defense evidence. However, when the State's own evidence raises substantial doubt about the defendant's conscious mental state, the State has failed to meet its burden.

Here, the State's evidence established that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent—levels at which unconsciousness is a recognized medical probability. The defendant could not remember any events after being struck by the tavern owner. The defendant offered to present evidence that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. This evidence, even if excluded from the jury's consideration, demonstrates that the State's evidence was insufficient to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant acted with malice.

### C. Intoxication Alone Does Not Establish Malice

The prosecution's assertion that "malice may be implied from the wanton and reckless nature of the defendant's driving" ignores the critical distinction between cases where the defendant is conscious and aware of his actions and cases where the defendant may be in an unconscious state. This Court has never held that intoxication, standing alone, establishes malice. See State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975) (holding that unconsciousness resulting from intoxication or trauma negates the intent required for criminal liability).

The prosecution cites no authority for the proposition that operating a vehicle while intoxicated, without more, constitutes sufficient evidence of malice for second-degree murder. Such a holding would effectively criminalize negligent driving by intoxicated individuals as murder—a result the Legislature has not enacted and this Court has not endorsed. **STATE OF NORTH CAROLINA v. DEFENDANT**

**NO. 84A85**

**FILED: 1984**

**DECIDED: [Current Date]**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This case comes before the Court on the State’s petition for review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing three convictions for second-degree murder. The specific issue is whether the Court of Appeals erred in finding, *sua sponte*, that the evidence at trial was insufficient to establish the element of malice necessary for a conviction of second-degree murder. After reviewing the rebuttal arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, we conclude that the Court of Appeals committed legal error in its assessment of the evidence. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Murder in the second degree is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a requisite element of the crime, the State must prove the existence of "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE TO ESTABLISH MALICE**

The State argues, and we agree, that the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss for insufficiency. The evidence tends to show that the defendant, with a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, operated a motor vehicle on a public highway. Witnesses observed the defendant driving erratically, including passing another vehicle in a "no passing" zone. Subsequently, the defendant struck a motorcycle from behind with sufficient force to force it from the road, resulting in the death of three individuals.

The Defense argues in its rebuttal that the State's evidence was insufficient because it failed to establish that the defendant was conscious at the time of the accident. The Defense contends that the defendant's high blood alcohol level and the head trauma sustained at the bar created a reasonable probability of unconsciousness, thereby negating the "intentional act" required for malice. While we acknowledge the Defense's reliance on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975), for the proposition that unconsciousness negates criminal liability, we find that the State's evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, permitted the jury to infer that the defendant was conscious and acting with malice.

The evidence established that the defendant stood up after being struck, walked to his car, entered the vehicle, started the engine, navigated onto the highway, and executed a passing maneuver in a "no passing" zone. These are complex, voluntary motor functions that support a reasonable inference of consciousness. As held in State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), the act of operating a vehicle while extremely intoxicated and subsequently violating traffic safety laws constitutes an "intentional act" for the purposes of establishing malice. Such conduct demonstrates a reckless disregard for the safety of others. The fact that the defendant was extremely intoxicated does not, as a matter of law, render his actions involuntary or unconscious to the degree that malice cannot be implied. The State is not required to negate the possibility of unconsciousness to survive a motion to dismiss; it is sufficient that the evidence supports the inference of malice.

The Court of Appeals concluded that there was "no evidence" of malice. This finding ignores the well-settled principle that malice can be implied from the wanton and reckless nature of the defendant's driving. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). The defendant’s extreme intoxication and his decision to drive, combined with his dangerous maneuvering of the vehicle, provide a sufficient evidentiary basis to support the jury’s finding of malice. Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions on the ground that the evidence was legally insufficient.

**III. THE DEFENSE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS**

The Defendant argues on appeal that the trial court erred by excluding evidence regarding his potential unconsciousness at the time of the accident. The defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975), and State v. Prevette, 43 N.C. App. 423, 259 S.E.2d 116 (1979), for the proposition that a defendant is not responsible if unconscious due to trauma or automatism. The Defense contends that the exclusion of this evidence was prejudicial error that prevented the jury from considering a complete defense.

While the defense of unconsciousness, if supported by competent evidence, can negate the "intentional act" required for malice, the admissibility of such evidence is a distinct matter from the sufficiency of the State's evidence. The trial court refused to permit the defendant to offer testimony on this issue. The Defendant contends this refusal was error. However, the Court of Appeals did not reverse the conviction based on an evidentiary error regarding the exclusion of testimony; rather, it reversed based on its finding that the State's evidence, standing alone, failed to show malice.

Because we find that the State's evidence was sufficient to support the conviction, the Court of Appeals' rationale for reversal—that there was "no evidence" of malice—is unsound. The issue of whether the trial court erred in excluding the defendant's evidence of unconsciousness is a separate legal question that was not the basis of the lower court's ruling and requires further consideration by the appellate court. The Defense's argument that the exclusion of evidence created a "vacuum" which the State improperly exploited is an argument regarding the weight of the evidence and the fairness of the trial procedures, not the sufficiency of the evidence to establish malice. As stated in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983), the sufficiency of the evidence is determined by the State's case alone.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury by finding "plain error" based on a lack of evidence of malice. The evidence of the defendant's intoxication and reckless driving was sufficient to allow the jury to infer malice under the standards set forth in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983), and State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). The Defense's arguments regarding the defendant's potential unconsciousness, while relevant to the merits of the appeal, do not alter the fact that the State presented sufficient evidence to survive a motion to dismiss.

Accordingly, we reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals. We remand this case to that court for consideration of the remaining issues raised by the Defendant, including the propriety of the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness defense and the sentencing findings, consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

Your Honor, Members of the Court:

The Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and in support of maintaining the Court of Appeals reversal of the defendant's three second-degree murder convictions. For the reasons set forth below, the Defense maintains that the evidence presented at trial was legally insufficient to establish the element of malice, and that the Court of Appeals properly identified this fundamental deficiency.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The defendant, having been convicted of three counts of second-degree murder, appealed his convictions to the Court of Appeals. That court, exercising its authority under North Carolina Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), identified plain error in the trial proceedings—specifically, the absence of any evidence establishing the element of malice essential to second-degree murder. The Defense acknowledges that the Court of Appeals raised this issue sua sponte, but maintains that this procedural mechanism was proper and necessary to prevent a manifest miscarriage of justice where the State failed to meet its constitutional burden of proof.

The prosecution asks this Court to reinstate the convictions by arguing that sufficient evidence of malice exists. The Defense responds that the prosecution's characterization of the evidence misapplies controlling legal standards, ignores critical facts demonstrating the defendant's likely unconsciousness, and improperly shifts the burden of proof. The Court of Appeals correctly identified the absence of malice evidence, and its decision should be affirmed.

## II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

### A. The Legal Standard for Second-Degree Murder

Murder in the second degree requires proof that the defendant unlawfully killed a human being "with malice but without premeditation and deliberation." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a requisite element, the State must nonetheless prove "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

The Defense does not dispute these legal standards. Rather, the Defense maintains that the evidence presented at trial failed to satisfy these requirements.

### B. The Prosecution's Evidence Failed to Establish Malice

The prosecution contends that the defendant's conduct—operating a motor vehicle while intoxicated, passing another vehicle in a no-passing zone, and striking a motorcycle—establishes malice through implied malice via reckless disregard for human life. The Defense submits that this argument conflates the act of driving while intoxicated with the specific mental state required for second-degree murder, and ignores critical evidence suggesting the defendant was unconscious at the time of the collisions.

The facts established at trial demonstrate the following: the defendant was involved in an altercation at Smokey's Lounge during which he was struck twice by the bar proprietor, fell, and struck his head on the base of a door. The defendant was described as "loud, vulgar, rowdy and drunk" prior to the altercation. After being struck, the defendant fell and hit his head. He then "rose," staggered to his car, and drove away. His blood alcohol level was later measured at between .24 percent and .32 percent—levels indicating severe intoxication.

The prosecution asks this Court to infer malice from the defendant's actions in driving his vehicle. However, such an inference requires proof that the defendant was conscious and acting voluntarily at the time of the collisions. The prosecution presented no direct evidence establishing the defendant's conscious state during the critical period when the collisions occurred. The prosecution's entire case rests on the circumstantial evidence that the defendant was able to perform certain physical actions—standing, walking, entering a vehicle, and driving.

The Defense submits that this evidence is insufficient for several reasons.

### C. The Evidence of Consciousness Was Inadequate

The prosecution argues that the defendant's ability to perform "complex, voluntary motor functions" establishes consciousness. However, the prosecution's reasoning proves too much. The mere performance of physical acts does not establish that those acts were undertaken with the malicious mental state required for second-degree murder. More fundamentally, the prosecution's argument fails to account for the well-established principle that human beings can perform complex physical actions while unconscious or in an automatistic state.

The Defense presented evidence through the prosecution's own witnesses that the defendant had consumed substantial alcohol, had been struck in the head causing him to fall and hit his head a second time, and had blood alcohol levels between .24 and .32 percent. The defendant attempted to offer testimony that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident, but the trial court refused to permit such evidence. This evidentiary exclusion, which the Defense contends was error, left the jury without critical information bearing on the defendant's mental state.

The prosecution cites State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), for the proposition that operating a vehicle while extremely intoxicated constitutes an "intentional act" sufficient to establish malice. However, Lang is distinguishable. In Lang, the defendant was aware of his intoxication and chose to drive anyway—a textbook example of conscious, voluntary conduct demonstrating reckless disregard for human life. Here, by contrast, the defendant was rendered unconscious by head trauma combined with extreme intoxication. The prosecution presented no evidence establishing that the defendant was aware of his actions or could have formed the malicious mental state required for second-degree murder.

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), is similarly misplaced. In Wilkerson, the defendant intentionally fired a weapon at a police officer, demonstrating clear conscious awareness of his actions. The instant case presents a fundamentally different scenario—one involving a defendant who was rendered unconscious by head trauma and whose actions, if any, were performed in a state of automatism.

The Defense maintains that the prosecution's evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, failed to establish that the defendant was conscious at the time of the collisions. Without proof of consciousness, there can be no proof of the "intentional act" required to establish malice. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983).

## III. THE DEFENSE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

It is well established in North Carolina that criminal liability requires proof of some intentional act. State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975). When a defendant acts while unconscious—whether due to sleep, automatism, or trauma—that act cannot form the basis of criminal liability because it is not "intentional" in the legal sense. Id. As this Court held in State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975), unconsciousness negates the mental element required for criminal responsibility.

Similarly, in State v. Prevette, 43 N.C. App. 423, 259 S.E.2d 116 (1979), the North Carolina Court of Appeals held that evidence of automatism—a state in which a person performs physical acts without conscious volition—can negate criminal liability if presented to the jury.

### B. The Trial Court's Error in Excluding Unconsciousness Evidence

The trial court committed reversible error by refusing to permit the defendant to offer testimony showing that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. The trial court also erred by refusing to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness.

The evidence before the trial court established: (1) the defendant was struck in the head and fell, striking his head a second time; (2) the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent; (3) the defendant could not remember anything after being struck at the bar; and (4) the defendant exhibited behavior consistent with someone who was unconscious or in an automatistic state.

This evidence was sufficient to require the trial court to permit the defendant to present his unconsciousness defense to the jury. The trial court's refusal to allow such evidence deprived the defendant of his right to present a complete defense and prevented the jury from considering whether the defendant possessed the requisite mental state for second-degree murder.

The prosecution argues that the exclusion of unconsciousness evidence is irrelevant to the sufficiency of the State's case. This argument misapprehends the relationship between these issues. The State's burden is to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including malice. If the State's evidence, considered in light of the defendant's proffered unconsciousness evidence, fails to establish malice, the convictions cannot stand. The excluded evidence was not merely relevant—it was potentially dispositive of the central issue in the case.

### C. The Prosecution's Burden Cannot Be Discharged by Excluding Defense Evidence

The prosecution argues that it need not "negate every possible defense theory" to survive a motion to dismiss. This is correct as far as it goes. However, the prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: (1) the State's burden to affirmatively prove each element of the crime, and (2) the trial court's authority to exclude relevant defense evidence.

The State's burden is to prove malice beyond a reasonable doubt. The prosecution cannot satisfy this burden by relying on the exclusion of defense evidence. Here, the prosecution's evidence, standing alone, failed to establish that the defendant was conscious at the time of the collisions. The excluded unconsciousness evidence would have further undermined the prosecution's case by demonstrating the defendant's likely lack of conscious awareness.

The prosecution's argument that "sufficiency is assessed based solely upon the State's evidence, not upon the absence of defense evidence," as stated in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983), is correct in the abstract. However, Robbins does not stand for the proposition that the State can benefit from the erroneous exclusion of defense evidence. Here, the trial court's exclusion of unconsciousness evidence prevented the jury from considering a complete picture of the defendant's mental state. This exclusion infected the proceedings and contributed to the jury's inability to properly evaluate the prosecution's case.

## IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS PROPERLY IDENTIFIED PLAIN ERROR

The prosecution argues that the Court of Appeals erred by invoking plain error sua sponte. The Defense responds that the Court of Appeals' identification of plain error was proper and necessary to prevent a manifest miscarriage of justice.

Under North Carolina law, plain error review is appropriate where there is "obvious defect affecting substantial rights" and where the error "would result in a miscarriage of justice." State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 273, 298 S.E.2d 696 (1984). Here, the Court of Appeals identified a fundamental deficiency in the prosecution's case—the absence of any evidence establishing malice. This deficiency constitutes obvious error affecting substantial rights. The prosecution failed to present evidence establishing that the defendant was conscious at the time of the collisions, without which there can be no proof of the "intentional act" required for second-degree murder.

The prosecution argues that "there was no 'plain error' because the State's evidence was legally sufficient." This argument is circular. The Court of Appeals found that the evidence was insufficient—that is precisely the plain error identified. The prosecution cannot bootstrap its way to sufficiency by arguing that no error occurred.

The Court of Appeals properly exercised its authority under Rule 52(b) to identify the manifest injustice that would result from allowing convictions to stand where no evidence of malice was presented.

## V. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's closing statement makes several specific arguments that warrant response.

The prosecution presented no evidence establishing the defendant's mental state during the critical period.

Second, the prosecution argues that "extreme intoxication does not, as a matter of law, render driving involuntary or unconscious to the degree that malice cannot be implied." This is a correct statement of law, but it does not support the prosecution's position. The prosecution must still prove that the defendant was conscious and acting voluntarily. The prosecution cannot rely on the mere fact of intoxication to establish consciousness; it must affirmatively prove consciousness. Here, the prosecution failed to do so.

Third, the prosecution argues that the defendant "bears no burden to prove unconsciousness." This is also correct, but it does not assist the prosecution. The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of second-degree murder, including malice. **STATE OF NORTH CAROLINA v. DEFENDANT**

**NO. 84A85**

**FILED: 1984**

**DECIDED: [Current Date]**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This case returns before us upon the State’s petition for review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing three convictions for second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, *sua sponte*, that the trial court committed plain error because there was "no evidence of malice." After reviewing the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, we reaffirm our preliminary view that the Court of Appeals erred in its assessment of the evidence. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW AND LEGAL FRAMEWORK**

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not required, the State must prove "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from evidence showing a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

On appeal, when reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence, we must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, giving the State the benefit of every reasonable inference. The State is not required to prove the defendant’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt to the exclusion of every reasonable hypothesis of innocence; rather, it must only produce substantial evidence of each element of the offense. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE TO ESTABLISH MALICE**

The central issue is whether the Court of Appeals correctly determined that there was "no evidence" of malice. The Defense argues in its closing statement that the State’s evidence was insufficient because it failed to establish that the defendant was conscious at the time of the accident. The Defense contends that the defendant’s head trauma and extreme intoxication (.24 to .32 percent blood alcohol) created a reasonable probability of unconsciousness, thereby negating the "intentional act" required for malice.

We find the Defense’s argument unpersuasive in light of the standard of review. The State presented evidence that the defendant, after being involved in an altercation at Smokey’s Lounge, stood up, walked to his car, entered the vehicle, started the engine, drove onto Highway 311, passed another vehicle in a "no passing" zone, and subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind with sufficient force to force it from the road. These actions constitute "complex, voluntary motor functions" that support a reasonable inference of consciousness and intent.

As held in State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), the act of operating a vehicle while extremely intoxicated and subsequently violating traffic safety laws constitutes an "intentional act" for the purposes of establishing malice. The evidence of the defendant’s erratic driving—specifically, passing in a "no passing" zone and forcing a motorcycle off the road—demonstrates a reckless disregard for the safety of others sufficient to imply malice. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978).

The Defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 689, 215 S.E.2d 579 (1975), and State v. Prevette, 43 N.C. App. 423, 259 S.E.2d 116 (1979), to argue that unconsciousness negates criminal liability. While this is a correct statement of law, the applicability of the unconsciousness defense is a question of fact for the jury, provided there is competent evidence to support it. The State is not required to negate the possibility of unconsciousness to survive a motion to dismiss. It is sufficient that the State’s evidence, standing alone, permits a jury to infer that the defendant was conscious and acting with malice. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

The Court of Appeals concluded that there was "no evidence" of malice. This was error. The defendant’s decision to drive while highly intoxicated, combined with his dangerous maneuvering of the vehicle, provided a sufficient evidentiary basis for the jury’s finding of malice. The fact that the defendant may have been unconscious is a defense theory that, if supported by evidence, is for the jury to weigh against the State's evidence of voluntary action. It does not render the State's evidence legally insufficient.

**III. THE EXCLUSION OF EVIDENCE AND PLAIN ERROR**

The Defense argues vigorously that the trial court erred by excluding evidence regarding the defendant’s potential unconsciousness and that the Court of Appeals properly identified this as plain error. The Defense contends that the exclusion of this evidence created a "vacuum" which prevented the jury from considering a complete defense.

We acknowledge the Defense’s contention that the trial court’s refusal to allow testimony on unconsciousness may have been prejudicial. However, the Court of Appeals did not reverse the conviction on the basis of an evidentiary error; it reversed on the finding that the State’s evidence was legally insufficient to establish malice. These are distinct legal concepts.

Plain error review is reserved for "obvious defects affecting substantial rights" where the error is "so fundamental that it would result in a miscarriage of justice." State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 273, 298 S.E.2d 696 (1984). Because the State presented substantial evidence of malice through the defendant's conduct, there was no "obvious defect" in the sufficiency of the evidence. The Court of Appeals improperly invoked plain error to correct a perceived deficiency in the State's case where none existed.

The Defense’s argument that the State benefited from the exclusion of evidence highlights a potential trial error, but it does not justify a reversal based on a lack of evidence. The proper remedy for the exclusion of the defense's unconsciousness evidence, if found to be error on appeal, is a new trial where that evidence can be heard. It is not a judicial determination that the State's evidence was legally insufficient to support the verdict.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury by finding "plain error" based on a lack of evidence of malice. The evidence of the defendant's intoxication and reckless driving was sufficient to allow the jury to infer malice under the standards set forth in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983), and State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). The Defense's arguments regarding the defendant's potential unconsciousness, while relevant to the fairness of the trial proceedings, do not alter the fact that the State presented sufficient evidence to survive a motion to dismiss.

Accordingly, we reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals. We remand this case to that court for consideration of the remaining issues raised by the Defendant, specifically the propriety of the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness defense and the sentencing findings, consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
