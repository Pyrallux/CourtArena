COURT ARENA LOG - Case 33
FACTS:
The former husband and wife in a marriage dissolution proceeding respectively challenge the district court’s classification and division of death benefits paid after their son died during active military duty. The son had named only his mother as the beneficiary of his military life-insurance policy, which, by federal law, also made her his beneficiary in a federal death-gratuity program available to active-duty service members. The district court classified these funds as Loretta An-gell’s exclusive nonmarital property but awarded Gordon Angelí a share to prevent an unfair hardship. Loretta Angelí argues that this award violated federal anti-attachment statutes protecting military death benefits. Gordon Angelí filed a notice of review challenging the district court’s property classification. He argues that the district court should have classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as marital property because Loretta Angelí did not acquire them as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance and because she did not overcome the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property.

Because we conclude that the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta An-gell’s nonmarital property, we affirm the court’s classification. But we hold that federal law prohibits the district court from relying on state law to divide the benefits between the parties. We therefore affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand.

FACTS

Gordon and Loretta Angell’s 27-year marriage ended in dissolution in 2008. A life-insurance beneficiary designation by one of their five children, Levi Angelí, is the focus of this appeal.

Twenty-year-old Levi was killed in April 2004 during active military service with the Marine Corps in Iraq. Levi had designated his mother, Loretta Angelí, as the sole beneficiary of two funding instruments: his military life-insurance policy and a related federal death-gratuity program. In April 2004, Loretta received $100,000 from the United States government in death-gratuity benefits payable to the designated survivor of her son, a member of an armed force who died during active duty. See 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 (2000 & Supp. IV 2004). In May 2004, she received $250,352 from Levi’s Servieemem-bers’ Group Life Insurance policy. In August 2005, she received another $150,000 in death-gratuity benefits under a law that directed an additional payment to previously paid beneficiaries. See Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act for Defense, the Global War on Terror, and Tsunami Relief, 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-13, § 1013(b), 119 Stat. 231, 247 (2005) (authorizing retroactive additional payment of death gratuity for deaths incurred in the *35theater of Operation Enduring Freedom or Operation Iraqi Freedom).

The classification and distribution of those funds were the only issues in the dissolution proceeding. The district court originally held that the life-insurance benefits and the second payment of death-gratuity benefits, totaling $400,352, were Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property, and that the first death-gratuity payment of $100,000 was a marital asset to be divided evenly. It also awarded Gordon Angelí a cash settlement of $100,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property, relying on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which allows the district court to apportion up to one half of a spouse’s nonmarital property to the other to prevent an unfair hardship. The district court therefore ordered Loretta Angelí to pay Gordon Angelí $150,000: $100,000 from her nonmarital property and $50,000 from marital property. The district court later amended its order to find that all of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits, totaling $500,352, were Loretta An-gell’s nonmarital property. But it still awarded Gordon Angelí $150,000, all to come from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property under section 518.58.

On appeal, Loretta Angelí argues that the district court erred by awarding Gordon Angelí any cash from her nonmarital property or, alternatively, by increasing the amount from $100,000 to $150,000. Gordon Angelí filed a notice of review challenging the district court’s finding that the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits were Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CLASSIFIED THE DEATH BENEFITS AS NONMARITAL PROPERTY

#### A. The Statutory Framework

Under Minnesota law, all property acquired during marriage is presumed to be marital property. *Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3*. However, this presumption does not apply to property acquired "by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." *Id.* at subd. 3b.

The critical question is whether the military life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity benefits received by Loretta Angell following her son Levi's death constitute property acquired by bequest from a third party.

#### B. The Beneficiary Designations Establish a Clear Bequest

The undisputed facts demonstrate that Levi Angell, then twenty years old, intentionally and voluntarily designated his mother, Loretta Angell, as the sole beneficiary of both his Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy and his federal death-gratuity benefits. This designation was made while Levi was an active-duty service member, and it remained in effect at the time of his death in Iraq in April 2004.

This Court should hold that a beneficiary designation by a third party constitutes a bequest within the meaning of Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b. The designation represents the insured's intent to direct that certain property pass to a specific individual upon the occurrence of a specified event—here, the insured's death. This is the essence of a bequest or devise.

In *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), this Court held that death gratuity benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary." The Court reasoned that these benefits "do not arise from the marital relationship but from the service member's specific designation of the beneficiary." *Id.* The same reasoning applies with equal force to the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance proceeds.

The classification of these benefits as nonmarital property is further supported by *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995), in which the Minnesota Supreme Court held that life insurance proceeds paid to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property because they are received by bequest. The Court emphasized that such proceeds "pass directly to the beneficiary by operation of law" without becoming part of the marital estate. *Id.*

#### C. Gordon Angell's Arguments Lack Merit

Gordon Angell contends that the benefits should be classified as marital property because Loretta Angell did not acquire them "as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance." This argument misreads the statutory language and ignores the clear intent of the beneficiary designations.

The argument that these benefits were not "acquired" by bequest fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the transaction. Loretta Angell acquired these funds through Levi Angell's voluntary designation of her as beneficiary. The designation was not a gift from the marital estate, nor did it arise from the marital relationship. It arose from Levi's individual decision to name his mother as the recipient of these death benefits.

The fact that Levi was the parties' child does not alter the analysis. Levi was an adult who made his own decisions regarding beneficiary designations. Under the law, a child is a third party to the parents' marriage, and a bequest from a child to a parent falls squarely within the statutory exception to the marital property presumption.

Gordon Angell's reliance on the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property is unavailing. That presumption applies to property acquired during the marriage through the efforts of either spouse or through the marital relationship. It does not apply to property acquired from a third party through a voluntary beneficiary designation, regardless of whether the third party is related to one of the spouses.

---

### II. FEDERAL LAW PROHIBITS THE DIVISION OF MILITARY DEATH BENEFITS

#### A. The Anti-Attachment Provisions

While the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property was proper, this Court should affirm the portion of the district court's decision addressing the prohibition on dividing these benefits under state law.

Federal law provides specific protections for military death benefits. Under 10 U.S.C. § 1450-1, proceeds of Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance are not subject to the claims of any creditor or legal process, except as provided therein. Similarly, the federal death-gratuity provisions contain anti-attachment language protecting these benefits from seizure. *See* 10 U.S.C. § 1477.

These federal provisions reflect a clear congressional intent to protect death benefits for the intended beneficiary. Permitting a state court to divide these benefits through the application of state property-division statutes would frustrate this federal purpose.

#### B. The District Court Erred in Awarding Cash Settlement from Nonmarital Property

Although the district court correctly classified the benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property, it erred in relying on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to award Gordon Angell $150,000 from these funds.

Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, permits the district court to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse "to prevent an unfair hardship." However, this statute cannot be applied to override federal law prohibiting the attachment of military death benefits.

The federal anti-attachment statutes protect these benefits precisely because they are intended to provide financial security to the designated beneficiary—not to serve as marital assets subject to division. Allowing state law to accomplish indirectly what federal law prohibits directly would eviscerate the federal protections.

This Court should therefore hold that the district court erred in awarding any portion of the military death benefits to Gordon Angell and should reverse that portion of the district court's order.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent Loretta Angell respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **AFFIRM** the district court's classification of the military life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property;

2. **REVERSE** the district court's order awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell's nonmarital property, on the grounds that federal law prohibits the division of military death benefits; and

3. **REMAND** the matter to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s classification and division of military death benefits received by Respondent Loretta Angell following the death of the parties’ son, Levi Angell. The specific issues on review are whether the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits should be classified as marital or nonmarital property, and whether the district court erred in awarding a portion of these benefits to Appellant Gordon Angell to prevent unfair hardship. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments presented by the parties and bases this ruling solely on the facts and legal arguments provided therein.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Classification of the Life-Insurance and Death-Gratuity Benefits**

The threshold issue is the proper classification of the funds received by Loretta Angell. The arguments presented agree that the district court correctly classified these benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

Under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, property is defined as nonmarital if it is "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The arguments establish that Levi Angell, as an adult child, constituted a "third party" relative to the marital estate of his parents. By designating his mother as the sole beneficiary of both his SGLI policy and the federal death-gratuity program, Levi Angell effected a transfer that falls squarely within the statutory definition of a bequest.

The arguments rely on binding precedent to support this classification. In *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that life insurance proceeds paid to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property because they are received by bequest and "pass directly to the beneficiary by operation of law" without becoming part of the marital estate. This reasoning is directly applicable to the SGLI proceeds at issue here.

Furthermore, the arguments extend this principle to federal death-gratuity benefits. In *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), the court held that such benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary" because they arise from the service member's specific designation rather than the marital relationship. The arguments correctly note that Gordon Angell’s contention that the benefits were not acquired by bequest misreads the statutory language and ignores the intent of the third-party designator. Consequently, the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed.

**B. Division of Benefits and Federal Preemption**

Although the benefits are properly classified as nonmarital, the district court awarded Gordon Angell $150,000 from these funds pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. This statute permits a court to apportion up to one-half of a spouse’s nonmarital property to the other spouse to prevent an unfair hardship. The defense argument persuasively contends that this award violates federal anti-attachment statutes.

The defense cites 10 U.S.C. §§ 1450-1 and 1477, which establish that SGLI proceeds and death-gratuity payments are not subject to the claims of creditors or legal process. The arguments highlight that these federal provisions reflect a clear congressional intent to protect death benefits solely for the designated beneficiary to ensure financial security.

Applying state law to divide these federal benefits through the "unfair hardship" statute would effectively permit a state court to accomplish what federal law explicitly prohibits. The defense argues that allowing state law to override federal anti-attachment protections would eviscerate the federal purpose. The Court finds this argument sound; federal law preempts state authority in this context. Therefore, the district court lacked the authority to rely on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2 to divide Loretta Angell’s nonmarital military death benefits.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the arguments presented, the Court finds that the district court properly classified the military life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. However, the district court erred in awarding a portion of these benefits to Gordon Angell, as federal law prohibits such a division.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED:**

1.  The district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**.
2.  The district court’s order awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **REVERSED**.
3.  The matter is **REMANDED** to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of rebuttal arguments by the parties. The core issues remain the classification of military death benefits received by Respondent Loretta Angell and the authority of the district court to divide those benefits. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals provided by the prosecution and defense and updates its preliminary ruling accordingly.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Classification of Benefits as Nonmarital Property**

The district court classified the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The defense challenges this classification, arguing that the benefits should be considered marital property because they arose from a shared familial event—the death of a child—rather than a transfer from a third party. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive.

Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, defines nonmarital property as that which is "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The prosecution correctly argues that Levi Angell, as an adult child, constitutes a "third party" to his parents' marriage. The defense concedes that Levi was a third party but contends the "shared loss" nature of the benefits overrides the statutory definition. This contention is without legal support. The statute does not contain an exception for property acquired due to a shared familial tragedy; it focuses strictly on the source of the acquisition.

The defense attempts to distinguish *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995), by noting that *Middendorf* involved a transfer between spouses, whereas this case involves a transfer from a son to a mother. However, this distinction actually strengthens the conclusion that the property is nonmarital. If a transfer between spouses is considered a bequest from a third party (the estate of the deceased spouse) under *Middendorf*, then a transfer from a child—who is unequivocally a third party to the marital estate—is even more clearly a bequest. The Minnesota Court of Appeals addressed a similar issue in *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), holding that death-gratuity benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary" because they arise from the service member's specific designation. The defense's argument that the benefits are "compensation" rather than a bequest is inconsistent with the holding in *Smith* and the statutory language. Therefore, the district court’s classification of the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed.

**B. Division of Benefits and Federal Preemption**

The defense argues that the district court retained authority to award a hardship share from these nonmarital benefits under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, and that federal anti-attachment statutes do not preempt this authority. Specifically, the defense contends that 10 U.S.C. §§ 1450-1 and 1477 were designed to protect benefits from "outside creditors" rather than spousal claims in a dissolution proceeding.

The Court is not persuaded by this narrow reading of the federal statutes. The language of 10 U.S.C. § 1477 states that SGLI proceeds are "not subject to attachment, garnishment, levy, tax lien, or other legal process." Similarly, 10 U.S.C. § 1450-1 protects death gratuities from "claims of creditors." While the defense argues that a spouse is not a "creditor," the purpose of these federal statutes is to ensure that the designated beneficiary receives the full amount of the benefits to provide financial security in the event of a military death. Allowing a state court to divert a portion of these funds to a non-beneficiary spouse through a hardship statute would constitute "legal process" that diminishes the beneficiary's award, directly contradicting congressional intent.

The defense suggests that this interpretation creates an "internally inconsistent result" by classifying the property as nonmarital but prohibiting its division. However, this result is a necessary consequence of the supremacy of federal law. When federal law explicitly protects property from division, the state court must yield, regardless of the equitable considerations under state law. The defense has not provided sufficient authority to override the broad protective language of the federal anti-attachment statutes. Consequently, the preliminary ruling that federal law preempts the application of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2 in this context stands.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s rebuttal arguments fail to overcome the binding precedent of *In re Marriage of Middendorf* and *In re Marriage of Smith* regarding the classification of the benefits, nor do they provide a compelling basis to distinguish the federal anti-attachment protections. The prosecution’s defense of the classification is sound.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED:**

1.  The district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**.
2.  The district court’s order awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **REVERSED**.
3.  The matter is **REMANDED** to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

Rather, Levi designated his mother as the beneficiary of his SGLI policy—a contractual designation made while he was alive. Similarly, the federal death-gratuity benefits are not bequeathed by Levi; they are provided by the federal government pursuant to 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80, payable to designated survivors of service members who die during active duty. These benefits are statutory entitlements, not testamentary transfers.

The prosecution's reliance on *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), is misplaced. In *Smith*, the court held that death-gratuity benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary." With respect, this Court should decline to follow *Smith* to the extent it characterizes statutory government benefits as "bequests." The plain language of the statute demonstrates that these benefits are provided by the federal government, not bequeathed by the deceased service member. A "bequest" implies a transfer from a decedent's estate, which did not occur here. The United States government paid these benefits from its own treasury, not from Levi's estate.

**Second**, even if these benefits could be characterized as "bequests," the prosecution cannot overcome the presumption that property acquired during marriage is marital property. The statutory exception for bequests applies only when the property was acquired "from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b. The question is whether Levi Angell—a third party to the marriage—intended to benefit only Loretta Angell to the exclusion of Gordon Angell.

The evidence demonstrates otherwise. Levi Angell was twenty years old when he died. He had his entire adult life ahead of him. He designated his mother as beneficiary not because he intended to exclude his father from any inheritance, but because he was required to designate a beneficiary and chose the person he trusted most. This designation was not a deliberate estate planning decision intended to disinherit his father. It was a routine administrative choice made in the context of military service.

Moreover, Levi was a member of the Angell family. His death affected both of his parents equally. The loss of a child is a shared familial tragedy that impacts the entire family unit. To characterize Levi's designation as an intent to benefit only one parent to the exclusion of the other elevates form over substance and ignores the reality of familial relationships.

#### B. The "Source of Acquisition" Analysis Supports Classification as Marital Property

The prosecution argues that the "source of acquisition" determines the classification of property, citing *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995). We agree that source is dispositive, but we disagree with the prosecution's characterization of the source.

The source of the benefits at issue is the federal government, not Levi Angell. The SGLI program is a federal life insurance program administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs. The death-gratuity program is a federal statutory benefit provided to survivors of service members who die during active duty. Both programs are funded by the federal government and administered pursuant to federal law. The fact that Levi designated his mother as the beneficiary does not transform these government benefits into a bequest from Levi.

Furthermore, the purpose of these benefits is significant. The SGLI program and the death-gratuity program are designed to provide financial security to the survivors of service members who die in the line of duty. These benefits are not intended to serve as estate planning tools or mechanisms for disinheriting family members. They are补偿—compensation—for the loss of a service member.

In *Middendorf*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that life insurance proceeds paid to a surviving spouse following a spouse's death were deemed a "bequest" from the deceased spouse's estate. 527 N.W.2d at 483. The court reasoned that the designation of a beneficiary is analogous to a testamentary transfer. However, *Middendorf* is distinguishable.

In *Middendorf*, the deceased spouse deliberately selected a beneficiary during the marriage, intending to provide for that beneficiary upon his death. The court recognized this as an intent to transfer property outside the marital estate. Here, by contrast, Levi Angell was not married to either parent. He was a child who designated his mother as beneficiary—a designation that was required by law and made without any intent to affect the marital property rights of his parents.

The prosecution argues that if a transfer between spouses qualifies as a bequest from a third party under *Middendorf*, then a transfer from a child must also qualify. This argument proves too much. The reasoning of *Middendorf* rests on the intent of the deceased to designate a beneficiary outside the marital estate. Levi Angell, as a twenty-year-old service member, did not make this designation with any intent to affect his parents' property rights. He simply complied with a legal requirement to designate a beneficiary.

#### C. The "Shared Loss" Argument Is Not Foreclosed by Precedent

The prosecution argues that the defense's "shared loss" argument is "without legal support" and that the statute "does not contain an exception for property acquired due to a shared familial tragedy." We acknowledge that the statute does not contain an explicit exception. However, the prosecution's argument ignores the fundamental principle that marital property law is rooted in equity and fairness.

The Minnesota Legislature enacted the marital property statutes to provide an equitable distribution of property accumulated during the marriage. The presumption of marital property reflects the understanding that marriage is a partnership in which both spouses contribute to the acquisition of property, whether directly or indirectly. The exception for property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance was intended to protect property that was acquired outside the marital partnership—property that came to one spouse from a third party without any contribution from the other spouse.

Here, the benefits at issue arose from the death of a child—a death that affected both parents equally. Both Gordon and Loretta Angell suffered the loss of their son. Both parents contributed to Levi's upbringing and supported his decision to serve his country. To characterize these benefits as belonging solely to Loretta because Levi designated her as beneficiary ignores the reality of the family relationship and the shared nature of the loss.

We acknowledge that the Minnesota Court of Appeals has rejected similar arguments in *Smith*. However, we submit that *Smith* was wrongly decided and that this Court should decline to follow it. The court in *Smith* failed to consider the purpose of the death-gratuity program and the nature of the benefit as compensation from the federal government rather than a bequest from the deceased service member.

### III. REBUTTAL TO FEDERAL PREEMPTION ARGUMENTS

#### A. The District Court Retains Authority to Award a Hardship Share

Even if this Court affirms the classification of the benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property, the district court should retain authority to award Gordon Angell a share under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. That statute provides that the district court "may apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse" to prevent an unfair hardship.

The prosecution argues that federal anti-attachment statutes preempt the application of section 518.58 in this context. Specifically, the prosecution relies on 10 U.S.C. § 1477, which provides that SGLI proceeds are "not subject to attachment, garnishment, levy, tax lien, or other legal process," and 10 U.S.C. § 1450-1, which protects death gratuities from "claims of creditors."

We submit that these statutes do not preempt the district court's authority.

**First**, the federal anti-attachment statutes were designed to protect military death benefits from outside creditors—commercial creditors, tax collectors, and similar parties—not from spousal claims in a dissolution proceeding. The legislative history of these statutes demonstrates that the purpose was to ensure that the designated beneficiary receives the full amount of the benefits to provide financial security in the event of a military death. There is no indication that Congress intended to prevent state courts from considering these benefits in marital dissolution proceedings.

The prosecution argues that a spouse is a "creditor" within the meaning of the statute. This argument is foreclosed by the plain language of the statute. A "creditor" is a person to whom a debt is owed. A spouse in a dissolution proceeding is not a creditor; the spouse is a party to the proceeding seeking an equitable distribution of marital property. The district court is not attaching the benefits to satisfy a debt; it is dividing the marital estate pursuant to state law.

**Second**, the application of section 518.58 does not constitute "legal process" within the meaning of 10 U.S.C. § 1477. The district court is not seeking to garnish, levy, or attach the benefits. It is simply considering these benefits as part of the overall marital estate and making an equitable distribution. The fact that the benefits are characterized as nonmarital does not preclude the court from considering them for purposes of a hardship allocation.

**Third**, even if the federal statutes could be interpreted to preempt the application of section 518.58, the preemption should not be applied in this case. The Supreme Court has held that federal preemption should not be inferred lightly and that there is a presumption against preemption of state law. See Wyeth v. Levine, 555 U.S. 555, 565 (2009). The prosecution has not demonstrated that Congress intended to preempt state marital property law in dissolution proceedings.

#### B. The Prosecution's Argument Leads to an Internally Inconsistent Result

The prosecution argues that the benefits should be classified as nonmarital property but cannot be divided. This result is internally inconsistent. If the benefits are nonmarital property, they should be subject to the same rules as other nonmarital property, including the district court's authority to apportion them under section 518.58. If the benefits cannot be divided, they should be classified as something other than nonmarital property.

The prosecution's argument would create a perverse incentive: a spouse could receive unlimited death benefits tax-free and free from division, while the other spouse receives nothing despite having contributed to the marriage and the family. This result cannot be what Congress intended when it enacted the federal anti-attachment statutes.

We acknowledge that the federal government has a strong interest in ensuring that military death benefits are paid to the designated beneficiary. However, this interest does not preclude a state court from considering these benefits in a dissolution proceeding. The district court can protect the beneficiary's interest while still considering the benefits for purposes of equitable distribution.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court:

2. **Affirm** the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property but **reverse** the district court's holding that federal law preempts the application of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, and **remand** for further proceedings to determine whether Gordon Angell is entitled to a hardship share.

The defense submits that the prosecution's arguments ignore the purpose of the marital property statutes, mischaracterize the nature of the death benefits, and seek to create an inequitable result that benefits one spouse at the expense of the other. The district court should be reversed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by the parties. The appeal concerns the classification and division of military death benefits received by Respondent Loretta Angell following the death of the parties' son, Levi Angell. The district court classified the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property but awarded Gordon Angell a portion of these funds under Minnesota’s hardship statute. The Court has considered the rebuttal arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense and hereby issues its final ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Classification of Benefits as Nonmarital Property**

The threshold issue is whether the district court correctly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, defines nonmarital property as property "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse."

The defense argues that the presumption of marital property should control because the benefits are statutory entitlements from the federal government rather than "bequests" from Levi Angell. The defense further contends that the "shared loss" of the parties' son precludes a finding that these benefits were intended to exclude Gordon Angell.

The Court is not persuaded by the defense’s attempt to distinguish the nature of these benefits. The defense asks this Court to disregard the holding of *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), which explicitly held that federal death-gratuity benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary." While the defense correctly notes that the funds are paid by the United States Treasury, the legal characterization of the transfer for property classification purposes is governed by the service member's designation. As the prosecution argues, and as established in *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995), the source of the right to receive the property is the deceased third party. Levi Angell’s designation of his mother as the sole beneficiary constitutes a bequest from a third party (Levi) to one spouse (Loretta).

The defense’s reliance on the "shared loss" theory, while emotionally compelling, cannot override the plain language of the statute. The Minnesota Legislature has provided a specific exception to the marital presumption for property acquired by bequest. The statute does not contain an exception for property acquired due to a shared familial tragedy. To adopt the defense’s argument would require the Court to judicially amend the statute, a task that falls to the legislature, not the judiciary. Therefore, the district court’s classification of the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed.

**B. Division of Benefits and Federal Preemption**

Having affirmed the classification, the Court must determine whether the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell a share of these nonmarital benefits under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. The defense argues that the federal anti-attachment statutes do not preempt the state court’s authority to apportion these funds for hardship purposes.

The defense relies on *Wyeth v. Levine*, 555 U.S. 555, 565 (2009), for the proposition that federal preemption should not be inferred lightly. The defense also argues that a spouse is not a "creditor" and that the district court’s order does not constitute "legal process" within the meaning of 10 U.S.C. § 1477.

The Court finds the prosecution’s position on preemption to be the correct interpretation of the law. 10 U.S.C. § 1477 states that SGLI proceeds are "not subject to attachment, garnishment, levy, tax lien, or other legal process." Similarly, 10 U.S.C. § 1450-1 protects death gratuities from "claims of creditors." The term "other legal process" is broad and encompasses judicial orders that direct a beneficiary to relinquish a portion of the funds to another party.

The defense’s argument that a spouse is not a "creditor" ignores the broader protective language of "legal process." Regardless of whether Gordon Angell is labeled a creditor or a spouse, the district court’s order requiring Loretta Angell to pay him $150,000 constitutes a legal process that divests Loretta of her protected benefits. Congressional intent in enacting these statutes was to ensure that the designated beneficiary receives the full amount of the benefits to provide financial security following a military death. Allowing a state court to divert these funds through a hardship statute would directly contravene that intent.

While the defense warns of an "internally inconsistent result" where property is nonmarital yet indivisible, this result is mandated by the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution. When state law conflicts with federal law, federal law prevails. The district court lacked the authority to divide these federally protected funds.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s arguments regarding classification and preemption are insufficient to overcome the clear statutory text and binding precedent. The benefits are properly classified as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property, and federal law prohibits their division.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED:**

1.  The district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**.
2.  The district court’s order awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **REVERSED**.
3.  The matter is **REMANDED** to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
