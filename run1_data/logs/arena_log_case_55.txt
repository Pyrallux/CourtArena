Ladies and gentlemen of the court, the central issue before you is whether the officer's search of the defendant's vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. The answer is unequivocally yes, because the officer conducted an unlawful pat-down of the defendant's person without reasonable suspicion, rendering all subsequent evidence— including the drug evidence found in the trunk— the fruit of an unconstitutional seizure.  

The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures. A pat-down of a person's outer clothing, as the officer conducted here, is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment and must be justified by reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). This is not a mere formality; it is a fundamental safeguard against arbitrary police intrusions. The officer himself testified that he had "no safety concerns" and that his weapon "was not drawn during the interaction" because he "had no fear for his safety or suspicion of any illegality from the initial contact based on defendant’s demeanor." (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4, 33:3-19, 34:8-35:13, 44:21-45:24.) He further acknowledged that the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative," with "no furtive or suspicious movements," "no signs of intoxication," and "no smell of marijuana." (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3.)  

When the officer asked, "if he had anything that would concern me," he had no objective basis to suspect the defendant was armed. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a pat-down cannot be justified by generalized suspicions or subjective hunches. *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 377 (1993) ("[A]n officer may conduct a frisk only if he reasonably believes the suspect is armed and dangerous"). Here, the officer’s own testimony confirms he had no such belief. He did not observe any bulges, weapons, or threatening gestures. He did not have reason to believe the defendant was involved in a crime that typically involves weapons. The defendant had merely been stopped for a suspended license—a non-violent traffic violation—and the officer’s own investigation revealed no threat to his safety.  

The officer’s claim that the defendant’s behavior was "unusual" or "overly cooperative" is irrelevant. Mere cooperation or politeness does not create reasonable suspicion of danger. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 501 (1983) ("[C]ourts have held that nervousness or unusual behavior alone is insufficient to justify a Terry frisk"). The officer’s subjective belief that the defendant was "being deceptive" by asking about ID procedures again—described by the court as not objectively reasonable—cannot transform routine compliance into a threat. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000) ("[T]he officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant [a] intrusion").  

Crucially, the officer never advised the defendant that he could refuse the pat-down. While voluntariness of consent is determined by the totality of circumstances, *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973), the absence of such advice—and the officer’s failure to establish any objective basis for suspicion—means the consent was not freely given. The defendant’s actions were not a voluntary waiver but a response to an unlawful demand for a search. The officer’s statement, "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have," created an impression that the defendant was suspected of criminal activity, yet the officer had no concrete facts to support that suspicion.  

When the officer conducted the pat-down without reasonable suspicion, he violated the Fourth Amendment. As the Supreme Court has made clear, evidence obtained from an unconstitutional search is inadmissible. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). The drug evidence found in the trunk was discovered only *after* this unlawful pat-down, making it the direct product of the officer’s unconstitutional conduct. The search of the car cannot be salvaged by the defendant’s subsequent consent because that consent was tainted by the prior illegal seizure. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963) ("Evidence is 'fruit of the poisonous tree' if it would not have been discovered but for the illegal conduct").  

In sum, the officer’s pat-down was unconstitutional because it lacked any objective basis for reasonable suspicion. The court must suppress all evidence derived from that violation. The Fourth Amendment does not permit police to conduct searches based on hunches or subjective impressions, especially when the officer himself acknowledges no safety concerns. This case is a textbook example of why constitutional safeguards exist: to prevent the government from intruding on personal liberty without justification. The evidence must be excluded.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The plaintiff mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter and the legal standards applicable to this case. Contrary to the prosecution's assertions, the officer's conduct did not constitute an unconstitutional seizure. Rather, the evidence demonstrates that the defendant voluntarily pulled over, voluntarily engaged in conversation with the officer, and voluntarily consented to both the pat-down and the search of his vehicle. The defense respectfully requests that the court deny the plaintiff's motion to suppress.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. There Was No Seizure Requiring Fourth Amendment Protections

The prosecution incorrectly frames this as a case involving an unlawful traffic stop. The facts establish that the defendant pulled his vehicle over on his own, without any action by the officer to initiate a stop. The officer testified that "regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60.) The officer activated his lights only after the defendant had already stopped, "so the driver would know it was a police officer and as protection in case anyone would come up from behind." (Suppression Tr. 13.)

A seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment requires "a governmental termination of freedom of movement through means intentionally applied." Brower v. Inyo County, 489 U.S. 593, 597 (1989). Here, the defendant voluntarily chose to stop his vehicle. The officer's activation of lights behind an already-stopped vehicle does not transform a voluntary stop into a seizure. See California v. Beheler, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983) (holding that a seizure requires "a governmental termination of freedom of movement").

The prosecution's reliance on the officer's subsequent actions—asking for license and registration—does not alter this analysis. The Supreme Court has recognized that "not every encounter between a police officer and a citizen constitutes a seizure." United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989). An officer may approach a vehicle and ask questions without converting the encounter into a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment protections. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). The defendant was free to decline to answer questions and leave at any time. See California v. Hodari D., 499 U.S. 621, 626 (1991).

### B. Even Assuming Arguendo a Seizure Occurred, the Pat-Down Was Justified by Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution contends that the pat-down was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. However, the prosecution ignores the facts establishing the officer's reasonable suspicion.

The officer testified that he formed reasonable suspicion based on several factors: (1) the defendant's presence in the area at a late hour, coming from Philadelphia; (2) the defendant's inquiry about ID procedures after already receiving a thorough explanation, which the officer reasonably interpreted as a "ruse"; (3) the defendant's overly cooperative behavior in immediately patting himself down and placing his hands on the vehicle; and (4) the officer's experience that such behavior is unusual. (Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9.)

The prosecution incorrectly argues that subjective impressions cannot form the basis of reasonable suspicion. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has held that "reasonable suspicion" is a mixed question of objective and subjective factors. The officer's subjective observations, when combined with objective facts, can justify a Terry stop. See Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968) (holding that the officer must have "specific and articulable facts" taken together with "rational inferences" to warrant a frisk).

The prosecution cites Florida v. Royer for the proposition that nervousness or unusual behavior alone is insufficient. However, Royer is distinguishable. In that case, the Court found insufficient suspicion where the defendant appeared nervous and had a large amount of luggage. Here, the officer observed specific behavior—the defendant's repeated questions about procedures already explained, combined with the unusual display of excessive cooperation by immediately patting himself down and placing his hands on the vehicle. These are specific, articulable facts that distinguish this case from generalized nervousness.

The prosecution's reliance on the officer's statement that he had "no fear for his safety" is misplaced. The officer's testimony must be read in context. He stated he had no fear "based on defendant's demeanor" at the initial contact (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19), but he later developed suspicion during the encounter. The officer testified that after the defendant re-engaged him about the ID procedures, he "was suspicious" (Suppression Tr. 54:14), and he formed reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down. (Suppression Tr. 56:15-16.)

### C. The Consent to Search Was Voluntary and Independent of Any Alleged Seizure

The prosecution's central argument—that the consent was tainted by an unlawful seizure—fails because there was no unlawful seizure, and alternatively, because the consent was independently voluntary.

The Supreme Court has held that "voluntariness is a question of fact to be determined from the totality of all the circumstances." Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 248 (1973). The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was involuntary. United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164, 177 n.14 (1974).

Here, the totality of circumstances demonstrates voluntary consent:

1. **The defendant initiated the interaction**: The defendant voluntarily pulled over and waited for his girlfriend. He was not under arrest.

2. **The defendant was informed he was free to go**: The officer explicitly told the defendant "he was free to go" after completing the paperwork. (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12.)

3. **The defendant was not in custody**: The defendant was standing outside his vehicle, had a ride coming, and was free to leave. The officer's weapon was not drawn. There were no indications of custody. See Oregon v. Mathiason, 429 U.S. 492, 495 (1977) (holding that a person is in custody only when "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave").

4. **The defendant's consent was affirmative and enthusiastic**: The officer testified that the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom" and then "placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25.) The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.)

5. **There was no coercion**: The officer did not draw his weapon, did not threaten the defendant, and did not physically restrain him. The defendant was fully cooperative and agreeable throughout.

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have" constitutes coercion. However, this statement, while suggestive, does not rise to the level of coercion required to invalidate consent. The Supreme Court has held that "the mere fact that the police may have made a suggestion does not vitiate consent." United States v. Place, 463 U.S. 696, 719 (1983). The statement was made after the defendant had already been informed he was free to go and after the defendant had already offered to be searched.

The prosecution's reliance on the absence of warnings about the right to refuse consent is unavailing. The Supreme Court has never required that a suspect be advised of the right to refuse consent. Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. 33, 40 (1996) ("We have never held that the Fourth Amendment requires that a suspect must be told he has a right to refuse consent before consent can be deemed voluntary").

### D. The Search of the Vehicle Was Supported by Independent Consent

Even assuming arguendo that the pat-down was unconstitutional, the search of the vehicle was based on the defendant's independent consent to search the car, which was given after the pat-down and was not the product of the alleged illegality.

The prosecution argues that "the search of the car cannot be salvaged by the defendant's subsequent consent because that consent was tainted by the prior illegal seizure." However, this argument presupposes that there was an illegal seizure, which for the reasons stated above, there was not.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that "evidence is 'fruit of the poisonous tree' if it would not have been discovered but for the illegal conduct." Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963). Here, the defendant independently consented to the search of his vehicle. The defendant offered to allow the search before the officer asked, stating "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 22:1-2.) The officer then asked if he could search the car, and the defendant said "yes." (Suppression Tr. 22:3-4.) This is a classic example of voluntary, independent consent.

The prosecution has not established that the defendant's consent was anything other than voluntary. The defendant was not in custody, was told he was free to go, had a ride coming, and voluntarily offered to allow the search. The consent was not the product of any unlawful coercion.

### E. The Prosecution's Characterizations of the Facts Are Incorrect

The prosecution mischaracterizes the facts in several respects:

1. The prosecution states that the officer "conducted an unlawful pat-down." This ignores the officer's testimony that he had reasonable suspicion based on the defendant's unusual behavior.

2. The prosecution states that the officer "had no objective basis to suspect the defendant was armed." This ignores the officer's testimony that he observed specific behavior—the overly cooperative pat-down and the ruse of re-asking about ID procedures—that gave him reasonable suspicion.

3. The prosecution states that the defendant "merely been stopped for a suspended license." This ignores that the defendant was never stopped by the officer—he pulled over on his own.

4. The prosecution states that "the officer never advised the defendant that he could refuse the pat-down." This is irrelevant, as no such warning is required. Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. at 40.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The court has reviewed the factual findings from the suppression hearing, as well as the opening arguments of the prosecution and defense. The central issue is whether the search of the defendant’s vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment, either due to an unlawful seizure or a lack of voluntary consent. Based on the provided facts and applicable precedent, the court finds that the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and not the product of an unconstitutional seizure. Therefore, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Encounter Was Consensual**

The prosecution argues that the encounter constituted a seizure requiring reasonable suspicion. However, the factual findings indicate that the defendant voluntarily pulled his vehicle over to the side of the road without any signal or command from the officer. (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60.) The officer merely followed the vehicle and activated his lights after it had already stopped, for the purpose of identification and safety. (Suppression Tr. 13.)

A seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment requires "a governmental termination of freedom of movement through means intentionally applied." *Brower v. Inyo County*, 489 U.S. 593, 597 (1989). Here, the defendant initiated the stop. The officer’s subsequent activation of lights behind an already-stopped vehicle, without any pursuit or command to stop, did not transform the encounter into a seizure. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991) (holding that police encounter is not a seizure unless the officer, "by means of physical force or show of authority, has in some way restrained the liberty of a citizen"). The defendant was free to leave at any point during the initial exchange of information regarding his girlfriend and his destination. See *California v. Hodari D.*, 499 U.S. 621, 628 (1991) (holding that a seizure requires either physical force or submission to officer's show of authority).

**B. The Pat-Down Was Consensual and Did Not Taint the Subsequent Search**

The prosecution contends that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. The court acknowledges that the officer testified he had "no safety concerns" and observed no furtive movements or weapons. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4, 33:3-19.) However, the record indicates that the pat-down was not a *Terry* frisk based on reasonable suspicion, but rather a consensual search.

The facts show that after the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the vehicle, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25.) The officer described the defendant’s demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.) Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248-249 (1973), a search is valid if consent is voluntarily given, without the need for probable cause or a warrant. The defendant’s affirmative actions and verbal invitation to be searched constitute clear consent. Therefore, even if the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for a non-consensual frisk, the pat-down was lawful based on the defendant’s consent.

**C. The Search of the Vehicle Was Supported by Voluntary Consent**

The prosecution argues that the search of the vehicle was the "fruit of the poisonous tree" due to the alleged illegality of the pat-down. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). This argument fails because the consent to search the vehicle was voluntarily given after the defendant was informed he was free to leave.

The factual findings establish that after the officer completed the paperwork for the suspended license citation, he told the defendant "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12.) The officer testified, "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) At this point, any seizure or detention that may have existed had terminated. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 454 (1980) (a person is seized only when, "by means of physical force or a show of authority, [he] is restrained from leaving").

It was the defendant who re-engaged the officer in conversation, asking again about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) Only after this re-engagement did the officer ask for consent to search the vehicle, to which the defendant replied, "yes." (Suppression Tr. 22:3-4.) Because the defendant was explicitly told he was free to go and chose to remain and re-initiate contact, his subsequent consent to search the vehicle was not tainted by any prior illegality. The causal chain between any potential prior error and the evidence was broken by the defendant's voluntary act of returning to the officer to converse. See *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975) (the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine does not apply when the connection between the illegal police conduct and the evidence is "attenuated").

The prosecution argues that the consent was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly held that "the Fourth Amendment does not require that a suspect be advised of the right to refuse consent." *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40 (1996). Furthermore, the officer’s statement regarding problems with people bringing items from Philadelphia does not rise to the level of coercion required to invalidate consent. See *United States v. Place*, 463 U.S. 696, 705 (1983) (consent is not invalid simply because the officer makes a suggestive statement).

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the factual findings, the defendant voluntarily initiated the stop, voluntarily consented to the pat-down, and—after being told he was free to leave—voluntarily re-engaged the officer and consented to the search of his vehicle. The prosecution has not demonstrated that the consent was involuntary or that it was the product of an unconstitutional seizure. Accordingly, the defendant’s motion to suppress is denied.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and a flawed application of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. The prosecution incorrectly assumes that a seizure occurred. However, the factual findings establish that the defendant voluntarily pulled his vehicle over to the side of the road without any signal, command, or pursuit from the officer. As this Court found, "the defendant initiated the stop. Under Florida v. ### B. The Pat-Down Was Consensual, Not a Terry Frisk

The defendant explicitly invited the search.

The prosecution cites Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), for the proposition that a pat-down requires reasonable suspicion. However, Terry applies to searches initiated by the police over the suspect's objection. Here, the defendant initiated the pat-down himself and explicitly consented to being searched. Under Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 248-249 (1973), a search is valid if consent is voluntarily given, without the need for probable cause or a warrant. The defendant's affirmative actions and verbal invitation to be searched constitute clear consent.

The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In Royer, the police detained the defendant at the airport and asked for his ticket and identification without reasonable suspicion. The defendant chose to remain and initiate further contact with the officer. Under United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 444, 454 (1980), a person is seized only when, "by means of physical force or a show of authority, [he] is restrained from leaving." Here, the defendant was not restrained—he voluntarily chose to stay and speak with the officer.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that when a suspect is told he is free to go and then voluntarily remains and consents, that consent is attenuated from any prior police conduct. In Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. at 501, the Court found consent invalid because the defendant was "being detained" at the time of consent. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The court has reviewed the factual findings from the suppression hearing, the opening arguments, and the rebuttals presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue for determination is whether the search of the defendant’s vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. Specifically, the court must address whether the pat-down of the defendant was an unconstitutional seizure under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and whether the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was tainted by any prior illegality. Based on the provided facts and applicable precedent, the court finds that the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and not the product of an unconstitutional seizure. Therefore, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down Was Consensual, Not a *Terry* Seizure**

The prosecution argues that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. The court agrees with the prosecution’s legal premise that a *Terry* frisk requires "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The court further acknowledges that the officer’s testimony regarding the defendant’s "unusual cooperation" and the alleged "ruse" regarding the ID procedures does not rise to the level of reasonable suspicion. As the prosecution correctly notes, generalized behavior or nervousness, without more, is insufficient to justify a frisk. See *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 10 (1989).

However, the prosecution’s argument fails because it mischaracterizes the nature of the pat-down. The factual findings establish that the officer did not initiate a frisk based on a safety concern. Rather, the defendant voluntarily initiated the search. After the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the vehicle, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25.)

Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248-249 (1973), a search is valid if consent is voluntarily given, without the need for probable cause or a warrant. The defendant’s affirmative actions and verbal invitation to be searched constitute clear consent. Because the pat-down was consensual, the Fourth Amendment’s requirement of reasonable suspicion for a *Terry* frisk is not implicated. The defendant was not seized for purposes of the pat-down; he waived his right to be free from the search. Therefore, the prosecution’s argument that the pat-down was an unlawful seizure is rejected.

**B. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Attenuated from Any Prior Illegality**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was the "fruit of the poisonous tree" derived from the alleged unlawful pat-down. See *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). This argument fails for two independent reasons: the lack of an underlying illegality and the attenuation of the consent.

**1. Lack of Underlying Illegality**

As established above, the pat-down was consensual and therefore lawful. Consequently, there was no "poisonous tree"—the initial search of the defendant’s person did not violate the Fourth Amendment. Without a constitutional violation, the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine does not apply.

**2. Attenuation of Consent**

Even assuming, *arguendo*, that the initial encounter or the pat-down was unlawful, the consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality to purge the primary taint. The Supreme Court in *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975), identified three factors to determine whether evidence is admissible despite a prior illegality: the temporal proximity of the arrest and the confession, the presence of intervening circumstances, and, particularly, the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct.

Here, the presence of intervening circumstances is dispositive. The factual findings indicate that after the officer completed the paperwork for the suspended license citation, he told the defendant "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12.) The officer testified, "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) At this moment, any seizure that may have existed terminated. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 454 (1980) (a person is seized only when restrained from leaving).

It was the defendant who voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation, asking again about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) Only after this re-engagement did the officer ask for consent to search the vehicle. The defendant’s voluntary act of returning to the officer to converse, after being told he was free to leave, constitutes a significant intervening circumstance that broke the causal chain between any prior police conduct and the consent to search.

The prosecution relies on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 501 (1983), where the Court held that consent given during an unlawful detention was invalid. However, *Royer* is factually distinguishable. In *Royer*, the defendant was not informed that he was free to leave, and the detention was illegal at the moment consent was given. Here, the defendant was explicitly told he was free to go, and the detention had ended before he re-engaged the officer and consented to the search. Because the defendant was not seized at the time he consented, the consent was voluntary and not the product of official coercion.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The court finds that the pat-down of the defendant was consensual and therefore did not violate the Fourth Amendment. Furthermore, even if the initial encounter were viewed with suspicion, the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was voluntarily given after he was told he was free to leave and after he voluntarily re-engaged the officer. This intervening conduct broke any causal chain between the initial stop and the search. Accordingly, the prosecution has not demonstrated that the search violated the defendant’s constitutional rights. The defendant’s motion to suppress is denied.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION AND OVERVIEW

May it please the Court, the prosecution's closing argument, while eloquently presented, fundamentally misapprehends the factual record and misapplies the legal framework governing Fourth Amendment analysis. The defense respectfully submits that the Court should uphold its well-reasoned ruling denying the motion to suppress and provide the following additional analysis in support thereof.

The prosecution's argument rests upon a single, fatal premise: that the pat-down of the defendant constituted an unlawful seizure requiring reasonable suspicion under Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). This premise is demonstrably false. The uncontradicted facts establish that the defendant voluntarily—without any prompting or coercion—patted himself down, placed his hands on his vehicle, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." This was not a Terry frisk initiated by the officer based upon safety concerns; it was a consensual encounter initiated by the defendant himself.

The prosecution's attempt to characterize this encounter as an unlawful seizure fails because it ignores the most basic principle of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence: that a search conducted pursuant to valid consent is constitutional regardless of whether probable cause or reasonable suspicion exists. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 248-249 (1973). The defendant waived his right to be free from a search of his person when he voluntarily invited the officer to search him.

---

## I. THE PAT-DOWN WAS CONSENSUAL, NOT A TERRY SEIZURE

### A. The Factual Record Establishes Voluntary Consent

The prosecution asks this Court to ignore the plain meaning of the defendant's actions and words. The officer testified that after being asked if he had anything that would concern the officer, the defendant:

1. "patted himself down from top to bottom, starting here, down a couple of— you know, down to his legs";
2. "placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search"; and
3. "said to me, you can search me."

Suppression Tr. 21:22-25, 23:18-24:2.

This testimony is uncontradicted. The defendant did not wait for the officer to initiate a pat-down. He did not submit to a search under duress. Rather, he affirmatively invited the search, taking the proactive step of patting himself down to demonstrate that he had no weapons. The prosecution's argument that this was an unlawful Terry seizure defies the factual record.

The prosecution relies upon Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), but that case is inapposite. In Royer, the defendant did not voluntarily invite a search; he was detained in a small room with two officers, his luggage was taken from him, and he was asked to consent to a search of his luggage. The Court held that consent given under such circumstances was presumptively invalid. Here, by contrast, the defendant was outdoors, in an open area, had already been told he was free to leave, and took the affirmative step of inviting the search.

### B. The Officer Had No Safety Concerns

The prosecution correctly notes that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a Terry frisk. Indeed, the officer testified that he "had no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), did not draw his weapon (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5), observed no suspicious movements (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25), and smelled no alcohol or drugs (Suppression Tr. 28:5-9, 38:14-17). The defense does not dispute that a Terry frisk would have been unconstitutional under these circumstances.

However, the critical point is that no Terry frisk occurred. The officer did not initiate the pat-down; the defendant did. The officer merely acceded to the defendant's explicit invitation. Under such circumstances, the Fourth Amendment is not implicated because the defendant waived his right to be free from a search of his person. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 248-49.

The prosecution's argument that the defendant's "unusual cooperation" somehow rendered the encounter coercive is directly contradicted by precedent. In United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1, 10 (1989), the Supreme Court held that nervous or cooperative behavior, without more, is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. If such behavior cannot establish reasonable suspicion, it certainly cannot render consent involuntary. The defendant's cooperation was evidence of his voluntariness, not of coercion.

---

## II. THE CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS VALID

### A. The Defendant Was Free to Leave

The prosecution's central argument is that the "free to go" statement was a "hollow fiction" because the defendant remained at the scene. This argument fails for two independent reasons.

First, the statement was not hollow. The officer explicitly told the defendant he was free to go (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The defendant then chose to remain at the scene to wait for his girlfriend—not because he was detained, but because he had no other transportation. This is the hallmark of a consensual encounter, not a seizure. See United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 444, 454 (1980) (a seizure occurs only when "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave").

The prosecution cites Mendenhall for the proposition that a seizure occurs when a person is surrounded by police vehicles with lights flashing. However, Mendenhall is distinguishable. In that case, the defendant was confronted by two officers in an airport terminal, was identified as a drug courier based on a "drug courier profile," and was asked to accompany the officers to a separate room. The Court held that a seizure had occurred. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go, was not asked to accompany the officer anywhere, and remained at the scene voluntarily to wait for a ride.

Second, even if a seizure had occurred, it had ended before the defendant consented to the search of his vehicle. The officer testified:

"I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car."

Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.

It was only after the officer began walking back to his car that the defendant re-engaged him in conversation (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). This voluntary re-engagement broke any causal chain between the initial encounter and the subsequent consent. The defendant was not seized at the moment he consented to the search of his vehicle.

### B. The Consent Was Attenuated from Any Prior Seizure

Even assuming arguendo that the initial encounter constituted a seizure, the consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated to purge any taint under Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975).

The Brown Court identified three factors to determine attenuation: (1) temporal proximity between the unlawful conduct and the consent, (2) the presence of intervening circumstances, and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. Here, all three factors favor the defense.

**Temporal Proximity:** Approximately fifteen minutes elapsed from the initial stop to the completion of the paperwork, and an additional period elapsed before the defendant re-engaged the officer and consented to the search. This is not a case where consent was given immediately upon the cessation of an unlawful detention.

**Intervening Circumstances:** The most critical factor is the presence of intervening circumstances. The officer told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant voluntarily chose to remain at the scene to wait for his girlfriend. The officer began walking back to his car. It was only at this point that the defendant re-engaged the officer. This sequence of events constitutes a significant intervening circumstance that broke the causal chain between any prior seizure and the consent. See United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. at 458 (consent valid when given after being told defendant was free to leave).

The prosecution argues that no intervening circumstances existed because "the pat-down was the direct result of the officer's illegal detention." This argument fails because, as established above, there was no illegal detention. The pat-down was consensual. But even assuming a detention occurred, the defendant was told he was free to go, the officer began walking away, and the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer. This is precisely the type of intervening circumstance that attenuates consent.

**Purpose and Flagrancy:** There is no evidence of purposeful or flagrant misconduct. The officer conducted a routine traffic stop, wrote a citation, and told the defendant he was free to go. The officer did not threaten the defendant, did not draw his weapon, and did not engage in any coercive conduct. The prosecution's characterization of the encounter as "coercive" is unsupported by the record.

### C. The Consent Was Voluntary Under Schneckloth

The prosecution argues that consent is invalid unless the defendant was advised that he could refuse consent. While such advice is a factor in determining voluntariness, it is not a constitutional requirement. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 248-49. The Supreme Court explicitly held that "the Fourth Amendment does not require proof of knowledge of the right to refuse consent" in order to establish voluntary consent. Id.

Here, the defendant voluntarily invited the officer to search him, then voluntarily consented to a search of his vehicle. The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable" (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). There is no evidence of coercion, duress, or intimidation. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the consent was involuntary. See Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157, 167 (1986) (the burden of proof is on the prosecution to show that consent was voluntary).

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

### A. The "Free to Go" Statement Was Not Meaningless

The prosecution argues that the "free to go" statement was meaningless because the defendant remained at the scene. This argument conflates being free to leave with being required to leave. A person may choose to remain in a location for any number of reasons—waiting for a ride, finishing a conversation, or simply because they have nowhere else to go—without being seized. The critical inquiry is whether a reasonable person would believe they were free to leave, not whether they actually left. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. at 454.

Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant chose to remain to wait for his girlfriend. This is the textbook definition of a consensual encounter, not a seizure.

### B. The Officer Did Not Request Consent to Search Based on the Pat-Down

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was "tainted" by the alleged unlawful pat-down. However, the officer asked to search the vehicle only after completing the pat-down and after the defendant had already invited the search of his person. The defendant was not asked to consent to the vehicle search as part of the pat-down; he was asked separately, after the pat-down was complete.

Moreover, the officer testified that he asked to search the vehicle based on "reasonable suspicion" arising from the defendant's conduct—specifically, the defendant's re-engagement about ID procedures and his "unusual cooperation" in patting himself down (Suppression Tr. 56:15-16, 56:21-57:9). While the defense disputes that these facts constitute reasonable suspicion, they do demonstrate that the officer's decision to ask for consent was based on the defendant's post-detention conduct, not on the pat-down itself.

### C. The Prosecution Misapplies Royer

The prosecution relies heavily on Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), but that case is distinguishable. In Royer, the defendant was detained in a small room with two officers, his luggage was taken from him without his consent, and he was asked to consent to a search. The Court held that consent given under these circumstances was invalid because the defendant was not told he was free to leave.

Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to leave, was not detained in any manner, and was not surrounded by officers. The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to go. This case is far more analogous to United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 444 (1980), where the Court held that consent was valid when given after the defendant was told she was free to leave.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution has failed to carry its burden of proving that the search of the defendant's vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. The pat-down of the defendant was consensual—the defendant voluntarily invited the search by patting himself down and explicitly stating, "you can search me." The defendant was told he was free to go, and the officer began walking back to his car before the defendant re-engaged him in conversation. The defendant's subsequent consent to search his vehicle was voluntary and attenuated from any prior seizure by significant intervening circumstances.

The prosecution's argument rests on the false premise that the defendant was seized at the time of the pat-down and subsequent consent. The factual record establishes otherwise. The defendant's motion to suppress was properly denied.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its ruling denying the motion to suppress.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The court has carefully reviewed the factual findings from the suppression hearing, the initial arguments, and the closing rebuttals presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The core issue remains whether the search of the defendant’s vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. Specifically, the court must determine if the pat-down of the defendant constituted an unconstitutional seizure under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and whether the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was voluntary or tainted by any prior illegality. After considering the parties' final arguments, the court adheres to its determination that the defendant’s consent was voluntary and not the product of an unconstitutional seizure. Accordingly, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down Was Consensual, Not a *Terry* Seizure**

The prosecution argues vigorously that the pat-down of the defendant was an unconstitutional seizure because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. The court agrees with the prosecution’s statement of the law: a *Terry* frisk requires "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The court also agrees that the officer’s subjective hunches regarding the defendant’s "unusual cooperation" or the alleged "ruse" regarding the ID procedures are insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. See *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 10 (1989).

However, the prosecution’s argument fails because it mischaracterizes the interaction as a police-initiated *Terry* frisk. The factual findings clearly establish that the officer did not initiate a pat-down for weapons based on a safety concern. Rather, the defendant voluntarily initiated the search of his person. After the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," placed his hands on the vehicle, spread his legs, and explicitly stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25.)

Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248-249 (1973), a search is valid if consent is voluntarily given, without the need for probable cause or a warrant. The defendant’s affirmative actions and verbal invitation to be searched constitute clear consent. Because the search of the defendant’s person was consensual, the Fourth Amendment’s requirement of reasonable suspicion for a *Terry* frisk is not implicated. The defendant was not seized for purposes of the pat-down; he waived his right to be free from the search. Therefore, the prosecution’s argument that the pat-down was an unlawful seizure is rejected.

**B. The Defendant Was Not Seized When He Consented to the Vehicle Search**

The prosecution contends that the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was invalid because it was given during an unlawful detention, citing *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983). The prosecution argues that the officer’s statement that the defendant was "free to go" was a "hollow fiction" given the presence of rotating police lights and the fact that the defendant had not yet left the scene.

The court finds this argument unpersuasive based on the specific factual findings of this case. The officer testified that after completing the paperwork for the suspended license citation, he told the defendant "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12.) Crucially, the officer testified that he "started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) This physical act of withdrawal is significant. Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 454 (1980), a person is seized only when, by means of physical force or a show of authority, the person is restrained from leaving. Here, the officer’s show of authority had ceased; he had communicated that the detention was over and physically began to disengage.

The prosecution’s reliance on *Royer* is misplaced. In *Royer*, the defendant was detained in a small room and not told he was free to leave. 460 U.S. at 501. Here, the encounter took place outdoors, and the defendant was explicitly told he was free to leave. The fact that the defendant chose to remain at the scene to wait for his girlfriend does not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure. A reasonable person in the defendant's position, having been told he was free to go and seeing the officer walk away, would feel free to leave, even if he chose to wait for a ride. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. at 454.

**C. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Attenuated from Any Prior Illegality**

Even assuming, *arguendo*, that the initial encounter or the pat-down was unlawful, the consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality to purge the primary taint. The Supreme Court in *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975), identified three factors to determine whether evidence is admissible despite a prior illegality: the temporal proximity of the arrest and the confession, the presence of intervening circumstances, and, particularly, the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct.

Here, the presence of intervening circumstances is dispositive. The factual findings indicate that the officer told the defendant he was "free to go" and began walking back to his patrol car. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) It was the defendant who voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation, asking again about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) Only after this re-engagement did the officer ask for consent to search the vehicle. The defendant’s voluntary act of returning to the officer to converse, after being told he was free to leave and seeing the officer retreat, constitutes a significant intervening circumstance that broke the causal chain between any prior police conduct and the consent to search.

The prosecution argues that the "free to go" statement was meaningless because the defendant was still physically present. However, the court must look to the totality of the circumstances. The officer had completed the citation, returned the other documents (though retaining the license for forfeiture), and physically withdrew. The defendant’s decision to stay and wait for a ride was a personal choice, not a product of police coercion. Therefore, the consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and not the fruit of any prior illegality.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The court finds that the pat-down of the defendant was consensual and therefore did not violate the Fourth Amendment. Furthermore, the defendant’s consent to search the vehicle was voluntarily given after he was told he was free to leave and after he voluntarily re-engaged the officer. This intervening conduct broke any causal chain between the initial stop and the search. Accordingly, the prosecution has not demonstrated that the search violated the defendant’s constitutional rights. The defendant’s motion to suppress is denied.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
