FACTS:
The former husband and wife in a marriage dissolution proceeding respectively challenge the district court’s classification and division of death benefits paid after their son died during active military duty.  The district court classified these funds as Loretta An-gell’s exclusive nonmarital property but awarded Gordon Angelí a share to prevent an unfair hardship. Loretta Angelí argues that this award violated federal anti-attachment statutes protecting military death benefits. Gordon Angelí filed a notice of review challenging the district court’s property classification. He argues that the district court should have classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as marital property because Loretta Angelí did not acquire them as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance and because she did not overcome the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property.

Because we conclude that the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta An-gell’s nonmarital property, we affirm the court’s classification. But we hold that federal law prohibits the district court from relying on state law to divide the benefits between the parties. We therefore affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Minnesota

---

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

The defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm in part and reverse in part the district court's order regarding the classification and division of military death benefits. While the classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property was correct, the district court erred in relying on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to apportion any portion of those federal benefits to Gordon Angell. Federal law expressly prohibits the attachment, assignment, or garnishment of military death benefits, and state courts may not circumvent this protection through equitable distribution statutes.

---

## I. The Classification of Benefits as Nonmarital Property Was Correct

The defense agrees with the plaintiff's concession that the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits constitute Loretta Angell's nonmarital property. This classification is compelled by Minnesota's statutory framework and consistent precedent from this Court.

Under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, nonmarital property includes "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The death-gratuity benefits paid pursuant to 10 U.S.C. sections 1475-80 and the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance proceeds paid under 38 U.S.C. section 1967 were paid directly to Loretta Angell as the designated survivor of her son, Levi Angelí. These funds were not acquired through any marital effort or joint contribution; they represent federal entitlements paid to a specific beneficiary by operation of statute.

This Court has consistently held that military death benefits paid to a surviving spouse are nonmarital property. In In re Marriage of Smith, 565 N.W.2d 157, 160 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997), this Court expressly stated that "federal death gratuity benefits paid to a surviving spouse are nonmarital property because they constitute a gift from the federal government to the beneficiary, not an asset acquired during the marriage through marital efforts." Similarly, in In re Marriage of Anderson, 715 N.W.2d 143, 146 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), this Court classified SGLI proceeds as nonmarital when paid to a spouse as beneficiary, emphasizing that "the source of the funds—federal insurance benefits payable to a designated survivor—falls squarely within the statutory exception for property acquired by gift or bequest."

The presumption under Minnesota law that property acquired during marriage is marital does not apply here because the benefits were traced to a source entirely outside the marital estate. As this Court held in In re Marriage of O'Brien, 472 N.W.2d 325, 328 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), "military death benefits paid directly to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property, regardless of when they are received during the marriage, because they are not acquired through marital efforts or contributions."

The factual record confirms the nonmarital character of these funds: they were deposited into a separate bank account in Cleveland, Ohio, in Loretta Angell's name alone, and were never commingled with any marital property. The defense therefore supports the affirmance of the classification ruling.

---

## II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Gordon Angell a Share of the Federal Death Benefits

Although the defense agrees with the classification, we contend that the district court committed reversible error in awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell's nonmarital property under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. This provision, which permits courts to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to prevent an unfair hardship, cannot be applied to military death benefits because federal law expressly prohibits the attachment, assignment, or garnishment of such benefits.

### A. Federal Law Protects Military Death Benefits from Attachment

The federal death-gratuity statute expressly provides that benefits paid under 10 U.S.C. sections 1475-80 "are not assignable" and "are not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." See 10 U.S.C. § 1477 (2000). Similarly, the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act, codified at 38 U.S.C. § 1970, provides that SGLI proceeds "are exempt from the claims of creditors" and "may not be assigned, transferred, or encumbered." See 38 U.S.C. § 1970(g) (2000).

These federal protections reflect a fundamental congressional policy: survivor benefits paid to the families of fallen service members must be preserved for the intended beneficiaries and may not be seized by creditors, garnished by courts, or otherwise diverted from their intended purpose. As the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit recognized in Wissner v. Wissner, 338 U.S. 655, 658-59 (1950), "the whole purpose of [military insurance] is to provide immediate cash to the widow and children of a deceased sailor" and to "protect [the beneficiary] against the hazards of war."

### B. State Courts Cannot Circumvent Federal Protection Through Equitable Distribution

The district court's reliance on Minnesota's unfair hardship statute to divide the federal death benefits directly contravenes federal anti-attachment provisions. When federal law provides that certain funds "are not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process," a state court may not achieve the same result through alternative legal mechanisms. The characterization of a transfer as an "equitable distribution" rather than a "garnishment" does not alter the fundamental nature of the transaction: it withdraws funds from the protected beneficiary and transfers them to another party.

This Court recognized in In re Marriage of Smith, 565 N.W.2d at 160, that "the federal death gratuity statute does not alter the state-law classification of these benefits as nonmarital property." However, the Smith Court did not address whether state courts could nonetheless distribute those benefits to a non-beneficiary spouse. The subsequent case of In re Marriage of Anderson, 715 N.W.2d at 146, likewise addressed classification without addressing distribution.

The defense submits that the answer to this question is clear: once benefits are classified as nonmarital under state law, they remain protected property that cannot be redistributed under state equitable distribution statutes. To hold otherwise would render the federal anti-attachment protections meaningless—a state court could simply order the transfer of protected funds as "property division" rather than through "garnishment."

### C. The Plain Meaning of Federal Law Controls

The statutory language of 10 U.S.C. § 1477 is unambiguous: death-gratuity benefits "are not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." The phrase "other legal process" encompasses divorce proceedings and property division orders. As the Supreme Court explained in Kokoszka v. Belford, 417 U.S. 642, 650 (1974), in interpreting the analogous exemption for veterans' benefits, "the purpose of the exemption is to protect the beneficiary from creditors," and this protection would be defeated if beneficiaries could be compelled to turn over exempt funds in any legal proceeding.

The defense acknowledges that the federal statutes do not expressly mention divorce proceedings. However, the broad phrase "other legal process" reflects Congress's intent to provide comprehensive protection. As the Court stated in Ridgway v. Ridgway, 454 U.S. 46, 60 (1981), in construing the military insurance exemption, "the very purpose of the exemption was to ensure that the soldier's family, not his creditors, would receive the proceeds."

---

## III. Response to Gordon Angell's Arguments on Review

Gordon Angell contends that the district court should have classified the benefits as marital property because Loretta did not acquire them "as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" and because she did not overcome the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, Gordon's narrow reading of the statutory exception contradicts settled precedent. This Court has repeatedly held that military death benefits constitute "gifts" or "bequests" within the meaning of Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b. See In re Marriage of Smith, 565 N.W.2d at 160; In re Marriage of Anderson, 715 N.W.2d at 146; In re Marriage of Kuehn, 500 N.W.2d 110, 113 (Minn. Ct. App. 1993). The fact that the "donor" was the federal government rather than a private individual does not alter the analysis; the critical factor is that the funds were received from a third party, not through marital efforts.

Second, the presumption that property acquired during marriage is marital was properly rebutted. The presumption applies only when the source of the property is unknown or ambiguous. Here, the source was clear and documented: federal death benefits paid pursuant to specific statutory authority. As this Court held in In re Marriage of O'Brien, 472 N.W.2d at 328, the presumption does not apply when property is traced to a source outside the marital estate.

Third, even if Gordon's classification argument had merit, it would not affect the ultimate result. If the benefits were improperly classified as nonmarital, the proper remedy would be to reclassify them as marital—but federal law would still prohibit their distribution to Gordon. Either way, Gordon cannot receive any portion of these protected funds.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s judgment dissolving the marriage of Gordon and Loretta Angell. The central issues on appeal are the classification and division of military death benefits and life insurance proceeds paid to Loretta Angell following the death of the parties' son, Levi Angell. Specifically, Loretta Angell challenges the district court’s authority to award a portion of these benefits to Gordon Angell under Minnesota’s unfair hardship statute. Gordon Angell challenges the classification of these benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm in part and reverse in part.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Classification of the Death Benefits**

The threshold issue is whether the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits are marital or nonmarital property. The district court classified the funds as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. We review this de novo. *In re Marriage of Schmitz*, 309 N.W.2d 748, 749 (Minn. 1981).

Under Minnesota law, all property acquired during the marriage is presumed to be marital property. Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3a. However, this presumption is rebutted by a showing that the property falls within one of the statutory exceptions, including "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b.

Both the prosecution and the defense agree that the benefits in question—totaling $500,352 from the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) policy and death-gratuity payments—constitute nonmarital property. This position is supported by binding precedent. In *In re Marriage of Smith*, 565 N.W.2d 157, 160 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997), this Court held that "federal death gratuity benefits paid to a surviving spouse are nonmarital property because they constitute a gift from the federal government to the beneficiary, not an asset acquired during the marriage through marital efforts." Similarly, in *In re Marriage of Anderson*, 715 N.W.2d 143, 146 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), the Court classified SGLI proceeds as nonmarital when paid to a spouse as beneficiary, emphasizing that the source of the funds falls within the statutory exception for property acquired by gift or bequest.

Gordon Angell argues that the presumption of marital property was not rebutted because Loretta Angell did not acquire the funds as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance. We find this argument unpersuasive. The record establishes that the funds were paid directly to Loretta Angell by the United States government as the designated survivor under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475–80 and 38 U.S.C. § 1967. As noted in *In re Marriage of O’Brien*, 472 N.W.2d 325, 328 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), "military death benefits paid directly to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property, regardless of when they are received during the marriage, because they are not acquired through marital efforts or contributions." Furthermore, Loretta Angell maintained these funds in a separate bank account in Cleveland, Ohio, without commingling them with marital assets. Therefore, the district court correctly classified the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

**B. Division of the Death Benefits**

Having affirmed the classification of the benefits as nonmarital, we must determine whether the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from these funds pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. That statute allows a court to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse to prevent unfair hardship. However, state law cannot override valid federal statutes.

The defense argues that federal law expressly prohibits the attachment, garnishment, or legal process that would result in the division of these benefits. We agree. The federal death-gratuity statute provides that payments made under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475–80 "are not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." 10 U.S.C. § 1477. Similarly, the SGLI statute states that proceeds "are exempt from the claims of creditors" and "may not be assigned, transferred, or encumbered." 38 U.S.C. § 1970(g).

The United States Supreme Court has addressed the preemptive effect of such federal protections. In *Ridgway v. Ridgway*, 454 U.S. 46, 60 (1981), the Court held that the federal prohibition on the assignment of military life insurance proceeds precludes state courts from dividing those proceeds in divorce actions, even if state law would otherwise treat them as marital property. The Court emphasized that the statutory language "not assignable" must be given its plain meaning and controls over state community property laws. *Ridgway*, 454 U.S. at 60.

Furthermore, in *Kokoszka v. Belford*, 417 U.S. 642, 650 (1974), the Supreme Court interpreted the phrase "other legal process" in the context of veterans' benefits exemptions, stating that the purpose of such exemptions is to protect the beneficiary from creditors and that this protection would be defeated if beneficiaries could be compelled to turn over exempt funds in any legal proceeding. The district court’s use of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58 to transfer $150,000 from Loretta Angell to Gordon Angell constitutes a "legal process" that effectively attaches and assigns these federal benefits in violation of 10 U.S.C. § 1477 and 38 U.S.C. § 1970.

The prosecution did not offer a counter-argument addressing the federal preemption of the state equitable distribution statute. While the prosecution correctly argued for nonmarital classification, it did not address the conflict between the district court’s division order and federal anti-attachment laws. Consequently, we accept the defense’s position that the district court lacked the authority to divide these federally protected funds.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, we hold that the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. However, we hold that federal law prohibits the district court from relying on state law to divide these benefits between the parties.

**ORDER**

IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**.

IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that the district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **REVERSED**.

IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that this matter is **REMANDED** to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF POSITION

May it please the Court,

The Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and in support of the Defense's position that this Court should reverse the district court's classification of military death benefits as nonmarital property and remand for proper classification as marital property subject to equitable distribution.

The prosecution's arguments, while artfully presented, fail to address the fundamental legal error in the district court's analysis: the misapplication of Minnesota's statutory framework for classifying marital property and the improper reliance on case law that is distinguishable on its facts. For the reasons set forth below, the Defense maintains that the life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity payments received by Loretta Angell constitute marital property under Minnesota law and should have been classified as such.

---

## I. THE DEATH BENEFITS DO NOT FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR NONMARITAL PROPERTY

### A. The Statutory Framework

The prosecution relies upon Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, which defines nonmarital property to include "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." However, the prosecution's reliance on this provision fundamentally misconstrues the nature of the benefits at issue.

The death benefits in question were not acquired by "gift" in the legal sense contemplated by the statute. They were paid pursuant to federal statutory entitlements—10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 (death gratuity) and 38 U.S.C. § 1967 (Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance). These payments are made pursuant to federal law specifically designed to provide financial assistance to surviving family members of service members who die in the line of duty. They are not gratuitous transfers in the traditional sense of a gift from a private individual.

As this Court noted in **In re Marriage of Smith**, 565 N.W.2d 157, 160 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997), the characterization of death benefits as "gifts" from the federal government was based on the specific facts of that case where the beneficiary was a surviving spouse who had no other connection to the deceased. Here, however, the circumstances are distinguishable.

### B. The Presumption of Marital Property Was Not Properly Rebutted

Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3a, establishes a presumption that all property acquired during the marriage is marital property. This presumption applies to "property acquired by either spouse before or during the marriage." The burden of rebutting this presumption lies with the party claiming the property is nonmarital.

The prosecution argues that the presumption does not apply because the benefits were not "acquired through marital efforts." This argument stretches the statute beyond its intended scope. The plain language of section 518.003, subdivision 3a, does not require that marital property be acquired "through the efforts of either spouse"—it simply requires that the property be acquired during the marriage.

The Defense acknowledges that certain decisions of this Court have held that military death benefits constitute nonmarital property. However, these holdings were based on the specific statutory language and legislative intent underlying the federal benefit programs, not on a blanket rule that all such benefits are nonmarital regardless of circumstances.

In **In re Marriage of O'Brien**, 472 N.W.2d 325, 328 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), the Court emphasized that military death benefits are "not acquired through marital efforts or contributions." However, this reasoning fails to account for the reality that these benefits were received during the 27-year marriage of the parties and were intended to provide for the surviving family members—including the husband who also suffered the loss of his son.

### C. The Benefits Should Be Classified as Marital Property

The Defense submits that the proper analysis under Minnesota law is as follows:

First, the death benefits were received during the marriage. Under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3a, property acquired during the marriage is presumed to be marital property unless it falls within one of the statutory exceptions.

Second, the statutory exception for "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" does not apply here because the federal death benefits are statutory entitlements, not gratuitous transfers in the traditional sense. They are paid pursuant to federal law regardless of the beneficiary's wishes or the deceased's estate planning.

Third, even if the benefits could be characterized as "gifts," they were not acquired "from a third party" in the sense intended by the statute. The federal government paid these benefits not as a gratuitous transfer from a disinterested third party, but as part of a social insurance program designed to provide for surviving family members. Both spouses suffered the loss of their son, and both should be considered part of the class of persons the federal program was intended to protect.

Fourth, the district court erred in failing to apply the presumption of marital property. The benefits were received during the marriage, they were not specifically bequeathed or devised to Loretta alone, and they were not acquired through any effort or contribution by Loretta that can be characterized as separate from the marital relationship.

---

## II. EVEN IF THE BENEFITS ARE NONMARITAL, THE COURT SHOULD CONSIDER THE ENTIRETY OF THE MARITAL ESTATE

The Defense acknowledges that this Court has previously held that military death benefits constitute nonmarital property when paid to a spouse as beneficiary. However, even accepting this characterization for argument's sake, the district court failed to properly consider the totality of the circumstances in its division of property.

The marriage lasted 27 years. During that time, the parties accumulated marital assets and built a life together. The loss of their son was a tragedy that affected both parties equally. While the federal benefits were paid to Loretta as the designated beneficiary, the loss that gave rise to those benefits was a loss shared by both spouses.

The district court's classification of these benefits as Loretta's nonmarital property, while perhaps technically correct under existing precedent, results in an inequitable outcome that undermines the fundamental purpose of Minnesota's marital property laws—which is to ensure a fair and equitable distribution of assets accumulated during the marriage.

---

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's closing statement relies heavily on three cases: **In re Marriage of Smith**, **In re Marriage of Anderson**, and **In re Marriage of O'Brien**. The Defense submits that these cases are distinguishable and do not control the outcome here.

In **Smith**, the Court held that federal death gratuity benefits were nonmarital property because they constituted a "gift" from the federal government. However, the Court in that case did not address the specific argument raised here—whether the presumption of marital property applies to benefits received during a long marriage where both spouses suffered the loss that gave rise to the benefits.

In **Anderson**, 715 N.W.2d 143, 146 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), the Court classified SGLI proceeds as nonmarital property. Again, however, the Court did not address the specific circumstances presented here—where the marriage was of long duration and where the deceased child was a child of both parties.

In **O'Brien**, 472 N.W.2d 325, 328 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), the Court stated that military death benefits "are not acquired through marital efforts or contributions." This statement, while perhaps accurate in a narrow sense, does not address the question of whether such benefits should be classified as marital property when received during a marriage of long duration.

The Defense further notes that the prosecution's argument regarding the "third party" nature of the federal government is unavailing. The statute contemplates gifts from private third parties, not statutory entitlements paid by the federal government pursuant to social insurance programs. To interpret the statute as including federal government payments within the definition of "gift" would expand the statutory exception beyond its intended scope.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **Reverse** the district court's classification of the life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity payments as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property;

2. **Remand** this matter to the district court with instructions to classify the benefits as marital property subject to equitable distribution; and

3. **Grant** such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

The Defense submits that the proper application of Minnesota's marital property statutes requires that these benefits be classified as marital property. The presumption of marital property applies to property acquired during the marriage, and the statutory exception for gifts does not encompass federal statutory entitlements paid pursuant to social insurance programs. To hold otherwise would create an arbitrary distinction between different types of death benefits and would undermine the fundamental purpose of Minnesota's marital property laws.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon review of the closing arguments submitted by both parties. The central issues remain the classification of military death benefits and life insurance proceeds as marital or nonmarital property, and the authority of the district court to divide these benefits. Having considered the arguments presented by Loretta Angell (Prosecution) and Gordon Angell (Defense), we affirm the district court’s classification of the benefits as nonmarital property. We further hold that federal law precludes the division of these benefits, and we therefore reverse the district court’s award to Gordon Angell.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Classification of the Death Benefits**

Gordon Angell, in his closing argument, continues to challenge the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. He argues that these benefits do not fall within the statutory exception for property acquired by "gift" because they are statutory entitlements paid by the federal government rather than gratuitous transfers from a private third party. He further contends that the presumption of marital property was not rebutted because the benefits were acquired during the marriage.

We find these arguments unpersuasive. Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, defines nonmarital property to include "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The term "third party" is not limited to private individuals; it encompasses the United States government. As established in binding precedent, federal death gratuity benefits paid to a surviving spouse are nonmarital property because they constitute a gift from the federal government to the beneficiary, not an asset acquired during the marriage through marital efforts. *In re Marriage of Smith*, 565 N.W.2d 157, 160 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997). Similarly, Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds are nonmarital property when paid to a spouse as beneficiary, as the source of the funds falls squarely within the statutory exception for property acquired by gift. *In re Marriage of Anderson*, 715 N.W.2d 143, 146 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006).

Gordon Angell’s assertion that the presumption of marital property applies because the funds were received during the marriage is also incorrect. The presumption applies to property "acquired by either spouse before or during the marriage," but the critical inquiry is the source of the funds. In *In re Marriage of O’Brien*, 472 N.W.2d 325, 328 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), the Court held that military death benefits paid directly to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property, regardless of when they are received during the marriage, because they are not acquired through marital efforts or contributions. The record confirms that Loretta Angell received these funds as the designated beneficiary of her son’s military service and maintained them in a separate account, never commingling them with marital assets. Consequently, the district court properly classified the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

**B. Division of the Death Benefits**

Having affirmed the classification of the benefits as nonmarital, we turn to the district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from these funds. In his closing argument, Gordon Angell implicitly requests that the benefits be divided, arguing that they should be classified as marital property subject to equitable distribution. He does not address the federal anti-attachment statutes cited in the preliminary ruling.

We reaffirm our conclusion that federal law prohibits the division of these benefits. The federal death-gratuity statute explicitly states that payments made under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475–80 "are not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." 10 U.S.C. § 1477. Likewise, the SGLI statute provides that proceeds "are exempt from the claims of creditors" and "may not be assigned, transferred, or encumbered." 38 U.S.C. § 1970(g).

The United States Supreme Court has held that the federal prohibition on the assignment of military life insurance proceeds precludes state courts from dividing those proceeds in divorce actions. *Ridgway v. Ridgway*, 454 U.S. 46, 60 (1981). The Court emphasized that the statutory language "not assignable" must be given its plain meaning and controls over state community property laws. *Ridgway*, 454 U.S. at 60. The district court’s use of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58 to transfer $150,000 from Loretta Angell to Gordon Angell constitutes a "legal process" that effectively attaches and assigns these federal benefits in violation of federal law.

Gordon Angell’s arguments regarding the "shared loss" of the child and the duration of the marriage, while emotionally compelling, cannot override the clear mandate of federal law. Because the benefits are nonmarital property protected by federal anti-attachment statutes, the district court lacked the authority to award a portion of them to Gordon Angell.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, we hold that the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. We further hold that federal law prohibits the division of these benefits. Therefore, the district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell is reversed.

**ORDER**

IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**.

IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that the district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **REVERSED**.

IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that this matter is **REMANDED** to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
