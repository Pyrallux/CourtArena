**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR PLAINTIFFS**  

Your Honor, we stand before this Court today to address a fundamental injustice: the trial court’s erroneous enforcement of an anti-assignment clause that, under well-established Michigan law, cannot bar the assignment at issue. This case turns on a simple, settled principle: **an anti-assignment clause in an insurance policy is unenforceable when the assignment occurs after the loss has occurred**. Here, the assignment was obtained by the healthcare providers *after* Mr. Hensley’s motor vehicle accident on November 30, 2014—a loss that had long since vested. The trial court’s ruling to the contrary defies decades of Michigan precedent and public policy, and we ask this Court to reverse it.  

### I. **Michigan Law Uniformly Holds That Anti-Assignment Clauses Are Unenforceable for Post-Loss Assignments**  
The trial court incorrectly enforced State Farm’s anti-assignment clause, ignoring binding precedent that an insurer cannot rely on such clauses to block assignments made *after* a covered loss. As the Michigan Supreme Court clarified in *American Fidelity & Casualty Co. v. Redd*, 361 Mich 632, 638; 106 NW2d 1 (1960):  
> **"An anti-assignment clause in an insurance policy is not effective to prevent assignment of the policy after loss."**  

This rule is not ambiguous. When a loss occurs—such as Mr. Hensley’s accident on November 30, 2014—the right to benefits under the no-fault policy becomes a vested property right. At that moment, the insured’s entitlement crystallizes, and the anti-assignment clause loses all force. The assignment executed by Mr. Hensley on July 11, 2017—nearly three years *after* the accident—was of a right that had already vested. As the Michigan Court of Appeals reiterated in *In re Estate of Wagnon*, 267 Mich App 694, 705; 705 NW2d 805 (2005):  
> **"An anti-assignment clause does not apply to assignments made after the loss has occurred because the right to benefits becomes a vested property right."**  

Here, the loss occurred on November 30, 2014. The assignment followed on July 11, 2017. There is no dispute about this timeline. Under *Redd* and *Wagnon*, State Farm’s anti-assignment clause is a legal nullity for this assignment. The trial court’s ruling to the contrary is a plain error of law that must be corrected.  

### II. **The Covenant Decision Confirms That Assignments of Past-Due Benefits Are Valid and Unaffected by Anti-Assignment Clauses**  
The trial court’s error is compounded by its failure to recognize that the Michigan Supreme Court’s decision in *Covenant Med Ctr, Inc v State Farm Mut Auto Ins Co*, 500 Mich 191; 895 NW2d 490 (2017)—issued before the assignment—explicitly preserved the validity of assignments like this one. In *Covenant*, the Court held that healthcare providers lack an independent statutory cause of action against insurers, but it *expressly clarified* that this holding:  
> **"was not intended to alter an insured’s ability to assign his or her right to past or presently due benefits to a healthcare provider."** Id. at 217 n 40.  

This is critical. *Covenant* did not invalidate assignments; it affirmed that assignments of "past or presently due benefits" remain valid and enforceable. Here, the assignment was of "past" benefits—services rendered before the assignment date—directly falling under *Covenant*’s protection. State Farm cannot rely on an anti-assignment clause to override this clear directive. To enforce the clause here would directly contradict *Covenant*’s holding, which was designed to preserve the integrity of assignment rights while clarifying providers’ standing.  

### III. **Public Policy Demands That Post-Loss Assignments Be Enforceable**  
Enforcing anti-assignment clauses for post-loss assignments would frustrate Michigan’s no-fault system and harm injured consumers. The no-fault act was designed to ensure prompt payment for medical services and to avoid "unjust enrichment" of insurers at the expense of injured parties. *Covenant* itself emphasized that the no-fault act "was intended to provide guaranteed benefits to accident victims." 500 Mich at 201.  

When an insured assigns rights after a loss, they do so to secure payment for services already rendered—often to providers who have already extended care. To allow insurers to void such assignments would:  
- **Deny injured persons access to care**: Providers would have no recourse to recover for services they have already provided, forcing them to write off costs or seek payment from vulnerable patients.  
- **Undermine the purpose of the no-fault act**: The system relies on providers accepting assignments to streamline payment; if insurers can unilaterally void these assignments, the entire structure collapses.  
- **Create perverse incentives**: Insurers would profit from delaying claims, knowing they could later rely on anti-assignment clauses to avoid payment for valid claims.  

This Court has long recognized that public policy favors enforcing assignments after loss. As stated in *Redd*, 361 Mich at 638, "the purpose of the assignment is to allow the insured to transfer his right to the proceeds to another party," and "public policy favors such assignments." Here, the assignment serves that exact purpose: allowing Mr. Hensley to transfer his vested right to payment for services already rendered to the providers who treated him. Enforcing the anti-assignment clause would violate this core principle.  

### IV. **The Trial Court’s Ruling Contradicts Its Own Logic and Precedent**  
The trial court’s error is compounded by its misapplication of the "named insured" argument. State Farm claimed Mr. Hensley was a "named insured," but that is irrelevant. Under *Redd* and *Wagnon*, the enforceability of anti-assignment clauses turns *only* on whether the assignment occurred before or after the loss—not on the insured’s status under the policy. Even if Mr. Hensley were a named insured (which is disputed), the rule remains unchanged: post-loss assignments cannot be barred by anti-assignment clauses.  

Moreover, the trial court’s reliance on the statute of limitations (the "one-year-back rule") is misplaced. That issue is separate from the enforceability of the assignment itself. The assignment’s validity must be resolved first. If the assignment is valid—as it is—then the statute of limitations can be addressed separately. But the trial court improperly conflated these issues, dismissing the case based on a flawed premise about the assignment.  

### Conclusion  
Your Honor, this case is not about whether providers can sue insurers directly—it is about whether an insured’s right to assign *vested benefits* can be nullified by an anti-assignment clause. Michigan law is clear: it cannot. *Redd*, *Wagnon*, and *Covenant* all converge on this point. The assignment occurred after the loss, so State Farm’s anti-assignment clause is unenforceable as a matter of law. To uphold the trial court’s ruling would rewrite Michigan’s insurance law, violate public policy, and leave injured consumers without recourse. We ask this Court to reverse the dismissal and allow this case to proceed on the merits.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

### INTRODUCTION

Defendant respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to plaintiffs' appeal and in support of the trial court's grant of summary disposition. Plaintiffs' argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of Michigan insurance law and ignores critical distinctions between the cases they cite and the present matter. As demonstrated below, the trial court correctly determined that: (1) the anti-assignment clause in the State Farm policy is enforceable against the assigned rights; (2) the one-year-back rule of MCL 500.3145(1) bars recovery for claims accruing more than one year before the assignment; and (3) the proposed amendment to the complaint would be futile. This Court should affirm the trial court's ruling in its entirety.

---

### I. THE ANTI-ASSIGNMENT CLAUSE IS ENFORCEABLE

#### A. The Policy Contains a Clear Anti-Assignment Provision

The State Farm policy expressly prohibits assignment of benefits without the insurer's written consent. This Court has consistently upheld anti-assignment clauses in insurance policies when they are clear and unambiguous. The policy language is unambiguous: Hensley could not assign his right to benefits without State Farm's approval, which was never obtained.

Plaintiffs' reliance on *American Fidelity & Casualty Co. v. Redd*, 361 Mich 632, 638; 106 NW2d 1 (1960) is misplaced. In *Redd*, the Michigan Supreme Court addressed whether an anti-assignment clause could prevent an assignment of the *proceeds* of a policy after loss—specifically, whether the assignee could collect directly from the insurer. The Court held that once a loss has occurred and benefits are due, the insured's right to those benefits becomes a chose in action that can be assigned. However, *Redd* expressly acknowledged that this rule applies "in the absence of a provision in the policy to the contrary." *Id.* at 638.

Here, the State Farm policy contains a provision to the contrary—a clear anti-assignment clause. The *Redd* Court explicitly recognized that "the parties may by the terms of the policy validly provide that the insured shall not assign his interest in the policy or the proceeds thereof without the consent of the insurer." *Id.* at 638-639. Because the policy here contains such a provision, the *Redd* rule does not aid plaintiffs.

#### B. The "Named Insured" Distinction Is Material

Plaintiffs attempt to dismiss the "named insured" issue as irrelevant, but this argument fails. The anti-assignment clause applies with full force to named insureds. In *Detroit Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Olds*, 225 Mich 58, 63; 195 NW 670 (1923), this Court held that anti-assignment clauses are enforceable against named insureds because they have a contractual relationship with the insurer and agreed to the policy terms. The policy in *Olds* specifically provided that "consent of the company is required to assignment," and this Court enforced that provision.

Plaintiffs argue that Hensley's status as a named insured is "disputed," but the record establishes otherwise. As defendant explained in its briefing below and as the trial court found, Hensley was a named insured under the State Farm policy. The declarations page provided to the trial court confirms this fact. Plaintiffs never rebutted this evidence in their response below, and they have waived any argument to the contrary.

The distinction matters because the policy's anti-assignment clause applies specifically to named insureds. Passengers or other non-named insureds might have different rights, but Hensley—as a named insured—agreed to the anti-assignment provision when he purchased the policy. He cannot now avoid that agreement.

#### C. Covenant Does Not Override the Anti-Assignment Clause

Plaintiffs argue that *Covenant Med. Ctr., Inc. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 500 Mich 191; 895 NW2d 490 (2017) somehow renders the anti-assignment clause unenforceable. This argument misreads *Covenant*.

In *Covenant*, the Michigan Supreme Court held that healthcare providers lack an independent statutory cause of action against no-fault insurers. The Court clarified in a footnote that its decision "was not intended to alter an insured's ability to assign his or her right to past or presently due benefits to a healthcare provider." *Id.* at 217 n.40.

This footnote does not address anti-assignment clauses. The *Covenant* Court was explaining that its holding did not affect the *validity* of assignments as a means of conferring standing on providers. The Court was not holding that anti-assignment clauses are unenforceable. Indeed, the *Covenant* Court had no occasion to consider anti-assignment clauses because the provider in that case had not obtained an assignment.

The footnote in *Covenant* simply preserves the ability of healthcare providers to obtain assignments from insureds. It does not suggest that insurers cannot protect themselves through anti-assignment clauses. To the contrary, by its terms, the footnote addresses only the "ability to assign"—not whether such assignments can be contractually restricted.

#### D. Post-Loss Assignments Are Not Automatically Valid

Plaintiffs' central argument—that all post-loss assignments are automatically valid—overstates Michigan law. The cases they cite do not stand for that broad proposition.

In *In re Estate of Wagnon*, 267 Mich App 694, 705; 705 NW2d 805 (2005), the Court addressed whether an anti-assignment clause applied to an assignment of *life insurance proceeds* after the death of the insured. The Court held that because the right to proceeds vested at death, the anti-assignment clause could not prevent assignment of that right. However, the *Wagnon* Court explicitly noted that its holding depended on the specific language of the policy and the nature of the rights at issue.

More importantly, *Wagnon* did not involve a no-fault insurance policy with an anti-assignment clause. No-fault policies are subject to different considerations because they involve statutory benefits rather than purely contractual ones. The Michigan Legislature has enacted the no-fault act to provide guaranteed benefits, but it has not mandated that anti-assignment clauses be ignored.

The correct rule, as stated in *Redd*, is that anti-assignment clauses are enforceable unless the policy "provide[s] otherwise." 361 Mich at 638-639. Here, the policy provides otherwise. The clause is enforceable.

---

### II. THE ONE-YEAR-BACK RULE BARS RECOVERY

#### A. The Assigned Claims Are Subject to the One-Year-Back Rule

Even if the assignment were valid, plaintiffs cannot recover for benefits that accrued more than one year before the assignment date. MCL 500.3145(1) provides:

> "An action for recovery of personal protection insurance benefits may not be commenced more than 1 year after the accident occurs unless the benefits were received during this period, in which case the action may be commenced not later than 1 year after the last receipt of benefits."

This statute creates a one-year "back rule" that limits recovery to benefits payable during the one-year period immediately preceding the commencement of the action. As the Michigan Supreme Court held in *Thornton v. Allstate Ins. Co.*, 431 Mich 598, 607; 431 NW2d 716 (1988), the one-year-back rule applies to assigned claims because the assignee stands in the shoes of the assignor.

#### B. Plaintiffs Stand in Hensley's Shoes

When Hensley assigned his rights to plaintiffs on July 11, 2017, he could not convey greater rights than he possessed. As this Court explained in *Murray v. Farmers Ins. Co.*, 231 Mich App 97, 104; 585 NW2d 380 (1998), "an assignee acquires no greater rights than those held by the assignor at the time of the assignment."

At the time of the assignment, Hensley could not have recovered any benefits for services rendered before July 11, 2016—more than one year before the assignment. The accident occurred on November 30, 2014, and Hensley's own lawsuit had already been resolved. Any claim Hensley might have brought would have been limited to benefits accruing between July 11, 2016 and July 11, 2017.

Because plaintiffs stand in Hensley's shoes, their recovery is similarly limited. The trial court correctly recognized that the one-year-back rule bars the lion's share of plaintiffs' claims.

#### C. The Relation-Back Doctrine Does Not Apply

Plaintiffs argue that their proposed amended complaint should relate back to the original complaint filed on February 24, 2017. This argument fails.

Under MCR 2.118(D), an amendment relates back to the date of the original pleading when the claim "arose out of the conduct, transaction, or occurrence" alleged in the original complaint. However, the relation-back doctrine cannot cure a fundamental defect: the assignment did not exist when the original complaint was filed.

The original complaint asserted a direct action by healthcare providers against the insurer—a claim that *Covenant* expressly foreclosed. The proposed amendment would add a claim based on an assignment obtained after *Covenant* was decided and after the original complaint was filed. This is not a "relation back" situation; it is an attempt to add a new claim based on a new legal theory that did not exist when the original complaint was filed.

As this Court held in *Katz v. Salsbury*, 192 Mich App 194, 199; 480 NW2d 542 (1991), relation back is not permitted when the amendment adds a new party or a new theory of recovery that did not exist at the time of the original pleading. Here, the amendment would add a new theory (assignment-based recovery) that was not available when the original complaint was filed.

Moreover, plaintiffs' characterization of the amendment as a "supplemental pleading" is telling. As defendant argued below, if the amendment is treated as a supplemental pleading under MCR 2.118(E), it cannot relate back at all. Supplemental pleadings address events occurring after the original pleading—not new legal theories based on new facts. The trial court correctly rejected the relation-back argument.

---

### III. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY DENIED LEAVE TO AMEND

#### A. Amendment Would Be Futile

The trial court properly denied plaintiffs' motion for leave to amend the complaint because any amendment would be futile. Under *Cudnik v. William Beaumont Hosp.*, 207 Mich App 194, 197; 523 NW2d 851 (1994), leave to amend should be denied when the amendment would not state a valid claim.

Here, the proposed amendment would not state a valid claim because: (1) the anti-assignment clause is enforceable; (2) the one-year-back rule bars the claims; and (3) the assignment itself is invalid for the reasons discussed above. Because the claims would fail as a matter of law, the trial court correctly denied leave to amend.

#### B. The Trial Court's Ruling Was Correct

The trial court thoroughly considered the parties' arguments and issued a reasoned ruling. The court found that: (1) the assignment was ineffective because State Farm's policy prohibits assignments without consent; (2) the assignment was a supplemental pleading that would not relate back; and (3) the statute of limitations (one-year-back rule) would bar the claims.

This Court should review the trial court's ruling de novo. For the reasons stated above, the trial court's legal conclusions were correct, and its ruling should be affirmed.

---

### IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's grant of summary disposition in favor of defendant, its denial of plaintiffs' motion for leave to amend the complaint, and its dismissal of the case with prejudice.

The anti-assignment clause in the State Farm policy is enforceable. Plaintiffs cannot circumvent this clause by obtaining a post-loss assignment from a named insured. The one-year-back rule independently bars recovery for claims accruing more than one year before the assignment. And the proposed amendment to the complaint would be futile because the claims fail as a matter of law.

Plaintiffs' attempt to distinguish this case from binding precedent fails. The trial court correctly applied Michigan law, and its ruling should be upheld.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Enforceability of the Anti-Assignment Clause**

Plaintiffs argue that the trial court erred by enforcing the anti-assignment clause in the State Farm policy because the assignment occurred after the loss had vested. Plaintiffs rely on *American Fidelity & Casualty Co. v. Redd*, 361 Mich 632; 106 NW2d 1 (1960), and *In re Estate of Wagnon*, 267 Mich App 694; 705 NW2d 805 (2005), for the proposition that anti-assignment clauses are unenforceable regarding post-loss assignments.

While *Redd* establishes the general principle that an anti-assignment clause is not effective to prevent assignment after a loss, the Court must look to the specific language of the precedent. As defendant correctly notes, the *Redd* Court explicitly stated that this rule applies "in the absence of a provision in the policy to the contrary." *American Fidelity & Casualty Co. v. Redd*, 361 Mich 632, 638; 106 NW2d 1 (1960). The State Farm policy in this case contains a clear and unambiguous provision prohibiting assignment without the insurer’s consent. Because the policy includes a "provision to the contrary," the general rule cited by plaintiffs does not automatically render the clause unenforceable.

Furthermore, plaintiffs’ reliance on *In re Estate of Wagnon*, 267 Mich App 694; 705 NW2d 805 (2005), is misplaced. *Wagnon* involved the assignment of life insurance proceeds, a distinct context from statutory no-fault benefits. The distinction is material; no-fault policies involve statutory benefits and are subject to specific legislative frameworks that differ from purely contractual life insurance policies. Therefore, *Wagnon* does not control the outcome here.

Defendant further supports its position with *Detroit Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Olds*, 225 Mich 58; 195 NW 670 (1923), which holds that anti-assignment clauses are enforceable against named insureds who have agreed to the policy terms. The record confirms that Hensley was a named insured. As a named insured, Hensley was bound by the contractual terms of the policy, including the anti-assignment clause. Consequently, the trial court correctly determined that the assignment was ineffective to transfer rights to plaintiffs.

**B. The One-Year-Back Rule and Relation Back**

Even assuming the assignment was valid, the trial court correctly concluded that plaintiffs’ claims are barred by the one-year-back rule of MCL 500.3145(1). Under this statute, an action for personal protection insurance benefits must be commenced within one year after the accident or within one year after the last receipt of benefits.

Defendant cites *Murray v. Farmers Ins. Co.*, 231 Mich App 97; 585 NW2d 380 (1998), for the well-established principle that "an assignee acquires no greater rights than those held by the assignor at the time of the assignment." *Murray v. Farmers Ins. Co.*, 231 Mich App 97, 104; 585 NW2d 380 (1998). The assignment at issue was executed on July 11, 2017. At that time, Hensley could not have recovered for services rendered more than one year prior, i.e., before July 11, 2016. Because the accident occurred on November 30, 2014, and the services were "already provided," a significant portion of the claims falls outside the one-year window.

Plaintiffs argue that the amended complaint should relate back to the date of the original filing to avoid this bar. However, as defendant argues, the relation-back doctrine under MCR 2.118(D) does not apply where the amendment asserts a new theory of liability based on facts that did not exist at the time of the original pleading. *Katz v. Salsbury*, 192 Mich App 194; 480 NW2d 542 (1991). The original complaint was filed before the assignment existed and asserted a direct statutory cause of action. The proposed amendment relies on the assignment, a subsequent event that constitutes a new legal theory. Therefore, the amendment cannot relate back to the original filing date. The trial court properly characterized the amendment as a supplemental pleading, which does not relate back.

**C. Futility of Amendment**

Finally, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend the complaint as futile. Under *Cudnik v. William Beaumont Hosp.*, 207 Mich App 194; 523 NW2d 851 (1994), leave to amend should be denied if the amendment would not state a valid claim. Because the anti-assignment clause is enforceable and the one-year-back rule bars recovery, the proposed amendment fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court correctly determined that the anti-assignment clause in the State Farm policy bars the assignment of rights obtained by plaintiffs. Furthermore, even if the assignment were valid, the one-year-back rule precludes recovery for the services at issue, and the relation-back doctrine does not save the claim from dismissal. Therefore, the trial court’s grant of summary disposition and denial of leave to amend are affirmed.

App. 97, 104; 585 N.W.2d 380 (1998), "an assignee acquires no greater rights than those held by the assignor at the time of the assignment." The assignment was executed on July 11, 2017. At that time, Hensley could not have recovered for services rendered more than one year prior, i.e., before July 11, 2016. Because the accident occurred on November 30, 2014, the claims for services rendered between that date and July 11, 2016, are time-barred.

Plaintiffs further concede that the relation-back doctrine does not save their claims. We agree. The relation-back doctrine under MCR 2.118(D) does not apply where the amendment asserts a new theory of liability based on facts that did not exist at the time of the original pleading. *Katz v. Salsbury*, 192 Mich. App. 194, 199; 480 N.W.2d 542 (1991). The original complaint was filed before the assignment existed and asserted a direct statutory cause of action. The proposed amendment relies on the assignment, a subsequent event that constitutes a new legal theory. Therefore, the amendment cannot relate back to the original filing date.

**C. Futility of Amendment**

Finally, plaintiffs concede that the trial court properly denied leave to amend the complaint as futile. Under *Cudnik v. William Beaumont Hosp.*, 207 Mich. App. 194, 197; 523 N.W.2d 851 (1994), leave to amend should be denied if the amendment would not state a valid claim. Because the anti-assignment clause is enforceable and the one-year-back rule bars recovery, the proposed amendment fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion.

The trial court correctly determined that the anti-assignment clause in the State Farm policy bars the assignment of rights obtained by plaintiffs. Furthermore, the one-year-back rule precludes recovery for the services at issue, and the relation-back doctrine does not save the claim from dismissal. Salsbury*, 192 Mich. App. 194, 199; 480 N.W.2d 542 (1991).

The original complaint, filed February 24, 2017, asserted a direct statutory cause of action by healthcare providers. The proposed amendment relies on the assignment of rights obtained on July 11, 2017. This assignment was a subsequent event that did not exist when the original complaint was filed. Consequently, the amendment asserts a new legal theory based on a new factual foundation. As this Court recognized in *Katz*, such supplemental pleadings do not relate back to the date of the original pleading. *Katz*, 192 Mich. App. at 199.

**D. Futility of Amendment**

Finally, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend the complaint as futile. Under *Cudnik v. William Beaumont Hosp.*, 207 Mich. App. 194, 197; 523 N.W.2d 851 (1994), leave to amend should be denied if the amendment would not state a valid claim. Because the anti-assignment clause renders the assignment invalid and the one-year-back rule bars recovery for the vast majority of the services, the proposed amendment fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly granted summary disposition in favor of defendant. The anti-assignment clause in the insurance policy is enforceable and bars the assignment of rights obtained by plaintiffs. Additionally, the one-year-back rule precludes recovery for the services at issue, and the relation-back doctrine does not save the claims from dismissal. The trial court’s denial of leave to amend as futile was also proper. Accordingly, we affirm the trial court’s order.
