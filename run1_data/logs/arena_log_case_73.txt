**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, today we present a clear case of legal error that demands reversal. The Director of Labor and Industrial Relations Appeals Board (LIRAB) misapplied the law in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim for the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma. This error stems from the Director’s fundamental misunderstanding of the applicable legal standard: *Flor v. Holguin* does not govern this case. As this Court has repeatedly held, *Flor* applies *only* to injuries by disease *resulting from the nature of the employment*—i.e., occupational diseases where the condition is peculiar to the job. But this is not an occupational disease claim. It is a claim for the *aggravation of a pre-existing condition*. For such claims, the law requires application of the **unitary test for proximate cause**, not the *Flor* test.  

Let me be unequivocal: *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977), stands for a narrow proposition. It holds that for a disease to be compensable as an occupational injury, the claimant must prove the disease *arose from the nature of the employment*—meaning the condition must be inherent to or uniquely caused by the job. But when a pre-existing condition is *aggravated* by employment-related factors, the law shifts entirely. As this Court has made clear, the unitary test governs: the claimant need only show that the employment was a *proximate cause* of the aggravation. That is a different standard, with different requirements. Here, the Director ignored this distinction and misapplied *Flor* to a claim that squarely falls under the unitary test. That error alone warrants reversal.  

Now, let me turn to the facts—facts that, under the correct legal standard, prove proximate cause beyond doubt. Van Ness had a long history of controlled asthma. He had successfully managed it for years through medication and avoidance strategies. But his employment at Lahainaluna High School changed everything. From late October 2005 through April 2006, severe vog blanketed Maui. During this period, Van Ness was required to perform strenuous physical labor *in the midst of that vog*. His job demanded he repeatedly climb hundreds of stairs daily—up to 20 minutes to ascend from the lower campus to his office, stopping to catch his breath—while battling worsening symptoms. His own testimony is uncontroverted: on high-vog days, he could not see the administrative building halfway down the campus; his coughing became "incredibly sharp, like a stabbing pain"; his heart pounded; his face turned red; and he frequently needed to refill his inhalers, exhausting his prescription months early.  

Critically, medical evidence confirms the causal link. Dr. Mathison, a specialist, documented that Van Ness’s symptoms "only partially controlled with medications" during the vog season, and that "volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash" were a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." Dr. Mathison further noted that Van Ness’s condition improved *only after he left Maui*—and worsened again upon return. This is not speculation; it is objective medical proof that employment exposure to vog, coupled with the physical exertion his job required, directly exacerbated his asthma.  

Yet the Director denied the claim based solely on the fact that vog was present "on the entire island of Maui." That reasoning is legally irrelevant under the unitary test. The unitary test does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace; it asks only whether employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation. If a worker with a pre-existing condition is exposed to a general hazard *because of their job duties*—and those duties significantly worsen the condition—the law compensates. Here, Van Ness’s job *required* him to be outdoors in high-vog conditions while performing physically demanding tasks. That is not a coincidence; it is the direct cause of the aggravation.  

To illustrate: imagine a firefighter with pre-existing heart disease. If smoke in the general environment triggers a heart attack, but the firefighter’s job requires him to breathe smoke while battling fires, the unitary test would find proximate cause—because the employment *intensified* the exposure. The same principle applies here. Vog may be island-wide, but Van Ness’s employment *forced him to endure it while exerting himself physically*—a combination that directly worsened his symptoms beyond what would have occurred in a non-employment setting.  

The Director’s error is profound. By applying *Flor*—a test for occupational diseases—to an aggravation claim, the Director imposed an impossible burden: proof that vog was exclusive to the school. That is not the law. Under the unitary test, the question is not *where* the vog was present, but *whether employment contributed to the harm*. And the evidence shows it did.  

Your Honor, this case is not about whether vog exists generally. It is about whether Van Ness’s work *caused* his injury to worsen. The Director’s misapplication of the law cannot stand. For the reasons stated, we ask this Court to reverse the denial and award benefits.  

Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE HAWAII LABOR AND INDUSTRIAL RELATIONS APPEALS BOARD

**VAN NESS, Claimant-Appellant**

**STATE OF HAWAII, DEPARTMENT OF EDUCATION, Employer-Appellee**

## INTRODUCTION

The Director of the Labor and Industrial Relations Appeals Board ("Director") correctly denied Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The Plaintiff-Appellant's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of both the applicable legal standard and the factual record. This defense responds to the Plaintiff's opening argument and demonstrates that: (1) the Director applied the correct legal standard; (2) even under the unitary test for proximate cause, the Plaintiff failed to establish the required causal connection; and (3) the evidence overwhelmingly supports the Director's finding that Van Ness's respiratory condition was not work-related.

---

## I. THE DIRECTOR APPLIED THE CORRECT LEGAL STANDARD

### A. The Nature of the Claim Determines the Applicable Test

The Plaintiff incorrectly asserts that the *Flor v. Holguin* test applies only to "occupational disease claims" and that "aggravation claims" require the "unitary test." This characterization misstates the law. The Hawaii Supreme Court in *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977), established the standard for determining whether a disease arises out of employment, and this standard applies to all disease claims, including aggravations of pre-existing conditions. The *Flor* court held that for a disease to be compensable, it must "aris[e] out of" the employment, which requires a nexus between the employment and the condition. *Flor*, 58 Haw. at 326, 568 P.2d at 419.

The "unitary test" to which the Plaintiff refers is not a separate legal standard but rather a formulation of proximate cause analysis used in workers' compensation cases. Under Hawaii law, a claimant must prove that the employment was a "proximate cause" of the condition—not merely that the condition existed while the claimant was employed. The question is whether the employment contributed to the harm in a way that makes the injury compensable under the Workers' Compensation Act.

### B. The Director Properly Analyzed Whether Employment Was a Proximate Cause

The Director's decision reflects the correct legal analysis. The Director found that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition" and that "there was insufficient evidence to support Van Ness's claim that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that [Van Ness's] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only." This analysis is consistent with the proximate cause requirement.

The Plaintiff argues that the unitary test does "not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace." However, this defense does not contend that the hazard must be unique. Rather, the question is whether employment was a *substantial contributing cause* of the aggravation. The Plaintiff conflates "proximate cause" with mere "but for" causation. Under workers' compensation law, the employment must be a significant factor—not merely a background condition—in causing the injury.

---

## II. THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT SUPPORT A FINDING THAT EMPLOYMENT WAS A PROXIMATE CAUSE OF VAN NESS'S ASTHMA AGGRAVATION

### A. Vog Was a General Environmental Condition Affecting All of Maui

The uncontradicted evidence demonstrates that vog was present across the entire island of Maui during the relevant period. The Plaintiff's own testimony established that vog was "significant" and that on severe days, he could not see the administrative building "halfway down the campus." However, Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. The Director found, and the evidence supports, that vog was present "on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment."

This is legally significant. Van Ness was exposed to vog both at work and at home. His residence in Kihei—where vog was "even worse"—was not part of his employment. The Plaintiff has not established that employment exposure was a proximate cause as opposed to his general environmental exposure.

### B. Van Ness's Employment Did Not Require Unusual Exposure to Vog

The Plaintiff argues that Van Ness's job "required him to be outdoors in high-vog conditions while performing physically demanding tasks." However, the evidence does not support this characterization.

Van Ness was a technology coordinator. His job duties involved maintaining computers in school buildings. While the Plaintiff emphasizes the physical demands of climbing stairs, this physical activity was not caused by vog—it was caused by the school's geography. The stairs existed regardless of vog levels. Van Ness would have had to climb the stairs whether or not vog was present.

Moreover, Van Ness's exposure to vog was not increased by his employment duties. He was not tasked with outdoor work that exposed him to vog specifically. His office was in the library at the highest point of campus—a location that, if anything, may have had *less* vog exposure than lower areas, given the mountain's elevation and the vog's tendency to settle in lower elevations. The Plaintiff's own testimony established that he "was not much of an outdoors person" and that his work at other schools on Oahu involved "upgrading the computers" rather than extensive outdoor activity.

### C. Van Ness's Own Choices Contributed to His Exposure

The record reveals that Van Ness chose to live in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis confirmed "the vog was even worse" than at Lahainaluna. This choice contributed significantly to his overall vog exposure. The Plaintiff has not explained why the employer should be responsible for exposure that Van Ness voluntarily incurred by choosing his residence.

Additionally, Van Ness traveled to California in October 2004, where he was hospitalized for a diaphragmatic hernia and contracted pneumonia. This pre-existing condition and its treatment preceded his Maui assignment and contributed to his respiratory difficulties.

### D. Medical Evidence Does Not Establish Employment as Proximate Cause

The Plaintiff relies heavily on Dr. Mathison's report, but this report does not establish what the Plaintiff claims.

Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness had "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during the vog season and that "volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash" was a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." However, Dr. Mathison did not opine that Van Ness's *employment* caused or contributed to his aggravation. He noted only that exposure to volcanic products aggravated Van Ness's condition—a condition that existed regardless of where Van Ness worked.

Dr. Mathison's statement that Van Ness "can be considered to be disabled by virtue of his asthmatic tendency and susceptibility to the volcanic pollutant effects" does not establish that employment was a proximate cause. The disability, if any, arose from Van Ness's pre-existing susceptibility to volcanic pollutants—an environmental condition affecting all of Maui, not merely his workplace.

Critically, Dr. Mathison did not have the opportunity to review all relevant medical records when preparing his report. Dr. Arora, the Independent Medical Examiner, noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." This observation is consistent with the record evidence that vog was island-wide.

### E. The Timeline Does Not Support a Finding of Employment-Related Aggravation

The evidence shows that Van Ness's symptoms improved when he left Maui and worsened when he returned—but this pattern is consistent with general environmental exposure, not employment-specific exposure. Van Ness testified that while in Philadelphia, his wheezing and shortness of breath "essentially resolved," and they returned "as soon as he returned to Maui." This pattern demonstrates that Van Ness's condition was sensitive to vog exposure generally, not specifically to exposure at Lahainaluna.

The fact that Van Ness improved after his transfer to Oahu in November 2006 does not establish employment as the cause. He also stopped being exposed to vog on Maui generally. The evidence does not isolate employment exposure from general environmental exposure.

## III. THE DIRECTOR'S FINDINGS ARE SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations Appeals Board (Director) denying Claimant’s claim for workers’ compensation benefits. The sole issue for determination in this preliminary ruling is whether the Director committed legal error in denying the claim by applying the incorrect legal standard to the facts presented. Specifically, the Court must determine whether the Director improperly applied the standard for occupational diseases established in *Flor v. Holguin* to a claim involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition, or whether the "unitary test" for proximate cause should have governed the analysis.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The Hawaii Supreme Court has established distinct legal standards for compensable injuries depending on whether the claim involves an occupational disease or the aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977), the court articulated the standard for occupational diseases, holding that for a disease to be compensable, it must "aris[e] out of" the employment, meaning the risk must be inherent to the nature of the employment or peculiar to the job. Under *Flor*, a claimant must demonstrate a causal connection between the disease and the employment conditions that are unique to that employment.

However, where a claimant suffers from a pre-existing condition that is aggravated by employment-related factors, the law requires the application of the "unitary test" for proximate cause. Under this standard, the inquiry is not whether the hazard was unique to the workplace, but whether the employment was a substantial contributing cause or a proximate cause of the injury or aggravation. The employment need not be the sole cause, nor must the risk be exclusive to the job, provided the employment contributed to the harm in a significant way.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Director denied Claimant’s benefits, finding that "the claimant’s respiratory problem and aggravation of his asthma condition was not work related" because the vog was present "on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director further concluded that there was insufficient evidence that exposure at Lahainaluna School was "so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only."

The Court finds that the Director misapplied the law by utilizing the *Flor* "uniqueness" standard to an aggravation claim. The claimant here, Van Ness, does not assert that his asthma originated as an occupational disease of technology coordination. Rather, the evidence establishes that he had a long history of "mild persistent asthma" which was largely controlled prior to his assignment at Lahainaluna. The claim is therefore one for the *aggravation* of a pre-existing condition.

Consequently, the correct legal standard is the unitary test for proximate cause. Under this test, the Director’s focus on whether the vog was "island-wide" is misplaced. The relevant inquiry is whether the employment conditions contributed to the aggravation of the condition. The record demonstrates that Van Ness’s employment at Lahainaluna required him to perform significant physical exertion—specifically, repeatedly climbing steep stairs and traversing a mountainous campus for seven to eight hours a day—while simultaneously being exposed to severe vog. Medical evidence from Dr. Mathison confirms that Van Ness experienced a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during this period and that volcanic smoke is a "well-recognized risk factor." Furthermore, Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness’s condition improved when he was away from Maui and worsened upon his return.

The Defense argues that the employment was not a proximate cause because vog is a general environmental condition and Van Ness’s residence in Kihei had worse vog. However, under the unitary test, the fact that a hazard exists generally does not preclude a finding that employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation. The evidence supports a finding that the physical demands of the job, performed in the specific environment of the school, combined with the vog exposure to precipitate the aggravation. The Director’s reliance on the "island-wide" presence of vog to deny the claim effectively imposed the *Flor* requirement that the risk be peculiar to the employment, which is not the law for aggravation claims.

Therefore, the Director committed legal error by failing to apply the unitary test for proximate cause. The Director's conclusion that there was no nexus between the employment and the injury was based on an erroneous view of the law, specifically the requirement that the exposure be unique to the workplace.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that the Director applied the incorrect legal standard to the facts of this case. The Director erred by applying the *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977), standard for occupational diseases to a claim involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. Under the correct unitary test for proximate cause, substantial evidence in the record suggests that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying benefits is VACATED, and the case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

As established in the preliminary ruling and acknowledged by the Defense, the correct legal standard for determining compensability of a pre-existing condition aggravated by employment is the "unitary test" for proximate cause. This standard is distinct from the "uniqueness" requirement articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977), which applies to occupational diseases originating from the employment.

Under the unitary test, a claimant must demonstrate that the employment was a "substantial contributing cause" of the injury or aggravation. The employment need not be the sole cause of the injury, nor must the risk be unique to the workplace, provided the employment conditions contributed to the harm in a significant way. *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977).

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Defense argues that the Director’s decision should be upheld or, alternatively, remanded for a determination of whether the employment was a substantial contributing cause. The Defense contends that the evidence shows Van Ness’s exposure to vog was worse at his home in Kihei than at work, and that the physical exertion required to climb stairs was merely a result of the school's geography rather than a condition of his employment.

The Court finds that the Defense’s arguments regarding the comparative severity of vog exposure and the nature of the physical exertion are factual inquiries that were never weighed by the Director under the correct legal standard. The Director’s decision explicitly relied on the finding that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" to conclude there was "no nexus between the claimant’s employment and his injury." This reasoning is inextricably tied to the *Flor* "uniqueness" requirement. By finding that the hazard was not unique to the workplace, the Director improperly concluded that the claim could not be compensable.

The Defense’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s statement that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" reinforces the fact that the Director applied the *Flor* standard. Under the unitary test, the fact that a hazard is general to the environment does not automatically absolve the employer of liability if the employment required the claimant to be exposed to that hazard while performing strenuous duties that exacerbated the condition. The record indicates that Van Ness was required to traverse the mountainous campus and climb stairs repeatedly as part of his job duties to service computers. Whether this combination of physical exertion and environmental exposure constitutes a "substantial contributing cause" is a question of fact that must be analyzed under the unitary test, not dismissed because the exposure was not exclusive to the workplace.

The Defense correctly notes that temporal coincidence alone does not establish causation. However, the Director did not engage in the necessary analysis of whether the employment conditions—specifically the physical demands of the job performed during the period of severe vog—substantially contributed to the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma. The Director’s failure to do so stems from the legal error of applying the *Flor* standard.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s arguments highlight valid factual disputes regarding the extent of exposure and the specific impact of the job duties. However, because the Director denied the claim based on the legal conclusion that the injury was not work-related due to the island-wide nature of the vog, the Director failed to make the necessary findings of fact under the unitary test.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision is VACATED. The case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must apply the unitary test for proximate cause and determine whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing cause of the aggravation of his asthma, considering all relevant evidence, including the comparative exposure at home versus work and the physical demands of the job.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon the closing arguments of the Prosecution (Claimant) and the Defense (Employer) following the preliminary ruling which vacated the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations Appeals Board (Director). The Director denied Claimant’s workers’ compensation claim, concluding that his injury was not work-related because the volcanic smog (vog) affecting him was present island-wide and not unique to his place of employment. The Court has previously determined that the Director applied the incorrect legal standard by relying on the "uniqueness" test of *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977), rather than the "unitary test" for proximate cause. The Defense concedes this error but argues that, even under the unitary test, the evidence is legally insufficient to support a finding that employment was a substantial contributing cause of Claimant’s injury. The Prosecution contends that the evidence establishes such a contribution as a matter of law.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The governing legal standard for determining compensability in this case is the "unitary test" for proximate cause. Under this standard, a claimant seeking compensation for the aggravation of a pre-existing condition must demonstrate that the employment was a "substantial contributing cause" of the injury or aggravation. *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977). The employment need not be the sole cause of the injury, nor must the risk be unique to the workplace, provided the employment conditions contributed to the harm in a significant way. *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977). This contrasts with the "uniqueness" requirement applicable to occupational diseases originating from employment, which the Director erroneously applied.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Defense requests that this Court uphold the Director’s denial of benefits or, alternatively, remand with specific instructions to find the evidence insufficient. The Defense argues that the evidence fails to establish that employment was a substantial contributing cause because (1) vog exposure was worse at Claimant’s home than at work; (2) the physical exertion required to climb stairs was a result of the school’s geography rather than a condition of employment; and (3) the medical evidence does not isolate workplace exposure as the cause of the aggravation.

The Court finds that the Defense’s arguments raise genuine disputes of material fact that preclude this Court from holding the evidence legally insufficient as a matter of law. However, these arguments also highlight that the Director never weighed these disputes under the correct legal standard.

**A. Physical Exertion as a Condition of Employment**

The Defense argues that the physical exertion required of Claimant—specifically, climbing the stairs and traversing the mountainous campus—was not a "condition of employment" but a consequence of the school's geography. The Court disagrees with the Defense's characterization. While the terrain itself is a geographical feature, the requirement that Claimant repeatedly traverse that terrain to service computers throughout the campus was a condition of his employment as the sole technology coordinator. Claimant testified that he was required to climb stairs and hike between buildings "daily" to perform his job duties. The necessity of performing strenuous physical activity in the course of one's duties is a classic condition of employment. Whether this exertion, combined with environmental exposure, substantially contributed to his injury is a factual question for the Director to assess under the unitary test.

**B. Comparative Exposure and the Unitary Test**

The Defense correctly notes that Vice Principal Dennis testified that the vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Claimant lived, than at the school. The Defense argues this precludes a finding of substantial contribution. However, under the unitary test, the fact that a hazard exists to a greater degree outside the workplace does not automatically absolve the employer of liability. The test asks whether the employment conditions contributed to the harm in a "significant way." *Flor v. Holguin*, 58 Haw. 321, 568 P.2d 415 (1977). The record indicates that Claimant was required to perform physical labor (climbing stairs) while exposed to vog at work, whereas his exposure at home was presumably during rest. The combination of physical exertion and environmental exposure at work presents a distinct causal factor that the Director must consider, even if the ambient vog was thicker at home. The Director’s failure to analyze this distinction stems from the legal error of applying the *Flor* standard.

**C. Medical Evidence**

The Defense argues that the medical evidence is inconclusive because Dr. Mathison failed to distinguish between workplace and home exposure. While Dr. Mathison’s report does not quantify the specific dosage of vog at each location, he diagnosed Claimant with "[m]ild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and recommended that the employer avoid placing him in areas with such exposure. Dr. Tom also noted "vog contributing" to Claimant’s symptoms and recommended a transfer due to "higher vog exposure" on Maui. This evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Claimant, supports a finding of causation. Conversely, Dr. Arora’s report, upon which the Director relied, explicitly stated he lacked access to medical records and did not offer a definitive opinion on causation. The Director must weigh the conflicting medical opinions and determine whether they establish that employment was a substantial contributing cause.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s arguments regarding the severity of home exposure and the nature of the physical exertion are factual disputes that cannot be resolved by this Court as a matter of law. The record contains sufficient evidence to support a finding that employment was a substantial contributing cause of Claimant’s injury, but it also contains evidence supporting the Defense's position. Because the Director denied the claim based on the legal conclusion that the injury was not work-related due to the island-wide nature of the vog—a conclusion predicated on the inapplicable *Flor* standard—the Director failed to make the necessary findings of fact under the unitary test.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision is VACATED. The case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must apply the unitary test for proximate cause and weigh the evidence regarding the comparative exposure at home versus work, the physical demands of the job, and the medical opinions to determine whether Claimant’s employment was a substantial contributing cause of the aggravation of his asthma.
