Your Honor, the central issue before this Court is whether the Flor v. Holguin test applies to determine whether the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma was proximately caused by or resulting from the nature of his employment. The answer is unequivocally no. Flor v. Holguin is strictly limited to cases involving injuries by disease that *result from the nature of the employment*—that is, occupational diseases where the condition itself is inherently peculiar to the job. However, this case concerns the *aggravation of a pre-existing condition* proximately caused by employment-related exposure, which falls squarely under the unitary test for proximate cause, not the Flor test.  

As this Court clearly articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 93 Haw. 49, 55, 996 P.2d 250, 256 (1999), "the Flor test is confined to determining compensability for injuries by disease that arise from the nature of the employment." The Court emphasized that "[f]or diseases not classified as occupational diseases, the general test of proximate cause applies." Here, Van Ness’s asthma is a pre-existing condition—his "mild persistent asthma" was documented long before his employment at Lahainaluna High School, with a history of control through medication and immunotherapy dating back to the 1990s. The claim is not that vog *caused* his asthma; it is that employment exposure to vog *aggravated* his pre-existing condition. This distinction is critical. Under Hawaii law, aggravation claims are evaluated under the unitary test for proximate cause, which requires only that employment be a "substantial factor" in the worsening of the condition, not that the hazard be unique to the workplace.  

The Director erred by misapplying the Flor test and erroneously requiring that vog be "peculiar to the employment" to establish compensability. This is a fundamental legal mistake. As this Court held in *Smith v. State*, 97 Haw. 29, 33–34, 33 P.3d 740, 744–45 (2001), "the mere fact that a hazard exists in the general environment does not preclude compensability for aggravation of a pre-existing condition where employment is a proximate cause of the injury." In *Smith*, the claimant suffered a heart attack while performing job duties during a heatwave—a hazard common to the general public—but the Court ruled it compensable because employment was a substantial factor in the triggering event. Similarly here, vog was present island-wide on Maui, but that fact is irrelevant under the unitary test. What matters is whether Van Ness’s employment required him to be exposed to vog during severe episodes, and whether that exposure proximately caused the aggravation of his asthma.  

The record is replete with evidence that employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation. Van Ness’s symptoms worsened specifically during work hours at Lahainaluna: he testified that severe vog days caused "a lot of coughing, wheezing," "real shortness of breath," and "incredibly sharp" pain, forcing him to stop climbing stairs to catch his breath. Dr. Tom’s clinical notes from December 23, 2005, explicitly referenced "vog" as a factor in his "difficulty breathing and coughing," and later noted that symptoms "never completely went away" from the December visit, with "vog contributing" to the condition. Dr. Mathison’s consultation report confirmed that Van Ness’s "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" occurred during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure from about November 2005 and continuing into the spring of 2006," and that "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" were a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." Crucially, Dr. Mathison observed that Van Ness’s symptoms "essentially resolved" while in Pennsylvania—away from Maui’s vog—and worsened upon returning to Maui, directly linking the exacerbation to vog exposure during employment.  

The Director’s finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" misapplies the law. Under the unitary test, the existence of a hazard in the general environment does not negate proximate cause; it only requires that employment be a substantial factor in the injury. As *Flor* itself recognizes, "the focus is on whether the employment contributed to the injury, not whether the hazard is exclusive to the workplace." Here, Van Ness was required to work at Lahainaluna during severe vog episodes—Vice Principal Dennis verified "unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding [his] December 23, 2005, visit," with "atmosphere so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School." His symptoms manifested precisely during work hours, and medical experts consistently attributed the aggravation to vog exposure at his place of employment.  

In sum, the Flor test is inapplicable because this is not a claim for a disease resulting from the nature of employment; it is an aggravation claim subject to the unitary test for proximate cause. Under that test, the evidence overwhelmingly shows that employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation. The Director’s denial of the claim rests on a legal error, and this Court should reverse.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

The Defendant-Appellant State of Hawai'i, Department of Education (hereinafter "DOE" or "Defendant") respectfully submits this opening response to the plaintiff's opening argument and requests that this Court affirm the Director's decision denying workers' compensation benefits.

The plaintiff mischaracterizes the legal issue before this Court. Contrary to the plaintiff's assertions, the Director properly analyzed this claim under the applicable legal framework, and substantial evidence supports the denial of benefits. Even accepting the plaintiff's framing of the unitary test, the plaintiff cannot demonstrate that employment was a proximate cause of his injury when the alleged hazard—vog—existed island-wide and the plaintiff himself resided in an area with severe vog exposure. The Director's decision should be affirmed.

---

## I. THE DIRECTOR PROPERLY DETERMINED THAT THE PLAINTIFF'S CLAIM FAILS BECAUSE VOG WAS NOT PECULIAR TO THE WORKPLACE

The Director correctly determined that the plaintiff's claim fails because the alleged harmful exposure—vog—was not peculiar to his employment at Lahainaluna High School. The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition."

This Court in *Flor v. Holguin*, 93 Haw. 49, 55, 996 P.2d 250, 256 (1999), established the test for determining compensability of disease claims: "the focus is on whether the employment contributed to the injury, not whether the hazard is exclusive to the workplace." However, the Court also recognized that for a disease to be compensable, it must arise "from the nature of the employment" and not merely be present in the general environment.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish his claim as an "aggravation" case, arguing that the unitary test for proximate cause applies. However, this distinction does not aid the plaintiff. Even under the unitary test, the employment must be a proximate cause of the injury. Here, the evidence demonstrates that vog was present island-wide, and the plaintiff's own testimony established that he lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted "the vog was even worse" than at the school.

The record contains the following undisputed evidence regarding the widespread nature of vog on Maui:

1. Vice Principal Dennis testified that "we experienced some unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding that winter break" and that "on some days the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School."

2. Vice Principal Dennis further testified that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where the plaintiff lived.

3. The plaintiff himself testified that "a film of volcanic ash would accumulate on his car and windshield" while driving, demonstrating that vog exposure occurred duringcommuting and was not confined to the workplace.

4. The plaintiff's medical records indicate that his symptoms improved when he traveled to Pennsylvania—away from Maui entirely—and worsened upon returning to Maui, but this does not distinguish between workplace and off-workplace exposure on Maui.

Under these circumstances, the Director properly determined that there was "insufficient evidence" to establish that the plaintiff's exposure to vog at Lahainaluna School specifically—and not his general exposure to vog on the island of Maui—was the proximate cause of his injury. The plaintiff has not established the requisite causal connection between his employment and his alleged harm.

---

## II. EVEN UNDER THE PLAINTIFF'S PROPOSED UNITARY TEST, THE CLAIM FAILS BECAUSE EMPLOYMENT WAS NOT A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR

The plaintiff argues that this Court should apply the unitary test for proximate cause, which requires only that employment be a "substantial factor" in the worsening of the condition. However, even accepting this framework, the plaintiff's claim fails as a matter of law.

In *Smith v. State*, 97 Haw. 29, 33–34, 33 P.3d 740, 744–45 (2001), the plaintiff relied on a similar theory, arguing that the mere fact that a hazard exists in the general environment does not preclude compensability. However, the *Smith* Court emphasized that the unitary test still requires a showing that employment was a proximate cause of the injury. The Court in *Smith* found compensability where the claimant was "performing job duties" during a heatwave and the employment itself contributed to the heat exposure. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff has not demonstrated that his employment at Lahainaluna contributed to his vog exposure beyond the general exposure experienced by all residents of Maui.

The plaintiff testified that his symptoms worsened during severe vog episodes, but he also acknowledged that vog was present across the entire island. His residence in Kihei—where Vice Principal Dennis noted vog was "even worse"—constituted a significant source of exposure independent of his employment. The plaintiff has not established that his workplace exposure was qualitatively different from or quantitatively greater than his off-work exposure.

Furthermore, the medical evidence does not support a finding that employment was a substantial factor. Dr. Mathison's report noted that the plaintiff experienced "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog" but did not distinguish between workplace and residential exposure. Dr. Mathison noted that the plaintiff's symptoms "essentially resolved" while in Pennsylvania but this merely demonstrates the general effect of vog on the plaintiff, not a workplace-specific causation.

The plaintiff argues that his symptoms "manifested precisely during work hours," but this temporal correlation does not establish causation. The plaintiff spent seven to eight hours daily at work, but he also spent significant time at his residence in Kihei. The mere fact that symptoms may have been present during work hours does not establish that employment was a substantial factor in causing those symptoms when the alleged cause—vog—was ubiquitous on the island.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The central issue on appeal is whether the Director erred in applying the legal standard for compensability. Specifically, the Court must determine whether the Director improperly applied the "peculiar to employment" test established in *Flor v. Holguin* to an aggravation of a pre-existing condition, or whether the "unitary test" for proximate cause should have governed the analysis.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In reviewing a decision of the Director, this Court applies the clearly erroneous standard to findings of fact and *de novo* review to conclusions of law. *Riverside Publ'ns v. O'Brien*, 96 Hawai'i 425, 432, 32 P.3d 71, 78 (2001). Whether the Director applied the correct legal standard in determining compensability is a question of law subject to *de novo* review.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Erred by Applying the *Flor v. Holguin* Test to an Aggravation Claim**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to show the exposure was overwhelming at the workplace. This reasoning implies that the hazard must be unique to or substantially greater at the workplace to be compensable. This approach aligns with the test for "injuries by disease" articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 93 Haw. 49, 55, 996 P.2d 250, 256 (1999), which requires that the disease arise "from the nature of the employment."

However, the prosecution correctly argues that *Flor* is strictly limited to occupational diseases where the condition itself results from the nature of the employment. Here, Van Ness does not claim that his employment *caused* his asthma; rather, he claims that his employment *aggravated* a pre-existing condition. As stated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 93 Haw. 49, 55, 996 P.2d 250, 256 (1999), "for diseases not classified as occupational diseases, the general test of proximate cause applies."

Van Ness’s medical history confirms that his "mild persistent asthma" predated his employment at Lahainaluna, dating back to at least 1989. Therefore, this is not a claim for an occupational disease, but a claim for the aggravation of a pre-existing disease. Consequently, the Director erred by applying the "peculiar to employment" standard inherent in the *Flor* analysis.

**B. The Unitary Test for Proximate Cause Applies**

Because this is an aggravation claim, the "unitary test" for proximate cause governs. Under this test, an injury is compensable if it arises "out of and in the course of employment," meaning the employment must be a "substantial factor" in causing the injury. *Smith v. State*, 97 Haw. 29, 33–34, 33 P.3d 740, 744–45 (2001).

The defense argues that even under the unitary test, the claim fails because vog was present island-wide and Van Ness lived in Kihei, where the vog was "even worse." While the presence of a hazard in the general environment is relevant, it is not dispositive. As held in *Smith v. State*, 97 Haw. 29, 33–34, 33 P.3d 740, 744–45 (2001), "the mere fact that a hazard exists in the general environment does not preclude compensability for aggravation of a pre-existing condition where employment is a proximate cause of the injury." In *Smith*, the court found compensability for a heart attack suffered during a heatwave, despite the heat being a general environmental hazard, because the employment duties were a substantial factor in bringing about the injury.

**C. Application of the Unitary Test to the Facts**

Applying the unitary test to the record provided, there is substantial evidence that employment was a substantial factor in aggravating Van Ness’s condition.

1.  **Increased Physical Exertion at Work:** Unlike his residence in Kihei, Van Ness’s workplace at Lahainaluna required significant physical exertion. The campus is situated on a steep mountain slope, requiring Van Ness to climb "fifty to sixty stairs" at the base of the library, "another eighty stairs" to the next level, and hike down to other buildings. He testified that this exertion caused him to stop to catch his breath and took "20 minutes" to climb uphill.
2.  **Environmental Conditions at Work:** The school buildings were non-air conditioned with open windows, leading to dust accumulation and overheating computers. Van Ness testified that he was exposed to vog at work, noting that on severe days he could not see the administrative building from the library.
3.  **Medical Correlation:** Dr. Tom’s clinical notes from December 23, 2005, referenced "vog" as a factor in Van Ness’s difficulty breathing. Dr. Mathison’s report noted that Van Ness’s symptoms "essentially resolved" while in Pennsylvania but returned upon his return to Maui, coinciding with the period of severe vog and his required physical exertion at work.

While the defense correctly notes that Van Ness was exposed to vog at home, the *combination* of the environmental hazard (vog) and the physical demands of the employment (climbing steep stairs) distinguishes the workplace exposure from the general exposure. The record indicates that Van Ness’s symptoms were significantly triggered by the exertion required to traverse the campus, a condition not present to the same degree in his home life. Therefore, a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that employment was a substantial factor in the aggravation of his asthma.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director committed legal error by applying the "peculiar to employment" standard of *Flor v. Holguin* to a claim involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. The correct legal standard is the unitary test for proximate cause. Under this standard, the evidence presented suggests that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his respiratory condition because it required him to perform strenuous physical activity in an environment contaminated by vog.

Accordingly, the prosecution’s arguments are legally sound. The Director’s decision denying the claim is based on an error of law and is not supported by the correct legal framework.

**RULING**

The Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is REVERSED. The case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with the application of the unitary test for proximate cause.

The Court found compensability because the employment "contributed to the heat exposure" by requiring the claimant to work in direct sunlight during the hottest part of the day. The employment duties in *Smith* actively increased the claimant's exposure to the hazard—the claimant was required to work outdoors in conditions that exacerbated the heat risk.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue remains whether the Director erred in denying the claim, specifically regarding the application of the "substantial factor" test to the facts of this case.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In reviewing a decision of the Director, this Court applies the clearly erroneous standard to findings of fact and *de novo* review to conclusions of law. *Riverside Publ'ns v. O'Brien*, 96 Hawai'i 425, 432, 32 P.3d 71, 78 (2001). Whether the Director applied the correct legal standard in determining compensability is a question of law subject to *de novo* review.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Unitary Test Applies and the Director Erred in Requiring "Overwhelming" Exposure**

The defense concedes that the unitary test for proximate cause, rather than the "peculiar to employment" test of *Flor v. Holguin*, governs this aggravation claim. However, the defense argues that even under the unitary test, the claim fails because Van Ness cannot establish that employment was a "substantial factor" in causing his injury.

The Director denied the claim, finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to show the exposure was "overwhelming" at the workplace. This reasoning misstates the legal standard. Under *Smith v. State*, 97 Haw. 29, 33–34, 33 P.3d 740, 744–45 (2001), a claimant need not show that the workplace exposure was "overwhelming" or that the hazard was unique to the workplace. Rather, the claimant must show that employment was a "substantial factor" in causing the injury. The Director’s requirement that the exposure be "overwhelming" constitutes legal error.

**B. The Defense’s Distinction of *Smith v. State* is Unpersuasive**

The defense argues that *Smith v. State* is distinguishable because, in *Smith*, the employment "actively increased" the claimant's exposure to heat by requiring manual labor outdoors, whereas here, the employment did not increase Van Ness's exposure to vog because vog was island-wide.

This argument ignores the interaction between the environmental hazard and the physical demands of the employment. In *Smith*, the court found compensability because the employment duties were a substantial factor in bringing about the injury during a heatwave. Similarly here, while vog was present island-wide, the evidence shows that Van Ness's employment duties required him to engage in strenuous physical exertion—climbing steep stairs and hiking across a mountainous campus—while exposed to that vog.

Van Ness testified that his shortness of breath "worsened when he walked uphill to his office" and that the exertion required him to stop and catch his breath. The defense argues that the medical evidence attributes the injury solely to vog and not to exertion. However, the "injury" is the aggravation of the asthma. The exertion required by the employment was a condition that interacted with the vog exposure to trigger the asthmatic symptoms. As in *Smith*, where the heat interacted with the physical labor to cause the heart attack, here the vog interacted with the physical exertion required to traverse the Lahainaluna campus to cause the respiratory aggravation. Therefore, employment was a substantial factor.

**C. The Presence of Dust at Work Distinguishes the Workplace Exposure**

The defense relies heavily on Vice Principal Dennis’s testimony that vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, to argue that residential exposure was greater than workplace exposure. However, this argument ignores other evidence in the record regarding workplace conditions specific to Van Ness's employment.

The record establishes that Van Ness has a history of allergic reactions to "dust mite and mold spores." Van Ness testified that the computers at Lahainaluna required more maintenance than those at other schools because they were "exposed to more dust and dirt" due to the lack of air conditioning and open windows. He frequently cleaned filters that were "clogged with dust." This exposure to dust, a known allergen for Van Ness, was a condition of his employment at Lahainaluna that was not present to the same degree in his home environment. This combination of vog exposure, dust exposure, and physical exertion distinguishes the workplace hazard from the general environmental hazard.

**D. The Director’s Reliance on Dr. Arora Was Clearly Erroneous**

The defense argues that Dr. Arora’s opinion remains valid and that the Director was entitled to credit it over Dr. Mathison’s report. The record shows that Dr. Arora conducted his examination "without access to Van Ness's medical records" and explicitly stated that it would be "unwise to address the [DOE’s] referral questions" until he had access to them. A medical opinion rendered without the benefit of the claimant's medical history cannot constitute substantial evidence to support a denial of benefits. In contrast, Dr. Mathison reviewed records dating back to 1991 and provided a comprehensive diagnosis linking Van Ness's symptoms to volcanic products. The Director’s reliance on an incomplete medical opinion was clearly erroneous.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s arguments do not overcome the legal and factual errors in the Director’s decision. The Director applied an incorrect legal standard by requiring "overwhelming" exposure rather than determining whether employment was a "substantial factor" in aggravating the pre-existing condition. Under the correct standard, the evidence that Van Ness was required to perform strenuous physical exertion in an environment containing both vog and high levels of dust—substances known to trigger his asthma—supports a finding of compensability.

Accordingly, the prosecution’s position is sustained. The Director’s decision is reversed.

**RULING**

The Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is REVERSED. The case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## IV. THE PHYSICAL EXERTION REQUIRED BY EMPLOYMENT WAS NOT A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR

The Prosecution argues that the physical exertion required to traverse the Lahainaluna campus interacted with vog exposure to trigger Mr. Van Ness's asthmatic symptoms. This argument fails because the medical evidence does not support a causal connection between physical exertion and the respiratory injury.

The Prosecution cites Mr. Van Ness's testimony that his "shortness of breath worsened when he walked uphill to his office." However, this testimony describes a symptom—a worsening of shortness of breath during physical exertion—not a cause of the underlying injury. The fact that physical exertion exacerbated Mr. Van Ness's symptoms does not mean that physical exertion was a substantial factor in causing his injury.

Dr. Mathison's report does not attribute Mr. Van Ness's respiratory injury to physical exertion. Rather, Dr. Mathison attributed the injury to "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." Dr. Mathison wrote that Mr. Van Ness's "symptoms were complicated by hernia of the right diaphragm," but he did not state that the physical exertion required by the employment caused or contributed to the hernia or the respiratory condition.

The Prosecution's argument that the physical exertion "interacted" with vog exposure is speculative and unsupported by the medical evidence. The Prosecution has not presented any medical opinion establishing that the physical exertion required by Mr. Van Ness's employment was a substantial factor in causing his injury. The mere fact that symptoms worsened during physical exertion does not establish causation.

---

## V. THE MEDICAL EVIDENCE DOES NOT SUPPORT A FINDING OF COMPENSABILITY

### A. Dr. Mathison's Report Does Not Establish Causation

The Prosecution relies heavily on Dr. Mathison's report to establish that employment was a substantial factor in causing Mr. Van Ness's injury. However, Dr. Mathison's report does not support the Prosecution's position.

Dr. Mathison diagnosed Mr. Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." This diagnosis attributes the exacerbation to "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution"—not to employment. Dr. Mathison did not state that Mr. Van Ness's employment was a substantial factor in causing his injury. He noted that Mr. Van Ness experienced symptoms during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure from about November 2005 and continuing into the spring of 2006," but he did not attribute the symptoms to employment.

The Prosecution cites Dr. Mathison's statement that "it behooves his employer not to place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." This statement is advisory and does not establish that employment was a substantial factor in causing Mr. Van Ness's injury. Dr. Mathison was expressing a general concern about placing asthmatic employees in areas with volcanic exposure—not a specific opinion that Mr. Van Ness's employment at Lahainaluna caused his injury.

Furthermore, Dr. Mathison's statement that Mr. Van Ness "has been relatively free of respiratory symptoms" since moving back to Oahu does not support a finding of compensability. The fact that symptoms resolved when Mr. Van Ness left Maui is consistent with the Director's finding that the vog was a general environmental hazard affecting the entire island, not a workplace-specific hazard.

### B. Dr. Arora's Opinion Was Properly Credited by the Director

The Prosecution attacks the Director's reliance on Dr. Arora's opinion, arguing that it was "inadequate" because Dr. Arora did not have access to Mr. Van Ness's medical records at the time of the examination. However, the Prosecution's argument mischaracterizes the record.

Dr. Arora's report states that he conducted an "extended interview and examination" of Mr. Van Ness and that he reviewed the claim file. While Dr. Arora noted that he did not have access to all medical records at the time of the examination, he was able to provide an opinion based on his clinical findings and the information provided to him. The Director was entitled to credit Dr. Arora's opinion, and the Prosecution's argument that the opinion was "inadequate" goes to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility.

Moreover, the Prosecution's argument that Dr. Arora's opinion "cannot constitute substantial evidence" is incorrect. Dr. Arora was an Independent Medical Examiner appointed by the Director, and his opinion constitutes substantial evidence regardless of whether he had access to all medical records. The fact that Dr. Mathison later reviewed additional records does not invalidate Dr. Arora's opinion.

## VI. THE DIRECTOR APPLIED THE CORRECT LEGAL STANDARD

The Prosecution argues that the Director applied an incorrect legal standard by requiring "overwhelming" exposure rather than applying the "substantial factor" test. The Defense submits that this argument mischaracterizes the Director's decision.

The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to show that exposure at the workplace was "so overwhelming" that the injury arose out of employment. This reasoning is consistent with the substantial factor test. The Director did not require that vog be unique to the workplace; rather, the Director found that the Prosecution failed to establish that employment was a substantial factor in causing the injury because the vog exposure was general and not peculiar to the workplace.

The Prosecution's argument that the Director "misstated the legal standard" is without merit. The Director correctly applied the substantial factor test and found that the Prosecution failed to meet its burden of proof. The Director's decision should be affirmed.

## VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Judge's Ruling and affirm the Director's decision denying Workers' Compensation benefits.

The Prosecution has failed to establish that Mr. Van Ness's employment was a substantial factor in causing his injury. The evidence establishes that: (1) vog was present throughout the island of Maui and was not peculiar to the workplace; (2) the physical exertion required by the employment was not a substantial factor in causing the injury; and (3) the medical evidence does not support a causal connection between employment and the injury.

The Director correctly applied the substantial factor test and found that the Prosecution failed to meet its burden of proof. The Director's decision should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The sole issue on appeal is whether the Director erred in concluding that Van Ness’s employment was not a substantial factor in causing the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma condition.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews the Director’s findings of fact under the clearly erroneous standard and the Director’s conclusions of law *de novo*. *Riverside Publ'ns v. O'Brien*, 96 Hawai'i 425, 432, 32 P.3d 71, 78 (2001). Whether the Director applied the correct legal standard in determining compensability is a question of law subject to *de novo* review.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Misapplied the "Substantial Factor" Test**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to show the exposure at work was "overwhelming." The Director concluded that because the vog was a general environmental hazard, there was no nexus between the employment and the injury.

This reasoning is legally erroneous. In *Smith v. State*, 97 Haw. 29, 33–34, 33 P.3d 740, 744–45 (2001), the Hawaii Supreme Court held that the "unitary test" for compensability applies to aggravation claims. Under this test, a claimant need not demonstrate that the risk of injury was "peculiar" to the employment or that the workplace exposure was "overwhelming." Rather, the claimant must show that the employment was a "substantial factor" in bringing about the injury. *Id.*

The Director’s requirement that the exposure be "overwhelming" improperly elevates the burden of proof beyond the "substantial factor" standard established in *Smith*. The defense argues that the Director’s language was merely a finding of fact regarding the insufficiency of the evidence. However, the Director’s focus on the island-wide nature of the vog as a dispositive factor demonstrates a misunderstanding of the law. A general environmental hazard does not automatically preclude a finding of compensability if the employment contributes to the injury.

**B. Employment Was a Substantial Factor in Causing the Injury**

Under *Smith*, employment is a substantial factor if it contributes materially to the occurrence of the injury. In *Smith*, the claimant suffered a heart attack during a heatwave while performing manual labor. The court found compensability because the employment duties were a substantial factor in bringing about the injury, even though the heat was a general environmental condition. *Smith*, 97 Haw. at 34, 33 P.3d at 745.

Here, the evidence establishes that Van Ness’s employment contributed materially to his respiratory aggravation. Van Ness testified that his job required him to repeatedly climb steep stairs and hike across a mountainous campus to service computers. He stated that his shortness of breath worsened when walking uphill and that the exertion required him to stop to catch his breath. This physical exertion was a condition of his employment that interacted with the environmental hazard of vog.

The defense argues that the physical exertion merely worsened the symptoms of an injury caused solely by vog. This argument mischaracterizes the nature of the injury. The injury claimed is the aggravation of the asthma itself. The evidence shows that the combination of physical exertion and exposure to vog triggered the asthmatic exacerbation. Just as the manual labor in *Smith* interacted with the heat to cause the heart attack, the physical exertion required to traverse the Lahainaluna campus interacted with the vog to cause the respiratory aggravation. Therefore, employment was a substantial factor.

**C. The Presence of Dust Further Supports Compensability**

The defense attempts to minimize the workplace hazard by arguing that vog was worse at Van Ness’s home in Kihei. However, this argument ignores other evidence regarding workplace conditions. Van Ness has a documented history of allergies to dust mites and mold spores. He testified that the computers at Lahainaluna required more maintenance than those at other schools because they were "exposed to more dust and dirt" due to the lack of air conditioning. He frequently cleaned filters that were "clogged with dust."

This exposure to dust, a known allergen for Van Ness, was a condition of his employment at Lahainaluna. The combination of vog exposure, dust exposure, and physical exertion created a cumulative risk at the workplace that was distinct from the risk posed by vog alone at his residence. This distinction supports the finding that employment was a substantial factor in the aggravation of his condition.

**D. The Director’s Reliance on Dr. Arora Was Clearly Erroneous**

The Director credited the report of Dr. Arora over that of Dr. Mathison. The record shows that Dr. Arora conducted his examination "without access to Van Ness's medical records" and explicitly stated that it would be "unwise to address the [DOE’s] referral questions" until he had access to them. A medical opinion rendered without the benefit of the claimant's medical history cannot constitute substantial evidence to support a denial of benefits.

In contrast, Dr. Mathison reviewed records dating back to 1991 and provided a comprehensive diagnosis linking Van Ness's symptoms to volcanic products. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness experienced a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during the period of vog exposure and recommended that his employer avoid placing him in areas with volcanic exposure. The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s incomplete opinion was clearly erroneous.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director applied an incorrect legal standard by requiring "overwhelming" exposure rather than determining whether employment was a "substantial factor" in aggravating the pre-existing condition. Under the correct standard, the evidence that Van Ness was required to perform strenuous physical exertion in an environment containing both vog and high levels of dust—substances known to trigger his asthma—supports a finding of compensability.

Accordingly, the prosecution’s position is sustained. The Director’s decision is reversed.

**RULING**

The Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is REVERSED. The case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
