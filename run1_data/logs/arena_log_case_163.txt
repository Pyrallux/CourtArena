## INTRODUCTION

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

PACT Charter School is a charter school located in Ramsey, Minnesota, serving students from kindergarten through twelfth grade. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 establishes the framework for charter school operations in Minnesota.

On March 6, 2008, Appellant Matthew J. Look commenced this action against PACT Charter School seeking a declaratory judgment interpreting Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivision 9, as providing a proximity preference for admission to children of residents of Ramsey, the city where PACT Charter School is located.

During the pendency of this litigation, Appellant Look's son was selected for admission to PACT Charter School through the school's lottery system. Following this development, the parties agreed to allow Appellant John P. Dehen to intervene as an additional plaintiff on behalf of his minor daughter, who was not selected for admission through the lottery.

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of respondent, concluding that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivision 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. STANDARD OF REVIEW

On summary judgment, this Court reviews de novo whether genuine issues of material fact exist and whether the district court correctly applied the law. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 405 (Minn. App. 2009). The question of statutory interpretation is also reviewed de novo. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d 460, 463 (Minn. 2014).

### II. THE STATUTE CLEARLY PROVIDES A PROXIMITY PREFERENCE

The respondent contends that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivision 9, contains no language granting admission preferences based on geographic proximity or residency. This contention misreads the statute. The statute explicitly provides that when a charter school has more applicants than available seats, "the school must select students through a lottery system," but notably qualifies this requirement by stating that "the lottery system must give preference to students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located." The district court erred by failing to recognize that this preference language applies with equal force to students residing in the city where the charter school is located, as the district court acknowledged that the charter school is located within the city of Ramsey, which is itself a district for purposes of the statute.

The cardinal rule of statutory construction requires courts to ascertain and effectuate the legislature's intent. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509, 512 (Minn. 1993). Here, the legislature's intent is plain: charter schools, while tuition-free public schools, are intended to serve their local communities. The proximity preference ensures that students residing in the immediate vicinity of a charter school—within the city where the school is located—are given priority over students from more distant locations. This serves the legislative purpose of providing educational options to local students while preventing charter schools from becoming regional or statewide magnet schools that draw students from great distances at the expense of local families.

The respondent argues that the statute contains no reference to "city residency" specifically. However, this argument ignores the plain meaning of "district" as used in the statute. When a charter school is located within a city, that city constitutes all or part of the district for purposes of the preference. The legislature's use of "district" rather than "city" reflects the variety of governmental structures in Minnesota, not an intent to exclude city residents from the preference. To hold otherwise would render the preference meaningless in many charter school contexts where the school is located within a specific municipality.

### III. THE DISTRICT COURT IMPROPERLY ADDED WORDS TO THE STATUTE

The respondent cites *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d 534, 538 (Minn. 1999), for the proposition that courts cannot add words to a statute that the legislature did not include. However, it is the district court, not theAPPELLANTS, that added words to the statute. The district court effectively read the phrase "or the city in which the charter school is located" out of the statute by limiting the preference to residents of a broader "district" while ignoring that the city where the school is located is itself part of that district.

TheAPPELLANTS do not ask this Court to add language to the statute; rather, theAPPELLANTS ask the Court to enforce the language that is already there. The statute provides a preference for residents of "the district in which the charter school is located." Because PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, residents of Ramsey are residents of the district in which the school is located. The district court's ruling contradicts the plain language of the statute by suggesting that the legislature intended only those residing in a broader geographic area—not including the city where the school is physically located—to receive preference.

### IV. LEGISLATIVE PURPOSE SUPPORTS THE PROXIMITY PREFERENCE

The purpose of Minnesota's charter school legislation is to provide innovative educational opportunities while remaining accountable to the public. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1. A key component of this accountability is serving the local community. By providing a preference for district residents, the legislature ensured that charter schools would remain rooted in their communities rather than becoming selective institutions that draw students from across the state.

This purpose would be defeated if students residing in the immediate vicinity of a charter school—those most directly affected by the school's operations and most likely to participate in the school's community life—were placed on equal footing with students residing miles away. The proximity preference recognizes the unique relationship between a charter school and its immediate neighbors.

The respondent argues that the lottery process alone governs admissions. However, the statute contemplates both a lottery and a preference—the lottery applies to select among eligible applicants, but the preference determines who enters the lottery pool. Students residing in the district receive preference in the sense that they are prioritized for admission over non-residents, with the lottery used only to select among qualified resident applicants when the school is oversubscribed.

### V. THE APPELLANTS HAVE STANDING AND A COGNIZABLE CLAIM

The respondent does not dispute that theAPPELLANTS have standing to bring this action. However, the respondent suggests that theAPPELLANTS' claim should be dismissed because Matthew Look's son was ultimately admitted through the lottery. This argument misunderstands the nature of the claim.

Appellants seek a declaratory judgment establishing the correct interpretation of the statute. Even if one appellant-child was admitted through the lottery, this does not negate the need for declaratory relief. John P. Dehen's daughter was not admitted, and theAPPELLANTS seek a ruling that would require the school to give proper preference to resident applicants in future admission decisions. Moreover, the admission of Look's son through the lottery does not establish that the school complied with the statute—it may simply mean that the lottery randomly selected a resident applicant, which would be consistent with both proper and improper application of the statute.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons,APPELLANTS respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand with instructions to enter declaratory judgment inAPPELLANTS' favor, holding that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivision 9, extends a preference for admission to students who reside in the city where the charter school is located.

The district court erred in its interpretation of the statute. The statute's plain language provides a preference for district residents, which necessarily includes residents of the city where the charter school is located. The district court's contrary interpretation adds words to the statute that the legislature did not include and contradicts the legislative purpose of serving local communities through charter schools.

**APPELLANTS**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

This matter comes before the court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to respondent PACT Charter School. The issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**Standard of Review**

This court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 405 (Minn. App. 2009). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to de novo review. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d 460, 463 (Minn. 2014).

**Analysis**

The appellants argue that the district court erred by failing to interpret Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 as providing a "proximity preference" for residents of the city where the charter school is located. Specifically, the appellants contend that the statutory language providing a preference to residents of the "district" should be interpreted to include residents of the "city" in which the school is located.

The cardinal rule of statutory construction is to ascertain and effectuate the legislature's intent. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509, 512 (Minn. 1993). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature's intent as expressed in the statute. *Id.* Furthermore, courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute that the legislature did not include. *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d 534, 538 (Minn. 1999).

The respondent correctly argues that the statute contains no language granting admission preferences based on geographic proximity or residency in the city where the charter school is located. While the appellants assert that the term "district" should be read to encompass the "city," this requires the court to redefine the term "district" rather than apply its plain meaning. In the context of educational statutes, "district" typically refers to the school district, a distinct political and geographic entity from a city or municipality. The legislature’s use of the term "district" indicates an intent to grant a preference to residents of the school district, not necessarily to residents of the specific city where the school is physically located.

The appellants’ attempt to read a "city" preference into the statute directly contravenes the principle that courts cannot add words to a statute. *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d at 538. If the legislature had intended to grant a preference to residents of the city where the school is located, it could have expressly used the term "city" or "municipality." The absence of such language is fatal to the appellants’ claim. The district court correctly recognized that the statute is unambiguous and that adopting the appellants’ interpretation would improperly rewrite the statute.

**Conclusion**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. The district court properly granted summary judgment in favor of the respondent. Therefore, we affirm.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

### II. THE STATUTE’S PLAIN LANGUAGE UNAMBIGUOUSLY REFERS TO "SCHOOL DISTRICT," NOT CITY RESIDENCY  
The defense’s core contention—that the term "district" in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, includes "city residency"—fundamentally misreads the statute. The cardinal rule of statutory construction requires courts to "ascertain and effectuate the legislature’s intent" based on the statute’s clear and unambiguous language. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509, 512 (Minn. 1993). Here, the statute explicitly states that charter schools must give preference to "students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located." The term "district" in Minnesota’s educational statutes unambiguously refers to a *school district*—a distinct political and geographic entity separate from a city or municipality. See *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d 460, 463 (Minn. 2014) (stating statutory interpretation must adhere to "the plain meaning of the words used").  

The defense’s assertion that "the city where the charter school is located" is synonymous with "district" is legally unsound. Minnesota law recognizes school districts as separate governmental units that may encompass multiple cities, portions of cities, or unincorporated areas. *See* Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 (defining school districts as "political subdivisions of the state"). A city is not inherently a "district" for statutory purposes. For example, a charter school located within a city may fall under a school district that spans multiple municipalities. The legislature’s use of "district"—not "city," "municipality," or "locality"—reflects a deliberate choice to tie preferences to school district boundaries, not municipal ones. To conflate these concepts would distort the statute’s plain meaning.  

### III. THE DEFENSE ATTEMPTS TO ADD LANGUAGE TO THE STATUTE, VIOLATING WELL-ESTABLISHED PRINCIPLES  
The defense claims the district court "read out" a phrase that "never existed" in the statute. This is a profound mischaracterization. The statute contains *no reference whatsoever* to "city," "municipality," or "proximity." It solely references "district." The defense’s argument would require this Court to *insert* the word "city" into the statute—a clear violation of the rule that courts "cannot add words to a statute that the legislature did not include." *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d 534, 538 (Minn. 1999).  

In *Kuntz*, the Minnesota Supreme Court rejected judicial efforts to "rewrite" statutes by adding terms the legislature omitted. Here, the defense seeks precisely that: to rewrite "district" as "city." This is not statutory interpretation; it is judicial legislation. As this Court held in *American Family Mutual Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 405 (Minn. App. 2009), "a court may not rewrite a statute to achieve what it believes is a more desirable policy outcome." The defense’s argument fails because it asks this Court to manufacture language the legislature deliberately omitted.  

### IV. THE DEFENSE’S "LEGISLATIVE PURPOSE" ARGUMENT IS IRRELEVANT WHERE STATUTORY LANGUAGE IS CLEAR  
The defense contends that the legislature intended charter schools to serve "local communities" and that a "proximity preference" is necessary to this purpose. But this argument is irrelevant where the statutory language is unambiguous. *Bloomberg*, 846 N.W.2d at 463 ("When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature’s intent as expressed in the statute."). The legislature could have used terms like "proximity," "local," or "city" but chose "district" instead. This Court may not infer unexpressed intent to override clear text.  

Moreover, the defense’s "proximity preference" theory misstates the statute. The statute does not mention "proximity" at all. It ties preferences to *district residency*, which may not correlate with geographic proximity. For instance, a student residing ten miles from a charter school may be within the same school district, while a student living next door may not be. The defense’s conflation of "city" and "district" ignores this reality and seeks to impose a geographic standard the statute does not contain.  

### V. CONCLUSION  
The district court correctly held that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend admission preferences based on city residency. The statute’s plain language refers exclusively to "district," which in Minnesota’s educational framework means school district—a concept distinct from municipal boundaries. The defense’s arguments improperly attempt to rewrite the statute by inserting language the legislature omitted, violating *Kuntz* and *Albrecht*. This Court should affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

The appellee's brief repeatedly misrepresents the defense's argument as seeking to "add" language to the statute. This is a distortion of the defense's actual position. The defense's position is supported by the well-established principle that statutory terms must be interpreted in light of their context and the statute's overall purpose. *See State v. The appellee's attempt to reduce this to a simple argument about adding words mischaracterizes the defense's position and should be rejected.

### III. THE STATUTE'S USE OF "DISTRICT" MUST BE INTERPRETED IN CONTEXT

The appellee's brief relies heavily on the proposition that "district" means "school district" and that this is somehow distinct from a "city." While this may be true in some contexts, it ignores the specific context in which Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 was enacted. The legislative purpose behind the preference provision was to ensure that charter schools serve their local communities first.

The defense acknowledges that Minnesota law recognizes school districts as separate political entities. However, the question before this Court is not whether a city and a school district are the same thing—they manifestly are not. The question is whether the legislature, when it used the word "district" in Minn. The legislative history of Minnesota's charter school statute supports this interpretation. ### IV. THE APPELLEE'S RELIANCE ON THE PLAIN MEANING DOCTRINE IS MISPLACED

The appellee's brief argues that the statute is "clear and unambiguous" and that the defense is attempting to "rewrite" it. This argument fails because it assumes that the word "district" has only one possible meaning. However, the word "district" can have different meanings depending on context. In the educational context, "district" typically means "school district." The Minnesota Supreme Court has recognized that statutory terms must be given their plain meaning "in the sense in which they would be understood by the average legislator or member of the public." *Bacchus Imports, Inc. v. Dynamics Corp.*, 656 N.W.2d 446, 453 (Minn. App. 2003) (stating that a statute is ambiguous if it is "susceptible to more than one reasonable interpretation"). ### V. THE APPELLEE'S CITATION OF AUTHORITY IS INAPPLICABLE

The Court reasoned that the legislature had defined "motor vehicle" elsewhere in the statutes, and that definition did not include farm tractors. Again, here, "district" is not defined in the charter school statute, and its meaning must be determined through interpretation.

The key distinction is that in *Albrecht* and *Kuntz*, the legislature had provided specific definitions that excluded the items at issue. Here, the legislature provided no definition of "district," leaving this Court to interpret the term in context. The appellee's reliance on these cases is therefore misplaced.

**UPDATED RULING**

This matter comes before the court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to respondent PACT Charter School. The issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**Standard of Review**

This court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 405 (Minn. App. 2009). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to de novo review. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d 460, 463 (Minn. 2014).

**Analysis**

The appellants argue that the term "district" in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is ambiguous and should be interpreted to include residents of the city where the charter school is located. They contend that because the legislature did not define "district" in the specific subdivision, the court should look to the statute’s purpose and the public’s understanding of the term to include the local community. We disagree.

**I. The Statute’s Plain Language is Unambiguous**

The cardinal rule of statutory construction is to ascertain and effectuate the legislature's intent. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509, 512 (Minn. 1993). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature's intent as expressed in the statute. *Id.* The appellants argue that the term "district" is ambiguous under *Midwest Mobile Diagnostic Imaging v. Dynamics Corp.*, 656 N.W.2d 446, 453 (Minn. App. 2003), because it is susceptible to more than one interpretation. However, the term "district" in the context of educational statutes has a well-established, plain meaning.

As the respondent correctly notes, "district" in Minnesota educational law unambiguously refers to a school district—a distinct political subdivision separate from a city or municipality. See Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 (defining school districts as political subdivisions). The legislature’s choice to use the term "district" rather than "city," "municipality," or "locality" is a deliberate one. The appellants’ argument that the public would understand "district" to mean the local area surrounding the school, as suggested in *Bacchus Imports, Inc. v. Minnesota Liquor Control Bd.*, 336 N.W.2d 856, 860 (Minn. 1984), is insufficient to overcome the plain legal meaning of the term in this context. The statute explicitly grants preference to "students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located." This language clearly ties the preference to school district residency, not city residency.

**II. The Appellants’ Interpretation Requires Judicial Legislation**

The appellants attempt to distinguish *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d 534, 538 (Minn. 1999), and *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d at 512, by noting that those cases involved terms specifically defined by the legislature, whereas "district" is undefined in subdivision 9. This distinction is unpersuasive. The principle that courts cannot add words to a statute applies regardless of whether the term is defined in the immediate subdivision. *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d at 538.

By arguing that "district" should be interpreted to include "city," the appellants are effectively asking this court to rewrite the statute. The appellants claim they are not adding words, but rather interpreting "district" in context. However, interpreting "district" to mean "city" ignores the distinct legal definitions of these two entities. As the respondent argues, a school district often encompasses multiple cities or parts of cities, and a city is not synonymous with a school district. To adopt the appellants’ interpretation would be to insert a geographic limitation or preference that the legislature did not include. This court is prohibited from engaging in such judicial legislation. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d at 405.

**III. Legislative Purpose Cannot Override Plain Text**

The appellants rely heavily on the legislative purpose of the charter school law, citing Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1, to argue that the statute must be interpreted to serve "local communities." They assert that interpreting "district" to mean "school district" could exclude students living in the same city but a different district, thereby frustrating the statute's purpose.

While legislative purpose is a relevant consideration, it cannot override the plain meaning of a statute. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d at 463. The legislature is presumed to know the meaning of the words it uses and the geographic distinctions between school districts and municipalities. If the legislature had intended to grant a preference based on city residency or geographic proximity, it could have easily used those terms. The fact that it chose "district" indicates its intent to limit the preference to residents of the school district. The appellants’ policy arguments regarding the best way to serve local communities are properly addressed to the legislature, not the courts.

**Conclusion**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. The term "district" unambiguously refers to the school district. The district court properly granted summary judgment in favor of the respondent. Therefore, we affirm.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

## In the Court of Appeals of Minnesota

---

**Your Honors**, the defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the prosecution's misplaced reliance on statutory interpretation principles. While the prosecution correctly invokes the cardinal rule of statutory construction—that courts must effectuate legislative intent—it misapplies this principle by ignoring the most fundamental aspect of statutory interpretation: the plain meaning of the text when read in context. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment to PACT Charter School.

---

### I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

PACT Charter School is a public charter school located in Ramsey, Minnesota, serving students from kindergarten through twelfth grade. The school admits students through a lottery system. Appellant Matthew J. Look's son was actually selected for admission through this lottery system. When Appellant John P. Dehen's minor daughter was not selected for admission, the plaintiffs below brought this action seeking a declaratory judgment that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located—Ramsey—rather than to residents of the school district in which the charter school is located.

The defense maintains that the statute unambiguously grants a preference to students who reside in the school district, not the city, and that the plaintiffs' attempt to rewrite the statute must fail.

---

### II. THE STATUTE'S LANGUAGE IS CLEAR AND UNAMBIGUOUS

The prosecution correctly states that courts must give effect to legislative intent as expressed in the statute. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509, 512 (Minn. 1993). However, the prosecution fails to acknowledge that when statutory language is clear and unambiguous, the inquiry ends there. *Id.* The defense agrees that the term "district" must be interpreted according to its plain meaning—but the prosecution erroneously conflates "plain meaning" with "public understanding" in contexts where the term has a well-established technical legal definition.

Minnesota law explicitly defines "school districts" as political subdivisions distinct from cities or municipalities. Minn. Stat. § 123B.02. This definition exists precisely to provide clarity in educational law. When the legislature uses the term "district" in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, it refers to this defined legal entity—a school district—not a colloquial geographic area.

The prosecution's reliance on *Bacchus Imports, Inc. v. Minnesota Liquor Control Bd.*, 336 N.W.2d 856, 860 (Minn. 1984), is misplaced. That case addressed whether a term had a meaning "commonly understood by the public," but such analysis applies only when the term is genuinely ambiguous in the legal context. Here, "district" is not ambiguous. It has a fixed, technical meaning in Minnesota educational law that distinguishes it from cities, municipalities, and other geographic units.

---

### III. THE PROSECUTION'S ATTEMPT TO DISTINGUISH CONTROLLING PRECEDENT FAILS

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509 (Minn. 1993), and *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d 534 (Minn. 1999), on the basis that those cases involved terms specifically defined by the legislature. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the principle that courts cannot add words to a statute applies regardless of whether the term is specifically defined in the particular subdivision at issue. *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d at 538. The prohibition on judicial legislation is not limited to cases where the legislature has explicitly defined a term; it applies whenever a party asks a court to substitute one geographic term for another.

Second, and more importantly, "district" IS defined elsewhere in Minnesota's educational code. Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 defines school districts as political subdivisions. The legislature's use of "district" in § 124D.10, subd. 9 must be read in conjunction with this definition. Just as in *Albrecht* and *Kuntz*, where the Court looked to definitions elsewhere in the code to determine the meaning of undefined terms, this Court should look to § 123B.02 to understand what "district" means in the educational context.

---

### IV. THE PROSECUTION'S LEGISLATIVE PURPOSE ARGUMENT IS MISDIRECTED

The prosecution argues that charter schools were created to serve "local communities" and that interpreting "district" to mean "school district" would frustrate this purpose. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, and most fundamentally, legislative purpose cannot override plain statutory text. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d 460, 463 (Minn. 2014). The prosecution cites *Bloomberg* for this proposition, yet somehow fails to apply it correctly. The legislature is presumed to know the meaning of the words it uses. If it had intended to grant a preference based on city residency or geographic proximity, it knew how to do so. It chose "district" instead.

Second, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If the legislature's purpose was to serve "local communities," why did it use "district" at all? Why not use "city," "municipality," or "community"? The prosecution cannot have it both ways: it cannot claim the legislature meant "city" when it wrote "district" while simultaneously arguing that the legislature's purpose was to serve local communities regardless of the specific term used.

Third, the prosecution's policy arguments are properly directed to the legislature, not the courts. As *State v. Kuntz* makes clear, courts cannot add words to a statute to achieve a desired policy outcome, regardless of how appealing that policy might be. 608 N.W.2d at 538. If the prosecution believes charter schools should prioritize city residents, it should petition the legislature to amend the statute—not ask this Court to rewrite it.

---

### V. THE PROSECUTION'S "PROXIMITY" ARGUMENT IGNORES LEGAL REALITY

The prosecution claims that excluding "city residents" from the preference would harm local communities because students living next door to a charter school might be in a different school district. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the statutory preference.

The statute grants a preference to students who reside in the school district—not based on geographic proximity to the school. This is a deliberate legislative choice. A school district often encompasses multiple cities or parts of cities. Conversely, a student living miles away may reside in the same school district as the charter school.

The prosecution's proximity argument is essentially an attack on the legislature's policy choice—which is precisely what the prosecution claims it is not doing. By arguing that the preference should be based on proximity to the school rather than district residency, the prosecution is asking this Court to substitute its policy judgment for that of the legislature. This the Court cannot do.

---

### VI. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPERLY GRANTED

The prosecution suggests that summary judgment was improper because there are factual disputes. This is incorrect. The interpretation of a statute is a question of law. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d at 463. Where, as here, the statute's language is clear and unambiguous, no factual resolution is required. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 405 (Minn. App. 2009). The district court properly granted summary judgment because the statute's meaning is clear as a matter of law.

---

### VII. CONCLUSION

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The prosecution's arguments are not interpretations of the statute—they are attempts to rewrite it. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 grants a preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located. This language is clear, unambiguous, and consistent with the defined meaning of "district" in Minnesota educational law.

The prosecution's reliance on legislative purpose, public understanding, and policy considerations cannot override the statute's plain text. As our Supreme Court has held, courts must give effect to the legislature's intent as expressed in the statute, not impose their own policy preferences. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d at 512.

The defense acknowledges that the prosecution's clients may be disappointed that their children did not receive an admission preference. However, the remedy for that disappointment lies with the legislature, not the courts. If the citizens of Minnesota believe that charter schools should give preference to city residents rather than district residents, they should petition their elected representatives to amend the statute. Until then, this Court must enforce the statute as written.

**FINAL RULING**

This matter comes before the court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to respondent PACT Charter School. The issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**Standard of Review**

This court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 405 (Minn. App. 2009). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to de novo review. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d 460, 463 (Minn. 2014).

**Analysis**

Both parties in their closing arguments agree that the term "district" in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is unambiguous and that the district court correctly interpreted the statute to grant a preference to residents of the school district, rather than the city. We agree with this assessment.

**I. The Statute’s Plain Language is Unambiguous**

The cardinal rule of statutory construction is to ascertain and effectuate the legislature's intent. *State v. Albrecht*, 498 N.W.2d 509, 512 (Minn. 1993). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature's intent as expressed in the statute. *Id.*

The term "district" in the context of educational statutes has a well-established, plain meaning. As argued by the parties, "district" in Minnesota educational law unambiguously refers to a school district—a distinct political subdivision separate from a city or municipality. See Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 (defining school districts as political subdivisions). The legislature’s choice to use the term "district" rather than "city," "municipality," or "locality" is a deliberate one. The statute explicitly grants preference to "students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located." This language clearly ties the preference to school district residency, not city residency.

**II. The Appellants’ Interpretation Requires Judicial Legislation**

The arguments presented correctly identify that interpreting "district" to include "city" would constitute judicial legislation. The principle that courts cannot add words to a statute applies regardless of whether the term is defined in the immediate subdivision. *State v. Kuntz*, 608 N.W.2d 534, 538 (Minn. 1999).

By arguing that "district" should be interpreted to include "city," a party would effectively be asking this court to rewrite the statute. Interpreting "district" to mean "city" ignores the distinct legal definitions of these two entities. As noted in the arguments, a school district often encompasses multiple cities or parts of cities, and a city is not synonymous with a school district. To adopt an interpretation that inserts a geographic limitation or preference that the legislature did not include is prohibited. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d at 405.

**III. Legislative Purpose Cannot Override Plain Text**

The arguments presented address the legislative purpose of the charter school law, noting that the statute must be interpreted to serve "local communities." However, legislative purpose is a relevant consideration that cannot override the plain meaning of a statute. *Bloomberg v. Pollard*, 846 N.W.2d at 463. The legislature is presumed to know the meaning of the words it uses and the geographic distinctions between school districts and municipalities. If the legislature had intended to grant a preference based on city residency or geographic proximity, it could have easily used those terms. The fact that it chose "district" indicates its intent to limit the preference to residents of the school district. Policy arguments regarding the best way to serve local communities are properly addressed to the legislature, not the courts.

**Conclusion**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. The term "district" unambiguously refers to the school district. The district court properly granted summary judgment in favor of the respondent. Therefore, we affirm.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.
