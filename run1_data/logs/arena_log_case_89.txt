COURT ARENA LOG - Case 89
FACTS:
When S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC acquired a nursing home previously operated by a different company, it decided to meet its immediate staffing needs in part by offering temporary employment to employees of the prior operator. S & F informed the employees it would consider hiring those who met S & F’s “operational needs” and it would hire them on an at-will basis, subject to a probationary period, to various drug and background checks, and to their signing an arbitration agreement. When it began operations, S & F implemented these and other new terms and conditions of employment.

The National Labor Relations Board later held that, because S & F failed to notify the employees that the terms and conditions of their employment would change, S & F was a “perfectly clear” successor within the meaning of NLRB v. Burns International Security Services, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972). As such, S & F was bound by the collective bargaining agreement (CBA) between its predecessor and the Service Employees International Union and had violated § 8(a)(1) and (5) of the National Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 158(a)(1) and (5), by unilaterally changing the terms and conditions of employment prescribed in that CBA. Consequently, the Board ordered S & F to restore the preexisting terms of employment and to make the employees whole with back pay. S & F petitions for review, and the Board cross-appeals for enforcement, of that order.

We hold the Board erred in concluding S & F was a “perfectly clear” successor and was not entitled to implement new terms and conditions of employment. We therefore grant in part the petition for review and deny in part enforcement of the *446Board’s order. We enforce the Board’s order as it relates to matters S & F does not contest.

I. Background

S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC (d/b/a Windsor Convalescent Center of North Long Beach) is affiliated with a chain of nursing and assisted living facilities managed by SnF Management and operated under the Windsor name. In 2004 S & F purchased Candlewood Care Center from Covenant Care Orange Inc. Covenant Care had collective bargaining agreements with the SEIU, Local 434B, covering two different bargaining units at Candlewood.

Prior to assuming control of the Candle-wood facility, S & F concluded it would need to increase the level of care, replace the staff, and renovate the building. Closer to taking over on July 1, 2004 S & F decided it could not replace the entire staff at once because the turnover would be too disruptive to the residents. Instead, S & F decided to hire some of Candlewood’s employees for up to 90 days while it continued to recruit new employees.

In June 2004 S & F caused applications for employment to be distributed to the existing staff. A cover sheet informed the employees that S & F “intends to implement significant operational changes” and “[c]urrent Candlewood employees interested in positions with [S & F] must submit the attached application for employment.” Further, it advised, only “[a]pplieants who meet the [Company’s] operational needs will be interviewed,” and any offer of employment “will be contingent on your passing a pre-employment physical, drug test and acceptable reference and background checks.” The job application itself required the applicant to affirm his or her understanding that successfully passing the tests and checks was a condition of employment, that any employment would be at will, and that S & F “can change benefits, policies and conditions at any time.”

At the end of June, S & F interviewed all the Candlewood employees who had submitted applications. In each interview, the applicant was informed that any possible employment would be temporary and would last no more than 90 days.

S & F’s director of human resources sent to each employee who was selected a letter dated June 30 with the subject line “OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT.” The letter explained, “As a temporary employee of Windsor, you are not eligible for company benefits.... Other terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor’s personnel policies and its employee handbook.” The letter further stated the employment was temporary because S & F had not yet been able to assess the employee’s “skills and abilities” or “the building’s ongoing operational and staffing needs.” It also noted that employment was at will and that “[n]o later than the expiration of the 90-day period, which ends on September 29th, your employment with Windsor will end, unless you are selected for regular employment.” In order to accept the offer the addressee was required to sign and date the letter. Those hired also had to sign an “Agreement to be Bound by Alternative Dispute Resolution Policy,” which provided that arbitration would be the exclusive means of resolving all disputes relating to termination of employment, unlawful discrimination, and sexual harassment.

S & F began operations on July 1 with approximately 120 employees. Most were former Candlewood employees newly employed on a temporary basis; 10 to 12 had not been employed by Candlewood. Seventeen Candlewood employees were not *447employed either because they had not applied or because they were not chosen.

At a staff meeting on July 9, S & F distributed to the employees handbooks describing Windsor’s policies and its terms and conditions of employment. The temporary employees received a handbook dated July 1, 2004 that did not include information about employer-provided benefits. The “regular” employees (i.e., those who had not worked for Candlewood) received a handbook dated January 1, 2004, reflecting the most recent revision of Windsor’s system-wide policies; this version did include information about employer-provided benefits.

S & F continued over the next three months to replace former Candlewood employees with new employees. By October 1 a majority of the company’s employees had been hired from outside the Candle-wood staff; some 30-40 of the Candlewood employees hired initially as temporary employees had been hired for regular employment and given the January 1 version of the handbook.

Also following the takeover, S & F invested about $500,000 to renovate the facility. Among other changes, S & F repainted the employee lounge and the hallway where Candlewood had hung a bulletin board for the use of the Union, at which time S & F removed the bulletin board.

Meanwhile, on July 1 the Union requested bargaining with S & F. The Company responded on July 7 that it had not yet hired a representative complement of employees, so the Union’s demand for bargaining was premature. The Union filed unfair labor practice charges, and the Board General Counsel issued a complaint alleging that when S & F began operating the facility oh July 1, 2004, it became “a successor to Candlewood” but refused to bargain with the Union, in violation of § 8(a)(1) and (5) of the Act. The complaint further alleged that since July 1, 2004 S & F had made unilateral changes at the former Candlewood facility — removing union-related materials from a bulletin board, prohibiting the posting of union materials, and implementing new employment policies — again in violation of § 8(a)(1) and (5), and had violated § 8(a)(3) in several ways unrelated to the issue of successorship.

Following a hearing, an Administrative Law Judge found S & F was a successor to Candlewood. According to the ALJ, the “temporary” employees were actually akin to probationary employees because their work was to be evaluated during the period of temporary employment and they did not have a definite anticipated termination date. Therefore the temporary employees could be counted in determining whether S & F had a substantial and representative complement of employees, and whether a majority of that complement were from Candlewood, when S & F began operations on July 1. Having answered both questions in the affirmative, the ALJ concluded S & F was obligated to recognize and to bargain with the Union as of July 1.

At the same time, the ALJ found S & F, although a successor to Candlewood, was not a “perfectly clear” successor because S & F’s pre-employment communications to the Candlewood employees put them on notice that the terms and conditions of their employment would change. Accordingly, although S & F was obliged to recognize and to bargain with the Union, it was entitled first to establish the initial terms and conditions of employment.

The Board affirmed the ALJ’s finding that S & F was a successor to Candlewood but went on, with Member Schaumber dissenting, to hold (1) as a matter of law, S & F was a “perfectly clear” successor and, (2) as a matter of fact, had “failed to clearly announce its intent to establish a *448new set of conditions prior to inviting former [Candlewood] employees to accept employment.” Windsor Convalescent Ctr. of N. Long Beach, 351 N.L.R.B. 975, 980 (2007). The Board concluded, therefore, that S & F did not have the right unilaterally to set the initial terms and conditions of employment. S & F petitions for review solely of the legal and factual underpinnings of this determination.

The Board also held, and S & F does not contest, that S & F refused to hire four union stewards, suspended or terminated certain employees for engaging in protected activity, and informed employees the facility would be nonunion, all in violation of § 8(a)(1) and (3) of the Act; and, although a successor employer, S & F failed to recognize and to bargain with the Union, in violation of § 8(a)(5) of the Act. In keeping with this court’s “longstanding rule,” Carpenters & Millwrights, Local Union 2471 v. NLRB, 481 F.3d 804, 808 (2007), “[t]he Board is entitled to summary enforcement of the uncontested portions of its order.” Flying Food Group Inc. v. NLRB, 471 F.3d 178, 181 (2006).

Let us be clear about the standard. When a new employer acquires a business and hires a substantial majority of the predecessor’s workforce, it becomes a successor under the National Labor Relations Act. But the law does not require that successor to adopt the predecessor’s terms. Instead, as this Court has long recognized, the successor may unilaterally establish new conditions if it makes known to the predecessor’s employees, *before* they accept employment, that the new terms will differ. That is the core of the "perfectly clear" successor doctrine: if the new employer’s communications are transparent about changes, it avoids the obligation to bargain over initial terms. The Board’s error here was not merely technical—it was foundational. It ignored the plain language of S & F’s communications and substituted its own interpretation for the clear evidence before it.  

Consider the facts. Before a single employee was hired, S & F distributed job applications with a cover sheet stating: "S & F intends to implement significant operational changes," and that only applicants meeting its "operational needs" would be interviewed. It further specified that employment would be contingent on passing physicals, drug tests, and background checks—and that *any* employment would be "at will" with benefits, policies, and conditions subject to change at any time. Every applicant had to affirm this understanding before proceeding. Then, for those selected, S & F sent explicit offer letters labeled "OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT." Those letters stated, without qualification: "As a temporary employee of Windsor, you are not eligible for company benefits." They emphasized that employment would "end" after 90 days unless the employee was "selected for regular employment." And they made clear that terms would be governed by Windsor’s policies and handbook—not the predecessor’s collective bargaining agreement.  

This was not a subtle hint or a vague disclaimer. It was a direct, unambiguous notice to every former employee: *Your job with us will not be the same as it was before.* The Board’s claim that S & F failed to "clearly announce" its intent to establish new conditions is indefensible. The evidence shows the opposite: S & F’s communications were designed to ensure employees knew the rules were changing. Temporary status was not a mere placeholder—it was the *only* status offered initially, with no benefits, no guarantee of continuation, and a hard deadline for termination. When S & F distributed handbooks on its first day of operation, the differences were stark: temporary employees received a handbook explicitly excluding benefits, while "regular" employees (new hires) received one that included them. The Board’s attempt to treat this as ambiguous is a distortion of reality.  

The Board also mischaracterized the temporary employment arrangement as probationary—a characterization unsupported by the record. S & F never told employees they were being hired on probation; it told them they were hired *temporarily*, with no benefits and a fixed end date. The distinction matters. Probationary status might imply a trial period under existing terms, but S & F’s communications made clear the terms themselves were new. The Board’s factual finding that S & F “failed to clearly announce” its intent ignores the actual words it used. When an employer states plainly that benefits will not be provided, that employment will end in 90 days, and that policies will change, it does not need to say more. The law does not require theatrical declarations—only clear, truthful notice.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit

---

**S & F MARKET STREET HEALTHCARE LLC, Petitioner v. NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD, Respondent**

---

## INTRODUCTION

The National Labor Relations Board ("the Board") properly applied the "perfectly clear" successor doctrine in this case. S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC's petition for review should be denied, and the Board's order should be enforced in full. The record establishes that S & F acquired the Candlewood facility, hired the predecessor's employees en masse, and failed to provide the clear and unambiguous notice required under established precedent to avoid binding itself to the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. S & F's arguments to the contrary distort the applicable legal standard and ignore the factual record.

---

## I. LEGAL STANDARD: THE "PERFECTLY CLEAR" SUCCESSOR DOCTRINE

The National Labor Relations Act imposes successor employer obligations when a new employer acquires a business and hires a substantial majority of the predecessor's workforce. *NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972). However, a successor employer may avoid the obligation to bargain over initial terms if it is not a "perfectly clear" successor—that is, if it provides clear and unambiguous notice to predecessor employees that it intends to establish new terms and conditions of employment before they accept employment. *See Burns*, 406 U.S. at 294-95; *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 772 F.2d 45, 51-52 (3d Cir. 1985), *enforced*, 482 U.S. 27, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987).

The burden on a successor employer seeking to avoid the "perfectly clear" successor label is substantial. The notice must be "perfectly clear" that the successor intends to establish a new set of conditions before inviting former employees to accept employment. *Burns*, 406 U.S. at 295. Vague references to possible changes or general disclaimers about at-will employment are insufficient. The successor must clearly announce its intent to establish new terms prior to the employees' acceptance of employment.

---

## II. APPLICATION OF THE DOCTRINE TO S & F

### A. S & F Was a Successor Employer

The parties do not seriously dispute that S & F was a successor employer. S & F acquired the Candlewood facility, assumed operations on July 1, 2004, and hired approximately 120 employees—most of whom were former Candlewood employees. This satisfies the threshold requirement for successor status under *Fall River Dyeing*, 772 F.2d at 51.

### B. S & F Failed to Provide Sufficient Notice

The critical issue is whether S & F provided the "perfectly clear" notice required to avoid binding itself to the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. The Board correctly concluded that it did not.

**The Cover Sheet and Application Did Not Provide Clear Notice of Changed Terms**

S & F argues that its cover sheet and job application provided adequate notice. This argument fails for several reasons. The cover sheet stated only that S & F "intends to implement significant operational changes"—a vague statement that does not notify employees that their terms and conditions of employment will change. The application required applicants to acknowledge that "S & F can change benefits, policies and conditions at any time." This generalized reservation of management rights does not constitute the required "perfectly clear" announcement that the successor intends to establish a new set of terms before employees accept employment. *See Burns*, 406 U.S. at 295 (holding that notice must be "perfectly clear" that new terms will be established prior to acceptance).

The application also stated that employment would be "at will." While at-will employment is a term of employment, it is not the type of specific notice required. The at-will disclaimer does not inform employees that the specific terms of the collective bargaining agreement—including benefits, seniority, and working conditions—will not carry over.

**The Offer Letters Did Not Provide Clear Notice**

S & F's offer letters, while more specific, still did not provide the required notice. The letters labeled positions as "temporary employment" and stated that temporary employees were "not eligible for company benefits." However, these letters did not explicitly state that the terms and conditions of employment would differ from those under the collective bargaining agreement. The letters did not reference the collective bargaining agreement or indicate that S & F was declining to adopt its terms.

Moreover, the letters' characterization of positions as "temporary" created ambiguity. The Board found that the "temporary" employees were actually analogous to probationary employees because they were to be evaluated during the 90-day period and did not have a definite anticipated termination date. This characterization is supported by the record: S & F retained approximately 30-40 of these "temporary" employees for regular employment after the 90-day period. The use of the term "temporary" rather than明确的终止日期 (definite end date) created confusion about whether these employees were being hired under new terms or merely on a trial basis under existing terms.

**The Handbooks Did Not Cure the Notice Deficiency**

S & F distributed handbooks on July 9, 2004—after operations began. This post-hoc production of handbooks cannot serve as pre-acceptance notice. The employees had already accepted employment before receiving any written terms. Furthermore, the handbooks themselves created inconsistency: temporary employees received a handbook without benefit information, while regular employees received a handbook with benefit information. This inconsistency underscores the ambiguity in S & F's communications.

---

## III. THE BOARD'S FINDINGS ARE SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

The standard of review requires this Court to uphold the Board's factual findings if they are supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 71 S.Ct. 456, 95 L.Ed. 456 (1951). The Board's finding that S & F failed to clearly announce its intent to establish new conditions is supported by substantial evidence.

The cover sheet's reference to "operational changes" is not the same as notice of changed employment terms. The job application's general disclaimer about management's ability to change policies does not clearly announce that the specific terms of the collective bargaining agreement will not apply. The offer letters' use of "temporary" employment, rather than明确的 termination date, created ambiguity about the nature of the employment relationship. The timing of the handbook distribution—after operations began—further undermines S & F's position.

---

## IV. RESPONSE TO S & F'S ARGUMENTS

### A. S & F Mischaracterizes the Legal Standard

S & F argues that it provided "unambiguous notice" that "new terms would apply." However, the applicable standard is not merely whether employees were on notice that something might change. The standard is whether the successor clearly announced its intent to establish a new set of conditions *before* employees accepted employment. *Burns*, 406 U.S. at 295. S & F's communications did not meet this standard.

### B. S & F Mischaracterizes the Record

S & F argues that its communications were "direct, unambiguous notice" that "your job with us will not be the same as it was before." This mischaracterizes the record. The cover sheet and application did not mention the collective bargaining agreement or indicate that its terms would not apply. The offer letters did not explicitly state that the predecessor's terms would not carry over. The communications focused on the temporary nature of employment and the contingency of passing tests—not on the specific terms and conditions that would or would not apply.

### C. The "Temporary" Label Does Not Provide Clear Notice

S & F argues that its use of "temporary" employment clearly indicated that terms would change. However, the label "temporary" is ambiguous. It could mean (1) employment for a fixed term under new terms, or (2) a probationary period under existing terms. The Board reasonably found that the latter interpretation was more consistent with the overall communications, particularly given that S & F retained many of these "temporary" employees for regular employment.

---

## CONCLUSION

The Board properly applied the "perfectly clear" successor doctrine. S & F failed to provide the clear and unambiguous notice required to avoid binding itself to the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. The Board's findings are supported by substantial evidence. S & F's petition for review should be denied, and the Board's order should be enforced in full.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of a decision and order of the National Labor Relations Board (the "Board"), and the Board's cross-application for enforcement. The Board found that S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC ("S & F") was a "perfectly clear" successor to Candlewood Care Center and, as such, was bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement (CBA) because it failed to provide adequate notice of its intent to establish new terms and conditions of employment. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the Board's decision is not supported by substantial evidence and that S & F provided sufficient notice to its employees of the changes in employment terms. Accordingly, the petition for review is granted in part, and enforcement of the Board's order is denied in part.

**I. Standard of Review**

Under the National Labor Relations Act, this Court must enforce the Board's order if the Board's factual findings are supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 71 S.Ct. 456, 95 L.Ed. 456 (1951). However, the Board's application of the "perfectly clear" successor doctrine to established facts presents a legal question which this Court reviews de novo. *See NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972).

**II. The "Perfectly Clear" Successor Doctrine**

Under *Burns International Security Services*, a successor employer is not automatically bound by the collective bargaining agreement of its predecessor. 406 U.S. at 284-85. However, if the successor employer hires a substantial and representative complement of the predecessor's employees and fails to give those employees clear and unmistakable notice—prior to hiring—that it intends to establish new terms and conditions of employment, the successor becomes a "perfectly clear" successor and is bound by the predecessor's CBA. *Id.* at 279-80. The burden is on the successor employer to provide such notice.

**III. Analysis of S & F’s Notice**

The Board concluded that S & F failed to clearly announce its intent to establish new conditions prior to the employees' acceptance of employment. The record, however, indicates otherwise.

Prior to assuming operations, S & F distributed a cover sheet and job applications to existing staff stating that the company "intends to implement significant operational changes" and that employment would be "at will," subject to S & F's ability to "change benefits, policies and conditions at any time." While these general statements might be insufficient on their own, the subsequent "OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT" letters provided the specific notice required by *Burns*.

The offer letters explicitly stated that the position was temporary, would end in 90 days unless the employee was selected for regular employment, and that the employee was "not eligible for company benefits." Furthermore, the letters specified that "other terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor’s personnel policies and its employee handbook," rather than the predecessor's CBA. By requiring employees to sign these letters before commencing work, S & F clearly notified them that the terms of their employment—specifically regarding benefits, duration, and the governing policy manual—would differ from those under the predecessor CBA.

The Board and the General Counsel argue that the term "temporary" was ambiguous and could be construed as a probationary period under existing terms. However, the explicit denial of benefits and the statement that employment would "end" after 90 days are material changes that cannot be ignored. The notice need not be a detailed repudiation of every CBA provision; it need only be clear that the old terms will not apply. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). Here, the combination of "at-will" status, the denial of benefits, and the fixed-term nature of the temporary employment constituted clear notice that S & F was establishing a new set of conditions.

The Board's reliance on the fact that the employee handbooks were distributed after operations began on July 9 is misplaced. The critical notice occurred at the time the offer letters were signed and accepted in late June. The handbooks merely filled in the details of the policies that the offer letters had already referenced.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Board erred in concluding that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor bound by the predecessor's CBA. S & F provided clear, written notice to its employees—prior to the commencement of employment—that it intended to implement new terms and conditions, including the elimination of benefits and the implementation of at-will, temporary employment. Therefore, S & F was entitled to set the initial terms of employment without bargaining.

The petition for review is granted in part, and the Board's order is denied in part to the extent it requires S & F to restore the preexisting terms of employment and pay back pay for the period in which S & F was entitled to set new terms. The Board's order is enforced as to those findings of unfair labor practices that S & F does not contest.

SO ORDERED.

**II. The "Perfectly Clear" Successor Doctrine**

Under *Burns International Security Services*, a successor employer is not automatically bound by the collective bargaining agreement of its predecessor. 406 U.S. at 284-85. However, if the successor employer hires a substantial and representative complement of the predecessor's employees and fails to give those employees clear and unmistakable notice—prior to hiring—that it intends to establish new terms and conditions of employment, the successor becomes a "perfectly clear" successor and is bound by the predecessor's CBA. *Id.* at 279-80. The burden is on the successor employer to provide such notice.

The notice must be "perfectly clear" that new conditions are being established—not that every detail is spelled out. *Burns*, 406 U.S. at 284-85. The Board’s attempt to impose a heightened standard—demanding explicit references to the collective bargaining agreement or a “detailed repudiation of every provision”—is directly contradicted by binding precedent. The law does not require a successor to cite the CBA by name or list every altered term; it requires only that employees understand their new employment relationship will differ fundamentally from the predecessor’s.

**FINAL RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of a decision and order of the National Labor Relations Board (the "Board"), and the Board's cross-application for enforcement. The Board found that S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC ("S & F") was a "perfectly clear" successor to Candlewood Care Center and, as such, was bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement (CBA) because it failed to provide adequate notice of its intent to establish new terms and conditions of employment. For the reasons set forth below, and having reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties, the Court finds that the Board's decision is not supported by substantial evidence and that S & F provided sufficient notice to its employees of the changes in employment terms. Accordingly, the petition for review is granted in part, and enforcement of the Board's order is denied in part.

**I. Standard of Review**

Under the National Labor Relations Act, this Court must enforce the Board's order if the Board's factual findings are supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 71 S.Ct. 456, 95 L.Ed. 456 (1951). However, the Board's application of the "perfectly clear" successor doctrine to established facts presents a legal question which this Court reviews de novo. *See NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972).

**II. The "Perfectly Clear" Successor Doctrine**

Under *Burns International Security Services*, a successor employer is not automatically bound by the collective bargaining agreement of its predecessor. 406 U.S. at 284-85. However, if the successor employer hires a substantial and representative complement of the predecessor's employees and fails to give those employees clear and unmistakable notice—prior to hiring—that it intends to establish new terms and conditions of employment, the successor becomes a "perfectly clear" successor and is bound by the predecessor's CBA. *Id.* at 279-80. The burden is on the successor employer to provide such notice.

**III. Analysis of S & F’s Notice**

The Board concluded that S & F failed to clearly announce its intent to establish new conditions prior to the employees' acceptance of employment. The arguments submitted by S & F demonstrate that the Board erred in this conclusion.

**A. The Standard of Notice Does Not Require Exhaustive Specificity**

S & F correctly argues that the Board misstates the legal standard by requiring "exhaustive specificity" in its notice. The Supreme Court has held that a successor employer need not detail every change to the predecessor’s collective bargaining agreement; it need only clearly communicate that *old terms will not apply*. The notice must be "perfectly clear" that new conditions are being established—not that every detail is spelled out. *Burns*, 406 U.S. at 284-85. The Board’s attempt to impose a heightened standard—demanding explicit references to the collective bargaining agreement or a “detailed repudiation of every provision”—is directly contradicted by binding precedent. The law does not require a successor to cite the CBA by name or list every altered term; it requires only that employees understand their new employment relationship will differ fundamentally from the predecessor’s.

**B. The Cover Sheet and Job Application Provided Adequate Warning**

The Board dismissed the cover sheet’s statement that S & F “intends to implement significant operational changes” as “vague.” This was error. The cover sheet did not merely hint at changes; it explicitly warned that employment would be contingent on passing physical, drug, and background checks, and that any job offer would be “at will” with S & F’s right to “change benefits, policies and conditions at any time.” This is not ambiguous—it is direct notice that the predecessor’s terms (including benefits and job security under the CBA) would not carry over. The Board’s claim that “at-will” status alone is insufficient ignores that *Burns* requires notice of *new terms*, and “at-will” employment is a material departure from the predecessor’s CBA (which typically included just-cause protections). When combined with the explicit right to alter “benefits, policies and conditions,” the notice was unambiguous: employees were being hired under a new regime.

**C. The Offer Letters Eliminated All Ambiguity**

The Board’s assertion that the offer letters’ use of “temporary” was “ambiguous” because it could mean “probationary” under existing terms is not supported by the record. The letters did not say merely “temporary”; they stated: (1) “You are not eligible for company benefits” (a direct repudiation of the predecessor’s CBA benefits); (2) “Your employment with [S & F] will end” after the 90-day period, “unless you are selected for regular employment” (a fixed termination date with no ambiguity about the temporary nature); and (3) “Other terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor’s personnel policies and its employee handbook” (not the predecessor’s CBA).

This is not ambiguous—it is a clear signal that the predecessor’s terms would not apply. The Board’s claim that “temporary” could mean “probationary under existing terms” is contradicted by the letter’s explicit denial of benefits and fixed end date. A “probationary period” under a CBA would still include benefits and job security; here, the letter explicitly stripped both. The law does not require a successor to say “the CBA is dead”—it requires notice that *new terms apply*, and these letters did exactly that.

**D. The Timing of Notice Was Proper**

The Board suggests that notice provided through the offer letters signed in late June was somehow untimely because the handbooks were distributed after operations began on July 9. This argument misapprehends the timing requirements of *Burns*. The critical inquiry is whether notice was provided *before employees accepted employment*—that is, before they became employees of the successor. Here, employees signed the offer letters in late June, before commencing work on July 1. This is precisely the timing *Burns* contemplates: notice given prior to hiring, allowing employees to make an informed decision about whether to accept employment under the new terms. The handbooks distributed on July 9 were not the vehicle for notice; they were supplementary materials that elaborated on the policies referenced in the offer letters.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Board erred in concluding that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor bound by the predecessor's CBA. S & F provided clear, written notice to its employees—prior to the commencement of employment—that it intended to implement new terms and conditions, including the elimination of benefits and the implementation of at-will, temporary employment. The Board's demand for "exhaustive specificity" is not supported by *Burns* or its progeny. Therefore, S & F was entitled to set the initial terms of employment without bargaining.

The petition for review is granted in part, and the Board's order is denied in part to the extent it requires S & F to restore the preexisting terms of employment and pay back pay for the period in which S & F was entitled to set new terms. The Board's order is enforced as to those findings of unfair labor practices that S & F does not contest.

SO ORDERED.
