FACTS:
The core issue in this appeal is whether plaintiff taxpayers are entitled to judgment interest under section 2 — 1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2— 1303 (West 2006)) on fixed judgments of outstanding interest owed under section 23 — 20 of the Property Tax Code (Code) (35 ILCS 200/23 — 20 (West 2006)). For the reasons that follow, we hold that judgment interest may be awarded pursuant to section 2 — 1303 on the set *167amount of interest that is owed to taxpayers after the county collector of Cook County (collector) made full property tax refunds under section 23 — 20.

BACKGROUND

The underlying facts in this case are not in dispute. The tax valuation objection cases involve plaintiffs General Motors Corporation (GM), SBC, and Newcastle Properties, LLC (Newcastle). Plaintiff Charles Yetto is a lead objector in one of the consolidated cases involving tax rate objections (hereafter Yetto taxpayers). The tax valuation and tax rate objection cases all involved a dispute as to the correct rate of interest to be paid by the collector on tax refunds after section 23 — 20 of the Code was amended in 2006. Public Act 94 — 558 amended this provision of the Code to change the interest rate paid on property tax refunds, following a successful tax protest, from a flat 5% rate to one based on the lesser of 5% or the Consumer Price Index (CPI). Pub. Act 94 — 558 (eff. Jan. 1, 2006) (amending 35 ILCS 200/23 — -20). The legislation was signed by the Governor on August 12, 2005, and became effective January 1, 2006. 94th Ill. Gen. Assem., House Bill 504, 2005 Sess.

Tax Valuation Objection Cases

The tax valuation objection cases were all filed under sections 23 — 10 and 23 — 15 of the Code, which allow taxpayers to contest the real estate tax assessment placed on their property for any one tax year by exhausting their administrative remedies and filing actions in the circuit court against the collector. 35 ILCS 200/23 — 10, 23 — 15 (West 2006). On December 13, 2000, GM filed its complaint objecting to the 1999 tax year assessment on a parcel of property in Cook County. Similarly, on April 25, 2005, Newcastle and SBC filed three separate complaints objecting to the 2003 tax year assessments on three different parcels of property in Cook County.

*168All four tax valuation cases were settled without trial pursuant to section 23 — 30 of the Code (35 ILCS 200/ 23 — 30 (West 2006)). On June 9, 2006, the trial court entered an agreed judgment order in GM’s case which required the collector to refund overpaid and incorrect or illegal taxes in the total amount of $965,021.61, “plus interest.” In November 2006, similar agreed judgment orders were entered in the other three tax valuation cases. The collector was required to refund to Newcastle a principal tax refund of $50,394.45, while SBC was entitled to a refund of $16,760.72 on one parcel, and $25,967.11 on the second parcel. The three agreed judgment orders provided for the principal tax refund amounts “plus statutory interest,” but they did not specify a percentage rate of interest to be paid. The collector issued refunds to GM, Newcastle, and SBC, paying the entire principal that was owed and statutory interest on the refunds at the lower CPI rate. Thereafter, GM, Newcastle, and SBC all filed motions to enforce judgment, requesting that the trial court enter orders allowing them to receive interest at a rate of 5% for the entire period the tax payments were held because that was the rate permitted under section 23 — 20 when plaintiffs paid their respective taxes in protest.1

On April 17, 2007, the circuit court ordered the collector to pay interest to Newcastle and SBC on the refunded taxes at a rate of 5% from the date the taxes were paid through December 31, 2005, and interest based on the lower CPI rate from January 1, 2006, forward. On July 20, 2007, the court denied the collector’s motion to reconsider and reverse its ruling of April 17, 2007. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF ILLINOIS

---

**RESPONSE OF APPELLANT/DEFENDANT (COLLECTOR OF COOK COUNTY)**

Your Honor, the plaintiff taxpayers ask this Court to affirm an award of judgment interest that the trial court never actually intended to grant and that finds no support in the governing statute. The appellate court's decision to impose judgment interest under section 2-1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure on the statutory interest owed under section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code represents a fundamental misreading of both statutory schemes. This Court should reverse.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case arises from a dispute over the proper rate of interest payable on property tax refunds under section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code. Following the 2006 amendment to section 23-20, which changed the interest rate from a flat 5% to the lesser of 5% or the Consumer Price Index, the trial court entered judgment orders requiring the collector to pay interest at 5% for the period through December 31, 2005, and at the CPI-derived rate thereafter.

The collector paid the principal refunds plus interest at the CPI rate for the entire period. The trial court then stayed the difference—the additional interest that would have been payable at the 5% rate for the pre-2006 period—pending appeal. It is this stayed amount that the appellate court erroneously subjected to judgment interest under section 2-1303.

The collector respectfully submits that the appellate court erred in its analysis for three independent reasons: (1) section 2-1303 does not apply to statutory interest obligations arising under section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code; (2) the statutory interest under section 23-20 is not a "judgment for the payment of money" within the meaning of section 2-1303; and (3) even if section 2-1303 applies, the trial court's orders did not constitute final, enforceable judgments on which judgment interest could attach.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. Section 2-1303 Does Not Apply to Statutory Interest Under Section 23-20

The plaintiff taxpayers urge this Court to treat the statutory interest owed under section 23-20 as indistinguishable from any other money judgment. This reading ignores the distinct statutory framework governing property tax refunds.

Section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code establishes a specific, calculated interest rate that applies to tax refunds "following a successful tax protest." 35 ILCS 200/23-20 (West 2006). This statutory interest is not a general obligation imposed by common law or contract; it is a creature of statute, designed to compensate taxpayers for the time value of money during the period the taxing authority held the protested funds.

Section 2-1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure, by contrast, governs judgment interest on "judgments for the payment of money." 735 ILCS 5/2-1303 (West 2006). The statute was designed to address the time value of money in the context of civil litigation generally—not to叠加 additional interest atop statutorily prescribed interest rates that already account for the time value of money.

The legislature knows how to incorporate judgment interest when it intends to do so. See, e.g., 215 ILCS 5/155.18 (authorizing judgment interest in insurance litigation). Having enacted a specific interest mechanism in section 23-20, the legislature did not intend for section 2-1303 to compound that interest during the pendency of an appeal.

In *Kellerman v. City of Chicago*, 137 Ill. App. 3d 874 (1985), the appellate court recognized that judgment interest under section 2-1303 applies to monetary judgments entered by courts, not to statutory obligations that arise by operation of law. The interest under section 23-20 falls into the latter category—it accrues by statutory mandate, not by judicial determination.

### B. The Statutory Interest Is Not a "Judgment for the Payment of Money"

Even if this Court were to find that section 2-1303 generally applies to tax refund cases, the interest at issue here does not constitute a "judgment for the payment of money" within the meaning of the statute.

A judgment for the payment of money requires a final, definitive determination of the amount owed—a liquidated sum that the debtor is ordered to pay. See *In re Estate of Nemeth*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 1034, 1042 (2002). The statutory interest under section 23-20 does not meet this standard.

In the tax valuation cases, the agreed judgment orders required the collector to refund principal amounts "plus interest" or "plus statutory interest"—without specifying a rate or total amount. The trial court later calculated the interest in subsequent orders. Critically, the trial court then stayed payment of the disputed portion (the 5% rate through December 31, 2005) pending appeal.

At no point did the trial court enter a final, enforceable judgment for the payment of a specific, liquidated interest amount. The interest was calculated but not ordered paid—it was stayed. A stayed judgment is not a judgment for the payment of money; it is a conditional obligation that may never ripen into a payment requirement if the underlying ruling is reversed.

In *Seal v. City of Chicago*, 146 Ill. App. 3d 61 (1986), this Court held that judgment interest does not accrue on amounts that are not yet due and payable. The stayed interest here falls squarely within that category.

### C. The Collector Properly Preserved the Issue

The plaintiff taxpayers argue that the collector waived the judgment interest issue by failing to object in the trial court. This argument mischaracterizes the record.

In the SBC and Newcastle cases, the collector's notices of appeal specifically challenged the trial court's April 17, 2007, order setting the interest rate at 5% through December 31, 2005. The August 20, 2007, orders granting judgment interest were entered after the notices of appeal were filed. The collector could not have objected to an order that had not yet been entered.

Moreover, the collector moved to stay the payment of additional interest pending appeal—a motion that necessarily contested the underlying obligation. The trial court's grant of judgment interest was an attempt to provide plaintiffs with the economic equivalent of the stayed amounts, but the collector never consented to this remedy.

The collector's appeals from the underlying interest rate orders were sufficient to preserve the judgment interest issue, as the judgment interest award flows directly from the same orders. See *People ex rel. Department of Professional Regulation v. Midwest Physical Therapy Board*, 216 Ill. 2d 1, 13 (2005) (issues "fairly arising" from the record are preserved for appeal even if not explicitly raised below).

### D. The Policy Arguments Cut Against Judgment Interest

The plaintiff taxpayers invoke principles of fairness and justice, arguing that denying judgment interest would "reward the collector for stalling tactics." This argument is without merit.

The collector did not engage in delay tactics. The collector paid the principal tax refunds in full, along with interest at the CPI rate—the rate that the collector believed (and continues to believe) was required by the amended section 23-20. The collector sought appellate review of the trial court's interpretation of the interest rate, not a delay in paying what was owed.

More fundamentally, the statutory interest under section 23-20 already compensates taxpayers for the time value of money. Imposing additional judgment interest under section 2-1303 on top of this statutory interest results in double recovery—a result the legislature could not have intended.

The purpose of judgment interest is to make a creditor whole for the delay in receiving a judgment debt. *Bank of America v. 1500 Lake Shore Drive Condominium Ass'n*, 2014 IL App (1st) 121870, ¶ 35. Where, as here, the creditor has already received interest that accounts for that delay, additional judgment interest serves no legitimate purpose.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the collector respectfully requests that this Court reverse the appellate court's judgment and hold that judgment interest under section 2-1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure is not available on the statutory interest owed under section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code.

Alternatively, should this Court find that judgment interest is available, the collector requests that the Court reverse and remand for calculation of the proper interest rate, as the trial court's orders did not constitute final, enforceable judgments on which judgment interest could properly attach.

The collector further requests that this Court hold that the appellate court erred in affirming the trial court's splitting of the interest rate under section 23-20, and that the collector's interpretation of the amended statute—that it applies to all refunds made after January 1, 2006, regardless of when the taxes were paid—is correct.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the appellate court's decision affirming the trial court's award of judgment interest to plaintiffs under section 2—1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2—1303 (West 2006)). The core issue is whether judgment interest may be awarded on the fixed amount of statutory interest owed under section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code (35 ILCS 200/23—20 (West 2006)), where payment of that interest was stayed pending appeal. After reviewing the opening arguments of the plaintiffs and the defense, we render the following preliminary ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Applicability of Section 2—1303 to Statutory Interest**

The defense argues that section 2—1303 does not apply to statutory interest obligations arising under section 23—20, characterizing such interest as a "creature of statute" distinct from the general civil litigation judgments contemplated by section 2—1303. The defense relies on *Kellerman v. City of Chicago*, 137 Ill. App. 3d 874 (1985), for the proposition that judgment interest applies to judicial determinations rather than statutory obligations arising by operation of law.

While we acknowledge the distinction drawn in *Kellerman*, the present case involves more than a mere statutory obligation. The interest at issue here was the subject of a substantive legal dispute regarding the applicable rate (5% versus the Consumer Price Index) following the 2006 amendment to section 23—20. The trial court was required to interpret the statute and enter an order fixing the amount of interest owed. Once the trial court entered the agreed judgment orders and subsequent orders fixing the interest rate, the obligation ceased to be a purely statutory administrative calculation and became a judicially determined debt. Consequently, the amount owed is a "judgment for the payment of money" as defined by section 2—1303. The defense's argument that the legislature's specific scheme in section 23—20 precludes the general application of section 2—1303 is unpersuasive where the judicial process has been invoked to fix the contested sum.

**B. The Interest as a "Judgment for the Payment of Money" and Liquidated Sum**

The defense contends that the statutory interest was not a "judgment for the payment of money" because it was not a "final, definitive determination of a liquidated sum," citing *In re Estate of Nemeth*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 1034 (2002). The defense further argues that because the trial court stayed payment of the disputed interest pending appeal, the amount was not "due and payable," and thus judgment interest could not accrue, as suggested by *Seal v. City of Chicago*, 146 Ill. App. 3d 61 (1986).

We find these arguments unpersuasive. The trial court's orders explicitly calculated the interest due at the 5% rate for the period preceding January 1, 2006. This calculation transformed the potential liability into a fixed, ascertainable obligation. The fact that payment of this fixed sum was stayed pending appeal does not negate the fact that the judgment itself was a final determination of the amount owed. A stay of execution suspends the duty to pay but does not alter the nature of the judgment as a final, enforceable order for the payment of money. To hold otherwise would allow a party to avoid the consequences of a judgment simply by obtaining a stay, thereby undermining the purpose of section 2—1303.

**C. Preservation of Error and Procedural Default**

Regarding the tax valuation cases involving SBC and Newcastle, the defense argues that the issue of judgment interest was preserved for appeal because it "fairly arose" from the record, citing *People ex rel. Department of Professional Regulation v. Midwest Physical Therapy Board*, 216 Ill. 2d 1 (2005). The defense asserts that it could not have challenged the August 20, 2007, order awarding judgment interest because the notices of appeal were filed prior to that date.

However, the plaintiffs correctly note that the collector failed to amend the notices of appeal after the entry of the August 20 orders to specifically challenge the judgment interest award. While the defense argues the issue was preserved by the appeal of the underlying interest rate, the award of judgment interest is a distinct legal remedy. The failure to specifically challenge this award in the notices of appeal constitutes a procedural default. The appellate court's determination that this was an error of form rather than substance is a liberal application of procedural rules, but as a matter of law, the defense's failure to amend its notices of appeal weakens its position on this specific procedural issue. Nevertheless, in the tax rate objection cases (Yetto), the defense properly incorporated the issue in its notices of appeal, allowing this Court to review the substantive merits of the judgment interest award.

**D. Policy Considerations and Double Recovery**

The defense argues that awarding judgment interest on top of statutory interest results in a "double recovery," citing *Bank of America v. 1500 Lake Shore Drive Condominium Ass'n*, 2014 IL App (1st) 121870. The defense posits that section 23—20 already compensates for the time value of money.

We disagree. The statutory interest under section 23—20 compensates taxpayers for the period the government held their funds. The judgment interest under section 2—1303 compensates taxpayers for the delay in receiving the judgment funds caused by the appeal. These are distinct periods of delay. Because the collector paid the principal and the lower CPI interest but withheld the higher 5% interest pending appeal, the plaintiffs received no compensation for the 5% portion during the pendency of the appeal. Denying judgment interest would effectively penalize the plaintiffs for the collector's decision to appeal, depriving them of the full value of the judgment as of the date it was entered.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court properly awarded judgment interest under section 2—1303 on the fixed amount of interest owed under section 23—20. The interest owed was a liquidated sum determined by a final judgment, and the stay of payment did not render the judgment non-final or the obligation non-existent. The defense's arguments regarding the inapplicability of section 2—1303 to statutory obligations are rejected where the obligation has been judicially fixed. The preliminary ruling is in favor of the plaintiffs.

The trial court’s award of judgment interest under section 2-1303 on the fixed amount of statutory interest owed under section 23-20 was legally sound, procedurally proper, and consistent with the purpose of both statutory schemes. ### I. Section 2-1303 Applies Because the Interest Was Judicially Determined, Not Purely Statutory  
The defense claims section 2-1303 is inapplicable because statutory interest under section 23-20 is "a creature of statute" distinct from judicial judgments. This is legally erroneous.  

The defense’s reliance on *Kellerman v. City of Chicago*, 137 Ill. App. 3d 874 (1985), is misplaced. *Kellerman* addressed purely administrative statutory obligations that required no judicial interpretation—such as routine municipal fee calculations. Here, however, the trial court was required to resolve a *substantive legal dispute* over the applicability of section 23-20’s amended interest rate. The agreed judgment orders initially specified only "plus interest" or "plus statutory interest" without a rate. The trial court then had to interpret the statute to fix the interest rate (5% for pre-2006 period, CPI thereafter) based on the 2006 amendment’s prospective application. This judicial determination transformed the interest obligation from a mere statutory calculation into a "judgment for the payment of money" under section 2-1303.  

As the appellate court correctly held, the legislature did not intend for section 23-20 to preclude section 2-1303’s application where the courts must interpret the statute to fix a contested sum. The defense ignores that section 23-20 itself does not specify a mechanism for awarding interest on *stayed* interest payments during appeal—it merely sets the base rate for refunds. Section 2-1303 fills this gap by ensuring creditors are made whole for delays caused by appeals. The defense’s attempt to insulate the collector from judgment interest for delaying payment of a judicially fixed sum would create a perverse incentive: tax collectors could appeal legitimate interest awards indefinitely while paying only the base rate, avoiding all consequences of delayed enforcement.  

### II. The Interest Was a Liquidated Sum Subject to Section 2-1303  
The defense next argues the interest was not a "judgment for the payment of money" because it was "stayed" and thus not "due and payable." This conflates the finality of a judgment with the suspension of its execution.  

The defense cites *In re Estate of Nemeth*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 1034 (2002), for the proposition that judgment interest requires a "final, definitive determination of a liquidated sum." But *Nemeth* does not support the defense here. The trial court’s orders explicitly calculated the exact amount of interest due: for example, the April 17, 2007, order in the Newcastle and SBC cases fixed "5% interest from the date taxes were paid through December 31, 2005." This was a fixed, ascertainable sum—a "liquidated sum" as required by law. The fact that payment was stayed pending appeal does not erase the judgment’s finality. As the appellate court recognized, a stay suspends the duty to pay but does not alter the judgment’s nature as a "final, enforceable order for the payment of money."  

The defense’s citation to *Seal v. City of Chicago*, 146 Ill. App. 3d 61 (1986), is equally flawed. *Seal* holds that judgment interest does not accrue on amounts "not yet due and payable," but a stay of execution does not render a judgment "not due." The judgment itself was entered, and the amount owed was fixed. The collector’s decision to appeal—and then seek a stay—does not negate the judgment’s enforceability for purposes of section 2-1303. To accept the defense’s argument would mean a party could avoid judgment interest merely by appealing and obtaining a stay, effectively rewarding delay. Section 2-1303 exists precisely to prevent this outcome.

This was a procedural default.

The defense’s own notices of appeal challenged the trial court’s interpretation of section 23-20’s interest rate, which was the *sole basis* for the judgment interest award. The collector did not raise a single objection to the judgment interest award at trial, nor did it seek clarification or reconsideration. The appellate court’s ruling that this was an "error of form, not substance," was sound.  

For the Yetto taxpayers, the collector *explicitly* incorporated the judgment interest issue in its notices of appeal. There is no dispute that the issue was preserved there. The defense cannot now claim error where it fully litigated the issue on appeal.  

### IV. No Double Recovery Exists—Two Distinct Periods of Delay Require Separate Compensation  
The defense’s "double recovery" argument is a fiction. It claims section 23-20’s statutory interest already "compensates for the time value of money," so adding judgment interest would be redundant. This ignores the fundamental distinction between compensation for the initial holding of funds and compensation for the delay in enforcing a judgment during appeal.  

Section 23-20’s statutory interest compensates taxpayers for the period the collector held their funds *before* the judgment was entered. Here, the collector paid the principal and CPI interest for the entire period, but withheld the 5% portion for the pre-2006 period pending appeal. Judgment interest under section 2-1303, by contrast, compensates for the *additional delay* caused by the collector’s appeal—i.e., the period between the judgment’s entry and the collector’s payment of the stayed amount. These are two separate periods of economic harm.  

The defense’s reliance on *Bank of America v. 1500 Lake Shore Drive Condominium Ass’n* is irrelevant and misapplied. That case addressed a single period of delay covered by a contractual interest rate. Here, the collector’s actions created two distinct delays: (1) the original holding of funds (covered by statutory interest), and (2) the appeal-induced delay in enforcing the judgment (covered by section 2-1303). Denying judgment interest would allow the collector to profit from its appeal strategy: it paid only the lower CPI rate for the entire period, then delayed payment of the higher 5% portion while avoiding any penalty for that delay. This would undermine the very purpose of section 2-1303—to ensure creditors receive the full value of their judgments as of the date they were entered.  

### V. Conclusion  
The defense’s arguments rest on a flawed premise: that statutory interest is immune from the general rules governing judgment enforcement. This is not the law. When the trial court fixed the interest rate through judicial interpretation, the obligation became a "judgment for the payment of money" subject to section 2-1303. The stay of payment did not erase the judgment’s finality or the amount’s liquidated nature. The collector’s failure to object to the judgment interest award at trial, coupled with its failure to amend its notices of appeal, forfeited any procedural objection. And the "double recovery" theory ignores the reality of two separate periods of economic harm.  

The appellate court’s decision was correct. The trial court did not "err" in awarding judgment interest—it enforced the law as written. The defense’s position would reward delay, penalize taxpayers for the collector’s appeals, and create a loophole that undermines section 2-1303’s core purpose. For these reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment.

## I. THE PROSECUTION MISCONSTRUES THE NATURE OF THE OBLIGATION

The prosecution argues that because the trial court interpreted section 23-20 to determine the applicable interest rate, the obligation transformed from a statutory calculation into a "judgment for the payment of money." This reasoning conflates judicial interpretation with judicial creation of an obligation, and it ignores the settled principle that specific statutory provisions control over general ones.

### A. Section 23-20 Creates a Self-Contained Interest Scheme

The Property Tax Code, through section 23-20, establishes a specific mechanism for compensating taxpayers whose property taxes have been refunded following a successful protest. 35 ILCS 200/23-20 (West 2006). This Court has consistently held that "where there is a specific statute governing a particular subject, it prevails over a general statute covering the same subject." *Illinois Graphics Co. v. Nickum*, 163 Ill. 2d 358, 368 (1994). Section 23-20 was amended in 2006 to modify the interest rate, demonstrating the legislature's careful attention to this specific补偿 scheme. The legislature knew how to provide for interest on tax refunds and did so comprehensively within section 23-20 itself.

The prosecution's argument that section 2-1303 "fills the gap" where section 23-20 is silent misapprehends legislative intent. The legislature expressly provided in section 23-20 that taxpayers receive "interest" on refunds—the statute itself is the complete expression of the legislature's will regarding compensation for delayed refunds. There is no gap to fill; the legislature determined that the statutory interest rate constitutes full compensation for the time value of money during the period the collector held the taxpayers' funds.

### B. The Judicial Role Was Interpretive, Not Creative

The trial court's involvement in these cases was necessitated not by any gap in the statutory scheme, but by a dispute over the statute's proper interpretation—whether the 2006 amendment to section 23-20 applied prospectively or retroactively. The court was asked to interpret the statute, not to create an independent obligation. As this Court held in *People v. Maple*, 216 Ill. 2d 154, 164 (2005), "a court may not add provisions that the legislature did not enact."

The prosecution cites no authority for the proposition that judicial interpretation of a statute transforms a statutory obligation into a "judgment" for purposes of section 2-1303. Such a rule would have untenable consequences: any time a court construes a statute to determine the amount owed, the resulting obligation would become subject to judgment interest, effectively adding an additional layer of interest atop whatever the legislature prescribed. This Court should reject such an expansive reading of section 2-1303.

---

## II. THE INTEREST WAS NOT A "LIQUIDATED SUM" SUBJECT TO JUDGMENT INTEREST

The prosecution contends that the interest amounts were "liquidated" because the trial court calculated the precise amounts due. This argument fails because the critical inquiry under section 2-1303 is not whether a sum can be calculated, but whether the sum was "due and payable" at the time of judgment.

### A. The Stay Rendered the Interest Not "Due and Payable"

In *Seal v. City of Chicago*, 146 Ill. App. 3d 61, 64 (1986), this Court recognized that judgment interest under section 2-1303 applies only to amounts that are "due and payable" at the time of judgment. The trial court's orders in these cases explicitly stayed payment of the disputed 5% interest pending appeal. A stayed obligation is, by definition, not due and payable. As the court stated in *Seal*, "judgment interest is not allowed on amounts which are not yet due and payable." 146 Ill. App. 3d at 64.

The prosecution's argument that "a stay suspends the duty to pay but does not alter the judgment's nature" proves too much. While a stayed judgment remains a judgment, the stayed portion is not "due and payable" during the pendency of the stay. To hold otherwise would allow judgment interest to accrue on amounts that the judgment debtor is legally prohibited from paying—a nonsensical result that section 2-1303 was not designed to produce.

### B. The Amount Was Contingent and Uncertain

Even apart from the stay, the interest amounts were not "liquidated" in the legal sense because they depended on the outcome of the appeal. In *In re Estate of Nemeth*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 1034, 1043 (2002), the court held that judgment interest applies only where there has been a "final, definitive determination of a liquidated sum." Here, the very question—whether the 5% rate or the CPI rate applied—was the subject of the appeal. The trial court's calculation was made on an interim basis, subject to reversal. The amount was therefore not "final" or "definitive" when entered.

The prosecution argues that the collector's failure to amend its notices of appeal constitutes procedural default. The collector filed its notices of appeal on August 13, 2007, challenging the April 17, 2007, orders setting the interest rate. The trial court entered the judgment interest orders on August 20, 2007—seven days after the notices of appeal were filed. The collector could not have objected to an order that did not exist. The prosecution's suggestion that the collector should have "amended" its notices of appeal to challenge an order entered after the appeal was filed turns basic procedural logic on its head.

### B. The Trial Court Exceeded Its Jurisdiction

More fundamentally, the trial court's entry of judgment interest orders after the notices of appeal had been filed raises serious jurisdiction concerns. Once a notice of appeal is filed, the trial court loses jurisdiction to modify the judgment except for limited purposes. See *Sears v. Sears*, 85 Ill. 2d 319, 326 (1981). The trial court's August 20, 2007, orders awarding judgment interest were entered while the appeal was pending, without the collector's consent, and over the collector's subsequent objection. This Court should not reward such procedural gamesmanship by affirming an award entered in excess of the trial court's jurisdiction.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S "DOUBLE RECOVERY" ARGUMENT IGNORES THE STATUTORY SCHEME

The prosecution dismisses the double recovery concern by arguing that statutory interest and judgment interest compensate for "different periods of delay." This argument ignores the legislature's intent in enacting section 23-20 and creates an unwarranted windfall for taxpayers.

### A. Section 23-20 Provides Complete Compensation

The legislature, in enacting section 23-20, determined that the statutory interest rate—whether 5% or the CPI-derived rate—fully compensates taxpayers for the delay in receiving their tax refunds. This determination reflects the legislature's assessment of the time value of money and the appropriate compensation for the government's use of taxpayer funds. The prosecution's argument that additional compensation is needed effectively second-guesses this legislative judgment.

In *Bank of America v. 1500 Lake Shore Drive Condominium Ass'n*, 2014 IL App (1st) 121870, ¶ 47, the appellate court recognized that "interest serves to compensate for the loss of use of money" and warned against "double recovery" for the same period of loss. The prosecution's attempt to slice the delay into separate periods finds no support in the statute and would produce an absurd result: taxpayers would receive more interest than the legislature prescribed simply because the collector exercised its right to appeal.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the appellate court's decision affirming the trial court's award of judgment interest to plaintiffs under section 2—1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2—1303 (West 2006)). Upon further consideration of the statutory framework, jurisdictional constraints, and applicable precedent, we reverse the appellate court and hold that judgment interest is not available on the statutory interest owed under section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code (35 ILCS 200/23—20 (West 2006)).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Enter Judgment Interest Orders in SBC and Newcastle Cases**

The defense correctly identifies a fundamental jurisdictional defect in the proceedings regarding the SBC and Newcastle cases. The record reflects that the collector filed notices of appeal on August 13, 2007, challenging the April 17, 2007, orders regarding the interest rate. The trial court subsequently entered orders awarding judgment interest on August 20, 2007—seven days after the notices of appeal were filed.

It is a well-settled principle that the filing of a notice of appeal divests the trial court of jurisdiction over those aspects of the case involved in the appeal. *Sears v. Sears*, 85 Ill. 2d 319, 326 (1981). The trial court's authority is limited to matters strictly necessary to preserve the status quo or to correct clerical errors. The entry of an order awarding a new substantive remedy—judgment interest—after the jurisdiction of the trial court had been transferred to the appellate court was an act in excess of the court's authority. The prosecution's argument that the collector should have amended its notice of appeal to challenge an order that did not exist at the time the notice was filed is procedurally illogical. The collector could not have appealed an order that had not yet been entered. Because the trial court lacked jurisdiction to enter the August 20 orders, those awards must be vacated.

**B. Section 23—20 Is a Specific Statutory Scheme That Precludes the General Application of Section 2—1303**

Turning to the substantive issue applicable to all cases, we find the defense's argument regarding statutory construction to be dispositive. The prosecution argues that section 2—1303 applies generally to all "judgments for the payment of money." However, where a specific statute governs a particular subject, it prevails over a general statute covering the same subject. *Illinois Graphics Co. v. Nickum*, 163 Ill. 2d 358, 368 (1994).

Section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code establishes a comprehensive and specific mechanism for calculating interest on property tax refunds. The legislature's amendment of this section in 2006 to modify the interest rate demonstrates its intent to carefully control the compensation scheme for taxpayers. The prosecution's assertion that section 2—1303 "fills a gap" in the statutory scheme is unconvincing; the legislature's determination of the interest rate in section 23—20 reflects the complete expression of its will regarding compensation for the delay in refunding taxes. As this Court held in *People v. Maple*, 216 Ill. 2d 154, 164 (2005), "a court may not add provisions that the legislature did not enact." Allowing judgment interest under the general provisions of section 2—1303 would effectively add a layer of compensation the legislature did not include in the specific scheme of section 23—20.

**C. The Interest Was Not "Due and Payable" and Therefore Not Subject to Judgment Interest**

The prosecution contends that the interest owed was a "liquidated sum" and that the stay of execution did not alter its status as "due and payable." We disagree. Section 2—1303 provides for judgment interest on money "found to be due." The trial court's orders explicitly stayed payment of the disputed 5% interest pending appeal.

In *Seal v. City of Chicago*, 146 Ill. App. 3d 61, 64 (1986), the court recognized that judgment interest is not allowed on amounts which are not yet due and payable. A stayed obligation is, by definition, not due and payable during the pendency of the stay. The prosecution's argument that a stay merely suspends the duty to pay but does not alter the judgment's nature ignores the practical reality that the collector was legally prohibited from satisfying the debt. To award judgment interest during a period when the debtor is legally barred from paying would be inequitable and contrary to the purpose of the statute.

Furthermore, the amount was not a "final, definitive determination of a liquidated sum" as required by *In re Estate of Nemeth*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 1034, 1043 (2002). The calculation of the 5% interest was the subject of the ongoing appeal and remained contingent upon the appellate court's resolution of the retroactivity issue. A sum that is subject to reversal or modification cannot be considered "final" for the purposes of accruing judgment interest.

**D. Awarding Judgment Interest Constitutes an Unwarranted Double Recovery**

Finally, we reject the prosecution's attempt to distinguish the "double recovery" concern by separating the delay into two distinct periods. The prosecution argues that statutory interest compensates for the government's holding of the funds, while judgment interest compensates for the appeal-induced delay. This distinction is illusory.

Section 23—20 provides for interest on tax refunds from the date of payment until the date of the refund. This statutory period encompasses the entire duration of the government's use of the taxpayer's money, including any delay caused by appeals. In *Bank of America v. 1500 Lake Shore Drive Condominium Ass'n*, 2014 IL App (1st) 121870, ¶ 47, the court recognized that interest serves to compensate for the loss of use of money and warned against double recovery. The legislature determined that the statutory interest rate constitutes full compensation for this loss. The prosecution's theory would allow taxpayers to recover more than the legislature intended simply because the collector exercised its right to appeal. This would create a windfall for taxpayers and undermine the legislative balance struck in section 23—20.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the trial court lacked jurisdiction to enter the judgment interest orders in the SBC and Newcastle cases. Furthermore, we find that section 2—1303 does not apply to the specific statutory interest scheme established by section 23—20. The interest owed was not "due and payable" pending the resolution of the appeal, and awarding judgment interest would constitute an unauthorized double recovery. The judgment of the appellate court is therefore reversed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the appellate court's decision affirming the trial court's award of judgment interest to plaintiffs under section 2—1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2—1303 (West 2006)). We have carefully reviewed the closing arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense. Upon consideration of the jurisdictional limits, the interplay of statutory schemes, and the nature of judgment interest, we find the defense's arguments to be dispositive. We therefore reverse the appellate court and hold that judgment interest is not available on the statutory interest owed under section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code (35 ILCS 200/23—20 (West 2006)).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Enter Judgment Interest Orders in SBC and Newcastle Cases**

The threshold issue in the SBC and Newcastle cases is whether the trial court possessed the jurisdiction to enter the August 20, 2007, orders awarding judgment interest. The record establishes that the collector filed notices of appeal on August 13, 2007, challenging the April 17, 2007, orders regarding the underlying interest rate. The trial court entered the judgment interest orders seven days later, on August 20, 2007.

The prosecution argues that the trial court retained jurisdiction to "implement the judgment" and "preserve the status quo." However, this argument misapprehends the scope of a trial court's authority post-appeal. It is a well-settled principle that the filing of a notice of appeal divests the trial court of jurisdiction over those aspects of the case involved in the appeal. *Sears v. Sears*, 85 Ill. 2d 319, 326 (1981). The trial court's authority is strictly limited to matters necessary to preserve the status quo or to correct clerical errors.

The August 20 orders did not merely preserve the status quo; they created a new substantive remedy—judgment interest—that had not been previously awarded. Because the collector had already filed her notice of appeal, the trial court lacked the authority to alter the rights of the parties by imposing this new monetary obligation. The prosecution's assertion that the collector should have amended her notice of appeal to challenge a non-existent order is procedurally untenable. A party cannot appeal an order before it is entered. Consequently, the trial court's entry of the judgment interest orders was an act in excess of its jurisdiction, and those awards must be vacated.

**B. Section 23—20 Is a Specific Statutory Scheme That Precludes the General Application of Section 2—1303**

Turning to the substantive issue applicable to all cases, we must determine whether section 2—1303 applies to interest owed under section 23—20. The prosecution contends that section 2—1303 fills a "gap" left by section 23—20 regarding delays caused by appeals. We disagree.

Where a specific statute governs a particular subject, it prevails over a general statute covering the same subject. *Illinois Graphics Co. v. Nickum*, 163 Ill. 2d 358, 368 (1994). Section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code establishes a comprehensive and specific mechanism for calculating interest on property tax refunds. The legislature's amendment of this section to modify the interest rate demonstrates its intent to carefully control the compensation scheme for taxpayers.

The prosecution argues that section 23—20 addresses the "origin" of the debt while section 2—1303 addresses the "delay" in payment. This distinction is artificial. Section 23—20 provides for interest on tax refunds "from the date of payment until the date of refund." This language encompasses the entire duration of the government's holding of the funds, including any delay resulting from appeals. The legislature's silence on the specific issue of judgment interest during a stay does not create a gap to be filled by general law; rather, it reflects the legislature's determination that the statutory interest rate constitutes full compensation. As this Court held in *People v. Maple*, 216 Ill. 2d 154, 164 (2005), "a court may not add provisions that the legislature did not enact." Allowing judgment interest under the general provisions of section 2—1303 would effectively add a layer of compensation the legislature did not include in the specific scheme of section 23—20.

**C. The Interest Was Not "Due and Payable" and Therefore Not Subject to Judgment Interest**

Section 2—1303 provides for judgment interest on money "found to be due." The prosecution argues that the interest was a "liquidated sum" and that the stay of execution did not alter its status as "due and payable." We find this argument unpersuasive.

The trial court's orders explicitly stayed payment of the disputed 5% interest pending appeal. In *Seal v. City of Chicago*, 146 Ill. App. 3d 61, 64 (1986), the court recognized that judgment interest is not allowed on amounts which are not yet due and payable. A stayed obligation is, by definition, not due and payable during the pendency of the stay. The prosecution's contention that a stay merely suspends the duty to pay but does not alter the judgment's nature ignores the practical reality that the collector was legally prohibited from satisfying the debt.

Furthermore, the amount was not a "final, definitive determination of a liquidated sum" as required by *In re Estate of Nemeth*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 1034, 1043 (2002). The calculation of the 5% interest was the subject of the ongoing appeal and remained contingent upon the appellate court's resolution of the retroactivity issue. A sum that is subject to reversal or modification cannot be considered "final" for the purposes of accruing judgment interest.

**D. Awarding Judgment Interest Constitutes an Unwarranted Double Recovery**

Finally, we reject the prosecution's attempt to distinguish the "double recovery" concern by separating the delay into two distinct periods. The prosecution argues that statutory interest compensates for the government's holding of the funds, while judgment interest compensates for the appeal-induced delay. This distinction is illusory.

Section 23—20 provides for interest on tax refunds from the date of payment until the date of the refund. This statutory period encompasses the entire duration of the government's use of the taxpayer's money, including any delay caused by appeals. In *Bank of America v. 1500 Lake Shore Drive Condominium Ass'n*, 2014 IL App (1st) 121870, ¶ 47, the court recognized that interest serves to compensate for the loss of use of money and warned against double recovery. The legislature determined that the statutory interest rate constitutes full compensation for this loss. The prosecution's theory would allow taxpayers to recover more than the legislature intended simply because the collector exercised her right to appeal. This would create a windfall for taxpayers and undermine the legislative balance struck in section 23—20.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the trial court lacked jurisdiction to enter the judgment interest orders in the SBC and Newcastle cases. Furthermore, we find that section 2—1303 does not apply to the specific statutory interest scheme established by section 23—20. The interest owed was not "due and payable" pending the resolution of the appeal, and awarding judgment interest would constitute an unauthorized double recovery. The judgment of the appellate court is therefore reversed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
