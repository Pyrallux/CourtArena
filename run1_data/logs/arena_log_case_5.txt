COURT ARENA LOG - Case 5
FACTS:
The State of Hawaii appeals a circuit court order granting a motion to suppress evidence filed by defendant David A. Haili before trial. Defendant was charged with possession of prohibited firearm in violation of Hawaii Revised Statutes § 134-8 (1976).

At approximately 10:30 p.m. on May 5, 1979, a security guard posted at the Kapaa Quarry  immediately reported the incident to the police . The guard furnished no other information.

A police bulletin of the incident was broadcasted. Within minutes, Officer Charles Davis was at the designated portion of the freeway and stood with his rifle at the chain link fence which serves to barricade the uncompleted end of the freeway. A few minutes later, Officer Davis observed a car travelling at a reasonable speed on the uncompleted freeway approaching with headlights on. He stopped the car. From a distance of sixty feet, he ordered the four occupants out of the car. Each immediately complied except Defendant Haili, who was leaning over and watching the officer. The officer raised his rifle and repeated his command. This time defendant obeyed, after causing a delay of five seconds.

At this point, additional officers arrived and they conducted a “pat down” of the four occupants. The four then sat on the ground and were guarded by one officer. The other officers scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight and spotted a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. They also saw a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat. Upon pushing the front seat forward, the officers saw a ukulele case, unlatched although the cover was down. Officer Davis opened the case and found a modified rifle and plastic boxes containing .22-caliber rifle ammunition.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF THE STATE OF HAWAII

### State of Hawaii, Appellant, v. David A. Haili, Appellee

---

**Introduction**

The Defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The State of Hawaii's appeal rests upon a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and a misapplication of constitutional principles. The officers' conduct in this case exceeded the bounds of the Fourth Amendment at multiple stages: the initial stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion, the warrantless scan of the vehicle's interior constituted an unlawful search, and the subsequent opening of the ukulele case was the fruit of these constitutional violations. The circuit court correctly applied established precedent in suppressing the evidence, and this Court should affirm that ruling.

---

**I. The Initial Stop of the Vehicle Was Not Supported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The State contends that the stop of the defendant's vehicle was lawful because it was based upon a police bulletin responding to a reported incident at the Kapaa Quarry. This argument fails because the facts establish that the police bulletin was issued based on virtually no information at all.

The record reflects that the security guard "furnished no other information" beyond reporting "the incident" to the police. The guard did not describe any suspect, any vehicle, any license plate number, or any specific criminal activity. The guard merely reported that "an incident" had occurred at the Kapaa Quarry. This barebones report, without more, cannot support a reasonable suspicion that any particular vehicle—let alone the defendant's vehicle—was involved in criminal activity.

As the United States Supreme Court held in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), a brief investigatory stop is permissible only "if the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot." The Court emphasized that the police officer "must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Id.* at 21. Here, Officer Davis had no such specific and articulable facts. He was responding to a bulletin that contained no description of any suspect or vehicle. The mere fact that a police bulletin was broadcast does not, in itself, create reasonable suspicion. The officer must have some factual basis connecting the particular person or vehicle stopped to the reported criminal activity. *See Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000) (holding that an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability cannot justify a Terry stop).

The State attempts to bolster its position by noting that the vehicle was traveling on an uncompleted segment of the freeway at 10:30 p.m. However, the fact that a vehicle is on an uncompleted freeway—even at night—does not, without more, give rise to reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. Members of the public may have legitimate reasons for being on uncompleted roadways, and the location alone cannot transform a vague bulletin into reasonable suspicion. The circuit court correctly recognized that the stop was not supported by the requisite level of suspicion.

---

**II. The Warrantless Scan of the Vehicle's Interior Constituted an Unlawful Search**

The State's primary argument is that Officer Davis's use of a flashlight to scan the interior of the vehicle was permissible because, under *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986), a flashlight illumination of a vehicle's interior from outside does not constitute a Fourth Amendment search. The Defense submits that this argument misapplies *New York v. Class* and ignores critical distinctions in the factual circumstances.

In *New York v. Class*, the Court held that an officer's observation of the interior of a vehicle from outside—while the officer was lawfully present—did not constitute a search when the officer was merely looking through the windshield to identify the vehicle's vehicle identification number (VIN). The Court emphasized that the VIN was located in a place "that was exposed to public view" and that the officer "did not have to remove any covering or enter the vehicle to obtain the VIN." *Id.* at 115. The Court reasoned that the defendant "could not have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the VIN, which is exposed to plain view from the outside of the vehicle." *Id.*

The instant case is distinguishable. Here, Officer Davis was not merely observing something exposed to public view from outside the vehicle. Rather, he used a flashlight to scan the interior of the vehicle—looking past the front seat, into the rear floor area—to conduct what was essentially a exploratory search of the vehicle's contents. The .22-caliber rifle casing was not visible from outside the vehicle under ordinary circumstances; it was only revealed because the officer used a flashlight to peer into the vehicle's interior and specifically scanned the floor area. This goes beyond the mere enhancement of natural vision that the *Class* Court contemplated and enters the realm of a warrantless search.

Moreover, the *Class* decision itself acknowledged that "the interior of an automobile is not per se a protected area" but emphasized that "there is a twofold basic automobile search: the vehicle itself and containers within the vehicle." *Id.* at 112 (quoting *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 157 (1925)). The critical question is whether the officer had probable cause to search. The State conflates the initial flashlight scan with the subsequent search, but the scan occurred before any probable cause had been established.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches, and that warrantless searches of vehicles are presumptively unreasonable unless they fall within a recognized exception. *See Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 454–55 (1971). The automobile exception, relied upon by the State, requires probable cause to believe that the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime. *See United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 809 (1982). At the moment Officer Davis scanned the interior with his flashlight, he had no probable cause. He had only a bare bulletin about an incident at Kapaa Quarry and the fact that the vehicle was on an uncompleted freeway. This was insufficient to justify a warrantless search.

The circuit court correctly ruled that the flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior constituted a search that required either a warrant or probable cause under the automobile exception. Neither existed at the time of the scan.

---

**III. There Was No Probable Cause to Search the Vehicle**

The State argues that probable cause existed under the totality-of-circumstances test articulated in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). The Defense submits that this argument fails because the facts known to Officer Davis at the time of the search did not establish probable cause.

Probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge... sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense." *Id.* at 238. The State points to several factors that it claims collectively established probable cause: (1) the security guard's report, (2) the vehicle's presence on the uncompleted freeway at night, (3) the defendant's five-second delay in exiting the vehicle, and (4) the discovery of the rifle casing. However, factors (1) through (3) do not give rise to probable cause, and factor (4) was discovered only *after* the unlawful scan.

The security guard's report, as noted above, contained no specific information about criminal activity. The guard did not report a crime; the guard merely reported "an incident." This is insufficient to establish probable cause. *See Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. at 271 (anonymous tip must be reliable and corroborated).

The vehicle's presence on an uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. is not inherently suspicious. The roadway, while uncompleted, was accessible, and the defendant had a right to be there unless there was some specific reason to believe he was engaged in criminal activity. The mere fact that a location is unusual does not create probable cause. *See Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979) (holding that being in an alley at night, without more, does not create reasonable suspicion).

The defendant's five-second delay in exiting the vehicle is similarly insufficient. The defendant was ordered at gunpoint by an officer pointing a rifle at him. Any reasonable person might pause momentarily when confronted with such a frightening situation. A momentary hesitation does not constitute evidence of criminal activity, let alone probable cause to believe a crime has been committed. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7–8 (1989) (requiring more than minor suspicious circumstances for probable cause).

Finally, the State's reliance on the automobile exception is misplaced. The automobile exception permits a warrantless search of a vehicle only when there is probable cause to believe the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime. *See Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 149 (1925). The State argues that the rifle casing provided probable cause. However, the casing was discovered only *after* the unlawful flashlight scan. The question is whether probable cause existed *before* the search. It did not.

The circuit court correctly held that the officers lacked probable cause to search the vehicle at the time they conducted the flashlight scan, and that the subsequent discovery of the rifle casing cannot retroactively justify an unlawful search.

---

**IV. The Opening of the Ukulele Case Was Not Supported by Probable Cause**

Even assuming arguendo that the flashlight scan was lawful, the subsequent opening of the ukulele case exceeded constitutional bounds. The State argues that the discovery of the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the vehicle. However, the rifle casing was found on the front floor, and the officers then pushed the front seat forward to access the rear floor area where the black case was located. This constitutes a search of areas and containers that were not in plain view.

The automobile exception permits a warrantless search of a vehicle and its contents when there is probable cause. *See United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 809 (1982). However, probable cause must exist to believe that the vehicle contains the specific contraband or evidence sought. Here, the officers had found a single rifle casing on the front floor. This does not establish probable cause to believe that a firearm was hidden in a ukulele case in the rear of the vehicle. The search of the ukulele case was a fishing expedition unsupported by probable cause.

Moreover, the officers opened the case without a warrant. The Supreme Court has held that the automobile exception permits the search of a vehicle but does not automatically authorize the warrantless opening of every container found within the vehicle. *See Florida v. Bizzarro*, 468 U.S. 447, 454 (1984). A warrantless container search requires probable cause to believe that the container holds contraband or evidence. *See California v. Acevedo*, 464 U.S. 672, 677 (1991). Here, there was no probable cause to believe that the ukulele case contained contraband. The mere presence of a case in a vehicle does not create probable cause. The circuit court correctly suppressed the evidence obtained from the ukulele case.

---

**V. The Pat-Down Search Was Not Justified by Terry**

The State argues that the officers' "pat down" of the four occupants was permissible under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). The Defense does not contest that a pat-down for officer safety may be permissible in some circumstances. However, the validity of the pat-down does not validate the subsequent unlawful search of the vehicle. The pat-down is a separate investigative action, and its lawfulness does not retroactively authorize a warrantless search of the vehicle that was not supported by probable cause.

Furthermore, even *Terry* requires reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 24. The officers here had no such suspicion. The four occupants had complied with the officer's commands (even the defendant, after a five-second delay), and there was no indication that any of them were armed. The pat-down was not justified by *Terry*, and any evidence obtained therefrom should also be suppressed.

---

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The officers' conduct in this case violated the Fourth Amendment at multiple stages: the initial stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion, the warrantless flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior constituted an unlawful search, and the subsequent opening of the ukulele case was not supported by probable cause. The circuit court correctly applied established constitutional principles in suppressing the evidence, and its ruling should be affirmed.

The State asks this Court to prioritize "practical realities of law enforcement" over constitutional protections. However, the Fourth Amendment exists precisely to protect individuals from the "practical realities" of overzealous law enforcement. As the Supreme Court has reminded us, "the Fourth Amendment is a fundamental constitutional guarantee, and we should not erode it." *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332, 353 (2009). The circuit court's suppression order properly balanced individual rights with law enforcement needs, and this Court should affirm it.

**Respectfully submitted,**

**Defense Counsel**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires the Court to determine whether the circuit court erred in granting the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a warrantless search of a vehicle. The State appeals the suppression order, arguing that the officers’ actions—the initial stop, the flashlight scan of the vehicle’s interior, and the subsequent search of a container within the vehicle—were constitutionally valid. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties and the applicable legal precedents, the Court concludes that the circuit court’s order must be reversed.

**II. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The Defense argues that the initial stop of the defendant’s vehicle was unlawful because the police bulletin was based on vague information from a security guard who "furnished no other information" regarding "the incident." While the bulletin itself may have lacked specificity, the stop was justified by the specific, articulable facts observed by Officer Davis at the scene.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), a police officer may briefly detain a person if the officer has "reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot." This standard is satisfied if the officer can "point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Id.*

Here, Officer Davis was positioned at the "uncompleted end of the freeway" barricaded by a chain-link fence. At 10:30 p.m., he observed a vehicle traveling on this uncompleted freeway. The presence of a vehicle on a restricted, uncompleted portion of a highway late at night constitutes specific and articulable facts supporting a reasonable suspicion of trespassing or other unauthorized activity. The Defense’s reliance on *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), is misplaced; that case involved an anonymous tip regarding an individual carrying a weapon without any corroboration. Here, the officer directly observed the vehicle in a location where it had no apparent lawful business. This observation provided an independent constitutional basis for the stop, regardless of the vagueness of the initial bulletin.

**III. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN DID NOT CONSTITUTE A SEARCH**

The Defense contends that the officers’ use of a flashlight to scan the vehicle’s interior constituted an unlawful search under the Fourth Amendment. This argument is unpersuasive in light of the Supreme Court’s holding in *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986).

In *New York v. Class*, the Supreme Court held that "the officer's observation of the interior of the vehicle from outside the vehicle, even with the aid of a flashlight, does not constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment." *Id.* at 115–16. The Court reasoned that such observations do not intrude upon any reasonable expectation of privacy because the interior of a vehicle is necessarily exposed to public view.

The facts indicate that Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene and used a flashlight to scan the interior of the car. The discovery of a ".22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor" occurred during this scan. The Defense attempts to distinguish *Class* by arguing that the officer was conducting an "exploratory search" rather than viewing something "exposed to public view." However, the rifle casing was on the floor of the vehicle, an area visible from the outside through the windows. The use of a flashlight merely enhanced the officer's natural vision; it did not constitute a physical intrusion into the vehicle. Therefore, the flashlight scan was not a search requiring a warrant or probable cause.

**IV. PROBABLE CAUSE JUSTIFIED THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE AND THE UKULELE CASE**

The discovery of the rifle casing during the lawful flashlight scan provided the officers with probable cause to search the entire vehicle, including the ukulele case found in the rear.

Under the "automobile exception" to the Fourth Amendment, established in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 149 (1925), police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband. Probable cause exists when "the facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge... are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

The sighting of a rifle casing on the floor of the car provided specific evidence of a firearm-related offense. A prudent person would reasonably believe that a rifle casing in a vehicle indicates the presence of a firearm, particularly given the suspicious circumstances of the vehicle being located on a restricted freeway at night. This established probable cause to search the vehicle for the weapon.

Furthermore, once probable cause exists to search a vehicle, it extends to any containers within the vehicle that could conceal the object of the search. In *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 809 (1982), the Supreme Court held that "probable cause to believe that a vehicle contains contraband justifies a search of every part of the vehicle that may conceal the object of the search." This principle was clarified in *California v. Acevedo*, 464 U.S. 672, 677 (1991), where the Court stated that if probable cause justifies the search of a vehicle, it justifies the search of any container within that vehicle that might hold the contraband.

The officers found a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat. Given the probable cause to search for a rifle based on the discovered casing, it was reasonable for the officers to search the ukulele case, as a ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle. The Defense’s argument that the casing did not provide probable cause to search the specific container is rejected under the principles of *Ross* and *Acevedo*.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion based on the vehicle's presence in a restricted area. The flashlight scan revealing the rifle casing was not a search under the Fourth Amendment. Finally, the discovery of the casing established probable cause to search the vehicle and the ukulele case found within it under the automobile exception.

Accordingly, the circuit court's order is **REVERSED**.

## II. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Factual Record

The prosecution argues that Officer Davis's observation of a vehicle on the uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. constituted specific, articulable facts justifying the stop. However, this argument ignores the fundamental principle that reasonable suspicion requires criminal activity, not merely innocent activity that might indicate a traffic violation or minor infraction.

As established in *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979), "the stop must be justified by some objective manifestation that a person has committed or is committing a crime." The prosecution has failed to identify what specific crime Officer Davis suspected was being committed when he observed a vehicle traveling on an uncompleted freeway. The mere presence of a vehicle on an uncompleted roadway suggests, at most, a traffic infraction or trespassing—neither of which justifies a Terry stop absent additional articulable facts indicating criminal activity.

The prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio* is misplaced. In *Terry*, the officers observed specific behavior—defendant Terry repeatedly walking past a store and looking into its windows—that led them to believe a burglary was about to occur. 392 U.S. at 6-7. Here, by contrast, Officer Davis observed nothing more than a vehicle traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on. This observation is indistinguishable from any other lawful driver who might have mistakenly entered a restricted area.

### B. The "Restricted Area" Argument Fails

The prosecution contends that the vehicle's presence on a "restricted, uncompleted highway segment" justified the stop. However, the facts establish only that the freeway was "uncompleted" and barricaded by a chain-link fence. There is no evidence that "No Trespassing" signs were posted, that the area was patrolled, or that unauthorized entry constituted a criminal offense rather than a mere civil violation.

In *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000), the Court emphasized that reasonable suspicion requires "particularized suspicion of wrongdoing." The prosecution has failed to establish that Officer Davis had any particularized suspicion that criminal activity—not merely a navigational error—was afoot. The vehicle was traveling at a "reasonable speed" with "headlights on," conduct entirely consistent with an innocent driver who mistakenly entered the wrong roadway.

The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *J.L.* fails because the critical deficiency here mirrors the problem in that case: the officer lacked particularized suspicion of criminal activity. Officer Davis observed a vehicle in a location where it "had no apparent lawful business"—but the absence of "apparent lawful business" is not equivalent to articulable facts indicating criminal activity.

### C. The Five-Second Delay Does Not Establish Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution's brief mentions that the defendant "delayed five seconds" before exiting the vehicle. This argument is unavailing because the delay occurred *after* the stop was already effectuated and cannot justify the initial seizure. Moreover, the defendant's compliance with the officer's commands—even after a brief hesitation—is consistent with an innocent person's reaction to being ordered at gunpoint by a police officer.

## III. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN CONSTITUTED AN UNLAWFUL SEARCH

### A. The Prosecution Misapplies *New York v. Class*

The prosecution invokes *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986), for the proposition that a flashlight scan of a vehicle's interior does not constitute a search. However, the prosecution misreads that holding and ignores critical distinctions in the factual circumstances.

In *Class*, the officer shined a flashlight through the windshield to read a vehicle identification number (VIN) that was partially obscured by a dashboard obstruction. 475 U.S. at 108-09. The Court emphasized that the VIN was "in plain view" and that the officer's action was "minimally intrusive." Id. at 115. The *Class* Court explicitly noted that its holding was limited: "We have no occasion to consider the search of a vehicle where the police have probable cause to search for contraband or evidence of a crime." Id. at 117 n.9.

Here, the officers did not simply peer through the windshield to observe something in plain view. Instead, they deliberately used a flashlight to "scan the interior of the car"—an exploratory action that goes beyond the limited observation permitted by *Class*. The prosecution concedes that the officers were looking for evidence of "the incident" reported by the security guard. This investigative purpose distinguishes the flashlight scan from the VIN inspection in *Class*.

### B. The Rifle Casing Was Not in "Plain View"

The prosecution claims the rifle casing was "plainly visible on the floor" and visible "through the windshield." This characterization contradicts the facts. The officers discovered the casing only after using a flashlight to "scan the interior of the car." If the casing had been plainly visible without the flashlight, no scan would have been necessary.

The prosecution's argument that "the flashlight merely enhanced his natural vision" proves too much. In *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 361 (1967) (Harlan, J., concurring), the Court established that the Fourth Amendment protects against reasonable expectations of privacy. A vehicle's interior may be "exposed to public view" in the daytime under optimal conditions, but at 10:30 p.m., the interior of a vehicle is not exposed to ordinary public observation. The use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior constitutes a search because it enables the officer to observe what would otherwise be invisible to the naked eye.

The prosecution's reliance on the "plain view" doctrine is unavailing because that doctrine applies only when "the police are lawfully in a position to observe the items" and the items are "in plain view." *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465 (1971). Here, the officers were not lawfully positioned to scan the vehicle's interior with a flashlight at the time they made their observation.

### C. The Search Exceeded the Scope of a Terry Frisk

Even assuming the initial stop was lawful, the officers' conduct exceeded the scope of a Terry frisk. Under *Terry*, an officer may conduct a pat-down for weapons only if "a reasonably prudent man in the circumstances would be warranted in the belief that his safety or that of others was in danger." 392 U.S. at 23. The officers here ordered all four occupants out of the vehicle and conducted a pat-down before scanning the interior. This sequence suggests the officers were conducting an investigative search, not a protective frisk.

The prosecution has not established that the officers had reason to believe the occupants were armed and dangerous. The defendant was "leaning over and watching the officer"—behavior consistent with curiosity, not criminal activity. The prosecution has failed to articulate specific facts indicating that a weapons frisk was justified.

## IV. THE ALLEGED DISCOVERY OF THE RIFLE CASING DID NOT ESTABLISH PROBABLE CAUSE

### A. The Flashlight Scan Was Unlawful, Making the Alleged Discovery Fruit of the Poisonous Tree

Even if the Court were to accept the prosecution's characterization of the flashlight scan (which the Defense disputes), the "discovery" of the rifle casing occurred as a direct result of an unlawful search. Under the exclusionary rule, evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search must be suppressed. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961).

The prosecution's argument that the casing provided probable cause for the subsequent search is circular. The casing was discovered during what the Defense contends was an unlawful flashlight scan. If the scan was unlawful, the casing is "fruit of the poisonous tree" and cannot provide probable cause for any subsequent search. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963).

### B. A Rifle Casing Alone Does Not Establish Probable Cause

Even if the Court were to find the flashlight scan lawful, the prosecution's probable cause argument fails on its merits. A single rifle casing—without more—does not establish probable cause to believe that a prohibited firearm is present in the vehicle.

Probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge... sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). The mere presence of a rifle casing in a vehicle does not indicate that a prohibited firearm is present. Rifle casings are commonly found at firing ranges, hunting sites, and in vehicles used for legitimate sporting purposes. The prosecution has presented no evidence connecting the casing to the defendant or to any specific criminal activity.

The prosecution relies on *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), and *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), but those cases involved probable cause based on specific evidence of contraband—not mere evidence of lawful activity. In *Ross*, the officers had received a tip from a reliable informant that the defendant was selling drugs from his vehicle. 456 U.S. at 801. Here, by contrast, the officers had no information connecting the defendant to criminal activity beyond the vague bulletin and the observation of a vehicle on an uncompleted roadway.

### C. The Search of the Ukulele Case Exceeded the Scope of the Automobile Exception

The prosecution argues that once probable cause exists to search a vehicle, it extends to all containers within the vehicle. This is a misstatement of the law.

In *California v. Acevedo*, 464 U.S. 672, 677 (1991), the Court held that "the police may search an automobile and any containers within it once they have probable cause to believe the container holds contraband." (Emphasis added). The key requirement is that probable cause must exist to believe the *specific container* holds contraband. Here, the officers had no probable cause to believe the ukulele case contained a firearm. A ukulele case is designed to hold a musical instrument, not a rifle. The fact that a modified rifle was ultimately found inside does not establish that officers had probable cause to search that particular container.

The prosecution's argument that "a ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle" proves too much. A guitar case, a briefcase, or a duffel bag could also "conceal" a rifle, but the Fourth Amendment does not permit searches based on such generalized capability. Probable cause must be particularized, not based on speculation that any container might hold contraband.

## V. THE DEFENSE CORRECTLY DISTINGUISHES THE PROSECUTION'S AUTHORITY

### A. The Prosecution's Reliance on *Ross* and *Acevedo* Is Misplaced

The prosecution cites *United States v. Ross* for the proposition that "probable cause to believe that a vehicle contains contraband justifies a search of every part of the vehicle that may conceal the object of the search." 456 U.S. at 809. However, in *Ross*, the Court emphasized that probable cause must first exist to believe the vehicle contains contraband. Here, no such probable cause existed prior to the unlawful flashlight scan.

Similarly, *California v. Acevedo* held that probable cause for a vehicle search justifies searching containers within the vehicle "that might hold the contraband." 464 U.S. at 677. The prosecution has failed to establish that the officers had probable cause to believe the ukulele case contained contraband.

### B. The Prosecution Conflates Reasonable Suspicion with Probable Cause

The prosecution attempts to bolster its argument by citing *Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989), but that case addressed reasonable suspicion for a stop, not probable cause for a search. The prosecution's brief conflates these distinct legal standards, which require different levels of justification.

Reasonable suspicion, required for a Terry stop, is a "less demanding" standard than probable cause. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325, 330 (1990). Probable cause, required for a full search, requires "facts and circumstances sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that a crime was being committed." *Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238. The prosecution has not established that the officers had probable cause to believe a crime was being committed prior to the alleged discovery of the rifle casing.

United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 361 (1967).

The Court rejects this distinction. The holding in *New York v. Class* is broad: "the officer's observation of the interior of the vehicle from outside the vehicle, even with the aid of a flashlight, does not constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment." *Id.* at 115–16. The Supreme Court explicitly stated that the Fourth Amendment does not prohibit police from observing into a vehicle from a lawful vantage point. The time of day does not alter this principle; the interior of a vehicle remains exposed to public view regardless of the ambient light conditions. The use of a flashlight merely aids the officer’s vision in a location where he is lawfully present; it does not constitute a physical intrusion.

The Defense’s argument that the scan was "exploratory" is irrelevant to the question of whether a search occurred under the Fourth Amendment. The officer was lawfully present at the vehicle following a valid *Terry* stop. Observing the interior of the car, including the floor where the rifle casing was found, falls within the "plain view" doctrine. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465 (1971). Because the officer did not open the door or physically intrude into the vehicle to see the casing, the scan was not a search.

**IV. PROBABLE CAUSE JUSTIFIED THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE AND THE UKULELE CASE**

The Defense argues that even if the scan were lawful, the discovery of a single rifle casing was insufficient to establish probable cause to search the vehicle or the ukulele case. The Defense asserts that a casing is consistent with innocent activity (e.g., hunting or target practice) and does not indicate the presence of a prohibited firearm. Furthermore, the Defense argues that *California v. Acevedo*, 464 U.S. 672 (1991), requires probable cause to believe the *specific container* holds contraband, and that a ukulele case is not a container typically associated with rifles.

The Court disagrees. Under the "automobile exception," police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 149 (1925). Probable cause is a flexible standard based on the "totality of the circumstances." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Here, the officers had probable cause based on the combination of suspicious circumstances: the vehicle was stopped in a barricaded, uncompleted freeway late at night, and a rifle casing was visible on the floor. While a casing alone might be innocent, in the context of a vehicle trespassing in a restricted area, a prudent person could reasonably believe that a firearm was present.

Regarding the search of the ukulele case, the Defense misinterprets the holding of *California v. Acevedo*. In *Acevedo*, the Supreme Court held that "the police may search an automobile and any containers within it once they have probable cause to believe the container holds contraband." *Id.* at 677. However, the Court clarified that this rule applies equally whether the probable cause attaches to the vehicle first or the container first. The Court stated, "If probable cause justifies the search of a lawfully stopped vehicle, it justifies the search of every part of the vehicle that may conceal the object of the search." *Id.* at 680 (quoting *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982) (Stevens, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)).

The Defense’s argument that probable cause must attach specifically to the ukulele case ignores the principle established in *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 820–21 (1982), that probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband authorizes the search of any container within the vehicle that could conceal the contraband. A ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle, particularly a "modified rifle" as described in the facts. The officers were not required to determine that the container definitely held a weapon; it was sufficient that the container was capable of concealing the object of the search. Therefore, the search of the ukulele case was lawful.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The initial stop was justified by reasonable suspicion based on the vehicle's presence in a restricted, barricaded area. The flashlight scan revealing the rifle casing was not a search under the Fourth Amendment. Finally, the discovery of the casing, combined with the suspicious circumstances of the stop, established probable cause to search the vehicle and the ukulele case found within it under the automobile exception.

Accordingly, the circuit court's order is **REVERSED**.

### STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the Prosecution's brief and urges this Court to sustain the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. While the Prosecution has attempted to characterize Officer Davis's conduct as constitutionally permissible, a careful examination of the Fourth Amendment principles governing searches and seizures reveals that the evidence obtained in this case must be excluded. The Defense addresses each stage of the encounter in turn.

## I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

The Prosecution contends that Officer Davis possessed reasonable suspicion to stop the Defendant's vehicle based on its presence on an "uncompleted freeway" that was "barricaded by a chain link fence." However, this argument conflates mere location with criminal activity and fails to establish the "specific and articulable facts" required by *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The Prosecution's reliance on the physical barricade is misplaced. A chain link fence marking an uncompleted roadway is not the equivalent of a "No Trespassing" sign, nor does it automatically confer authority upon police to stop any vehicle that happens to enter the area. The critical deficiency in the State's position is the complete absence of any indication that Officer Davis observed the vehicle actually crossing or breaching the barricade. The facts establish only that the vehicle was "travelling at a reasonable speed on the uncompleted freeway approaching" the officer's position. This suggests the vehicle was already on the roadway when observed—not that it had just forcibly entered through a barrier.

As this Court noted in its preliminary analysis, the location here is distinguishable from *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979), which involved a high-crime area. Yet the Prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Brown* actually underscores a critical point: the Supreme Court in *Brown* required more than mere presence in an unusual location to justify a stop. Here, the Prosecution asks this Court to hold that an uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. is so inherently suspicious that any vehicle found there may be stopped without further justification. This reasoning would eviscerate the reasonable suspicion requirement by creating a per se rule that any vehicle in an unusual location is subject to detention. Such a rule is foreclosed by *Terry* and its progeny.

The Prosecution argues that *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000), is inapposite because Officer Davis did not rely on an anonymous tip. Yet the Prosecution's argument proves too much. In *J.L.*, the Supreme Court emphasized that reasonable suspicion requires "particularized suspicion of wrongdoing." *Id.* at 271. The mere fact that a vehicle is on an uncompleted freeway does not, without more, give rise to such particularized suspicion. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with headlights on—conduct entirely consistent with lawful driving. The Prosecution has identified no specific facts indicating that the Defendant was engaged in criminal activity rather than merely taking a wrong turn or exploring the area out of curiosity. Under *Terry*, such a hunch is insufficient to justify a seizure.

The Defense therefore respectfully requests that this Court find the initial stop was unlawful for lack of reasonable suspicion.

---

## II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN CONSTITUTED AN UNLAWFUL SEARCH

The Prosecution relies heavily on *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106, 115–16 (1986), for the proposition that a flashlight scan of a vehicle's interior does not constitute a search. However, the Prosecution misapplies this precedent and ignores critical distinctions.

In *Class*, the officer used a flashlight to illuminate the vehicle identification number (VIN) located on the dashboard—a location specifically designed to be visible to persons outside the vehicle. The Supreme Court emphasized that the VIN "is not hidden from view" but is "located in a plain view on the dashboard." *Id.* at 114. The Court specifically noted that the officer "did not have to remove any covering or displace any material in order to view the VIN." *Id.* at 115.

The facts here are materially different. Officer Davis used a flashlight to scan the interior of the vehicle, illuminating areas that would not otherwise have been visible at 10:30 p.m. This was not a limited inspection of an exposed identifier but rather an exploratory scan of the vehicle's interior. The Prosecution's argument that "time of day does not alter this analysis" misreads the Fourth Amendment. The Amendment protects reasonable expectations of privacy, and the expectation of privacy in a vehicle's interior is greater when ambient lighting is reduced. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 361 (1967), the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places—and a vehicle's interior at night, when visibility is limited, implicates a heightened expectation of privacy.

The Prosecution argues that the scan was permissible under the "plain view" doctrine. However, the plain view doctrine requires that the officer be lawfully present at the location where the evidence is observed. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465 (1971). If the initial stop was unlawful—as the Defense contends—then Officer Davis was not lawfully present at the vehicle, and any observations made during the subsequent scan cannot be justified under the plain view doctrine.

Moreover, even assuming the stop was lawful, the Prosecution cannot rely on plain view when the officer actively illuminated areas that were not otherwise visible. The plain view doctrine permits observation of what is already exposed to public view; it does not authorize police to artificially enhance their vision to discover what would otherwise be hidden. The Prosecution's argument would permit police to use spotlights, flashlights, and binoculars to peer into every vehicle stopped for any reason—a result the Fourth Amendment was designed to prevent.

The Defense therefore requests that this Court find the flashlight scan constituted an unlawful search.

---

## III. THE DISCOVERY OF THE RIFLE CASING DID NOT ESTABLISH PROBABLE CAUSE

Even if this Court were to find the flashlight scan lawful, the Prosecution's claim that the rifle casing established probable cause to search the vehicle fails for multiple reasons.

First, the Prosecution's reliance on the "totality of the circumstances" under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), requires this Court to consider the context of the discovery. However, the Prosecution's argument that a rifle casing in a vehicle on an uncompleted freeway is inherently suspicious conflates mere suspicion with probable cause. A rifle casing is entirely consistent with lawful activity—target practice, hunting, or simple possession of a firearm for self-defense. The Prosecution has presented no evidence that the Defendant was engaged in unlawful activity based solely on the presence of a casing.

Second, even if the casing gave rise to some suspicion, it did not establish probable cause to believe the vehicle contained contraband. Probable cause requires a "fair probability" that contraband will be found. *Id.* at 238. The presence of a single rifle casing—no weapon, no ammunition, no other indicia of criminal activity—does not meet this standard. The Prosecution's argument that a "prudent person would reasonably believe" criminal activity was afoot ignores the requirement that probable cause be based on facts, not speculation.

Third, the Prosecution's reliance on *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 149 (1925), is misplaced. While the automobile exception permits warrantless searches when probable cause exists, the Supreme Court has consistently held that probable cause must be based on more than mere presence in a suspicious location. The Prosecution asks this Court to find that a single rifle casing, without more, establishes probable cause. This would convert the automobile exception into a generalized license to search any vehicle found in unusual circumstances—a result that contradicts the Fourth Amendment's protections.

## IV. THE SEARCH OF THE UKULELE CASE WAS UNLAWFUL

The Prosecution argues that the search of the ukulele case was justified under the automobile exception as articulated in *California v. Acevedo*, 464 U.S. 672, 677 (1991), and *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 820–21 (1982). However, this argument fails because it assumes, without proof, that probable cause existed for the vehicle in the first instance.

Even accepting the Prosecution's reading of *Ross*—that probable cause to search a vehicle justifies the search of any container within it—this principle applies only when probable cause exists for the vehicle. As argued above, the Prosecution has failed to establish probable cause. A single rifle casing, viewed in the context of lawful activity, does not create a "fair probability" that contraband is present in the vehicle.

Moreover, the Prosecution's argument that the ukulele case could conceal a rifle misses the point. The Fourth Amendment does not permit a search based on the theoretical possibility that contraband could be hidden in a container. Rather, probable cause must exist to believe that the container actually holds contraband. *Acevedo*, 464 U.S. at 677. The Prosecution has presented no facts suggesting that the ukulele case contained a rifle—it was simply a container that happened to be capable of holding one.

The Prosecution's reliance on the fact that the case was "unlatched" is unavailing. An unlatched case is not probable cause to search it. Many individuals leave cases unlatched for convenience, and an unlatched ukulele case is entirely consistent with lawful transportation of a musical instrument.

Because each stage of the encounter was unlawful—the initial stop lacked reasonable suspicion, the flashlight scan constituted an unlawful search, and the subsequent searches were not supported by probable cause—all evidence obtained as a result must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. Your Honor, the Defense has demonstrated that the Prosecution's case rests on a chain of constitutional violations. The initial stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion. **STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal from a circuit court order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The evidence consists of a modified rifle and ammunition discovered within a ukulele case in Defendant’s vehicle. The Court has carefully considered the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. While the Defense presents a compelling narrative regarding the potential for innocent explanations, the Court finds that the binding precedents governing Fourth Amendment jurisprudence support the legality of the officer's conduct. The initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion, the flashlight scan did not constitute a search, and the subsequent search of the vehicle and its containers was justified by probable cause. Accordingly, the circuit court’s order is reversed.

**II. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The Defense argues that the initial stop was unlawful because Officer Davis lacked the "specific and articulable facts" required by *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The Defense contends that the vehicle’s presence on the uncompleted freeway, driving at a reasonable speed with headlights on, was consistent with innocent conduct, such as a wrong turn or curiosity. The Defense further asserts that because Officer Davis did not personally observe the vehicle crossing the chain-link fence, the stop was based on mere location.

The Court finds the Defense’s argument unpersuasive. Reasonable suspicion is a low standard, requiring only "particularized suspicion of wrongdoing" rather than proof of a crime. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000). Here, Officer Davis was stationed at the "uncompleted end of the freeway" which was "barricaded by a chain link fence." The facts state that the vehicle was observed "travelling... on the uncompleted freeway." A vehicle operating on a roadway that is physically barricaded and uncompleted at 10:30 p.m. is inherently suspicious. Unlike the public alley in *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 52 (1979), this location was a restricted area where vehicles had no apparent lawful business. The physical barricade serves as objective notice that entry is unauthorized.

The Defense’s suggestion that the vehicle might have been "lost" or "exploring" does not negate the reasonable suspicion that the driver was committing trespassing. While innocent explanations are possible, the officer was not required to rule them out before initiating a brief investigatory stop. *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 274 (2002). The totality of the circumstances—the late hour, the barricaded location, and the presence of the vehicle—provided Officer Davis with the specific, articulable basis necessary for a *Terry* stop.

**III. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN DID NOT CONSTITUTE A SEARCH**

The Defense contends that the flashlight scan constituted an unlawful search, distinguishing *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986), on the grounds that the scan in this case was "exploratory" rather than the limited VIN inspection in *Class*. The Defense argues that the use of a flashlight at 10:30 p.m. intruded upon the Defendant’s reasonable expectation of privacy under *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 361 (1967).

The Court rejects this distinction. The holding in *New York v. Class* is broad and directly applicable: "the officer's observation of the interior of the vehicle from outside the vehicle, even with the aid of a flashlight, does not constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment." *Id.* at 115–16. The Supreme Court explicitly stated that the Fourth Amendment does not prohibit police from observing into a vehicle from a lawful vantage point. The Court further noted that "the distinction between 'looking' at an object and 'looking for' an object is without substance." *Id.* at 114. Therefore, the Defense’s argument that the scan was "exploratory" is irrelevant to the question of whether a search occurred.

The time of day does not alter this principle; the interior of a vehicle remains exposed to public view regardless of the ambient light conditions. The use of a flashlight merely aids the officer’s vision in a location where he is lawfully present; it does not constitute a physical intrusion. Observing the interior of the car, including the floor where the rifle casing was found, falls within the "plain view" doctrine. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465 (1971). Because the officer did not open the door or physically intrude into the vehicle to see the casing, the scan was not a search.

**IV. PROBABLE CAUSE JUSTIFIED THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE AND THE UKULELE CASE**

The Defense argues that the discovery of a single rifle casing was insufficient to establish probable cause to search the vehicle or the ukulele case. The Defense asserts that a casing is consistent with innocent activity (e.g., hunting or target practice) and does not indicate the presence of a prohibited firearm. Furthermore, the Defense argues that *California v. Acevedo*, 464 U.S. 672 (1991), requires probable cause to believe the *specific container* holds contraband, and that a ukulele case is not a container typically associated with rifles.

The Court disagrees. Under the "automobile exception," police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 149 (1925). Probable cause is a flexible standard based on the "totality of the circumstances." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Here, the officers had probable cause based on the combination of suspicious circumstances: the vehicle was stopped in a barricaded, uncompleted freeway late at night, and a rifle casing was visible on the floor. While a casing alone might be innocent, in the context of a vehicle trespassing in a restricted area, a prudent person could reasonably believe that a firearm was present. The presence of ammunition casing suggests the presence of a weapon, which is particularly concerning given the unauthorized location of the vehicle.

Regarding the search of the ukulele case, the Defense misinterprets the holding of *California v. Acevedo*. In *Acevedo*, the Supreme Court held that "the police may search an automobile and any containers within it once they have probable cause to believe the container holds contraband." *Id.* at 677. However, the Court clarified that this rule applies equally whether the probable cause attaches to the vehicle first or the container first. The Court stated, "If probable cause justifies the search of a lawfully stopped vehicle, it justifies the search of every part of the vehicle that may conceal the object of the search." *Id.* at 680 (quoting *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982) (Stevens, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part)).

The Defense’s argument that probable cause must attach specifically to the ukulele case ignores the principle established in *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 820–21 (1982), that probable cause to search a vehicle for contraband authorizes the search of any container within the vehicle that could conceal the contraband. A ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle, particularly a "modified rifle" as described in the facts. The officers were not required to determine that the container definitely held a weapon; it was sufficient that the container was capable of concealing the object of the search. Therefore, the search of the ukulele case was lawful.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The initial stop was justified by reasonable suspicion based on the vehicle's presence in a restricted, barricaded area. The flashlight scan revealing the rifle casing was not a search under the Fourth Amendment. Finally, the discovery of the casing, combined with the suspicious circumstances of the stop, established probable cause to search the vehicle and the ukulele case found within it under the automobile exception.

Accordingly, the circuit court's order is **REVERSED**.
