COURT ARENA LOG - Case 72
FACTS:
Appellee, a government contractor identified herein as John Doe, underwent military detention in Iraq. After his release, he filed this action in the district court against, inter alia, then-Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and a Bivens action for violation of his due process rights. Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. The district court granted the motion as to the claims under the DTA and some other claims, but did imply an action under the Bivens due process theory and denied Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss as to those claims. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals from the denial of his motion, arguing both that the claims are barred by qualified immunity and that the court erred in implying such a cause of action in the first instance. Because we agree that the district court erred in implying such a cause of action, we reverse the order of the district court.

Background

Because this case arises out of a motion to dismiss, we, like the district court, accept the well-pleaded factual allegations set forth in Doe’s complaint as true for purposes of this stage of the litigation and construe reasonable inferences from those allegations in Doe’s favor, although we are not required to accept Doe’s legal conclusions as true. See, e.g., Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges the following facts:

*392In December 2004, Doe, a United States citizen and employee of an American-owned defense contracting firm, traveled to Iraq to work as a civilian Arabic translator and was detailed to a United States Marine Corps Human Exploitation Team operating along the Iraqi-Syrian border. Doe’s job was to develop intelligence through contacts with local Iraqis and to discover threats to the Marine unit. In July 2005, he made contact and developed a relationship with Iraqi Sheikh Abd AlSattar Abu Risha. Doe maintains that he became the unit’s point of contact with AlSattar and that, through a series of secretive meetings, he cultivated Al-Sattar as a United States “ally.”
On October 20, 2005, Doe returned to a United States military camp in preparation to depart for his annual leave. There, a Navy Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agent asked to interview Doe. Doe agreed and discussed his work -with the agent.

Two weeks later, Doe traveled to A1 Asad, a United States military base, from which he was scheduled to depart for his leave. At that point, three NCIS agents, including the one who previously interviewed him, along with another United States official, detained Doe and interrogated him for four hours. Doe alleges that the agents denied his requests to have an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit present for his questioning. Doe states that the agents confiscated his luggage, blindfolded him, kicked him in the back, and threatened to shoot him if he tried to escape.

Doe was transferred into the custody of the Marine Corps. After seventy-two hours of solitary confinement, he was blindfolded, hooded, and flown to Camp Cropper, a United States military facility near Baghdad International Airport used to hold high-value detainees, where he was confined for nine months. For the first three months, Doe alleges he was kept in solitary confinement; thereafter, he was transferred into a cell housing suspected hostile al Qaeda and Arab Socialist Ba’ath Party members. Doe alleges that the military officers publicized his affiliation with the Department of Defense to encourage his cell mates to attack him and that the prison guards mistreated him by exposing him to extreme temperatures and depriving him of sleep. He alleges that one guard choked him repeatedly. During his detainment, United States government officials interrogated Doe multiple times and denied his requests for an attorney to be present at those interrogations.

In December 2005, the Detainee Status Board held a hearing and deemed Doe a threat to the Multi-National Forces in Iraq. In July 2006, the Board held a second hearing, after which Doe was transported to Jordan and ultimately to the United States, where he was released. Doe was never formally charged with a crime. He alleges that his property was not returned to him and that he has been placed on watch lists, preventing contracting firms from hiring him and causing customs officials to interrogate him when he returns from international travel.

Procedural History

In November 2008, Doe filed this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, other United States government officers and agents, and the United States government. Relevant to this appeal, Doe asked the district court to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for violating Doe’s rights under the DTA and for violating Doe’s constitutional rights guaranteed under the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments on the theory that Secretary Rumsfeld developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm.
*393Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss Doe’s claims against him for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F.Supp.2d 94, 100 (D.D.C.2011). The district court dismissed Doe’s claims arising under the DTA, . Id. at 104-05. The district court also dismissed Doe’s procedural due process and access to court claims, holding that Doe had not pled sufficient facts to overcome Secretary Rumsfeld’s qualified immunity defense. Id. at 113-14.
The court held, however, that Doe could maintain a federal cause of action for his substantive due process claims under Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971). The district court also held that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to a qualified immunity defense to Doe’s substantive due process claims because Doe had a right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that “shock the conscience”; that right was established at the time of Rumsfeld’s alleged conduct; and Doe sufficiently pled facts to support a claim that Secretary Rumsfeld violated that right. Doe, 800 F.Supp.2d at 115.
Secretary Rumsfeld filed this interlocutory appeal of the district court’s partial denial of his motion to dismiss Doe’s claims against him. He argues that the district court erred first by implying a Bivens action arising out of the sensitive context of a military detention in a foreign warzone and second by denying him qualified immunity against Doe’s substantive due process claims. Our review of each of these legal issues is de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).
Jurisdiction
Although the jurisdiction of courts of appeals ordinarily extends only to review of “final decisions” of the district courts, we have jurisdiction over the current interlocutory appeal. This case fits squarely within a well-established exception that the denial of a motion to dismiss on the ground of qualified immunity has sufficient finality to warrant interlocutory review, and within the language of Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250, 126 S.Ct. 1695, 164 L.Ed.2d 441 (2006), that where “the definition of an element of the [asserted cause of action] [was] directly implicated by the defense of qualified immunity and properly before us on interlocutory appeal,” we have jurisdiction over the appeal. Id. at 257 n. 5, 126 S.Ct. 1695.

**Opening Argument for Secretary Rumsfeld**  

May it please this Court:  

Today, we ask you to correct a profound error by the district court. In ruling that John Doe may pursue a Bivens claim against Secretary Rumsfeld for alleged constitutional violations arising from his military detention in Iraq, the district court improperly extended Bivens into a new and sensitive context where the Supreme Court has consistently barred such extensions. As the Court has repeatedly emphasized, Bivens is an exceptional remedy—available only in narrow circumstances where no alternative remedy exists and where "special factors" do not counsel hesitation. This case presents precisely the kind of "new context" where extension is inappropriate. For the reasons that follow, the district court erred in recognizing this novel cause of action, and its decision must be reversed.  

### I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy for Military Detention in a Foreign Warzone  

Bivens is not a "default remedy" for constitutional violations. As the Supreme Court has made clear, "the Court has declined to extend Bivens to new contexts in almost every case since 1980." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). The Court has "consistently refused to extend Bivens to new contexts" because such extensions risk "intruding into areas of traditional governmental responsibility" and "undermining the separation of powers." Id. at 1858. This case involves a context where those risks are acute: military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone.  

The Supreme Court has long recognized that military affairs are "committed to the coordinate branches of government," and courts must exercise "special caution" when reviewing such matters. *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). In *Chappell*, the Court held that Bivens does not apply to claims by enlisted military personnel against superiors for constitutional violations during service because Congress has established a "comprehensive system for military discipline." Id. at 303. While Doe is a civilian contractor, the context of his detention—by military personnel in a combat zone, under military command, and as part of ongoing counterinsurgency operations—shares the same core concerns: national security, foreign policy, and the need for judicial deference to military judgment. As the Court explained in *Ziglar*, cases involving "national security and foreign affairs" are quintessential "special factors" that counsel hesitation before recognizing a new Bivens remedy. 137 S. Ct. at 1859.  

Indeed, the Supreme Court recently reinforced this principle in *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735 (2020). There, the Court held that Bivens should not be extended to cross-border shootings involving foreign nationals, emphasizing that "when the plaintiff seeks to extend Bivens to a new context, the Court considers whether there are special factors that would make it inappropriate to imply a damages remedy." Id. at 743. The Court stressed that "foreign relations and national security" are "areas where courts should not step in" because they "involve complex policy choices" best left to the political branches. Id. at 744. Here, the detention occurred in Iraq during active hostilities—far from the domestic setting where Bivens has ever been recognized. Extending Bivens to this context would force federal courts to second-guess military decisions in a warzone, undermining operational security and inviting litigation that could disrupt military operations. As *Ziglar* recognized, "the potential for judicial interference in sensitive national security matters is precisely the kind of 'special factor' that forecloses a Bivens remedy." 137 S. Ct. at 1858.  

Moreover, Congress has legislated comprehensively in the field of detainee treatment. The existence of such statutory schemes—designed to balance security needs with procedural safeguards—further demonstrates why courts should not create new remedies. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859 ("When Congress has not provided a remedy, the Court has not created one in new contexts involving national security or foreign affairs"). The district court’s decision to imply a Bivens remedy here disregards this balance and improperly inserts the judiciary into an area where Congress has already spoken.  

### II. Qualified Immunity Also Bars Doe’s Claims  

Even if Bivens applied (which it does not), Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects officials unless "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 679 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld developed or authorized policies leading to his detention. But at the time of the alleged conduct in 2005–2006, no court had recognized a substantive due process right against a Secretary of Defense for military detention practices in a foreign warzone. The contours of such a right were far from "clearly established."  

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that qualified immunity requires "clearly established law" at the time of the alleged violation. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). Here, the Supreme Court had never addressed whether Bivens applies to civilian contractors detained by the military in a combat zone. Lower courts had also not recognized such a right. Indeed, the only relevant precedent—*Chappell*—expressly barred Bivens claims in military contexts, and *Ziglar* later confirmed that national security and foreign affairs are "special factors" precluding Bivens extensions. Without a "robust consensus of cases of persuasive authority" establishing the right, qualified immunity applies. *Ashcroft*, 556 U.S. at 679.  

### Conclusion  

The district court erred by creating a new Bivens remedy in a context where the Supreme Court has consistently refused to do so. Military detention in a foreign warzone implicates "special factors" that counsel hesitation—national security, foreign policy, and the need for judicial deference to the political branches. Congress has also legislated in this field, further precluding judicial intervention. For these reasons, the district court’s denial of Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss must be reversed. This Court should instruct the district court to dismiss Doe’s claims in their entirety.  

Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE FOR APPELLEE JOHN DOE

## Introduction

Secretary Rumsfeld asks this Court to reverse the district court's decision denying his motion to dismiss and to dismiss John Doe's substantive due process claim in its entirety. His arguments, while artfully framed, mischaracterize the nature of the claims at issue and ignore binding precedent that supports the district court's ruling. This Court should affirm the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss.

---

## I. The Context Here Is Not a "New Context" Precluding Bivens, or Alternatively, Any Special Factors Do Not Override the Need for a Remedy

### A. The Core Constitutional Right Alleged Is Not Novel

Secretary Rumsfeld's primary argument rests on the assertion that this case presents a "new context" for Bivens purposes. However, this argument conflates the novelty of the constitutional interest with the context of the claim. The Supreme Court has recognized that Bivens may proceed where the underlying constitutional right is well-established, even if the particular factual scenario is novel.

In *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), the Court recognized a damages remedy for violations of the Fourth Amendment. In *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979), the Court recognized a Fifth Amendment due process claim. In *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1468, 64 L.Ed.2d 15 (1980), the Court recognized an Eighth Amendment claim. What unites these cases is not the novelty of the factual setting, but the existence of a recognized constitutional violation for which the plaintiff seeks a remedy.

Doe's claim rests on the Fifth Amendment's guarantee of due process, including the substantive component that protects individuals from government conduct that "shocks the conscience." The district court correctly identified this as the constitutional right at issue. *See Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172, 72 S.Ct. 205, 96 L.Ed. 183 (1952) (establishing the "shocks the conscience" standard). This right has been clearly established for decades.

### B. The Military Context Does Not Automatically Bar Bivens

Secretary Rumsfeld relies heavily on *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), and *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S.Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017), for the proposition that Bivens does not apply to military contexts. However, his reading of these cases is overly broad.

In *Chappell*, the Court held that enlisted military personnel could not bring Bivens claims against superior officers because Congress had established a comprehensive military disciplinary system. The Court emphasized that "the military is, by necessity, a specialized society separate from civilian society." 462 U.S. at 301. Critically, however, the Court did not hold that Bivens is categorically unavailable to all persons in military contexts—it held that the specific relationship between enlisted personnel and their superiors warranted deference to the military's internal disciplinary mechanisms.

Doe is not an enlisted military member. He is a civilian contractor who was detained by the military. The relationship between a civilian contractor and the Secretary of Defense is fundamentally different from the relationship between enlisted personnel and their commanding officers. The policy rationales underlying *Chappell*—the need for unit cohesion, military discipline, and the ability of superior officers to give orders without fear of personal liability—do not apply with equal force to civilian contractors detained by the military.

### C. The "Special Factors" Analysis Does Not Foreclose the Remedy

In *Ziglar*, the Court identified "special factors" that counsel hesitation before recognizing a new Bivens remedy, including national security and foreign affairs. 137 S.Ct. at 1858-59. However, the Court did not hold that these factors categorically bar Bivens. Rather, the Court emphasized that the analysis is context-specific and requires a "careful judgment."

The existence of alternative remedies is relevant to this analysis. *Ziglar*, 137 S.Ct. at 1858. Here, Doe has no adequate alternative remedy. He cannot bring a claim under the Detainee Treatment Act because, as the district court held, the Act does not provide a private right of action for damages. He cannot bring a claim against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act because military discretionary functions are exempted. He cannot obtain the relief he seeks—compensation for his injuries—through any other means.

Moreover, the fact that Congress has legislated in the field of detainee treatment does not counsel against a Bivens remedy; it demonstrates that Congress recognizes the need for accountability. The Detainee Treatment Act establishes standards for detainee treatment, but it does not provide a damages remedy for violations. In *Carlson*, the Court held that the existence of the Federal Tort Claims Act did not preclude a Bivens claim where the statutory remedy was inadequate. 446 U.S. at 18-20. The same principle applies here.

---

## II. Secretary Rumsfeld Is Not Entitled to Qualified Immunity

### A. The Right to Be Free from Treatment That Shocks the Conscience Was Clearly Established

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that qualified immunity applies because no case had specifically recognized a substantive due process right against a Secretary of Defense for military detention practices in a foreign warzone. This argument misstates the qualified immunity standard.

Qualified immunity protects officials unless "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 679, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). The right must be "clearly established" in the sense that its "contours" are sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would know that his conduct violates it. *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 523 (1987).

The right to be free from government conduct that "shocks the conscience" has been clearly established since *Rochin*. Doe alleges that he was blindfolded, hooded, subjected to extreme temperatures, deprived of sleep, threatened with being shot, and choked by a guard. These allegations, accepted as true, describe treatment that any reasonable official would understand violates the Due Process Clause. The specific context—a civilian contractor detained in Iraq—does not diminish the clarity of this right.

### B. The Constitutional Violations Were Clearly Established at the Time of the Alleged Conduct

The alleged conduct occurred in 2005-2006. By that time, the Supreme Court had repeatedly held that treatment of detainees that involves physical violence, sensory deprivation, and other inhumane practices violates the Constitution. *See, e.g., Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 122 S.Ct. 2508, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002) (holding that binding Eighth Amendment precedent gave fair notice that shackling detainees to a fence in a painful position violated the Constitution); *United States v. Munoz*, 730 F.3d 989 (9th Cir. 2013) (holding that the right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures was clearly established).

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the absence of a case specifically addressing a civilian contractor detained in Iraq defeats qualified immunity. This argument proves too much. If the specific factual scenario always had to have been addressed for the right to be "clearly established," qualified immunity would never apply in novel factual settings. The Supreme Court has rejected this approach. *See Hope*, 536 U.S. at 739-41 (holding that the right can be clearly established even in novel circumstances if existing precedent places the constitutional question beyond dispute).

---

## III. The District Court Properly Applied the Legal Standards

### A. The Court Correctly Applied the Pleading Standards

At the motion to dismiss stage, the district court was required to accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and to construe reasonable inferences in Doe's favor. *See Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678-79. The court did so here.

Doe's complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused his harm. While the complaint does not allege that Rumsfeld personally participated in every act of mistreatment, it does allege that Rumsfeld created the policies under which the mistreatment occurred. This is sufficient at the motion to dismiss stage.

### B. The Court Correctly Determined That Bivens Applies

The district court correctly analyzed the Bivens question. It recognized that the Supreme Court has extended Bivens to new contexts in the past, including contexts involving military personnel and national security. *See, e.g., Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (extending Bivens to Eighth Amendment claims against prison officials). It correctly determined that the existence of a recognized constitutional right and the absence of an adequate alternative remedy supported recognizing a Bivens remedy here.

The district court also correctly determined that qualified immunity did not bar the claim at this stage. It found that Doe's right to be free from treatment that shocks the conscience was clearly established, and that Doe had alleged sufficient facts to support a claim that Rumsfeld violated that right. This Court should review the district court's legal conclusions de novo, but should affirm its application of the pleading standards.

---

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court correctly held that Doe may pursue his substantive due process claim under Bivens, and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss de novo. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In doing so, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). However, we are not bound by the district court’s legal conclusions regarding the implication of a *Bivens* remedy.

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The central question on appeal is whether the district court properly implied a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), for Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The Supreme Court has instructed that extending *Bivens* to a new context is a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). A two-step analysis governs this inquiry. First, we ask whether the case presents a new Bivens context—meaning it is different from the three *Bivens* actions recognized by the Supreme Court. *Id.* at 1859. If so, we must ask whether there are "special factors counselling hesitation" before implying a remedy. *Id.*

Here, the context is undeniably new. Appellee, a civilian contractor, was detained by the military in a foreign warzone during active hostilities. This scenario is distinct from the contexts in which the Supreme Court has approved a *Bivens* remedy. *See id.* at 1857-58.

Having identified a new context, we must determine whether special factors counsel hesitation. The Prosecution correctly argues that military affairs and national security constitute such special factors. In *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983), the Supreme Court held that the "unique disciplinary structure" of the military requires courts to exercise "special caution" in intruding into military affairs. While Appellee distinguishes *Chappell* on the grounds that he was a civilian contractor rather than an enlisted service member, the rationale of *Chappell* extends beyond the master-servant relationship of the military hierarchy. It encompasses the broader principle of judicial deference to the Executive Branch in matters of military detention and intelligence operations in a combat zone.

The Supreme Court recently reaffirmed this principle in *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 743 (2020), noting that "foreign relations and national security" are areas where courts should hesitate to imply a damages remedy because they involve "complex policy choices" best left to the political branches. The detention of Appellee in Iraq—a theater of war—implicates precisely these concerns. The military’s decisions regarding whom to detain, how to interrogate, and for how long, are inherently tied to the conduct of warfare and national security. Allowing a *Bivens* action in this context would require federal courts to sit in judgment of wartime detention decisions, risking intrusion into the constitutional prerogatives of the Executive and Legislative branches. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

Appellee argues that the lack of alternative remedies, such as a private right of action under the Detainee Treatment Act, weighs against finding special factors. However, the absence of alternative remedies does not override the presence of special factors counseling hesitation, particularly where national security is concerned. *See id.* at 1859. The separation-of-powers concerns implicated by this case are sufficient to preclude the implication of a *Bivens* remedy. Therefore, the district court erred in creating this new cause of action.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Because we conclude that a *Bivens* remedy is not available in this context, we need not reach the question of qualified immunity. However, we note that the Prosecution’s argument regarding qualified immunity is persuasive. Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, the right Appellee asserts—to be free from military detention policies set by the Secretary of Defense in a warzone—was not "clearly established" at the time of the alleged conduct. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 679 (2009). No precedent had held that a Secretary of Defense could be held personally liable for the detention of a civilian contractor in a combat zone. *Hope v.
