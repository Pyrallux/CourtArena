Your Honor, the Fourth Amendment prohibits warrantless searches and seizures absent reasonable suspicion or probable cause. Here, the officer conducted a pat-down of the defendant’s person without reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous, a clear violation of *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and misapplication of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. The evidence demonstrates that the officer's conduct was lawful at every stage of the encounter, and the search was conducted pursuant to a valid consent. Accordingly, this Court should deny the suppression motion and uphold the denial of the suppression motion by the trial court.

## I. THE INITIAL ENCOUNTER DID NOT CONSTITUTE A SEIZURE

The prosecution contends that the officer effectuated an unlawful seizure when he activated his lights. This argument fails because no seizure occurred until the defendant voluntarily pulled over.

The facts establish that the defendant "immediately pulled over to the right and stopped on the shoulder of the road" on his own initiative, *before* any police contact. The officer testified unequivocally that "regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." This Court credited this testimony. A seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment requires either a physical application of force or a submission to the assertion of authority. *See California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983); *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980).

Here, the defendant exercised his own judgment to pull over. The mere activation of police lights does not constitute a seizure when the driver independently decides to stop. *See Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106 (1977) (per curiam) (distinguishing between lawful orders to exit and mere requests). The defendant was not seized until he submitted to the officer's authority—which occurred only after he voluntarily stopped and the officer approached.

Furthermore, even assuming *arguendo* that activation of the lights constituted a show of authority, the defendant was already stopped and had no obligation to remain. The officer's stated purpose in activating lights was legitimate: "so the driver would know it was a police officer and as protection in case anyone would come up from behind." This constitutes a legitimate community caretaking function. *See Cady v. Dombrowski*, 413 U.S. 433 (1973); *Wright v. State* (Maryland Court of Special Appeals, 2013) (discussing community caretaking doctrine).

## II. EVEN IF A SEIZURE OCCURRED, IT WAS LAWFUL

Assuming a seizure occurred, the officer possessed reasonable suspicion to initiate the encounter. The prosecution misstates the record when suggesting no reasonable suspicion existed.

The officer observed the defendant's vehicle "cocked to the right" in the center lane well past the marked stop line at 3:42 a.m. The vehicle was traveling thirty-five miles per hour in a forty-five mile-per-hour zone. The officer, with fifteen years of law enforcement experience, testified that part of his patrol was to look for drunk driving, "especially on a weekend at that time of night." While the officer ultimately did not smell alcohol or observe signs of intoxication, the observations at the time of the stop provided reasonable suspicion to investigate. *See United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981) (reasonable suspicion can be based on observations of driving behavior indicative of impairment).

Moreover, the officer's subsequent investigation revealed that the defendant was driving on a suspended license—a criminal offense. This validates the initial encounter. *See Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) (holding that a brief investigatory stop is permissible when supported by reasonable suspicion).

## III. THE PAT-DOWN WAS JUSTIFIED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution argues that the pat-down (frisk) was unlawful because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates two distinct concepts. The pat-down described in the facts was not a *Terry* frisk for weapons based on fear for officer safety. Rather, the officer asked if he could search the defendant, and the defendant "placed his hands on the side of the vehicle" and consented. The officer testified that "he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." This was a consensual encounter, not a compelled frisk.

Second, even if the pat-down was conducted as part of the search, the officer possessed reasonable suspicion. The officer testified that he formed "reasonable suspicion" based on: (1) where the defendant was coming from (Philadelphia); (2) the defendant's re-engagement about the license seizure form after already receiving a thorough explanation; and (3) the defendant's "overly cooperative" behavior in placing his hands on the vehicle. While this Court may not have found these reasons individually persuasive, they constitute articulable facts that, viewed objectively, could support reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989) (reasonable suspicion requires specific, articulable facts, not mere hunches).

Third, the prosecution's reliance on *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), is misplaced. In *Dickerson*, the Court held that a pat-down may not exceed the scope of a *Terry* frisk. Here, the officer did not conduct a pat-down based on safety concerns; he conducted a consensual search. The officer's testimony that he had "no safety concerns" does not negate the validity of a consensual search.

## IV. THE CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS VOLUNTARY

The prosecution's central argument—that consent was tainted by an unlawful seizure—fails because the consent was validly obtained.

### A. The Defendant Was Not in Custody

The facts demonstrate that the defendant was not detained against his will. The officer testified that after explaining the license seizure form, the defendant was "free to go." The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming and was "not intending to drive anywhere." The defendant was standing "behind his car with nothing impeding him from the driver's door of his car." The officer explicitly told him "he was free to go."

The subsequent conversation was initiated by the defendant, who "engaged me again in conversation" by asking about the license seizure form. When the officer asked "if I could ask him a question," the defendant "turned around and came back" and said "yes, I can—he would answer my questions." This was a consensual conversation between a free citizen and an officer. *See Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991) (a consensual encounter does not constitute a seizure).

### B. The Consent Was Voluntary

The prosecution invokes *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), for the proposition that consent obtained during an unlawful detention is involuntary. This argument fails because there was no unlawful detention.

In *Royer*, the defendant was detained in a small room with two officers for fifteen minutes before giving consent. Here, the defendant was told he was free to go, was not handcuffed, was not removed from the scene, and the officer's weapon was never drawn. The defendant voluntarily agreed to answer questions and voluntarily consented to the search.

The officer testified that defendant's demeanor in giving consent was "agreeable." The defendant "offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" before the officer asked about searching the car. This demonstrates voluntary, affirmative consent. *See Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) (voluntariness is determined from the totality of the circumstances).

### C. The Prosecution's Argument Regarding the Scope of Royer Is Misplaced

The prosecution's reliance on *Royer* is also flawed because *Royer* addressed consent obtained during an unlawful detention. Here, the defendant was told he was free to go, his girlfriend was en route, and he voluntarily chose to engage further with the officer. The defendant was not in custody for Fourth Amendment purposes. *See Michigan v. Chesternut*, 486 U.S. 567 (1988) (the test for seizure is whether a reasonable person would have felt free to leave).

## V. THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE WAS PROPERLY LIMITED TO ITS SCOPE

Even assuming the pat-down of the defendant was somehow improper, the prosecution has not established that this tainted the subsequent vehicle search. The officer first searched "the compartment of the vehicle, then moved into the trunk." This search was based on the defendant's consent to search his person and vehicle.

The defendant explicitly consented by stating "I can search him" and placing his hands on the vehicle. The officer did not obtain written consent, but written consent is not required. *See United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002) (consent may be verbal or inferred from conduct).

The prosecution has not demonstrated that the defendant's consent was anything other than voluntary. The defendant was fully cooperative, polite, and respectful throughout the encounter. He was not threatened, coerced, or pressured. The officer's weapon was never drawn. Under the totality of the circumstances, the consent was valid. *See Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543 (1968) (the government bears the burden of proving consent).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's denial of the suppression motion. The officer's conduct was lawful at every stage: the initial encounter was either consensual or supported by reasonable suspicion; the defendant was not unlawfully seized; the consent to search was voluntarily given by a person who was free to leave; and the search of the vehicle was properly conducted pursuant to that consent. The prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving a Fourth Amendment violation.

The evidence discovered during the lawful search should not be suppressed.

This matter comes before the Court on a motion to suppress evidence obtained during a search of the defendant’s vehicle. The Court has reviewed the factual findings from the suppression hearing, as well as the opening arguments presented by the prosecution and the defense. The central issue is whether the search of the defendant’s vehicle was conducted in violation of the Fourth Amendment, specifically whether the consent to search was voluntary or tainted by an unlawful seizure and detention.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Encounter and Seizure**

The prosecution argues that the initial encounter constituted an unlawful seizure because the officer activated his lights without reasonable suspicion of a traffic violation. The defense counters that no seizure occurred because the defendant pulled over voluntarily, and alternatively, that the officer’s actions were justified by a community caretaking function.

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648 (1979). A seizure occurs when, "in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980). Here, the officer activated his rotating lights and approached the defendant’s vehicle. While the defendant pulled over prior to the officer’s activation of the lights, the officer’s subsequent approach and activation of emergency lights constitute a show of authority that would lead a reasonable person to believe they were not free to leave. *See California v. Hodari D.*, 499 U.S. 621 (1991).

However, the officer testified that he "did not initiate a traffic stop" and that the car pulled over on its own. Furthermore, the officer testified that his purpose in activating the lights was for safety and identification, consistent with the "community caretaking" exception to the Fourth Amendment. *See Cady v. Dombrowski*, 413 U.S. 433 (1973). Even if the initial seizure was not supported by reasonable suspicion of a traffic violation, the discovery of the defendant’s suspended license provided probable cause to issue a citation and detain the defendant for that purpose. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648 (1979).

**B. The Duration of the Detention and the "Free to Go" Statement**

The prosecution contends that the entire encounter was an unlawful detention, rendering any subsequent consent involuntary under *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983). The defense argues that the detention ended when the officer told the defendant he was "free to go."

The record clearly establishes that after completing the paperwork for the suspended license citation, the officer told the defendant he was "free to go." The officer testified, "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." This statement is critical. Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), once an officer communicates that a citizen is free to leave, the seizure has ended. The fact that the officer’s lights remained on for safety does not negate this conclusion, particularly where the officer had begun walking back to his patrol car. *See Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991) (noting that the presence of a police officer, without more, does not constitute a seizure).

**C. The Re-engagement and Consent to Search**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search was tainted by the prior unlawful detention. The defense argues that the consent was voluntary because the defendant re-initiated the encounter after being told he was free to leave.

The facts indicate that after the officer said he was free to go and began walking away, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation." The defendant asked again about the ID procedures, and the officer explained them a second time. The officer then asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant "turned around and came back to me. And he said, yes, I can — he would answer my questions." Only then did the officer ask about contraband, at which point the defendant placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched.

Consent to a search is valid if it is voluntary under the "totality of the circumstances." *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). A consensual search does not violate the Fourth Amendment. *See Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991). Here, the chain of causation between any potential initial illegality and the consent was broken by the officer’s statement that the defendant was free to go and the defendant’s voluntary decision to re-engage the officer. *See Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975) (holding that Miranda warnings are not alone sufficient to break the causal chain, but here the defendant was released and re-initiated contact). The defendant was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and the officer’s weapon was not drawn. The defendant’s demeanor was described as "agreeable," and he initiated the physical act of consent by placing his hands on the vehicle.

**D. The Pat-down of the Defendant**

The prosecution argues that the pat-down of the defendant was an unlawful *Terry* frisk because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. *See Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). The defense correctly notes that the pat-down was part of a consensual search, not a frisk for weapons.

The record shows that the defendant "offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" before the officer asked to search the car. Because the defendant consented to the search of his person, the officer was not required to meet the *Terry* standard of reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. *See United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002) (holding that consent to search on a bus was voluntary despite the presence of armed officers). The officer’s testimony that he had "no safety concerns" is consistent with a consensual search rather than a compelled frisk.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided, the prosecution has not established that the search of the defendant’s vehicle was unconstitutional. While the initial stop may have been based on limited articulable facts, the detention was justified by the discovery of the suspended license. Critically, the officer unequivocally told the defendant he was "free to go" before the consent to search was sought. The defendant’s voluntary re-engagement with the officer and his affirmative consent to be searched render the search valid under the Fourth Amendment. The evidence obtained should not be suppressed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress is denied.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The "Free to Go" Statement Unambiguously Ended Any Seizure

The prosecution argues that the entire encounter constituted a continuous unlawful detention. This argument fails as a matter of law. The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that a seizure ends when an officer unequivocally communicates that the citizen is free to leave. In *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), the Court established that a person is seized only when a reasonable person would not believe they are free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly stated: "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car."

This testimony is unambiguous. The officer communicated that the defendant was free to leave, and the officer himself began walking away. Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), the mere presence of a police officer does not constitute a seizure. The prosecution cannot overcome the clear testimony that the defendant was explicitly released from custody.

### B. The Defendant's Voluntary Re-Engagement Broke Any Causal Connection

The prosecution's reliance on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In *Royer*, the defendant was held in a small room with his luggage while officers retained his ticket and identification. The circumstances there involved a coercive detention that never ended before consent was obtained. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go, his ride was en route, and he voluntarily chose to re-engage the officer.

The defense submits that even if some prior illegality existed, the defendant's voluntary re-initiation of contact broke any causal chain. In *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), the Supreme Court held that Miranda warnings alone are insufficient to purge the taint of an illegal arrest. However, the Court has also recognized that a voluntary re-initiation of contact can dissipate the connection between any prior illegality and subsequent consent. The facts here demonstrate that the defendant asked additional questions, the officer asked permission to ask further questions, and the defendant affirmatively agreed. This was a wholly new encounter initiated by the defendant.

### C. The Consent Was Voluntary Under the Totality of the Circumstances

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was involuntary. See *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The totality of the circumstances here demonstrates voluntariness:

1. **No Coercive Environment**: The officer's weapon was never drawn. The officer testified he had "no safety concerns." The defendant was not handcuffed, arrested, or physically restrained in any manner.

2. **Defendant's Demeanor**: The officer described the defendant as "agreeable" and "cooperative." The defendant was polite, respectful, and fully compliant throughout. There were no signs of fear, confusion, or coercion.

3. **Affirmative Acts of Consent**: The defendant did not merely passively submit; he affirmatively placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched. This demonstrates voluntary cooperation, not submission to authority.

4. **No Misrepresentation**: The officer did not make any threats or promises to obtain consent. The defendant was not misled about the consequences of refusing.

5. **Explicit Release**: Critically, the defendant had just been told he was free to go. He knew he was not obligated to answer further questions or permit any search.

Under *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), consent can be voluntary even when officers are present and armed. The Supreme Court emphasized that the Cooley of the Fourth Amendment is voluntariness, not the absence of any police presence. Here, far from the coercive environment in *Drayton*, the defendant was explicitly released and free to leave.

The defense also maintains that the officer's initial approach was justified under the community caretaking doctrine. In *Cady v. The officer testified that he activated his lights for safety and identification purposes, particularly important at 3:42 AM when the defendant was stopped at an unusual location. This community caretaking function provides an additional, independent justification for the initial contact.

### E. The Discovery of the Suspended License Provided Probable Cause

Once the officer discovered the defendant's license was suspended, he had probable cause to issue a citation and detain the defendant for that purpose. In *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648 (1979), the Supreme Court recognized that traffic stops are permissible when an officer observes a violation. Here, the officer's check revealed the suspended license, providing clear probable cause for the citation and the temporary detention while paperwork was completed.

The prosecution has failed to establish that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional. The critical sequence of events demonstrates that:

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in opposition to the Prosecution's arguments and in support of reversing the trial court's denial of the Motion to Suppress. The Defense contends that the search of Mr. defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment, and the evidence obtained therefrom should be suppressed. The Prosecution has failed to meet its burden of establishing that the search was conducted pursuant to a valid consent that was freely and voluntarily given, untainted by the prior unlawful seizure.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts as found by the trial court establish the following critical sequence of events:

The officer did not initiate a traffic stop. The defendant pulled his vehicle over on his own, without any police directive to do so. The officer testified that "Regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." The officer activated his emergency lights, approached the defendant, and began an investigation.

During the encounter, the officer discovered that the defendant's driver's license was suspended. The officer wrote a citation and a license forfeiture form. The officer then explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go." The defendant's girlfriend was en route to pick him up, and he was not intending to drive.

After being told he was free to go, the defendant re-engaged the officer by asking about the license seizure form. The officer then asked if he could ask the defendant a question. The defendant agreed. The officer stated, "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have" and asked if the defendant "had anything that would concern me." Without any probing, the defendant placed his hands on the side of the vehicle and offered to be searched.

The trial court found as a fact that "at some point during the encounter that night he formed a determination to search defendant's car."

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Initial Seizure

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. A seizure occurs when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544 (1980).

Here, the defendant was seized from the moment the officer activated his emergency lights and pulled in behind the defendant's vehicle. The activation of police lights is a significant show of authority that would cause a reasonable person to believe they were not free to leave. The trial court erred in finding otherwise. The officer's subsequent statement that the defendant was "free to go" did not retroactively validate an unlawful seizure that had already occurred.

### B. The "Free to Go" Statement Did Not Dissipate the Taint of the Unlawful Seizure

The Prosecution argues that the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" terminated the seizure and rendered any subsequent consent valid. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the trial court's finding that a seizure occurred is supported by the record. The officer activated his lights, blocked the defendant's vehicle, and conducted a license check. A reasonable person would not have felt free to leave during this period. The subsequent statement telling the defendant he was free to go came only after the officer had completed his investigation and issued a citation.

Second, even assuming the "free to go" statement effectively terminated the detention, the subsequent consent was still tainted by the prior unlawful seizure. In Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), the Supreme Court held that Miranda warnings alone do not purge the taint of an illegal arrest. The same principle applies here. The defendant was seized without reasonable suspicion, and the officer's subsequent questioning and request for consent were direct fruits of that unlawful seizure.

The Prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), is misplaced. In Bostick, the defendant was approached on a bus where he was already seated. The Court emphasized that the encounter was consensual because the defendant was free to leave. Here, by contrast, the defendant was initially seized through the show of police authority. The Bostick framework applies only when there has been no seizure.

### C. The Consent Was Not Voluntary

Even if this Court finds that the initial seizure had terminated, the consent obtained was not voluntary under the totality of the circumstances as required by Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973).

The Prosecution bears the burden of proving voluntariness by a preponderance of the evidence. The Defense submits that the Prosecution has failed to meet this burden.

**1. The Coercive Environment**

While the officer did not draw his weapon, the mere presence of police lights, the presence of two officers, and the fact that the defendant had just been cited for driving while license suspended created a coercive environment. The defendant was standing behind his vehicle, his license was being seized, and he was waiting for his girlfriend to pick him up. He was in no position to exercise true freedom of choice.

**2. The Defendant's Subjective Belief**

The defendant had just been told he was "free to go," but he had also just been issued a citation and had his license seized. A reasonable person in this situation would not believe they were truly free to refuse a police officer's request. The officer's subsequent question—"Can I ask you a question?"—followed by the statement about "people bringing things out of Philadelphia," created an atmosphere of continued police authority.

**3. The Officer Failed to Advise the Defendant of His Rights**

The officer did not advise the defendant that he could refuse consent to search. He did not advise the defendant that he was free to leave. He did not provide any warnings whatsoever. The failure to inform the defendant of his right to refuse is a significant factor weighing against voluntariness. See Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491, 498 (1983) (noting that the failure to advise the defendant of his rights is relevant to voluntariness analysis).

**4. The Defendant's "Offer" to be Searched Was Not Voluntary Consent**

The Prosecution emphasizes that the defendant "offered" to be searched before being asked. However, this "offer" came only after the officer asked if the defendant "had anything that would concern me." This question, following the officer's statement about "people bringing things out of Philadelphia," was clearly designed to elicit consent. The defendant's response—placing his hands on the vehicle—was not an independent, voluntary offer. It was a response to police questioning.

The trial court found that the officer had "formed a determination to search defendant's car" before obtaining consent. This finding establishes that the officer was seeking evidence of a crime and used the consent inquiry to obtain that evidence. Such a purpose is inconsistent with a truly voluntary consent.

### D. The Community Caretaking Doctrine Does Not Justify the Search

The Prosecution may argue that the officer's initial approach was justified under the community caretaking doctrine as recognized in Cady v. Dombrowski, 413 U.S. 433 (1973). This argument fails.

The community caretaking doctrine allows officers to approach vehicles for safety purposes unrelated to criminal enforcement. However, this doctrine does not justify a full search of a vehicle based on the defendant's statement that he was "lost." The officer observed no signs of intoxication, no contraband, and no evidence of any crime. The community caretaking doctrine cannot be stretched to cover a fishing expedition for drugs based on the officer's subjective suspicions.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The Prosecution likely argues that the defendant voluntarily consented to the search. This argument fails for the following reasons:

**First**, the consent was not voluntary because it was obtained following an unlawful seizure. The officer's initial activation of his lights and blocking of the defendant's vehicle constituted a seizure. The subsequent consent was tainted by this illegality.

**Second**, the "free to go" statement did not dissipate the taint because the officer continued to engage the defendant in conversation and request permission to search. The causal chain between the unlawful seizure and the consent was not broken.

**Third**, the defendant was not adequately informed of his right to refuse consent. The officer never advised the defendant that he could decline the search. The officer never told the defendant that he was free to leave. The officer never advised the defendant of any rights whatsoever.

**Fourth**, the coercive nature of the encounter weighed against voluntariness. The defendant was alone, at night, with two police officers present, his license had just been seized, and he was waiting for his girlfriend. These circumstances do not support a finding of voluntary consent.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the Motion to Suppress and grant the Motion to Suppress. The search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional because:

1. The defendant was seized without reasonable suspicion or probable cause;
2. The subsequent consent was not voluntary under the totality of the circumstances;
3. The consent was tainted by the prior unlawful seizure; and
4. The officer failed to advise the defendant of his right to refuse consent.

The evidence obtained from the search should be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. The defendant's rights under the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 8 of the Pennsylvania Constitution were violated.

The Defense requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling and suppress the evidence.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on a motion to suppress evidence obtained during a search of the defendant’s vehicle. The Court has previously issued a preliminary ruling denying the motion to suppress. The Court has now reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties. The prosecution did not submit a rebuttal argument. The defense submitted a closing statement arguing that the search was unconstitutional and requesting that the motion to suppress be granted. Upon consideration of the defense’s arguments and the factual record, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling and denies the motion to suppress.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Seizure and the "Free to Go" Statement**

The defense argues that the defendant was seized unlawfully when the officer activated his emergency lights and that the subsequent statement that the defendant was "free to go" did not retroactively validate the seizure. The Court acknowledges that a seizure occurs when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980). However, the defense’s argument fails to account for the totality of the circumstances surrounding the initial encounter.

The factual findings indicate that the defendant pulled his vehicle over on his own initiative; the officer did not initiate the stop. The officer activated his lights for safety reasons and approached the vehicle. Even if the initial encounter constituted a seizure requiring justification, the "community caretaking" doctrine provides a valid basis for the officer’s actions. Cady v. Dombrowski, 413 U.S. 433, 441 (1973). Given the early hour (3:42 A.M.) and the defendant’s stationary position at an intersection, the officer’s approach to check on the driver’s welfare was reasonable.

More importantly, the defense’s argument regarding the "free to go" statement is undermined by a contradiction within its own closing statement. In the "Statement of Facts" section, the defense concedes that "The officer then explicitly told the defendant he was 'free to go.'" However, in the legal analysis section, the defense argues that "The officer never told the defendant that he was free to leave." This internal inconsistency severely weakens the defense’s claim that the defendant was unaware of his freedom to leave. The record clearly supports the finding that the officer communicated that the detention had concluded.

**B. Purging the Taint of the Initial Seizure**

The defense contends that the "free to go" statement did not dissipate the taint of the alleged unlawful seizure, relying on Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590 (1975). In *Brown*, the Supreme Court held that Miranda warnings alone are not sufficient to break the causal chain between an illegal arrest and a subsequent confession. However, the Court also recognized that an act of free will by the defendant can dissipate the taint. Id. at 603.

Here, the causal chain was broken not merely by the officer’s statement, but by the defendant’s voluntary re-engagement. The officer testified that he told the defendant he was free to go and started walking back to his patrol car. It was the defendant who re-initiated the contact by asking further questions about the license forfeiture form. This voluntary act of returning to the officer to continue the conversation constitutes a significant intervening act of free will, severing the connection between any prior illegality and the subsequent consent. See Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491, 501-02 (1983) (distinguishing between consensual encounters and coercive detentions).

**C. Voluntariness of Consent**

The defense argues that the consent to search was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse and because the environment was coercive. The Court disagrees.

1.  **Failure to Advise of Rights**
    The defense relies on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), to suggest that the failure to advise the defendant of his right to refuse consent renders the consent involuntary. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly rejected this argument. In *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996), the Court held that "the Fourth Amendment does not require that a lawfully seized defendant be advised that he is 'free to go' before his consent to a search will be recognized as voluntary." Furthermore, *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 226-27 (1973), established that knowledge of the right to refuse is not a prerequisite to a voluntary consent. Therefore, the officer’s failure to inform the defendant that he could decline the search does not render the consent involuntary.

2.  **Totality of the Circumstances**
    Under the totality of the circumstances test, the consent was voluntary. *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 226. The facts indicate that the officer’s weapon was not drawn, the officer had no safety concerns, and the defendant was polite, cooperative, and "agreeable." The defendant was not subjected to physical force or threats. While the presence of two officers and the activation of police lights are factors to consider, they are not dispositive. See United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002) (holding that police presence on a bus did not render consent involuntary).

    Crucially, the defendant’s consent was affirmative and unambiguous. After the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant voluntarily placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched. This proactive behavior demonstrates a willingness to cooperate rather than a submission to authority.

**D. The Defense's "Ruse" Argument**

The defense suggests that the officer’s statement regarding "people bringing things out of Philadelphia" was designed to elicit consent. Even if this were true, it does not render the consent involuntary. Police are permitted to ask questions, and deception alone does not necessarily vitiate consent. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 436-37 (1991) (holding that police conduct does not render a seizure unconstitutional unless it is coercive). The defendant’s response to the officer’s question was to offer consent; he was not compelled to do so.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s closing arguments fail to persuade the Court that the search violated the defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights. The "free to go" statement, combined with the defendant’s voluntary re-engagement, effectively terminated any prior seizure and purged the taint of any potential illegality. The consent to search was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances, and the officer was not required to advise the defendant of his right to refuse. The prosecution’s failure to submit a rebuttal does not alter this outcome, as the defense has not met its burden of proving a constitutional violation.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress is denied. The evidence obtained from the search of the defendant’s vehicle is admissible.
