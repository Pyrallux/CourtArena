**II. ARGUMENT**

**A. The Confidential Informant's Identity Was Directly Relevant to the Suppression Motion, Not Merely to Guilt or Innocence**

The prosecution incorrectly asserts that the informant disclosure issue was unrelated to the suppression motion because guilt was not at issue. This argument ignores the defense's explicit and repeated assertions that the informant's identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause foundation of the search and seizure.

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution guarantees the right to be secure against unreasonable searches and seizures. When the state seeks to suppress evidence, the burden falls upon the government to demonstrate that the search and seizure were lawful. As the Supreme Court held in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), the task of the reviewing court is to determine whether, under the totality of the circumstances, the issuing magistrate had a substantial basis for finding probable cause. This determination necessarily requires examination of the informant's reliability, credibility, and basis of knowledge.

Here, defense counsel explicitly stated that "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress, even on a Motion to Suppress." The defense argued that "if she set him up, it goes to her reliability" and that "the CI's identity... goes to the reliability in regard to the probable cause." These statements directly connect the disclosure request to the suppression motion's core factual inquiry—whether officers had probable cause to conduct the search.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Jones*, 536 F.3d 1265 (10th Cir. 2008), is misplaced. In *Jones*, the defendant sought to reserve issues unrelated to the suppression motion itself. Here, by contrast, the informant's identity goes to the very heart of the suppression analysis—the existence of probable cause. The informant's reliability is not a collateral matter; it is the foundation upon which the search warrant or search incident to arrest was based.

**B. The Defense Did Not Waive the Informant Disclosure Issue Because the Issue Was Properly Before the Trial Court in Connection with the Suppression Motion**

The prosecution's waiver argument fails because the defense timely raised the disclosure issue in connection with the suppression motion. The record demonstrates that defense counsel sought disclosure of the informant's identity precisely because it was essential to challenging the probable cause determination.

In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), the Supreme Court held that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity "has limits where the disclosure is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." While the prosecution correctly notes that Roviaro addresses fairness in determining guilt or innocence, the "cause" before the trial court was not merely a theoretical determination of guilt—it was the concrete question of whether the search and seizure complied with the Fourth Amendment.

The trial court itself acknowledged the relevance of the informant's reliability to the suppression inquiry when it stated: "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This acknowledgment demonstrates that the trial court recognized the informant's reliability as a proper subject of inquiry. However, without disclosure of the informant's identity, the defense could not effectively test this reliability determination through cross-examination or presentation of contradictory evidence.

**C. The Prosecution's Interpretation of the Dispositive Agreement Would Create Unconstitutional Results**

The prosecution's argument, if accepted, would allow the state to obtain a conviction through a guilty plea while simultaneously denying the defendant the ability to challenge the constitutional basis for the search. This result contradicts fundamental principles of due process.

When a defendant agrees to a dispositive suppression motion, the agreement typically contemplates that if the motion is granted, the case is dismissed, and if denied, the defendant pleads guilty. However, this structure presupposes that the suppression ruling will be based on a complete record that allows meaningful review. Here, the defense sought disclosure of the informant's identity precisely because that identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause finding that formed the basis for the search.

To hold that the defendant waived this issue by pleading guilty would be to hold that the defendant must either accept a conviction based on potentially unconstitutional search or forego any challenge to the search's legality. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI) in the context of a dispositive motion to suppress. The Appellant argues that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause for the search and to support a defense of entrapment. The Appellee argues that the Appellant waived this issue by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress, which limited the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search and foreclosed litigation of guilt or innocence issues such as entrapment.

Based on the provided facts and arguments, the Court finds that the Appellee’s argument is more persuasive. The existence of a clear dispositive agreement, confirmed by defense counsel, operated to waive issues not directly related to the suppression hearing. The Appellant’s request for disclosure, based on "new information" suggesting the CI "set him up," constitutes a challenge to the guilt or innocence of the Appellant (specifically entrapment) rather than a challenge to the probable cause established by the CI’s past reliability. Therefore, the trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Agreement and Waiver of Non-Suppression Issues**

The record establishes that the parties entered into a binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The prosecutor explicitly outlined this agreement, stating, "the agreement was that this was a dispositive Motion to Suppress; and after the court ruled on that Motion to Suppress, the court would then make a decision as to sentencing." Crucially, defense counsel confirmed this understanding, admitting, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to—like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress."

The legal effect of such an agreement is well-defined. As stated in *United States v. Jones*, 536 F.3d 1265, 1270 (10th Cir. 2008), "A defendant who pleads guilty pursuant to a plea agreement that conditions the plea on the outcome of a suppression motion waives all non-jurisdictional defenses and objections except those specifically reserved in the agreement." Similarly, in *State v. Johnson*, 350 S.W.3d 732, 737 (Tex. App. 2011), the court held that "when a defendant agrees to a dispositive motion to suppress, he waives all issues not related to the suppression hearing."

Here, the Appellant agreed to resolve the case solely on the basis of the suppression ruling. By confirming the agreement was "dispositive," the Appellant accepted the procedural framework that issues of guilt or innocence would not be litigated. The prosecutor correctly noted that if the defense wished to attack the CI regarding guilt or innocence, "they should have had a trial." The defense cannot now seek to broaden the scope of the hearing after receiving an adverse ruling on the suppression motion.

**B. The Scope of *Roviaro* and the "Fair Determination of a Cause"**

The Appellant relies on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), arguing that the CI’s identity was essential to a fair determination of the cause. However, *Roviaro* does not grant an unlimited right to disclosure; it requires disclosure only when the informant’s identity is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause."

In this context, the "cause" before the trial court was narrowly defined by the dispositive agreement: the determination of probable cause for the search. The trial court addressed this issue by hearing testimony regarding the CI’s reliability. The judge stated, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This finding aligns with the "totality of the circumstances" test for probable cause outlined in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

The Appellant’s argument that the CI "set him up" and that her belongings were found in the car does not pertain to the *initial* probable cause for the search or the CI’s historical reliability. Rather, these allegations suggest the CI was a material participant in the offense, potentially supporting a defense of entrapment. As the prosecution argued, this is a "matter of guilt or innocence in a trial." Because the Appellant agreed to a dispositive motion, he waived the right to litigate this entrapment defense. Therefore, the CI’s identity was not "essential to a fair determination" of the limited issue before the court—the suppression motion.

**C. The "New Information" Does Not Override the Waiver**

The defense contends that "new information" received the day before the hearing justified re-urging the motion to disclose. However, the nature of this information—that the CI was with the Appellant and her belongings were in the car—further supports the conclusion that the defense sought to explore the CI’s role in the alleged crime (entrapment) rather than the validity of the tip used to establish probable cause.

The trial court acted within its discretion in determining that the reliability of the CI for the purpose of the suppression motion had already been established through testimony regarding "past tips." The defense’s attempt to use the CI’s identity to impeach that reliability or assert entrapment falls outside the scope of the suppression hearing. The right to present a defense, as protected by *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 331 (2006), is not absolute where, as here, the defendant has voluntarily agreed to a procedural mechanism that limits the issues to be litigated.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The Appellant entered into a valid agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive, thereby waiving issues relating to guilt or innocence, including the entrapment defense implied by the "set up" allegation. The request for disclosure was not essential to the determination of probable cause, which was the sole issue reserved by the agreement. The prosecution’s argument that the waiver precludes this challenge is sound.

Accordingly, the Court preliminarily rules in favor of the prosecution/State.

The defense erroneously cites *Roviaro v. The trial court properly considered the informant’s reliability for suppression purposes based on testimony about “past tips” and “accuracy,” satisfying the *Illinois v. The defense’s post-hoc claim that the informant’s presence during the offense affects *probable cause* is legally unsupported. Probable cause is assessed based on information available *at the time of the search*, not on post-offense allegations about the informant’s role in the crime. The defense’s assertion that the prosecution’s interpretation “would effectively gut constitutional protections” is hyperbolic and contradicted by the record. The defense waived its right to challenge the informant’s role in the offense itself by agreeing to a dispositive suppression motion. This is not a failure of Fourth Amendment protections but a recognition of procedural boundaries.  

*Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 331 (2006), does not apply here. That case concerned the exclusion of third-party guilt evidence at trial, but the defense never proceeded to trial. The right to present a defense is constrained by procedural agreements, and the defense voluntarily waived trial by accepting the dispositive arrangement. As the prosecutor stated, “if it was a dispositive Motion to Suppress, what that typically means to the State is that the judge is going to litigate an issue; and if he rules in our favor, the defendant pleads.” The defense cannot now claim it was denied due process when it knowingly and explicitly agreed to this framework.

The defense moved for disclosure *during* the suppression hearing, but its arguments centered on entrapment (“if she set him up, it goes to her reliability” and “the confidential informant was with appellant that day”). As the trial court correctly noted, this is a “defense matter for trial,” not a suppression issue. The defense cannot preserve an issue it waived by agreement and then recharacterize it as relevant to suppression after receiving an adverse ruling.  

#### CONCLUSION  
The court of appeals did not err. The defense’s disclosure request was waived under *Jones* and *Johnson* because it sought to litigate guilt/innocence issues outside the dispositive suppression agreement. *Roviaro* does not apply to the narrow issue before the trial court, and the defense’s constitutional arguments are contradicted by the record. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the Appellant’s challenge to the trial court’s denial of his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause for the search and to support a defense of entrapment. The Appellee maintains that the Appellant waived this issue by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress, which limited the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search and foreclosed litigation of guilt or innocence issues.

After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. The Appellee’s argument that the dispositive agreement operated as a waiver of the Appellant’s disclosure request is persuasive. The "new information" presented by the defense—that the CI "set him up" and that her belongings were found in the car—pertains to the CI’s participation in the offense and the defense of entrapment, rather than to the historical reliability of the CI’s tip used to establish probable cause. Consequently, the trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Agreement Waived the Right to Litigate Entrapment**

The record confirms that the parties entered into a binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The defense counsel explicitly acknowledged this agreement, stating, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to—like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress." As established in *United States v. Jones*, 536 F.3d 1265, 1270 (10th Cir. 2008), a defendant who pleads guilty pursuant to a plea agreement that conditions the plea on the outcome of a suppression motion "waives all non-jurisdictional defenses and objections except those specifically reserved in the agreement." Similarly, *State v. Johnson*, 350 S.W.3d 732, 737 (Tex. App. 2011), holds that "when a defendant agrees to a dispositive motion to suppress, he waives all issues not related to the suppression hearing."

The defense argues that the dispositive agreement only waived the right to a trial, not the right to fully litigate the suppression motion. However, the "new information" sought to be introduced—the fact that the CI was with the Appellant and her belongings were found in his car—is inextricably linked to the defense of entrapment. As the prosecution correctly noted, this is a "matter of guilt or innocence in a trial." By agreeing to the dispositive motion, the Appellant waived the right to litigate entrapment. Therefore, the defense cannot now resurrect this waived issue under the guise of a challenge to the CI’s reliability for probable cause.

**B. The "Set Up" Evidence Does Not Challenge Probable Cause Under *Gates***

The defense contends that the CI’s alleged involvement in "setting up" the Appellant goes to her reliability, which is a valid component of the probable cause inquiry. While reliability is a factor, the specific evidence offered here—that the CI was present during the offense and her belongings were in the car—does not undermine the *initial* probable cause determination.

Probable cause is evaluated under the "totality of the circumstances" test set forth in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). This test focuses on the information available to the officers at the time of the search. The trial court found the CI reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The defense’s "new information" regarding the CI’s behavior during the alleged crime does not retroactively invalidate the accuracy of her past tips or the information known to the officers at the time they established probable cause. Rather, this information suggests the CI was a material participant in the offense, which is relevant to entrapment, not the validity of the search warrant or arrest.

**C. *Roviaro* Does Not Compel Disclosure in This Context**

The defense relies on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), arguing that the CI’s identity was "essential to a fair determination of a cause." However, *Roviaro* does not grant an absolute right to disclosure; it requires a balancing of the public interest in protecting the flow of information against the individual's right to prepare their defense.

In this case, the "cause" before the trial court was narrowly defined by the dispositive agreement: the legality of the search. The defense’s need for the CI’s identity was predicated on showing that the CI "set him up," which, as established above, is an entrapment defense. Because the Appellant waived the right to litigate entrapment, the CI’s identity was not "essential to a fair determination" of the limited issue before the court. The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion, as the defense had already agreed to resolve the case without litigating the very facts (the CI's role in the offense) that the disclosure would have revealed.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The Appellant entered into a valid dispositive agreement, which waived the right to litigate issues of guilt or innocence, including the entrapment defense suggested by the "new information." The request for disclosure was not essential to the determination of probable cause, which was the sole issue reserved by the agreement. The prosecution’s arguments regarding the waiver and the scope of the hearing are sound.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

**Ladies and gentlemen of the Court,** the prosecution asks this Court to affirm a ruling that effectively denied the Appellant his constitutional right to present a complete defense. We respectfully submit that the prosecution's arguments rest on a fundamental mischaracterization of the dispositive agreement and a misapplication of controlling precedent. We will demonstrate that: (1) the dispositive agreement did not waive the right to challenge probable cause through CI disclosure; (2) the "new information" directly pertains to the reliability of the confidential informant and thus to the validity of the search; (3) *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), compels disclosure where, as here, the informant is a material witness to the defense of entrapment; and (4) the constitutional right to put on a defense cannot be waived without explicit language reserving no such right.

---

## I. THE DISPOSITIVE AGREEMENT DID NOT WAIVE THE RIGHT TO CHALLENGE PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution invokes *United States v. Jones*, 536 F.3d 1265 (10th Cir. 2008), for the proposition that a defendant who agrees to a dispositive motion to suppress "waives all non-jurisdictional defenses and objections except those specifically reserved in the agreement." This proposition, however, assumes a waiver far broader than what actually occurred in this case.

The critical distinction this Court must recognize is between waiving the right to a trial and waiving the right to fully litigate the suppression motion itself. The dispositive agreement specified that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive"—meaning the outcome of that motion would determine whether the case proceeded or concluded. However, the agreement did not, and could not, strip the Appellant of his right to challenge the very basis upon which the search was conducted: the probable cause determination.

As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the defense counsel explicitly stated: "At the time when Judge Hill was here, we did not have the new information regarding the CI." This distinction is fatal to the prosecution's waiver argument. The "new information"—that the confidential informant "set him up" and that her belongings were found in his car—was received after the initial agreement was made. The defense specifically argued that this new information was relevant to the CI's reliability, which goes to the heart of the probable cause inquiry.

The prosecution conflates two distinct issues: (1) the historical reliability of the CI's past tips, and (2) the CI's potential participation in or orchestration of the offense. While the former might have been waived, the latter was not. The defense is not attempting to relitigate guilt or innocence; rather, it seeks to demonstrate that the CI's tip—which formed the basis for probable cause—was unreliable because the CI herself may have been involved in planting evidence or orchestrating the offense. This is a classic challenge to probable cause, not an attempt to litigate entrapment at a suppression hearing.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Johnson*, 350 S.W.3d 732 (Tex. App. 2011), is similarly misplaced. In *Johnson*, the defendant agreed to a dispositive suppression motion and then attempted to raise entirely new issues unrelated to the search. Here, by contrast, the defense's request for CI disclosure is directly connected to the suppression motion—specifically, to the reliability of the informant whose tip established probable cause. The "new information" does not introduce a new defense; it bolsters the existing challenge to probable cause by suggesting the CI had motive to fabricate or exaggerate her tip.

---

## II. THE "NEW INFORMATION" DIRECTLY PERTAINS TO PROBABLE CAUSE UNDER *ILLINOIS V. GATES*

The prosecution argues that probable cause is evaluated under the "totality of the circumstances" test set forth in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), and focuses solely on information available to officers at the time of the search. This is correct. However, the prosecution errs in concluding that the "new information" does not undermine the initial probable cause determination.

Under *Gates*, the reliability of an informant is a critical component of the probable cause analysis. The Court held that "the informant's veracity or reliability" and "the basis of the informant's knowledge" are "relevant" and must be "illuminated" in the probable cause inquiry. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 230. The prosecution is correct that the trial court found the CI reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." But this finding does not render the "new information" irrelevant.

The defense's "new information"—that the CI was present during the offense, that she "set him up," and that her belongings were found in his car—goes directly to the informant's credibility and potential bias. If the CI participated in orchestrating the offense, she had every incentive to provide information that would ensure the Appellant's arrest, regardless of whether that information was accurate. This is precisely the type of information that undermines an informant's reliability under *Gates*.

The prosecution argues that this information "does not retroactively invalidate the accuracy of her past tips." But this framing misapprehends the defense's argument. The defense does not claim that past tips were inaccurate; rather, it claims that the CI's involvement in the offense demonstrates that her tip in this particular case was potentially unreliable or motivated by factors other than mere citizen civic duty. The *Gates* totality-of-the-circumstances analysis requires courts to consider not only the historical accuracy of an informant's tips but also the circumstances surrounding the specific tip at issue. The CI's presence during the offense and her alleged role in "setting up" the Appellant are highly relevant to assessing the reliability of her tip in this case.

The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as an "entrapment" defense is a red herring. While the same evidence might ultimately support an entrapment defense at trial, that does not make it any less relevant to the probable cause inquiry. An informant who "sets up" a defendant has a motive to provide false or exaggerated information to law enforcement. This motive speaks directly to the informant's reliability—the very question this Court must answer in evaluating probable cause.

---

## III. *ROVARIO V. UNITED STATES* COMPELS DISCLOSURE

The prosecution attempts to limit *Roviaro* by arguing that the "cause" before the trial court was "narrowly defined" by the dispositive agreement. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, *Roviaro* establishes a general principle that the informant's identity must be disclosed when "essential to a fair determination of a cause." 353 U.S. at 60-61. The "cause" here is not narrowly defined by the parties' agreement; it is defined by the Constitution. The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to present a complete defense, and this right cannot be contracted away through a plea agreement without explicit language. The Appellant's defense—that he was "set up" by the CI—is precisely the type of defense that *Roviaro* contemplates: one that requires disclosure of the informant's identity to be fully and fairly presented.

Second, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If the parties could by agreement limit the scope of *Roviaro*, they could effectively eviscerate the protections established by that case. The prosecution's position would allow prosecutors to insulate informants from disclosure simply by conditioning a plea on a dispositive suppression motion—a result that *Roviaro* expressly rejected.

The prosecution notes that the defense counsel framed the issue as "a dispositive Motion to Suppress." But this framing was a response to the prosecution's characterization, not a waiver of the defense's right to disclosure. The defense consistently argued that the CI's identity was necessary to challenge probable cause—the very issue before the court on the suppression motion. The prosecution cannot now claim that the defense waived the right to disclosure when the defense's argument was predicated on the necessity of disclosure for the suppression motion.

In *Roviaro*, the Supreme Court held that "the privilege of confidentiality... is limited by the fundamental requirements of fairness." 353 U.S. at 61. Here, fairness requires disclosure because: (1) the CI is a material witness to the alleged offense; (2) the defense claims the CI "set him up"; (3) the CI's credibility is directly relevant to the probable cause determination; and (4) without disclosure, the Appellant cannot fully and fairly present his defense to the suppression motion.

---

## IV. THE CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE CANNOT BE WAIVED WITHOUT EXPLICIT RESERVATION

The prosecution argues that "a guilty plea is a waiver of all non-jurisdictional defenses" under *United States v. Broce*, 488 U.S. 563 (1989). But *Broce* is distinguishable. In *Broce*, the defendants entered unconditional guilty pleas and then attempted to challenge their pleas on collateral attack. The Court held that a guilty plea waives the right to contest the plea's voluntariness or the underlying facts that were stipulated to in the plea.

Here, by contrast, the Appellant did not enter an unconditional guilty plea. He entered a plea that was expressly conditioned on the outcome of the suppression motion. The prosecution's own characterization of the agreement confirms this: "if he rules in our favor, the defendant pleads; and if he rules not in the State’s favor, that we dismiss the case." This is a conditional plea, not an unconditional one. The Appellant preserved his right to challenge the suppression ruling on appeal.

Moreover, the prosecution cannot point to any language in the agreement whereby the Appellant expressly waived the right to challenge the CI's disclosure request. The defense specifically raised the issue of "new information" after the agreement was made, and the trial court never ruled on that issue before the Appellant entered his plea. Under these circumstances, the prosecution cannot claim that the Appellant knowingly and voluntarily waived a right that was never actually adjudicated.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right "to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right is fundamental to the fairness of criminal proceedings. The Appellant's defense—that he was "set up" by the CI—cannot be presented without the CI's identity. To hold that the Appellant waived this right by agreeing to a dispositive suppression motion would allow the prosecution to profit from the very unfairness that the Sixth Amendment was designed to prevent.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we respectfully request that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. The Appellant did not waive his right to challenge probable cause through CI disclosure. The "new information" is directly relevant to the CI's reliability under *Gates*. *Roviaro* compels disclosure where, as here, the informant is a material witness to the defense of entrapment. And the constitutional right to present a complete defense cannot be waived without explicit language reserving no such right.

The prosecution's arguments, while artfully presented, ultimately ask this Court to permit what the Constitution forbids: the conviction of a defendant who has been denied the opportunity to present a complete defense. The Appellant is entitled to know the identity of the person who allegedly "set him up" so that he may challenge the reliability of the informant whose tip formed the basis for his arrest. Anything less offends the fundamental principles of due process and fair play that this Court is sworn to uphold.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the Appellant’s challenge to the trial court’s denial of his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause for the search and to support a defense of entrapment based on "new information" discovered after the parties entered into a dispositive agreement. The Appellee maintains that the Appellant waived this issue by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress, which limited the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search and foreclosed litigation of guilt or innocence issues.

After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The Appellee’s argument that the dispositive agreement operated as a waiver of the Appellant’s disclosure request is persuasive. The "new information" presented by the defense—that the CI "set him up" and that her belongings were found in the car—pertains to the CI’s participation in the offense and the defense of entrapment, rather than to the historical reliability of the CI’s tip used to establish probable cause. Consequently, the trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Agreement Waived the Right to Litigate Entrapment**

The record confirms that the parties entered into a binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. Defense counsel explicitly acknowledged this agreement, stating, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to—like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress." As established in *United States v. Similarly, *State v. Johnson*, 350 S.W.3d 732, 737 (Tex. App. 2011), holds that "when a defendant agrees to a dispositive motion to suppress, he waives all issues not related to the suppression hearing."

The defense argues that the dispositive agreement only waived the right to a trial, not the right to fully litigate the suppression motion. However, the "new information" sought to be introduced—the fact that the CI was with the Appellant and her belongings were in his car—is inextricably linked to the defense of entrapment. As the prosecution correctly noted, this is a "matter of guilt or innocence in a trial." By agreeing to the dispositive motion, the Appellant waived the right to litigate entrapment. Therefore, the defense cannot now resurrect this waived issue under the guise of a challenge to the CI’s reliability for probable cause. The fact that this information was received "yesterday," as argued by defense counsel, does not negate the binding nature of the agreement to limit the hearing to the dispositive issue of the search's legality.

**B. The "Set Up" Evidence Does Not Challenge Probable Cause Under *Gates***

The defense contends that the CI’s alleged involvement in "setting up" the Appellant goes to her reliability, which is a valid component of the probable cause inquiry. While reliability is a factor, the specific evidence offered here—that the CI was present during the offense and her belongings were in the car—does not undermine the *initial* probable cause determination.

Probable cause is evaluated under the "totality of the circumstances" test set forth in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). This test focuses on the information available to the officers at the time of the search. The trial court found the CI reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The defense’s "new information" regarding the CI’s behavior during the alleged crime does not retroactively invalidate the accuracy of her past tips or the information known to the officers at the time they established probable cause. Rather, this information suggests the CI was a material participant in the offense, which is relevant to entrapment, not the validity of the search warrant or arrest. The defense's attempt to characterize the CI's participation as a "motive to lie" regarding the tip is unpersuasive where the trial court already found the CI reliable based on historical performance, and the "new information" speaks to the crime's execution, not the tip's fabrication.

**C. *Roviaro* Does Not Compel Disclosure in This Context**

The defense relies on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), arguing that the CI’s identity was "essential to a fair determination of a cause." However, *Roviaro* does not grant an absolute right to disclosure; it requires a balancing of the public interest in protecting the flow of information against the individual's right to prepare their defense.

In this case, the "cause" before the trial court was narrowly defined by the dispositive agreement: the legality of the search. The defense’s need for the CI’s identity was predicated on showing that the CI "set him up," which, as established above, is an entrapment defense. Because the Appellant waived the right to litigate entrapment, the CI’s identity was not "essential to a fair determination" of the limited issue before the court. The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion, as the defense had already agreed to resolve the case without litigating the very facts (the CI's role in the offense) that the disclosure would have revealed. The defense's argument that the "cause" is defined by the Constitution rather than the agreement ignores the procedural context of a voluntary plea agreement, where defendants may waive constitutional rights in exchange for benefits. *United States v. Broce*, 488 U.S. 563, 569 (1989).

The trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The Appellant entered into a valid dispositive agreement, which waived the right to litigate issues of guilt or innocence, including the entrapment defense suggested by the "new information." The request for disclosure was not essential to the determination of probable cause, which was the sole issue reserved by the agreement. The prosecution’s arguments regarding the waiver and the scope of the hearing are sound.
