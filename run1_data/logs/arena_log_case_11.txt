Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), joinder is proper when offenses arise from a common scheme or connected acts, and evidence of each offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other as other acts evidence. This standard is met here because Salinas’s intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were inextricably linked through a continuous pattern of abuse designed to control and silence his victims. As this Court held in *State v. Johnson*, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401–402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401–402 (Ct. App. 1995), joinder is appropriate when offenses are part of a single course of conduct, and the evidence of each would be relevant to the other. Here, Salinas’s phone calls from jail—where he threatened M.S. and V.G. to change statements, influence testimony, and prevent reporting—directly furthered his broader scheme of sexual abuse against V.G. This is not a case of isolated incidents but a cohesive strategy of intimidation to maintain dominance over his victims, as recognized in *State v. Eason*, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429–430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), which affirmed joinder when intimidation was used to suppress reporting of sexual assaults and was part of the same transaction.  

The evidence of intimidation would also be admissible in a separate sexual assault trial to explain V.G.’s delay in reporting and the context of her fear and compliance, which is a well-established exception to the other acts rule. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court stated in *State v. Pharr*, 115 Wis. 2d 334, 345, 340 N.W.2d 498, 501 (1983), crimes are properly joined when they are part of a common scheme and the evidence of each is relevant to the other. Here, the intimidation evidence directly contextualizes why V.G. remained silent for years and why she initially cooperated with Salinas during the domestic violence proceedings—both critical to understanding the sexual assault charges. The circuit court correctly joined the charges, as the State’s evidence overlapped in showing Salinas’s ongoing pattern of abuse, and joinder would not confuse the jury but rather present a complete narrative of the victimization. The court of appeals erred in reversing this judgment, as the charges were undeniably connected under settled precedent.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

The prosecution's argument conflates distinct criminal episodes that lack the temporal and factual nexus required for proper joinder under Wisconsin law. The court of appeals correctly identified that the circuit court erred in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges because these offenses arose from separate criminal episodes, served different purposes, and were improperly united to prejudice the defendant. The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the court of appeals reversal and remand for separate trials.

## I. THE INTIMIDATION CHARGES AROSE FROM THE DOMESTIC VIOLENCE CASE, NOT THE SEXUAL ASSAULT CASE

The prosecution mischaracterizes the nature of the intimidation charges by suggesting they were part of a unified scheme to conceal sexual abuse. This assertion ignores the clear chronological and factual record establishing that the intimidation was directed at influencing the domestic violence case proceedings, not concealing sexual assaults that the victims had not yet reported.

The record demonstrates that the intimidation charges stem exclusively from phone calls Salinas made to M.S. from jail between his arrest on October 26, 2009, and his sentencing on May 11, 2010. Paragraph 5 of the facts explicitly states: "The intimidation charges at issue here stem from those phone calls. The State asserted that transcripts from the phone calls show Salinas's attempt to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The phone calls were explicitly about the domestic violence incident—specifically, getting M.S. to change her statement regarding the strangulation and battery, and convincing V.G. to help with the sentencing.

Critically, V.G. did not disclose the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010—two days after Salinas's sentencing on the domestic violence charges. Paragraph 8 establishes: "On May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas's sentencing on the domestic violence convictions, V.G. told her boyfriend, E.D., that Salinas had been sexually assaulting her for the past three years." The prosecution's own timeline establishes that the sexual assaults were not reported until after the intimidation had concluded. As a matter of logical necessity, Salinas could not have intimidated witnesses to conceal sexual assaults that no one—not even the victims—knew had occurred.

The prosecution's argument in *State v. Eason*, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429–430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), is distinguishable because in that case, the intimidation was directly connected to suppressing reporting of sexual assaults. Here, by contrast, the intimidation predated any report of sexual abuse and was directed at witnesses in an entirely separate criminal proceeding.

## II. THE CHARGES LACK THE COMMON SCHEME OR CONNECTED ACTS REQUIRED FOR JOINDER

Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), joinder is proper only when offenses "are based on the same act" or "are connected acts or transactions that constitute parts of a common scheme or plan." The prosecution bears the burden of establishing that joinder is proper, and this burden is not met merely by showing that the offenses involved the same defendant and victims.

In *State v. Lehman*, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982), the court held that joinder requires "a relationship between the offenses that is more than incidental or superficial." The offenses must be "connected" in a meaningful way—not merely occurring in the same household or involving the same victims at different times.

Here, the sexual assault charges and intimidation charges lack any meaningful connection:

**Different temporal episodes**: The alleged sexual assaults occurred from approximately 2006 through October 26, 2009. The intimidation occurred between October 2009 and May 2010. These are separate time periods with no overlap.

**Different motivating purposes**: The intimidation was designed to influence the domestic violence case—specifically, to get M.S. to change her statement about the strangulation and to get V.G. to provide favorable testimony at sentencing. The sexual assaults, as alleged, were for the defendant's sexual gratification. These are distinct criminal objectives.

**Different victims' interests**: While both M.S. and V.G. were victims of intimidation, the intimidation was not designed to conceal the sexual assaults. The prosecution's own evidence established that Salinas wanted M.S. to say he "did not try to strangle her and he did not threaten to kill her" and wanted V.G. to say Salinas "did not hit her." These statements relate to the domestic violence charges, not sexual assault.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Johnson*, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401–402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401–402 (Ct. App. 1995), is misplaced. In *Johnson*, the court found joinder appropriate where the offenses were "part of a single course of conduct" involving the same victim over a limited time period. Here, by contrast, the intimidation and sexual assaults involved different time periods, different purposes, and different criminal motivations.

## III. THE JOINDER PREJUDICED THE DEFENDANT BY ALLOWING IMPROPER CHARACTER EVIDENCE

Even if some technical basis for joinder existed, the circuit court erred in allowing the joinder because it resulted in substantial prejudice to the defendant. The prosecution used the joined trial to present a "pattern of violence" narrative that improperly appealed to the jury's emotions and conveyed the impression that Salinas was a generally dangerous person.

In opening statements, the prosecutor explicitly told the jury the evidence would show "a pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, repeated sexual assault of a child occurred to the victim, [V.G.], occurred within a family environment where her mother, [M.S.], was also a victim." This statement improperly characterized the case as being about Salinas's general propensity for violence, rather than the specific charges.

The prosecutor further stated: "That is a day of horror but that is also a day that stopped what was happening to [V.G.] That is a day that family got help. That is the day that [V.G.] stopped being assaulted from the defendant." This statement improperly linked the domestic violence incident to the sexual assaults in a way that suggested Salinas was guilty of all charges because he was a violent person.

In *State v. Gardner*, 130 Wis. 2d 227, 234, 387 N.W.2d 308, 312 (Ct. App. 1986), the court held that joinder is improper when "the evidence of one offense would not be admissible in the trial of the other if the offenses were tried separately" because joinder "may cause the jury to infer that the defendant is a bad person who probably committed all the acts alleged."

The evidence of the domestic violence incident—which included the strangulation, the knife, and threats—was not admissible in a separate trial on the sexual assault charges as other acts evidence. The prosecution argued below that this evidence would be admissible to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting, but this argument fails because V.G.'s delay was adequately explained by her fear of Salinas generally, not specifically by the domestic violence incident. The prosecution's attempt to introduce this evidence served only to paint Salinas as a violent person who must have committed the sexual assaults.

The circuit court's jury instruction regarding prior convictions (paragraph 27) could not cure the prejudice from this improper narrative. The damage was done during opening statements, when the prosecution framed the case as a "pattern of violence" matter.

## IV. THE INTIMIDATION EVIDENCE WAS NOT ADMISSIBLE IN A SEPARATE SEXUAL ASSAULT TRIAL

The prosecution argues that the intimidation evidence would be admissible in a separate sexual assault trial to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, V.G. adequately explained her delay without reference to the intimidation. She testified she didn't report the sexual assaults "because I just wanted him gone because I thought that with him gone it just wouldn't be going through all the things that I was going through, and I thought that was just — I was afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed of everything." This testimony provides the complete explanation for her delay.

Second, the intimidation was not causally connected to V.G.'s delay in reporting. The intimidation occurred after the domestic violence arrest and was designed to influence that case. V.G. didn't report the sexual assaults during this period because she didn't want to report them—not because she was afraid of retaliation for reporting. Her fear of Salinas was already present during the period of alleged sexual assaults.

Third, even if some minimal relevance existed, the probative value of the intimidation evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The intimidation evidence—including threats to kill himself and the child—created an inflammatory narrative that would have overwhelmed the sexual assault evidence in a separate trial.

In *State v. Sullivan*, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997), the court held that evidence of other acts must be "so strongly probative of a material issue in the case that its probative value outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice." The intimidation evidence here does not meet this standard.

## V. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY REVERSED THE CONVICTIONS

The court of appeals properly applied the law to the facts of this case when it reversed Salinas's convictions. The charges were improperly joined, and the error was not harmless.

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that joinder was harmless error. In *State v. Williams*, 197 Wis. 2d 537, 548, 541 N.W.2d 230, 235 (Ct. App. 1995), the court held that the prosecution must show "beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict." The prosecution cannot meet this burden here because the joined trial allowed the prosecution to present an inflammatory "pattern of violence" narrative that improperly appealed to the jury's emotions.

The defense closing argument highlighted the prejudice: Salinas's lawyer argued that V.G. and M.S. had "very vivid" memories of the domestic violence incident but V.G. had "very little recall of the sexual assaults." This argument recognized that the joined trial allowed the jury to conflate the domestic violence evidence with the sexual assault evidence.

The prosecution's case on the sexual assault charges was not overwhelming. V.G.'s testimony was the primary evidence, and the defense challenged her credibility. The introduction of the domestic violence evidence—which was more vivid and recent than the alleged sexual assaults—likely influenced the jury's assessment of V.G.'s credibility and Salinas's general character.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the court of appeals decision reversing the convictions and remand for separate trials on the intimidation and sexual assault charges. The charges were improperly joined because they arose from separate criminal episodes, lacked a common scheme or plan, and were joined only to allow the prosecution to present an inflammatory "pattern of violence" narrative that prejudiced the defendant. The court of appeals correctly identified these errors, and its decision should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the circuit court erred in joining the charges of intimidation of a witness with the charges of repeated sexual assault of a child. Specifically, we must determine if these offenses arose from a "common scheme or plan" as defined by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), or if they were distinct episodes that should have been tried separately to prevent prejudice to the defendant. After reviewing the arguments presented by the prosecution and the defense, and applying the relevant statutory and case law to the facts provided, the Court finds that the circuit court improperly joined the charges. The decision of the court of appeals reversing the conviction is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to join or sever charges is within the discretion of the circuit court. State v. Denny, 120 Wis. 2d 614, 622, 357 N.W.2d 12, 15 (Ct. App. 1984). However, whether joinder is authorized by statute is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. State v. Hamm, 146 Wis. 2d 130, 143, 430 N.W.2d 584, 589 (Ct. App. 1988). Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), joinder is appropriate only if the offenses "(1) are of the same or similar character, or (2) are based on the same act or transaction, or (3) are connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan." If the statutory requirements for joinder are not met, the circuit court errs as a matter of law.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The prosecution argues that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of a "continuous pattern of abuse" and a "cohesive strategy of intimidation," citing State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401–402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401–402 (Ct. App. 1995). While the facts indicate a disturbing history of abuse, the legal standard for joinder requires more than a general pattern of bad behavior; it requires that the offenses be "connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan."

The defense correctly identifies a critical temporal and factual disconnect in the record. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made between October 2009 and May 2010. The record explicitly states that these calls were an attempt to "pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The objective of this intimidation was to influence the outcome of the pending domestic violence case, specifically regarding the strangulation and battery of M.S.

Crucially, the sexual assaults were not reported to the authorities until May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. As a matter of logic and fact, Salinas could not have engaged in a common scheme to intimidate witnesses to conceal sexual assaults that were unknown to the authorities and, according to the record, had not yet been disclosed by the victim. The intimidation was directed at a specific legal proceeding (the domestic violence sentencing) regarding specific acts (strangulation and battery), not the suppression of the sexual assault allegations.

This case is distinguishable from State v. Eason, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429–430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), cited by the prosecution. In *Eason*, the intimidation was directly linked to the suppression of the sexual assault charges that were already the subject of the investigation. Here, the intimidation preceded the report of the sexual assaults and was aimed at a different set of crimes. Without a shared criminal objective linking the intimidation to the sexual assaults, the "common scheme" requirement of Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is not satisfied. See State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982) (holding that joinder requires a relationship between offenses that is "more than incidental or superficial").

**B. Admissibility of Other Acts Evidence**

Even if the offenses were deemed part of a common scheme, joinder requires that the evidence of one offense be admissible in a separate trial for the other. State v. Pharr, 115 Wis. 2d 334, 345, 340 N.W.2d 498, 501 (1983). The prosecution argues that the evidence of domestic violence and intimidation was admissible to explain the victim's delay in reporting the sexual assaults.

However, the defense persuasively argues that the probative value of the domestic violence evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The details of the domestic violence incident—Salinas strangling M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill the child—are highly inflammatory. While this evidence might be relevant to the intimidation charges, its relevance to the specific charge of repeated sexual assault of a child is tangential. The victim's delay in reporting was adequately explained by her testimony regarding fear, shame, and the general threat of being sent away, which did not rely on the specific details of the domestic violence incident.

Under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2), evidence of other acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show that the person acted in conformity therewith. The risk here is that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults because he was a "bad person" who committed violent acts against M.S., rather than on the specific evidence regarding V.G. As noted in State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997), evidence must be "so strongly probative of a material issue in the case that its probative value outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice." The vivid nature of the domestic violence evidence, coupled with the prosecution's "pattern of violence" narrative, suggests that the danger of unfair prejudice was high.

**C. Prejudice and Propensity Evidence**

The defense has demonstrated that the joinder resulted in significant prejudice. During opening statements, the prosecutor explicitly framed the case as a "pattern of violence," linking the domestic violence incident directly to the sexual assaults. This narrative invited the jury to infer that because Salinas was violent toward M.S., he must also be guilty of sexually assaulting V.G. This is precisely the type of propensity reasoning that joinder rules are designed to prevent. State v. Gardner, 130 Wis. 2d 227, 234, 387 N.W.2d 308, 312 (Ct. App. 1986).

The circuit court's limiting instruction, advising the jury that prior convictions are not proof of guilt, was insufficient to cure this prejudice. The prejudice occurred not just through the admission of the evidence, but through the prosecution's strategic use of the joined charges to paint a picture of Salinas as a violent predator, thereby lowering the State's burden of proof on the sexual assault charges. Given that the State's case on the sexual assaults relied heavily on the victim's testimony, which the defense challenged regarding her credibility and memory, the introduction of the vivid, uncontroverted evidence of the domestic violence likely had a decisive impact on the jury's verdict.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the charges of intimidation and sexual assault did not arise from a common scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence case and could not have been part of a scheme to conceal sexual assaults that had not yet been reported. Furthermore, the joinder of these charges improperly exposed the defendant to prejudice by allowing the jury to decide the sexual assault charges based on Salinas's violent character rather than the specific evidence of the sexual assaults. The error was not harmless. Therefore, the Court affirms the decision of the court of appeals reversing the convictions and remanding for separate trials.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of the offenses and ignores the fundamental legal error that the court of appeals correctly identified. The charges of intimidation of a witness and repeated sexual assault of a child were improperly joined under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) because they did not arise from a "common scheme or plan." The intimidation charges stemmed from Salinas's attempts to influence the domestic violence case—a proceeding that was entirely separate from the sexual assault allegations, which had not yet been reported when the intimidation occurred. The prosecution's attempt to recast these distinct episodes as a unified "course of conduct" contradicts the plain facts of the record and misapplies Wisconsin's joinder jurisprudence. This Court should affirm the court of appeals' decision and reinstate the separate trials.

## II. THE CHARGES DID NOT ARISE FROM A COMMON SCHEME OR PLAN

### A. The Intimidation Was Specifically Directed at the Domestic Violence Case

The prosecution argues that Salinas's intimidation of M.S. and V.G. was part of a "broader effort to suppress all evidence of his abuse." This argument ignores the explicit factual record. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls Salinas made from jail between October 2009 and May 2010. As the facts explicitly state, "the State asserted that transcripts from the phone calls show Salinas's attempt to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The purpose of the intimidation was to influence the outcome of the pending domestic violence case—specifically, the charges of domestic violence strangulation and suffocation and domestic violence battery to which Salinas had already entered Alford pleas.

The prosecution cannot overcome this fundamental temporal and factual disconnect. The sexual assaults were not reported until May 13, 2010—two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. As the court of appeals correctly observed, "as a matter of logic and fact, Salinas could not have engaged in a common scheme to intimidate witnesses to conceal sexual assaults that were unknown to the authorities and, according to the record, had not yet been disclosed by the victim."

### B. The Prosecution's Cited Cases Are Distinguishable

The prosecution relies heavily on State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401–402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401–402 (Ct. App. 1995), but that case is materially distinguishable. In Johnson, the intimidation was directly linked to the suppression of sexual assault charges that were already the subject of investigation. The defendant in Johnson made threats specifically to prevent the victim from reporting sexual abuse that was already known to authorities. Here, by contrast, the intimidation preceded any report of sexual assault and was aimed at a completely different set of crimes—the strangulation and battery of M.S.

The prosecution also cites State v. Eason, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429–430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), for the proposition that "intimidation to suppress testimony about unreported sexual assault was properly joined with the assault charges." However, the facts here demonstrate that the intimidation was not aimed at suppressing testimony about sexual assault—it was aimed at influencing a sentencing proceeding on entirely different charges. V.G. had not reported the sexual assaults at the time of the phone calls, so Salinas could not have been trying to suppress testimony about assaults that were unknown to anyone but himself and V.G.

### C. The "Logical Relationship" Standard Requires More Than Incidental Connection

The prosecution invokes the "logical relationship" standard from State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982), but ignores that court's explicit holding that joinder requires a relationship "more than incidental or superficial." The Lehman court rejected the notion that any logical connection between offenses justifies joinder. Here, the connection between the intimidation and the sexual assaults is precisely the type of incidental relationship that Lehman disapproves—both involve violence against household members, but that is insufficient to establish a common scheme or plan.

The prosecution's argument that Salinas's general "pattern of control" constitutes a common scheme reads the statutory requirement out of existence. Under the prosecution's theory, any defendant who commits multiple crimes against the same family members could have those crimes joined, regardless of when they occurred or whether they shared a common criminal objective. This is not the law. As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the "common scheme or plan" requirement demands that the offenses be "connected with" each other in a meaningful way—not merely that they involve the same victims or occurred in the same household.

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS NOT PROPERLY ADMISSIBLE IN A SEPARATE TRIAL

### A. The Domestic Violence Evidence Was Not Relevant to the Sexual Assault Charges

The prosecution argues that the domestic violence evidence was admissible to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting the sexual assaults. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, V.G. herself provided sufficient explanation for her delay without reference to the specific domestic violence incident. She testified that she did not report the sexual assaults "because I just wanted him gone because I thought that with him gone it just wouldn't be going through all the things that I was going through, and I thought that was just — I was afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed of everything." She also testified that she worried her mother "would be ashamed" and "blame herself." This testimony adequately explains the delay without requiring the highly prejudicial details of the domestic violence incident.

Second, even if some background evidence were relevant, the specific details admitted—the strangulation of M.S., the knife, the threat to kill the child—were not necessary to explain V.G.'s fear. The prosecution could have established V.G.'s fear through general testimony about Salinas's threats to "take her little brother away or send her away" without the vivid details of the October 26, 2009 incident.

### B. The Probative Value Was Outweighed by Unfair Prejudice

Under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2), evidence of other acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show that the person acted in conformity therewith. The court of appeals correctly applied this principle in State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997), holding that evidence must be "so strongly probative of a material issue in the case that its probative value outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice."

The domestic violence evidence in this case fails this standard. The details of Salinas strangling M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill his own child are horrifically inflammatory. While this evidence might have some minimal relevance to explain V.G.'s general fear, its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger that the jury would convict Salinas of the sexual assaults because he was a "bad person" who committed violent acts—not because of specific evidence regarding V.G.'s allegations.

The prosecution argues that this evidence was admissible to show "motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident." However, the prosecution never articulated how the specific domestic violence incident proved any of these elements regarding the sexual assault charges. The domestic violence incident did not show motive for the sexual assaults (which allegedly began when V.G. was 13), did not show opportunity (which existed regardless of the domestic violence), and did not show any other relevant element. The evidence was admitted, if at all, solely to paint Salinas as a violent predator—a classic improper character inference.

## IV. THE JOINER CAUSED UNFAIR PREJUDICE

### A. The Prosecution's Opening Statement Invited Propensity Reasoning

The most damaging aspect of this case was the prosecution's explicit framing of the trial as a "pattern of violence" case. During opening statement, the prosecutor told the jury:

"The evidence would show a 'pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, [that] repeated sexual assault of a child occurred to the victim, [V.G.], occurred within a family environment where her mother, [M.S.], was also a victim.'"

This statement directly invited the jury to infer that because Salinas committed domestic violence, he must also be guilty of sexual assault. This is precisely the type of propensity reasoning that joinder rules are designed to prevent. State v. Gardner, 130 Wis. 2d 227, 234, 387 N.W.2d 308, 312 (Ct. App. 1986).

The prosecution argues that this narrative was "legally permissible context" to explain the victims' behavior. But the prosecution went far beyond providing context—it explicitly argued that the domestic violence incident was connected to the sexual assaults in a way that proved Salinas's guilt. This is improper and constitutes the exact prejudice that requires severance.

### B. The Limiting Instructions Were Insufficient to Cure the Prejudice

The prosecution relies on the circuit court's jury instructions to argue that any potential prejudice was cured. However, the prejudice in this case occurred not through the admission of evidence alone, but through the prosecution's strategic use of that evidence in opening statement and closing argument. The prosecution's "pattern of violence" narrative was designed to inflame the jury's passions and lower the State's burden of proof.

As the court of appeals correctly noted, "the prejudice occurred not just through the admission of the evidence, but through the prosecution's strategic use of the joined charges to paint a picture of Salinas as a violent predator, thereby lowering the State's burden of proof on the sexual assault charges." The circuit court's generic instruction that "prior convictions are not proof of guilt" was insufficient to counteract this specific and targeted assault on Salinas's character.

Furthermore, the prosecution's closing argument reinforced the improper propensity inference:

"[Salinas] is very concerned. To this point he's been able to keep them from calling the police. He's been able to intimidate them, use threats, use violence to make sure the police don't get involved."

This argument explicitly linked the domestic violence to the sexual assaults in a way that invited the jury to use character-based reasoning. The limiting instructions could not possibly have cured this prejudice.

### C. The Evidence on the Sexual Assaults Was Weak

The prosecution's case on the sexual assaults relied heavily on V.G.'s testimony, which the defense challenged regarding her credibility and memory. Salinas's lawyer argued in closing that V.G. had "very little recall of the sexual assaults" and that the "glossing over" of the allegations should convince the jury they never happened. In this context, the introduction of the vivid, uncontroverted evidence of the domestic violence likely had a decisive impact on the jury's verdict. The jury did not need to believe V.G.'s testimony about the sexual assaults if they already believed Salinas was a violent monster who must have committed those acts.

## V. CONCLUSION

The prosecution has not demonstrated that the charges arose from a common scheme or plan. The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence case and could not have been aimed at concealing sexual assaults that had not yet been reported. The evidence of the domestic violence was not properly admissible in a sexual assault trial because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. And the joinder resulted in significant prejudice through the prosecution's improper "pattern of violence" narrative.

The court of appeals correctly applied the law to the facts of this case. The charges should have been tried separately. This Court should affirm the decision of the court of appeals and remand for separate trials.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the circuit court erred in joining the charges of intimidation of a witness with the charges of repeated sexual assault of a child. Specifically, we must determine if these offenses arose from a "common scheme or plan" as defined by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), or if they were distinct episodes that should have been tried separately to prevent prejudice to the defendant. After reviewing the rebuttals of the prosecution and the defense, and applying the relevant statutory and case law to the facts provided, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. The circuit court improperly joined the charges. The decision of the court of appeals reversing the conviction is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to join or sever charges is within the discretion of the circuit court. State v. Denny, 120 Wis. 2d 614, 622, 357 N.W.2d 12, 15 (Ct. App. 1984). However, whether joinder is authorized by statute is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. State v. Hamm, 146 Wis. 2d 130, 143, 430 N.W.2d 584, 589 (Ct. App. 1988). Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), joinder is appropriate only if the offenses "(1) are of the same or similar character, or (2) are based on the same act or transaction, or (3) are connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan."

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The prosecution argues that the charges formed a "single course of conduct" under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), citing State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982), for the proposition that a "logical relationship" or "mutual connection" is sufficient. The prosecution contends that Salinas's intimidation was part of a "broader effort to suppress all evidence of his abuse," including the sexual assaults.

The defense persuasively counters that this argument ignores the fundamental temporal and factual disconnect in the record. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made between October 2009 and May 2010. The record explicitly states that these calls were an attempt to "pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The objective of this intimidation was to influence the outcome of the pending domestic violence case, specifically regarding the strangulation and battery of M.S.

Crucially, the sexual assaults were not reported to the authorities until May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. As a matter of logic and fact, Salinas could not have engaged in a common scheme to intimidate witnesses to conceal sexual assaults that were unknown to the authorities and, according to the record, had not yet been disclosed by the victim. The intimidation was directed at a specific legal proceeding (the domestic violence sentencing) regarding specific acts (strangulation and battery), not the suppression of the sexual assault allegations.

The prosecution relies on State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401–402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401–402 (Ct. App. 1995), and State v. Eason, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429–430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), to support the joinder of intimidation with sexual assault charges. However, as the defense correctly notes, these cases are distinguishable. In *Johnson* and *Eason*, the intimidation was directly linked to the suppression of sexual assault charges that were already the subject of investigation or disclosure. Here, the intimidation preceded the report of the sexual assaults and was aimed at a different set of crimes. Without a shared criminal objective linking the intimidation to the sexual assaults, the "common scheme" requirement of Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is not satisfied. See State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982) (holding that joinder requires a relationship between offenses that is "more than incidental or superficial").

**B. Admissibility of Other Acts Evidence**

Even if the offenses were deemed part of a common scheme, joinder requires that the evidence of one offense be admissible in a separate trial for the other. State v. Pharr, 115 Wis. 2d 334, 345, 340 N.W.2d 498, 501 (1983). The prosecution argues that the evidence of domestic violence and intimidation was admissible to explain the victim's delay in reporting the sexual assaults, citing State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997).

The defense correctly argues that the probative value of the domestic violence evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. V.G. testified that she did not report the sexual assaults because she was "afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed," and specifically feared Salinas would "take her little brother away or send her away." This testimony provided a sufficient explanation for her delay without the introduction of the specific, inflammatory details of the October 26, 2009 domestic violence incident—Salinas strangling M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill the child.

Under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2), evidence of other acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show that the person acted in conformity therewith. The risk here is that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults because he was a "bad person" who committed violent acts against M.S., rather than on the specific evidence regarding V.G. As noted in State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997), evidence must be "so strongly probative of a material issue in the case that its probative value outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice." The vivid nature of the domestic violence evidence, coupled with the prosecution's "pattern of violence" narrative, suggests that the danger of unfair prejudice was high.

**C. Prejudice and Propensity Evidence**

The defense has demonstrated that the joinder resulted in significant prejudice. The prosecution argues that the circuit court's limiting instructions cured any potential prejudice, citing State v. Williams, 197 Wis. 2d 537, 548, 541 N.W.2d 230, 235 (Ct. App. 1995). However, the prejudice occurred not just through the admission of the evidence, but through the prosecution's strategic use of the joined charges to paint a picture of Salinas as a violent predator.

During opening statements, the prosecutor explicitly framed the case as a "pattern of violence," linking the domestic violence incident directly to the sexual assaults. This narrative invited the jury to infer that because Salinas was violent toward M.S., he must also be guilty of sexually assaulting V.G. This is precisely the type of propensity reasoning that joinder rules are designed to prevent. State v. Gardner, 130 Wis. 2d 227, 234, 387 N.W.2d 308, 312 (Ct. App. 1986).

The circuit court's limiting instruction, advising the jury that prior convictions are not proof of guilt, was insufficient to cure this prejudice. Given that the State's case on the sexual assaults relied heavily on the victim's testimony, which the defense challenged regarding her credibility and memory, the introduction of the vivid, uncontroverted evidence of the domestic violence likely had a decisive impact on the jury's verdict.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the charges of intimidation and sexual assault did not arise from a common scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence case and could not have been part of a scheme to conceal sexual assaults that had not yet been reported. Furthermore, the joinder of these charges improperly exposed the defendant to prejudice by allowing the jury to decide the sexual assault charges based on Salinas's violent character rather than the specific evidence of the sexual assaults. The error was not harmless. Therefore, the Court affirms the decision of the court of appeals reversing the convictions and remanding for separate trials.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## State v. Salinas
### Court of Appeals of Wisconsin (Pending Review)

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF POSITION

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's closing argument, while artfully crafted, fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the charges and the requirements for joinder under Wisconsin law. The defense maintains that the circuit court erred in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges, and that this error was not harmless. The court of appeals correctly reversed the convictions, and this Court should affirm that decision.

The prosecution asks this Court to view the charges in the "context of Salinas's entire conduct" and to find a "single, unified scheme of domination and control." However, the law requires more than a vague narrative of "control" to justify joinder. Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) expressly requires that the offenses arise from a "common scheme or plan," and the case law interprets this requirement strictly. The defense will demonstrate that the prosecution's arguments fail to satisfy this standard and that the joinder resulted in substantial prejudice to the defendant.

---

## II. REBUTTAL: THE CHARGES DID NOT ARISE FROM A COMMON SCHEME OR PLAN

### A. The Prosecution's "Common Scheme" Argument Fails as a Matter of Law

The prosecution contends that Salinas's intimidation was part of a "broader effort to suppress all evidence of his abuse, including the sexual assaults." This argument, however, ignores the explicit factual record and collapses under scrutiny.

The record is unambiguous that Salinas's phone calls from jail were made "to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The objective was specifically directed at the pending domestic violence case—specifically, the strangulation and battery charges arising from the October 26, 2009 incident.

Critically, the sexual assaults were not reported to authorities until May 13, 2010—two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. As this Court has recognized, Salinas could not have engaged in a scheme to conceal sexual assaults that were unknown to the authorities and had not yet been disclosed by the victim. The intimidation was directed at a specific legal proceeding regarding specific acts, not the suppression of allegations that did not yet exist.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Johnson*, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401-402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401-402 (Ct. App. 1995), and *State v. Eason*, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429-430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), is misplaced. In both *Johnson* and *Eason*, the intimidation was directly linked to the suppression of sexual assault charges that were already the subject of investigation or disclosure. In *Johnson*, the defendant threatened the victim to prevent her from testifying about ongoing sexual abuse. In *Eason*, the defendant attempted to influence a witness to recant allegations that had already been reported.

Here, by contrast, the intimidation preceded the report of the sexual assaults and was aimed at a different set of crimes. The prosecution cannot establish a "common scheme" where the target of the intimidation was entirely unrelated to the crimes that would later form the basis of the sexual assault charges. As this Court noted in *State v. Lehman*, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982), joinder requires a relationship between offenses that is "more than incidental or superficial." The prosecution has failed to establish any such relationship.

### B. The "Logical Relationship" Argument Does Not Support Joinder

The prosecution argues that the charges share a "logical relationship" because they involve the same victims, same household, and same pattern of conduct. However, this argument conflates similarity with common scheme.

Wisconsin law permits joinder when offenses are "of the same or similar character," "based on the same act or transaction," or "connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan." Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The prosecution does not—and cannot—argue that these offenses are of the same or similar character (they are distinctly different crimes: sexual assault versus intimidation), nor that they are based on the same act or transaction (the phone calls occurred in 2009-2010, while the sexual assaults allegedly occurred from 2007-2009).

This leaves only the "common scheme or plan" prong, which the prosecution attempts to satisfy through the vague assertion of a "pattern of control." However, mere similarity of victims or general thematic overlap does not constitute a common scheme. As this Court recognized in *Lehman*, joinder requires a "mutual connection" that is more than incidental. The fact that Salinas committed multiple crimes against multiple victims in the same household does not create a "common scheme" linking the intimidation of witnesses in the domestic violence case to the sexual assault of V.G.

## III. REBUTTAL: THE EVIDENCE WAS NOT ADMISSIBLE IN A SEPARATE TRIAL

### A. The "Delay in Reporting" Evidence Was Not Necessary

The prosecution argues that the domestic violence evidence was admissible to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting the sexual assaults. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, V.G. provided sufficient testimony explaining her delay without the introduction of the specific domestic violence evidence. She testified that she did not report the sexual assaults "because I just wanted him gone because I thought that with him gone it just wouldn't be going through all the things that I was going through, and I thought that was just — I was afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed of everything." She also testified that she was worried her mom "would be ashamed" and "blame herself." This testimony provided a sufficient basis for the jury to understand the delay without the inflammatory details of the October 26, 2009 incident.

Second, even if some evidence of Salinas's violence was relevant to explain the delay, the probative value of the specific evidence introduced—Salinas strangling M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill his own child—was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2), evidence of other acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show that the person acted in conformity therewith.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Sullivan*, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997), is unavailing. In *Sullivan*, this Court held that evidence of other acts may be admissible when it is "so strongly probative of a material issue in the case that its probative value outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice." Here, the domestic violence evidence was not strongly probative of any material issue in the sexual assault case—it merely painted Salinas as a violent person, inviting the jury to convict him based on character rather than evidence of the specific crimes charged.

### B. The Prosecution's Narrative Was Improper Propensity Reasoning

The prosecution's assertion that its argument was "not about Salinas's character" is directly contradicted by the record. During opening statements, the prosecutor told the jury the evidence would show a "pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, [that] repeated sexual assault of a child occurred to the victim." This narrative invited the jury to infer that because Salinas was violent toward M.S., he must also be guilty of sexually assaulting V.G.

This is precisely the type of propensity reasoning that joinder rules are designed to prevent. As this Court noted in *State v. Gardner*, 130 Wis. 2d 227, 234, 387 N.W.2d 308, 312 (Ct. App. 1986), joinder can result in prejudice when the jury uses evidence of one crime to infer criminal disposition regarding another crime. The prosecution's "pattern of violence" argument was a textbook example of this improper reasoning.

## IV. REBUTTAL: THE PREJUDICE WAS NOT CURED BY JURY INSTRUCTIONS

### A. The Limiting Instructions Were Inadequate

The prosecution argues that the circuit court's limiting instructions cured any potential prejudice. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the circuit court's instruction regarding prior convictions only addressed the fact of Salinas's prior convictions, not the improper use of the domestic violence evidence to infer guilt on the sexual assault charges. The court instructed the jury that "evidence has been received in this case that the defendant, Luis Salinas, has been convicted of crimes" and that "you must not use it for any other purpose." However, the court did not instruct the jury that it could not use the domestic violence evidence—which was not the subject of a prior conviction in this trial—to infer that Salinas was guilty of the sexual assaults.

Second, as this Court recognized in *State v. Williams*, 197 Wis. 2d 537, 548, 541 N.W.2d 230, 235 (Ct. App. 1995), limiting instructions can cure prejudice only when the evidence is relevant to a legitimate issue in the case. Here, the domestic violence evidence was not relevant to any legitimate issue in the sexual assault trial—it was relevant only to show Salinas's bad character. No limiting instruction could cure the fundamental problem of admitting irrelevant, prejudicial evidence.

### B. The Prejudice Was Decisive

The prosecution's case on the sexual assaults relied heavily on V.G.'s testimony, which the defense challenged regarding her credibility and memory. Salinas's lawyer argued that V.G. had "very little recall of the sexual assaults" while having "very vivid" memories of the domestic violence incident. This defense strategy was undermined by the introduction of the vivid domestic violence evidence, which likely had a decisive impact on the jury's verdict.

The prosecution's closing argument explicitly invoked the domestic violence incident to argue that Salinas was capable of the sexual assaults. The prosecutor told the jury that October 26, 2009 was "a day of horror" and asked them to connect this "day of horror" to the sexual assault allegations. This argument transformed the trial from a determination of guilt on the specific charges into a referendum on Salinas's character. The limiting instructions were insufficient to counteract this improper reasoning.

The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges arose from a common scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence case and could not have been part of a scheme to conceal sexual assaults that had not yet been reported. Furthermore, the joinder of these charges improperly exposed Salinas to prejudice by allowing the jury to decide the sexual assault charges based on his violent character rather than the specific evidence regarding the sexual assaults.

The court of appeals correctly reversed the convictions, and this Court should affirm that decision. The charges should have been tried separately, and the introduction of the domestic violence evidence in the sexual assault trial constituted reversible error.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the circuit court erred in joining the charges of intimidation of a witness with the charges of repeated sexual assault of a child. Specifically, we must determine if these offenses arose from a "common scheme or plan" as defined by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), or if they were distinct episodes that should have been tried separately to prevent prejudice to the defendant. After reviewing the closing arguments of the prosecution and the defense, and applying the relevant statutory and case law to the facts provided, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The circuit court improperly joined the charges. The decision of the court of appeals reversing the conviction is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to join or sever charges is within the discretion of the circuit court. State v. Denny, 120 Wis. 2d 614, 622, 357 N.W.2d 12, 15 (Ct. App. 1984). However, whether joinder is authorized by statute is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. State v. Hamm, 146 Wis. 2d 130, 143, 430 N.W.2d 584, 589 (Ct. App. 1988). Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), joinder is appropriate only if the offenses "(1) are of the same or similar character, or (2) are based on the same act or transaction, or (3) are connected with or constitute parts of a common scheme or plan."

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The prosecution argues that the charges formed a "single course of conduct" under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), contending that Salinas's intimidation was part of a "broader effort to suppress all evidence of his abuse," including the sexual assaults. The prosecution relies on State v. Johnson, 193 Wis. 2d 392, 401–402, 534 N.W.2d 397, 401–402 (Ct. App. 1995), and State v. Eason, 178 Wis. 2d 422, 429–430, 504 N.W.2d 415, 418 (Ct. App. 1993), to support the proposition that intimidation to suppress testimony is joinable with assault charges.

The defense persuasively counters that this argument ignores the fundamental temporal and factual disconnect in the record. The record explicitly states that the phone calls constituting the intimidation were made to "pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The objective of this intimidation was to influence the outcome of the pending domestic violence case, specifically regarding the strangulation and battery of M.S.

Crucially, the sexual assaults were not reported to the authorities until May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. As a matter of logic and fact, Salinas could not have engaged in a common scheme to intimidate witnesses to conceal sexual assaults that were unknown to the authorities and, according to the record, had not yet been disclosed by the victim. The intimidation was directed at a specific legal proceeding (the domestic violence sentencing) regarding specific acts (strangulation and battery), not the suppression of the sexual assault allegations.

The prosecution's reliance on *Johnson* and *Eason* is misplaced. In those cases, the intimidation was directly linked to the suppression of sexual assault charges that were already the subject of investigation or disclosure. Here, the intimidation preceded the report of the sexual assaults and was aimed at a different set of crimes. Without a shared criminal objective linking the intimidation to the sexual assaults, the "common scheme" requirement of Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is not satisfied. See State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 322 N.W.2d 174, 178 (Ct. App. 1982) (holding that joinder requires a relationship between offenses that is "more than incidental or superficial").

**B. Admissibility of Other Acts Evidence**

Even if the offenses were deemed part of a common scheme, joinder requires that the evidence of one offense be admissible in a separate trial for the other. State v. Pharr, 115 Wis. 2d 334, 345, 340 N.W.2d 498, 501 (1983). The prosecution argues that the evidence of domestic violence and intimidation was admissible to explain the victim's delay in reporting the sexual assaults, citing State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997).

The defense correctly argues that the probative value of the domestic violence evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. V.G. testified that she did not report the sexual assaults because she was "afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed," and specifically feared Salinas would "take her little brother away or send her away." This testimony provided a sufficient explanation for her delay without the introduction of the specific, inflammatory details of the October 26, 2009 domestic violence incident—Salinas strangling M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill the child.

Under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2), evidence of other acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show that the person acted in conformity therewith. The risk here is that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults because he was a "bad person" who committed violent acts against M.S., rather than on the specific evidence regarding V.G. As noted in State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 672, 686, 575 N.W.2d 21, 27 (Ct. App. 1997), evidence must be "so strongly probative of a material issue in the case that its probative value outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice." The vivid nature of the domestic violence evidence, coupled with the prosecution's "pattern of violence" narrative, suggests that the danger of unfair prejudice was high.

**C. Prejudice and Propensity Evidence**

The defense has demonstrated that the joinder resulted in significant prejudice. The prosecution argues that the circuit court's limiting instructions cured any potential prejudice, citing State v. Williams, 197 Wis. 2d 537, 548, 541 N.W.2d 230, 235 (Ct. App. 1995). However, the prejudice occurred not just through the admission of the evidence, but through the prosecution's strategic use of the joined charges to paint a picture of Salinas as a violent predator.

During opening statements, the prosecutor explicitly framed the case as a "pattern of violence," linking the domestic violence incident directly to the sexual assaults. This narrative invited the jury to infer that because Salinas was violent toward M.S., he must also be guilty of sexually assaulting V.G. This is precisely the type of propensity reasoning that joinder rules are designed to prevent. State v. Gardner, 130 Wis. 2d 227, 234, 387 N.W.2d 308, 312 (Ct. App. 1986).

The circuit court's limiting instruction, advising the jury that prior convictions are not proof of guilt, was insufficient to cure this prejudice. Given that the State's case on the sexual assaults relied heavily on the victim's testimony, which the defense challenged regarding her credibility and memory, the introduction of the vivid, uncontroverted evidence of the domestic violence likely had a decisive impact on the jury's verdict.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the charges of intimidation and sexual assault did not arise from a common scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence case and could not have been part of a scheme to conceal sexual assaults that had not yet been reported. Furthermore, the joinder of these charges improperly exposed the defendant to prejudice by allowing the jury to decide the sexual assault charges based on Salinas's violent character rather than the specific evidence of the sexual assaults. The error was not harmless. Therefore, the Court affirms the decision of the court of appeals reversing the convictions and remanding for separate trials.
