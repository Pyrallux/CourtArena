Your Honor, the Plaintiff respectfully submits that the Director erred in denying compensation because the aggravation of Mr. Van Ness's asthma was proximately caused by his employment, as established under Hawai'i law for occupational diseases. The controlling precedent is *Flor v. This standard directly governs the proximate causation analysis for occupational diseases, as *Flor* establishes that proximate causation is determined by whether the employment created the specific conditions causing the disease—not by a separate "unitary test." 

Here, the evidence demonstrates that Mr. Van Ness's job at Lahainaluna High School presented conditions peculiar to his employment that proximately caused his asthma exacerbation. ## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

The Defendant-Appellant Department of Education (hereinafter "DOE" or "Defendant") respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the Plaintiff's appeal of the Director's decision denying workers' compensation benefits. The Plaintiff, Michael Van Ness, seeks compensation for an alleged aggravation of his pre-existing asthma condition while employed as a technology coordinator at Lahainaluna High School on Maui.

The Director correctly denied compensation because the Plaintiff failed to establish that his alleged injury arose out of and in the course of his employment. The Plaintiff's claim rests on the theory that exposure to "vog" (volcanic smoke and pollution) at Lahainaluna High School aggravated his pre-existing asthma. However, under the controlling legal standard established by the Hawai'i Supreme Court in *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. 56, 26 P.3d 567 (2001), the Plaintiff cannot establish that his injury arose out of his employment because the alleged causative agent—vog—is a general environmental condition that exists throughout the island of Maui and is not peculiar to the Plaintiff's employment at Lahainaluna High School.

Furthermore, the evidence demonstrates that the Plaintiff's pre-existing asthma condition was subject to multiple exacerbating factors independent of his employment, including his pre-existing medical condition, his decision to reside on Maui during the relevant time period, and his physical condition following surgery. The Director properly credited the opinion of the independent medical examiner, Dr. Ajit S. Arora, who concluded that the causal relationship between the Plaintiff's employment and his alleged injury was not established.

For these reasons, the Director's decision should be affirmed.

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS PERTINENT TO DEFENSE

Critically, the Plaintiff's own testimony established that he chose to live on Maui during the relevant time period. ## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Governing Standard for Workers' Compensation in Hawai'i

Under Hawai'i Revised Statutes Chapter 386, workers' compensation benefits are available only when an employee sustains a personal injury "arising out of and in the course of employment." HRS § 386-3. The "arising out of" requirement necessitates a causal connection between the employment and the injury. *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 62, 26 P.3d at 573.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, the Hawai'i Supreme Court addressed the standard for determining whether an injury "arises out of" employment in the context of an occupational disease. The Court held that "for injuries by disease, the 'arising out of' requirement is satisfied when the disease results from conditions peculiar to the employment." *Id.* at 63, 26 P.3d at 574 (emphasis added).

The Court explicitly rejected a "unitary test" approach and held that proximate causation is determined by whether the employment created the specific conditions causing the disease. *Id.* However, critically, the Court emphasized that the conditions must be "peculiar to the employment"—meaning they must be conditions to which the public generally is not exposed. *Id.*

### B. The Plaintiff's Claim Fails Because Vog Is Not Peculiar to His Employment

The Plaintiff cannot satisfy the "arising out of" requirement because the alleged causative agent—vog—is a general environmental condition that exists throughout the island of Maui and is not peculiar to the Plaintiff's employment at Lahainaluna High School.

Vog is a well-known environmental phenomenon on the island of Maui resulting from volcanic emissions from Haleakalā volcano. The Plaintiff's own testimony established that vog was present throughout the island during the relevant time period. The Plaintiff testified that "there was a significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006." *Id.* He further testified that on days when the vog was severe, he was unable to see the administrative building "halfway down the campus" from the library. *Id.*

The Plaintiff acknowledged that he lived on Maui during this period and that his residence was subject to the same vog conditions as his workplace. The Plaintiff chose to live on Maui, and his alleged exposure to vog was not limited to his employment. This is analogous to cases where employees claim injuries from general environmental conditions, such as pollution or weather, that exist in the community where the employee works and resides.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, the Court emphasized that for a disease to "arise out of" employment, the employment must create conditions to which the public generally is not exposed. 96 Haw. at 63, 26 P.3d at 574. Here, the Plaintiff cannot establish that vog exposure is peculiar to his employment at Lahainaluna High School because vog exists throughout Maui and affects all residents and visitors to the island.

Dr. Arora correctly observed in his report that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." This observation reflects the fundamental flaw in the Plaintiff's claim: the alleged causative agent is a general environmental condition, not a condition peculiar to the Plaintiff's employment.

### C. The Plaintiff's Pre-Existing Condition and Voluntary Exposure

The Plaintiff had a pre-existing asthma condition that predated his employment at Lahainaluna High School. The Plaintiff's asthma was manageable with medication and avoidance of triggers. The Plaintiff testified that while living on O'ahu prior to his transfer to Maui, he "never exhausted the entire thirteen-month supply" of his inhaler and "only refilled his prescription when the inhalers expired." *Id.*

The Plaintiff chose to accept employment on Maui and chose to reside on Maui during the relevant time period. The Plaintiff's voluntary exposure to general environmental conditions on Maui—vog—is not sufficient to establish that his alleged injury arose out of his employment. The Plaintiff was not required to work on Maui, and he was not required to reside on Maui. His alleged exposure to vog was a result of his personal choices, not his employment.

This case is distinguishable from cases where the employment specifically exposes employees to hazardous conditions that are not present in the general environment. Here, the alleged causative agent (vog) is present throughout Maui, and the Plaintiff's alleged injury resulted from his voluntary decision to live and work on Maui, not from any condition peculiar to his employment.

### D. The Director Properly Credited the Medical Evidence

The Director properly credited the opinion of Dr. Arora, who concluded that the causal relationship between the Plaintiff's employment and his alleged injury was not established. Dr. Arora's observation that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" correctly identifies the legal deficiency in the Plaintiff's claim.

The Plaintiff argues that Dr. Arora's opinion should be discounted because he did not have access to the Plaintiff's medical records at the time of his examination. However, Dr. Arora's observation about vog not being unique to the school environment does not require medical records—it is a factual observation about the nature of vog as an environmental phenomenon.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations' decision denying Claimant Michael Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution (Claimant) and the Defense (Department of Education) regarding the preliminary ruling. Consequently, the preliminary ruling is withdrawn, and the Director's decision denying the claim is affirmed.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The central legal issue is whether Claimant's respiratory injury "arose out of" his employment. The controlling precedent in Hawai'i for occupational diseases is *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. 56, 26 P.3d 567 (2001). In *Flor*, the Hawai'i Supreme Court held that "for injuries by disease, the 'arising out of' requirement is satisfied when the disease results from conditions peculiar to the employment." *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 63, 26 P.3d at 574.

The Court must address the Prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Flor*. The Prosecution argues that *Flor* applies only to the "nature of employment" prong of the "arising out of" inquiry, and that proximate causation should be determined by the "unitary test"—whether employment was a contributing factor. This argument is rejected. As the Defense correctly notes, *Flor* explicitly rejected the unitary test for occupational diseases. The *Flor* Court stated that the unitary test "merely asks whether employment was a contributing factor to the injury" and "fails to distinguish between those injuries that occur as a result of the nature of the employment and those that occur as a result of other factors." *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 62, 26 P.3d at 573. Therefore, the "conditions peculiar to the employment" test is the sole standard for determining whether a disease, such as Claimant's asthma exacerbation, arises out of employment.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Application of the "Conditions Peculiar to the Employment" Test**

Under *Flor*, the Claimant must demonstrate that his disease resulted from conditions peculiar to his employment. The Defense argues persuasively that the vog exposure at issue was a general environmental condition, not one peculiar to the Claimant's employment.

The record establishes that vog was present throughout the island of Maui during the relevant period. Claimant testified that there was a "significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006" and that he observed volcanic ash accumulating on his car while driving. While the Prosecution argues that the *combination* of physical exertion (climbing stairs) and vog exposure created a condition peculiar to the employment, this argument fails under the *Flor* standard.

The physical exertion required of Claimant—climbing stairs and traversing the campus—does not transform a general environmental hazard (vog) into a condition peculiar to the employment. The risk of breathing vog while exerting oneself is not unique to the workplace; it is a risk faced by any resident of Maui who engages in physical activity outdoors during a vog event. The fact that Claimant's home was air-conditioned and his workplace was not does not render the vog itself a condition of his employment. Rather, it simply means he was exposed to a general environmental condition while at work. Because vog is a general risk to the public on Maui, it cannot satisfy the "conditions peculiar to the employment" requirement. *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 63, 26 P.3d at 574.

**B. Evaluation of Medical Evidence and Director's Findings**

The Director relied on the report of Dr. Arora to deny the claim. The Prosecution criticized this reliance, noting that Dr. Arora did not have access to Claimant's full medical records. However, Dr. Arora's conclusion that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" is consistent with the legal determination that vog is a general environmental condition. While Dr. Mathison opined that vog exposure contributed to Claimant's symptoms and recommended he avoid such exposure, this medical causation does not equate to legal causation under the "arising out of" standard. Even if the employment contributed to the injury in a medical sense, the injury is not compensable if it did not arise out of conditions peculiar to the employment.

The Director was entitled to credit Dr. Arora's opinion regarding the nature of the exposure, as it aligns with the undisputed fact that vog is an island-wide phenomenon. The Director's finding that the injury was not work-related because the exposure was not unique to the school is supported by substantial evidence and is consistent with the *Flor* standard.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the Director did not err in denying Claimant's workers' compensation claim. The Prosecution's attempt to apply the unitary test is contrary to the holding of *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. 56, 26 P.3d 567 (2001). The evidence demonstrates that the vog exposure causing Claimant's injury was a general environmental condition, not a condition peculiar to his employment. Therefore, the injury did not "arise out of" his employment.

The dispositive legal issue is whether Claimant's injury, an aggravation of his pre-existing asthma, "arose out of" his employment. The controlling precedent in Hawai'i is *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. 56, 26 P.3d 567 (2001). In *Flor*, the Hawai'i Supreme Court held that "for injuries by disease, the 'arising out of' requirement is satisfied when the disease results from conditions peculiar to the employment." *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 63, 26 P.3d at 574. The Court further rejected the "unitary test," which merely asks whether employment was a contributing factor, finding that it "fails to distinguish between those injuries that occur as a result of the nature of the employment and those that occur as a result of other factors." *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 62, 26 P.3d at 573.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Prosecution's Attempt to Distinguish *Flor* is Unpersuasive**

The Prosecution argues that *Flor* applies only to "new occupational diseases" and that the "unitary test" should govern claims involving the aggravation of pre-existing conditions. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive for two reasons.

First, the plain language of *Flor* applies to "injuries by disease." An aggravation of a pre-existing disease constitutes an injury by disease. The *Flor* Court did not limit its holding to instances where the disease originated *de novo* from the employment; rather, it focused on whether the injury resulted from conditions peculiar to the employment. The policy rationale behind *Flor*—preventing the workers' compensation system from becoming a substitute for general health insurance for environmental hazards—applies with equal force to aggravations. If the condition causing the aggravation is a general environmental risk, it remains non-compensable regardless of whether the underlying disease was pre-existing.

Second, the Prosecution has not cited any authority, nor is the Court aware of any, holding that the "conditions peculiar to the employment" test applies only to new diseases. The Defense correctly notes that this Court's preliminary ruling explicitly established that the "conditions peculiar to the employment" test is the "sole standard" for determining whether a disease, such as Claimant's asthma exacerbation, arises out of employment. The Prosecution's request to apply the unitary test is effectively a request to overrule the controlling precedent of *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. 56, 26 P.3d 567 (2001), which this Court cannot do.

**B. Application of the "Conditions Peculiar to the Employment" Test**

Under the *Flor* standard, the Claimant must demonstrate that his injury resulted from conditions peculiar to his employment. The record clearly establishes that the vog exposure at issue was a general environmental condition, not one peculiar to the Claimant's employment.

Claimant testified that there was a "significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006" and that volcanic ash accumulated on his car and windshield while driving. This testimony confirms that vog was an island-wide phenomenon. Dr. Arora, the Independent Medical Examiner, corroborated this, noting that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely."

The Prosecution argues that the *combination* of physical exertion (climbing stairs) and vog exposure created a condition peculiar to the employment. The Court rejects this contention. The risk of breathing vog while exerting oneself is not unique to the workplace; it is a risk faced by any resident of Maui who engages in physical activity outdoors during a vog event. The physical exertion required of Claimant—climbing stairs and traversing the campus—does not transform a general environmental hazard into a condition peculiar to the employment. *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. at 63, 26 P.3d at 574.

The Prosecution also points to the lack of air conditioning and the presence of dust in the school buildings. However, these factors do not alter the nature of the vog exposure. The vog entered the buildings through open windows; it was not generated by the school's operations. The fact that Claimant's home was air-conditioned and his workplace was not may explain the *degree* of exposure, but it does not render the exposure itself a condition peculiar to the employment. The source of the injury (vog) remains a general risk to the public.

**C. The Director's Findings are Supported by Substantial Evidence**

The Director denied the claim, finding that the injury was not work-related because the exposure was not unique to the school. The Director relied on Dr. Arora's report, which concluded that vog is not a factor associated with the school uniquely. While the Prosecution emphasizes Dr. Mathison's opinion that vog contributed to Claimant's symptoms, medical causation is not equivalent to legal causation under the *Flor* standard. Even if the employment contributed to the injury in a medical sense, the injury is not compensable if it did not arise out of conditions peculiar to the employment. The Director's findings are supported by substantial evidence in the record and are consistent with the *Flor* standard.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the Director did not err in denying Claimant's workers' compensation claim. The Prosecution's attempt to limit *Flor v. Holguin*, 96 Haw. 56, 26 P.3d 567 (2001), to new diseases is contrary to the precedent's plain language and intent. The evidence demonstrates that the vog exposure causing Claimant's injury was a general environmental condition, not a condition peculiar to his employment. Therefore, the injury did not "arise out of" his employment.

**THEREFORE**, it is ORDERED that the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations denying benefits is AFFIRMED.
