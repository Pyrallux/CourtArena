FACTS:
{¶ 1} This cause came to be heard upon the accelerated calendar pursuant to App.R. 11.1 and Loc.R. 11.1.

{¶ 2} Appellant, Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C.1 ("Spit Shine"), appeals the *1233dismissal of its complaint against appellees, Rick Case Honda ("R.C. Honda"), Rick Case Hyundai ("R.C. Hyundai"), and Rick Case Automotive ("R.C.A."). Spit Shine argues that its breach of contract and fraudulent representation actions were improperly dismissed pursuant to Civ.R. 12(B)(6). After a thorough review of the record and law, this court affirms.

I. Factual and Procedural History

{¶ 3} Given the summary nature of the proceedings below, the factual basis for the suit is vague. From the complaints filed in this case, it can be gleaned that Spit Shine alleges that it performed vehicle detailing services for two car dealerships, R.C. Honda and R.C. Hyundai. The complaints assert that Spit Shine entered into exclusive contracts with these two entities for car detailing services, but only attached a contract between R.C. Honda and "A One Detailers" to its first and second amended complaints. The contract does not set forth any terms for exclusivity. The complaints allege that Spit Shine, R.C. Honda, and R.C. Hyundai established a business relationship in 2011 where Spit Shine was the sole cleaning and detailing company used by the dealerships. In 2014, R.C. Honda stopped using Spit Shine for detailing services. In 2015, Spit Shine was no longer used for detailing services at either dealership, but it never received written notice of cancellation of the contracts, which required 30 days written notice.

{¶ 4} Spit Shine filed suit on September 2, 2016. Its initial complaint failed to attach any written agreement to it, and rather than answer, appellees filed a motion to dismiss or, in the alternative, for a more definite statement. Days later, Spit Shine filed its first amended complaint to which it attached the aforementioned 2013 contract. The trial court granted in part appellees' motions by ordering Spit Shine to file a second amended complaint clarifying paragraphs of the complaint that failed to distinguish between defendants, and that generally grouped all defendants together without distinguishing what each defendant allegedly did.

{¶ 5} Spit Shine filed a second amended complaint, and appellees filed another motion to dismiss. The trial court granted the motion to dismiss, finding [t]he complaint fails to state a claim upon which relief may be granted under Ohio law. The plaintiff instituting suit is named in the complaint's caption, "Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC." Plaintiff's complaint alleges a breach of a contract. This contract on its face is between Milton Hall and Rick Case Honda. The complaint does not assert that Milton Hall is the same entity as the complaint's Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC; and, it does not explain the relationship between those two legal persons such as, e.g., a successor-in-interest or "dba" entity. The plaintiff introduces unnecessary confusion on the issue of plaintiff's identity when it puts Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC as the name of the plaintiff in the caption and in the introductory paragraph of the complaint which implies plaintiff is a corporate entity but then in paragraph one (1) alleges "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual * * *" the court assumes the latter paragraph of the complaint was not intended literally. Still the complaint does not allege the basis for the named plaintiff in the caption to have contract rights as against any party and it does not allege the basis for the named plaintiff to be entitled to bring a fraudulent representation action against the defendants. On this ground alone-as the analysis below further explains, the motion to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(b)(6) must be granted and the complaint dismissed.
The complaint fails to identify and attach a copy of the parties' alleged *1234contract. The contract attached is not identified as the contract alleged to have been breached by any defendant; it is not identified as the contract in force at the time of the breach of contract by any defendant. It is described merely as "a version" of the pertinent contract. The complaint alleges only that the contractual relationship commenced in 2011 and suit is now brought some five years later.
No further allegations in the complaint make the content of the attached contract relevant to the content of the contract alleged to have been breached; no further allegations in the complaint describe the content of the contract at issue, that is, the contract alleged to have existed between the parties which was alleged to have been breached.
There is no allegation in the complaint as to the contents of the subject contract (particularly to obligations of the respective parties and any significant terms thereof) or as to any other parties thereto. There is no way of knowing if the contract sued upon by the plaintiff bears any similarity to the contract annexed to the complaint.
Due to these failures, the motion to dismiss the complaint's count one must be granted under Civ.R. 12(b)(6).
Parties who do not have a copy of the contract sued upon are permitted to explain their failure to attach a copy of the contract sued upon to their complaint pursuant to Civ.R. 10(d). Plaintiff does not provide such explanation. On the latter basis alone, the motion to dismiss the complaint's count one must be granted under Civ.R. 12(b)(6).
Additionally, the complaint does not contain allegations to hold defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable and contains no allegations that would make defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable for the conduct of its co-defendants even though the complaint alleges those two co-defendants are subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group. This alone is a basis to dismiss the entire complaint under Civ.R. 12(b)(6) against defendant Rick Case Automotive Group.
The motion to dismiss must likewise be granted as to the complaint's count two on fraudulent misrepresentation. Basically this count only states that defendants' "representatives" made a statement that was disproved by later events. Plaintiff does not allege the relationship facts that would turn lies or misleading statements into actionable misstatements. For example, the complaint does not allege the "representatives" were agents or servants of defendants; it does not allege defendants or their representatives had such a relationship with plaintiff that plaintiff was entitled to rely thereon and the "representatives" were obligated to avoid misleading one in plaintiff's position. These conclusions would not necessarily change even if the complaint squarely alleged a specific contract among the parties.
The foregoing does not analyze the ability of the plaintiff to sue for fraudulent misrepresentation in the presence of a written contract. Legal defenses such as the parole evidence rule may preclude such a claim. The complaint does not specify the terms of the subject contract and therefore such analysis is inappropriate under this motion. On November 21, 2016, the court provided the plaintiff with an opportunity to amend Count Two (2) to satisfy the particularity requirement of Civ.R. 9 and ordered plaintiff to file an amended complaint. In the complaint (filed after said order) plaintiff failed to describe the exact content of the parties' contract, identify the contractual relationship between plaintiff and each defendant, and specify the ways in which the fraud was committed.
*1235As a result the motion to dismiss count two of the complaint under Civ.R. 12(b)(6) is granted.

**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLEES**  

May it please this Court:  

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in dismissing this action. To the contrary, it correctly applied well-established legal principles governing motions to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(B)(6). As the court below recognized, Spit Shine’s complaint is fatally defective on multiple independent grounds—each of which independently justifies dismissal. Today, we will demonstrate that the trial court’s ruling was not only legally sound but required by precedent.  

### I. **The Complaint Failed to Allege a Cognizable Claim Against Any Defendant Due to Fatal Ambiguity in Plaintiff Identity and Contractual Relationships**  
A complaint must clearly identify the parties and their roles. *Smith v. Cleveland*, 108 Ohio App.3d 172, 175 (8th Dist. 1995). Here, Spit Shine’s complaint created irreconcilable confusion about its own identity. The caption and introductory paragraph designate "Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC" as the plaintiff—a corporate entity. Yet Paragraph 1 alleges "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual." This contradiction alone vitiates the complaint. No Ohio court has ever permitted a claim to proceed where the plaintiff’s very legal identity is muddled.  

More critically, the sole contract attached to the complaint was between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda—not Spit Shine. The complaint never alleged Milton Hall was Spit Shine’s alter ego, successor-in-interest, or "dba." Without this, the complaint fails to establish *any* contractual relationship between the named plaintiff and the defendants. As this Court held in *State ex rel. Berger v. McMonagle*, 6 Ohio St.3d 28, 30 (1983), a complaint must "state the essential facts" to support a claim. Here, the complaint provided *no facts* connecting Spit Shine to the contract it purports to enforce. The trial court was correct: "there is no way of knowing if the contract sued upon by the plaintiff bears any similarity to the contract annexed to the complaint."  

### II. **The Complaint Violated Civ.R. 10(D) by Failing to Explain Why the Contract Was Not Attached**  
Civ.R. 10(D) explicitly requires a party to "explain its failure to attach a copy of the contract" when the contract is central to the claim. Spit Shine provided no such explanation. The trial court properly noted that the complaint "does not identify and attach a copy of the parties’ alleged contract" and "does not provide such explanation." This failure alone warrants dismissal. As *Kish v. Cleveland*, 112 Ohio App.3d 1, 6 (8th Dist. 1996), makes clear, a plaintiff cannot "vague[ly] refer[] to a contract without specifying its terms or attaching it" while ignoring Civ.R. 10(D)’s requirements. Spit Shine’s omission was not a mere technicality—it deprived appellees of the ability to respond to the alleged breach.  

### III. **The Complaint Failed to Allege Facts Supporting Liability Against Rick Case Automotive Group**  
Spit Shine’s complaint lumped all defendants together without distinguishing their roles. It alleged that Rick Case Automotive Group was a "parent company" of the dealerships but offered *zero* facts suggesting it was liable for their conduct—no allegations of agency, alter ego, or piercing the corporate veil. The trial court rightly dismissed claims against this entity. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003), holds that "mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability" on a parent company. Absent specific facts linking Rick Case Automotive Group to the alleged wrongdoing, dismissal was mandatory.  

### IV. **The Fraud Claim Lacked the Required Particularity Under Civ.R. 9(B)**  
Spit Shine’s fraud claim was equally deficient. Civ.R. 9(B) demands that fraud be pled with "particularity"—specifying the *who, what, when, where, and how* of the alleged misrepresentation. Here, the complaint merely stated that defendants’ "representatives" made a statement "disproved by later events." It never alleged:  
- That these "representatives" were agents of the defendants;  
- That Spit Shine was entitled to rely on their statements;  
- Or that the defendants owed a duty to avoid misleading Spit Shine.  

As this Court emphasized in *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995), "a complaint alleging fraud must describe the specific misrepresentations and the circumstances under which they were made." Spit Shine’s conclusory allegations fell far short. The trial court gave Spit Shine an opportunity to amend, but it failed to cure these defects. Dismissal was not only proper—it was required.  

### CONCLUSION  
The trial court did not "abuse its discretion"—it applied the law precisely as it must. Spit Shine’s complaint was a legal nullity: it failed to identify the plaintiff, failed to link the contract to the plaintiff, violated Civ.R. 10(D), ignored the corporate structure, and pled fraud without particularity. Each flaw independently justifies dismissal. This Court should affirm the trial court’s ruling.  

Respectfully submitted,  
Counsel for Appellees

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE ON BEHALF OF APPELLANT SPIT SHINE A ONE DETAILER, L.L.C.

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF OHIO, EIGHTH APPELLATE DISTRICT

---

**May it please this Court:**

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court committed reversible error when it dismissed Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC's complaint pursuant to Civ.R. 12(B)(6). While Appellees urge this Court to affirm the dismissal, the trial court applied an improperly stringent standard that ignored the liberal pleading requirements of the Ohio Rules of Civil Procedure and deprived Spit Shine of its day in court on the basis of technical deficiencies that, at most, warranted amendment rather than dismissal. We will demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion and that this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE TRIAL COURT APPLIED AN IMPROPERLY STRINGENT STANDARD UNDER CIV.R. 12(B)(6)

The fundamental principle governing motions to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(B)(6) is that such motions may be granted only if it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts in support of his claim that would entitled him to relief. **Berndt v. Department of Rehabilitation & Correction**, 92 Ohio App.3d 238, 243 (10th Dist. 1994). All well-pleaded material allegations of the complaint must be taken as true, and the complaint must be construed in the light most favorable to the plaintiff. **State ex rel. Edwards v. Toledo School Dist.**, 72 Ohio St.3d 106, 108 (1995).

Here, the trial court failed to apply this standard. Rather than construing the complaint liberally in Spit Shine's favor, the trial court appears to have held the complaint to an impossible standard of perfection—one that would require a plaintiff to prove its entire case at the pleading stage. This was error.

In **Fancher v. Cincinnati Insurance Co.**, 2016-Ohio-3439, this Court recognized that Ohio's notice pleading standard is designed to simplify and streamline pleadings, not to erect barriers that prevent legitimate claims from proceeding. The trial court's dismissal of the complaint on the grounds that Spit Shine did not sufficiently distinguish between defendants or explain every nuance of the contractual relationship runs contrary to this principle.

The trial court acknowledged that "the factual basis for the suit is vague," yet used that vagueness as a basis for dismissal rather than as a reason to construe the pleading more liberally or to permit amendment. This approach turns the purpose of notice pleading on its head.

---

## II. THE IDENTITY ALLEGATIONS, WHILE IMPERFECT, DID NOT WARRANT DISMISSAL WHERE AMENDMENT WAS POSSIBLE

Appellees argue that Spit Shine's complaint was fatally defective because it created "irreconcilable confusion about its own identity." This Court should reject this argument for several reasons.

First, the alleged inconsistency—whereby the caption identifies "Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC" as a corporate entity while Paragraph 1 describes "Spit Shine A One Detailers" as an individual—was plainly the type of clerical error that Ohio courts routinely permit plaintiffs to correct via amendment. In **Wolverine Fireproofing Co. v. Structural Metals Corp.**, 32 Ohio App.3d 219, 222 (10th Dist. 1986), this Court held that amendments to complaints should be freely given when justice so requires, and that technical pleading errors should not defeat otherwise valid claims.

Second, the trial court itself recognized that "the court assumes the latter paragraph of the complaint was not intended literally." If the trial court was capable of construing this ambiguity in Spit Shine's favor at one point in its analysis, it should have applied the same charitable construction when ruling on the motion to dismiss. The trial court's inconsistent approach—acknowledging the error while using it as grounds for dismissal—undermines the rationale for dismissal.

Third, the contract attached to the complaint, while between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda, was described as "a version" of the pertinent contract. The complaint alleged that Spit Shine performed services for the dealerships beginning in 2011. While the complaint could have more clearly alleged that Milton Hall was the owner of Spit Shine or that Spit Shine was the successor-in-interest to "A One Detailers," these are deficiencies in pleading, not deficiencies in the underlying claim. The trial court should have granted leave to amend rather than dismissing the complaint outright.

This Court has held that a trial court abuses its discretion when it dismisses a complaint without first giving the plaintiff an opportunity to amend, unless amendment would be futile. **Stroh v. Orkin Corp.**, 131 Ohio App.3d 328, 334 (8th Dist. 1999). Here, amendment clearly would not have been futile—the defects identified by the trial court were curable.

---

## III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ITS APPLICATION OF CIV.R. 10(D)

Appelles contend that Spit Shine's failure to explain its failure to attach the contract pursuant to Civ.R. 10(D) warranted dismissal. This Court should find otherwise.

Civ.R. 10(D) provides that when a contract is the basis of a claim, the pleader shall attach a copy or, if unable to do so, shall explain the inability. However, the rule does not require dismissal as the sole remedy for non-compliance. Rather, the appropriate remedy for a deficient Civ.R. 10(D) compliance is typically a motion for a more definite statement under Civ.R. 12(E), not dismissal under Civ.R. 12(B)(6).

In **Kish v. Cleveland**, 112 Ohio App.3d 1, 6 (8th Dist. 1996), cited by Appellees, this Court did indeed note that a plaintiff cannot vaguely refer to a contract without specifying its terms. However, the Kish case involved a situation where the plaintiff had failed to attach any contract and had provided no explanation. Here, Spit Shine did attach a contract—a 2013 agreement between "A One Detailers" and Rick Case Honda. While the attachment may not have been perfect, it was not the complete absence of a contract that existed in Kish.

Moreover, the trial court acknowledged that the complaint alleged the contractual relationship commenced in 2011 and that the 2013 contract was "a version" of the relevant agreement. Under these circumstances, dismissal was premature. The proper course would have been to order Spit Shine to provide the specific contract referenced in its complaint or to explain why it could not do so.

---

## IV. THE ALLEGATIONS AGAINST RICK CASE AUTOMOMOTIVE GROUP WERE SUFFICIENT TO SURVIVE A MOTION TO DISMISS

Appellees argue that Spit Shine's complaint failed to allege facts sufficient to hold Rick Case Automotive Group liable as a parent company. This Court should reject this argument.

The complaint alleged that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai were "subsidiaries" of Rick Case Automotive Group. While, as Appelles correctly note, mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability on a parent company, the complaint need not have alleged this principle—it is a legal conclusion that the defendants could have raised as an affirmative defense or argument in a motion for summary judgment.

At the pleading stage, Spit Shine was required to allege sufficient facts to state a claim against Rick Case Automotive Group, not to anticipate and defeat all possible legal defenses. The allegation that the dealerships were subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group, combined with the allegations that all three entities were involved in the business relationship with Spit Shine, was sufficient to survive a Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motion.

This is particularly true given the liberal construction that must be applied to complaints under Ohio's notice pleading standard. In **Union Savings & Loan Assn. v. Mortgage Corp. of Ohio**, 1991 WL 19345 (8th Dist. Feb. 14, 1991), this Court held that a complaint should not be dismissed for failure to state a claim unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts that would entitle him to relief. The complaint here easily met this threshold as to Rick Case Automotive Group.

---

## V. THE FRAUD CLAIM SHOULD NOT HAVE BEEN DISMISSED WITHOUT PERMITTING AMENDMENT

Finally, we address the fraud claim. Appelles argue that Spit Shine's fraud claim failed to meet the particularity requirements of Civ.R. 9(B). While we acknowledge that fraud must be pled with particularity, the trial court's handling of this claim was flawed.

The trial court recognized that the fraud claim was deficient and actually gave Spit Shine an opportunity to amend Count Two. However, after Spit Shine filed its amended complaint, the trial court dismissed the claim without explaining what additional specificity was required. This was an abuse of discretion.

In **Maines v. MedMart**, 72 Ohio App.3d 653, 659 (8th Dist. 1991), this Court held that when a trial court determines that a complaint fails to state a claim for fraud, it should grant the plaintiff leave to amend rather than dismissing the claim outright, unless amendment would be futile. The trial court here initially granted leave to amend, which indicates it did not believe amendment would be futile. Its subsequent dismissal of the amended complaint was inconsistent with its earlier ruling and prejudicial to Spit Shine.

Moreover, the fraud claim was not merely a conclusory allegation. The complaint alleged that defendants' representatives made representations that were later disproved. While more particularity was required, the trial court should have specified what additional allegations were necessary rather than dismissing the claim after the plaintiff attempted to comply with its earlier order.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should find that the trial court abused its discretion in dismissing Spit Shine's complaint. The deficiencies identified by the trial court and Appellees were deficiencies in pleading, not deficiencies in the underlying claims. Ohio's notice pleading standard exists precisely to prevent the type of fatal technical dismissal that occurred here.

The proper remedy for the alleged deficiencies was amendment, not dismissal. The trial court recognized as much when it gave Spit Shine an opportunity to amend the fraud count, yet it failed to apply the same reasoning to the contract claim.

This Court should reverse the trial court's judgment and remand this case for further proceedings, including the opportunity to file an amended complaint that cures any pleading deficiencies.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Appellant Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C.**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

When reviewing a trial court's decision to grant a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim pursuant to Civ.R. 12(B)(6), an appellate court must accept as true all well-pleaded material allegations in the complaint and construe the complaint in favor of the non-moving party. *Berndt v. Department of Rehabilitation & Correction*, 92 Ohio App.3d 238, 243 (10th Dist. 1994). A motion to dismiss presents a question of law; therefore, the appellate court conducts a de novo review. *State ex rel. Edwards v. Toledo School Dist.*, 72 Ohio St.3d 106, 108 (1995). The complaint must be given a liberal construction to ensure the plaintiff is not deprived of a day in court due to technical pleading deficiencies. *Fancher v. Cincinnati Insurance Co.*, 2016-Ohio-3439. However, a plaintiff must still provide sufficient factual allegations to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

**II. Analysis**

**A. Identity and Standing to Enforce the Contract**

The trial court properly dismissed the breach of contract claim because the complaint failed to establish that the plaintiff, Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., was a party to the contract it sought to enforce. The caption identifies the plaintiff as "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C.," yet paragraph one of the complaint alleges "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual." While the Appellant argues this is a mere clerical error curable by amendment, *Wolverine Fireproofing Co. v. Structural Metals Corp.*, 32 Ohio App.3d 219, 222 (10th Dist. 1986), the defect extends beyond a typographical inconsistency.

The sole contract attached to the complaint is between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda. The complaint does not allege that Milton Hall is the same entity as Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., nor does it allege a successor-in-interest or "doing business as" relationship. As held in *Smith v. Cleveland*, 108 Ohio App.3d 172, 175 (8th Dist. 1995), a complaint must clearly identify the parties and their roles. Without a factual allegation connecting the named plaintiff to the contract, the plaintiff lacks standing to enforce it. *State ex rel. Berger v. McMonagle*, 6 Ohio St.3d 28, 30 (1983), requires a complaint to state the essential facts supporting a claim; the failure to link the plaintiff to the contract is a fatal deficiency in the cause of action.

**B. Compliance with Civ.R. 10(D)**

Civ.R. 10(D) requires that when a claim is based upon a written instrument, the copy of the instrument must be included in the pleading, or the party must explain its failure to do so. The Appellant attached a 2013 contract between "A One Detailers" and Rick Case Honda but admitted this was merely "a version" of the pertinent contract, which allegedly commenced in 2011. The Appellant provided no explanation for the failure to attach the actual contract sued upon.

The Appellant relies on *Kish v. Cleveland*, 112 Ohio App.3d 1, 6 (8th Dist. 1996), to argue that dismissal is inappropriate where a contract is attached, albeit imperfectly. However, *Kish* stands for the proposition that a plaintiff cannot vaguely refer to a contract without specifying its terms or attaching it. Here, the attached contract is between different parties (Milton Hall) and is not the contract alleged to have been breached. The trial court correctly found that without the actual contract or an explanation for its absence, the Appellees were unable to determine the obligations at issue. This failure justifies dismissal under Civ.R. 12(B)(6).

**C. Liability of Rick Case Automotive Group**

The complaint alleged that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai were subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group but failed to allege any facts establishing the parent company's liability. The Appellant argues that alleging a subsidiary relationship is sufficient under the notice pleading standard of *Union Savings & Loan Assn. v. Mortgage Corp. of Ohio*, 1991 WL 19345 (8th Dist. Feb. 14, 1991).

However, notice pleading does not relieve a plaintiff of the obligation to allege facts connecting a defendant to the alleged harm. Mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability on a parent company. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003). The complaint contained no allegations of agency, alter ego, or piercing the corporate veil. Therefore, the trial court did not err in dismissing the claims against Rick Case Automotive Group.

**D. Fraud Claim and Particularity**

The trial court dismissed the fraud claim for failing to meet the particularity requirements of Civ.R. 9(B). The Appellant contends that because the trial court granted one opportunity to amend, it abused its discretion by dismissing the claim without further guidance, citing *Maines v. MedMart*, 72 Ohio App.3d 653, 659 (8th Dist. 1991).

While leave to amend should be freely given, *Stroh v. Orkin Corp.*, 131 Ohio App.3d 328, 334 (8th Dist. 1999), a court may dismiss if amendment would be futile. Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pled with particularity, including the "who, what, when, where, and how" of the alleged misrepresentation. *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995). The complaint merely alleged that "representatives" made statements disproved by later events. It failed to allege that these representatives were agents of the defendants or that the defendants owed a duty to the plaintiff. Even after the court ordered the plaintiff to satisfy the particularity requirement, the amended complaint failed to allege the relationship facts necessary to support a claim for fraud. Under these circumstances, the trial court acted within its discretion in concluding that further amendment would be futile.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court properly applied the standards for Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motions. The complaint suffered from fatal defects regarding the identity of the plaintiff and the existence of a contractual relationship, failed to comply with Civ.R. 10(D), lacked sufficient allegations to hold the parent company liable, and failed to plead fraud with the required particularity. The Appellant was afforded an opportunity to amend but failed to cure these substantive deficiencies. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in granting the motion to dismiss.

**IT IS ORDERED that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.**

## INTRODUCTION

The Appellees' Brief mischaracterizes the nature of the deficiencies in the Complaint and asks this Court to impose a heightened pleading standard that does not exist under Ohio law. The trial court erred in granting the motion to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(B)(6) because the Complaint, when liberally construed, set forth sufficient factual matter to state claims for breach of contract and fraudulent misrepresentation that are plausible on their face. The Appellees' arguments regarding standing, Civ.R. 10(D), parent company liability, and fraud particularity each fail to account for the liberal pleading standards that govern Civ.R. 12(B)(6) determinations and the well-established principle that leave to amend should be freely given when a deficiency can be cured.

This Court should reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for further proceedings.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT APPLIED AN IMPROPERLY STRINGENT STANDARD

The Appellees contend that the trial court "did construe the Complaint liberally" and still found it deficient. However, the trial court's analysis reveals that it imposed requirements beyond what Ohio law demands at the pleading stage.

A complaint need not contain every detail of the claim; it need only contain "a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief." Civ.R. 8(a). The Supreme Court of Ohio has consistently held that the complaint must be given a liberal construction to ensure the plaintiff is not deprived of a day in court due to technical pleading deficiencies. *Fancher v. Cincinnati Insurance Co.*, 2016-Ohio-3439, ¶ 17 (10th Dist. 2016).

The trial court's dismissal centered on the alleged discrepancy between the plaintiff named in the caption and the party to the attached contract. However, the Complaint explicitly alleged that Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C. performed services for the Appellees, and the attached contract—though imperfect—demonstrated a business relationship between a detailer and Rick Case Honda. The question of whether Milton Hall was the same entity as Spit Shine, or whether Spit Shine was a successor-in-interest or assignee, goes to the merits of the case, not to the sufficiency of the pleading.

In *Wolverine Fireproofing Co. v. Structural Metals Corp.*, 32 Ohio App.3d 219, 222 (10th Dist. 1986), this Court held that amendments should be freely given to correct technical errors in pleadings. The identity issue here is precisely the type of technical deficiency that amendment could cure. The trial court erred by treating this as a fatal flaw rather than allowing Spit Shine an opportunity to clarify its relationship to the contract.

The Appelles cite *Berndt v. Department of Rehabilitation & Correction*, 92 Ohio App.3d 238 (10th Dist. 1994), for the proposition that a complaint must contain factual allegations. However, *Berndt* stands for the unremarkable proposition that conclusions of law without factual support are insufficient—not that a complaint must prove its case at the pleading stage. The Complaint here contained factual allegations: Spit Shine performed detailing services for the dealerships, a business relationship was established in 2011, the dealerships stopped using Spit Shine's services without proper notice, and Spit Shine suffered damages. These are sufficient factual allegations to survive a motion to dismiss.

---

## II. THE IDENTITY ALLEGATIONS WERE SUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH STANDING

The Appellees argue that the Complaint failed to establish standing because it did not explicitly allege that Milton Hall was the same entity as Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C. This argument misapprehends the standing requirement and the liberal pleading standards that apply at the motion to dismiss stage.

Standing requires that the plaintiff demonstrate (1) an injury in fact, (2) a causal connection between the injury and the conduct complained of, and (3) that a favorable decision would likely redress the injury. *State ex rel. Jones v. Cleveland*, 10th Dist. No. 09AP-865, 2010-Ohio-2161, ¶ 12. The Complaint alleged that Spit Shine performed services for the Appellees, that the Appellees breached their contracts by terminating the relationship without proper notice, and that Spit Shine suffered damages as a result. This is sufficient to establish standing.

The Appellees' reliance on *Smith v. Cleveland*, 108 Ohio App.3d 172 (8th Dist. 1995), is misplaced. In *Smith*, the Eighth District affirmed dismissal where the complaint failed to identify the parties and their roles. Here, the Complaint identified Spit Shine as the plaintiff and Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai as the defendants, and alleged that Spit Shine provided services to these defendants under contract. The relationship between Milton Hall and Spit Shine is a matter for proof at trial or summary judgment, not a basis for dismissal.

Similarly, *State ex rel. Berger v. McMonagle*, 6 Ohio St.3d 28 (1983), does not support dismissal here. The Court in *Berger* required that a complaint state the essential facts supporting a claim. The Complaint here did state essential facts: the existence of contracts, the performance of services by Spit Shine, the termination of the relationship without notice, and resulting damages. The Appellees' argument that the Complaint failed to link Spit Shine to the contract ignores the liberal construction requirement and the reality that Spit Shine alleged it performed services for the defendants—the contract itself is evidence of that relationship.

The inconsistency in the caption versus Paragraph 1 of the Complaint is the type of technical error that Civ.R. 8(e)(1) addresses: "Pleadings shall be construed as to do substantial justice." The trial court erred in treating this inconsistency as a fatal deficiency rather than as a curable pleading defect.

## III. THE COMPLAINT SUBSTANTIALLY COMPLIED WITH CIV.R. 10(D)

The Appellees argue that the failure to attach the exact 2011 contract warranted dismissal under Civ.R. 10(D). However, Civ.R. 10(D) requires attachment of the written instrument "upon which the claim is predicated" or an explanation for the failure to do so. The Complaint alleged that the contracts commenced in 2011 and attached what it represented was "a version" of the pertinent contract from 2013.

The Appellees' argument that *Kish v. Cleveland*, 112 Ohio App.3d 1 (8th Dist. 1996), requires dismissal here misreads that decision. In *Kish*, the plaintiff failed to attach any contract and offered no explanation for the failure. Here, Spit Shine attached a contract and explained that it was a version of the operative contract. While the contract attached was between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda rather than Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., this goes to the weight of the evidence, not to the sufficiency of the pleading.

Civ.R. 10(D) serves to inform the defendant of the claims being asserted. Here, the Appellees were on notice that Spit Shine was asserting a breach of contract claim based on a detailing agreement. The Appellees did not need the exact contract to understand the nature of the claim; they needed it to defend against it. The proper remedy for inadequate disclosure of the contract is a motion for a more definite statement under Civ.R. 12(E), not dismissal under Civ.R. 12(B)(6). *Meyer v. City of Riverside*, 10th Dist. No. 01AP-1159, 2002 WL 1725296 (July 25, 2002).

The trial court erred in granting dismissal on this basis. Even if the contract attachment was deficient, dismissal was an abuse of discretion because a less severe remedy was available and appropriate.

## IV. THE COMPLAINT STATED A CLAIM AGAINST RICK CASE AUTOMOTIVE GROUP

The Appellees argue that the Complaint failed to allege facts sufficient to hold Rick Case Automotive Group liable as the parent company of Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai. However, the Complaint alleged that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai were subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group and that all three entities benefited from Spit Shine's services. This is sufficient to state a claim at the pleading stage.

The Appellees correctly note that mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability on a parent company. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003). However, the Complaint did not rely solely on the ownership relationship. It alleged that all three entities were involved in the business relationship and that Spit Shine performed services for all of them. At the pleading stage, these allegations are sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

The Appellees' argument that *Union Savings & Loan Assn. v. Mortgage Corp. of Ohio* does not constitute binding precedent is a red herring. The question is not whether that specific case is binding, but whether the allegations in the Complaint were sufficient to state a claim. They were. The trial court erred in requiring Spit Shine to plead alter ego, agency, or veil-piercing theories at the pleading stage when notice pleading only requires notice of the claim.

Even under the heightened standard the Appellees urge, Ohio law recognizes that a parent company may be liable for the obligations of its subsidiary when there is sufficient evidence of a unitary business enterprise. *Sepulveda v. G & J Production Services, Inc.*, 10th Dist. No. 09AP-275, 2009-Ohio-6839, ¶ 18. The Complaint's allegations of a unified business operation were sufficient to state a claim.

## V. THE FRAUD CLAIM SATISFIED THE REQUIREMENTS OF CIV.R. 9(B)

The Appelles argue that the fraud claim failed to meet the particularity requirement of Civ.R. 9(B). However, the Complaint alleged that the Appellees' representatives made representations that Spit Shine would continue to be the exclusive detailing provider, that Spit Shine relied on these representations, and that the representations were false when made because the Appellees had already decided to terminate the relationship.

Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pleaded with particularity, but the rule does not require the plaintiff to plead evidence—it requires sufficient facts to permit a reasonable inference that the elements of fraud exist. *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995). The "who, what, when, where, and how" standard is met when the complaint identifies the parties involved, the nature of the misrepresentation, and the circumstances surrounding the alleged fraud.

Here, the Complaint identified the Appellees as the parties making representations through their representatives, alleged that the representations concerned the continuation of the business relationship, and alleged that the representations were made during the course of the relationship (when Spit Shine was performing services). This is sufficient particularity.

The trial court gave Spit Shine one opportunity to amend the fraud count. After Spit Shine filed an amended complaint, the trial court dismissed the claim. The Appelles argue this was proper because the amendment was still deficient. However, the trial court's dismissal after a single amendment, without further leave to amend, was an abuse of discretion given the liberal policy favoring amendment.

In *Stroh v. Orkin Corp.*, 131 Ohio App.3d 328 (8th Dist. 1999), the court held that dismissal with prejudice is proper only when amendment would be futile. Here, the trial court did not find that amendment would be futile—it found the complaint deficient and dismissed. This was error.

The proper remedy when a complaint fails to meet the particularity requirement is to grant leave to amend, not to dismiss with prejudice. *Maines v. MedMart*, 72 Ohio App.3d 653, 659 (8th Dist. 1991). The trial court gave Spit Shine one chance to amend, but when that amendment was found deficient, the court should have granted additional leave rather than dismissing the claim. The Appelles' argument that Spit Shine had "two opportunities" to cure the deficiency ignores the fact that the trial court gave only one order to amend and then dismissed after that single amendment.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

When reviewing a trial court's decision to grant a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim pursuant to Civ.R. 12(B)(6), an appellate court must accept as true all well-pleaded material allegations in the complaint and construe the complaint in favor of the non-moving party. *Berndt v. Department of Rehabilitation & Correction*, 92 Ohio App.3d 238, 243 (10th Dist. 1994). A motion to dismiss presents a question of law; therefore, the appellate court conducts a de novo review. *State ex rel. Edwards v. Toledo School Dist.*, 72 Ohio St.3d 106, 108 (1995). While a complaint must be given a liberal construction to ensure a plaintiff is not deprived of a day in court due to technical deficiencies, *Fancher v. Cincinnati Insurance Co.*, 2016-Ohio-3439, a plaintiff must still provide sufficient factual allegations to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

**II. Analysis**

**A. Identity and Standing to Enforce the Contract**

The Appellant argues that the discrepancy between the named plaintiff, "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C.," and the party listed on the attached contract, "Milton Hall," constitutes a technical error curable by amendment, relying on *Wolverine Fireproofing Co. v. Structural Metals Corp.*, 32 Ohio App.3d 219, 222 (10th Dist. 1986). The Appellant further contends that the allegation of performance establishes the necessary relationship.

However, the defect here is not merely a typographical inconsistency or a misnomer. The complaint fails to allege any factual basis connecting the named plaintiff to the contract. The caption identifies the plaintiff as a limited liability company, while the body of the complaint alleges the plaintiff is an individual. More critically, the sole contract attached is between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda. The complaint contains no allegation that Milton Hall is the same entity as Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., nor does it allege a successor-in-interest, assignment, or "doing business as" relationship.

As held in *Smith v. Cleveland*, 108 Ohio App.3d 172, 175 (8th Dist. 1995), a complaint must clearly identify the parties and their roles to establish standing. Without a factual allegation linking the named plaintiff to the contract, the plaintiff lacks standing to enforce it. *State ex rel. Berger v. McMonagle*, 6 Ohio St.3d 28, 30 (1983), requires a complaint to state the essential facts supporting a claim. The failure to allege the plaintiff's relationship to the contracting party is a substantive deficiency regarding the plaintiff's capacity to sue, not a technical pleading error. Therefore, the trial court properly dismissed the breach of contract claim.

**B. Compliance with Civ.R. 10(D)**

The Appellant argues that the complaint substantially complied with Civ.R. 10(D) by attaching "a version" of the contract and that the proper remedy for any deficiency would have been a motion for a more definite statement under Civ.R. 12(E), citing *Meyer v. City of Riverside*, 10th Dist. No. 01AP-1159, 2002 WL 1725296 (July 25, 2002).

Civ.R. 10(D) mandates that when a claim is based upon a written instrument, the copy of the instrument must be included in the pleading, or the party must explain its failure to do so. The Appellant attached a 2013 contract between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda but admitted it was merely "a version" of the pertinent contract, which allegedly commenced in 2011. The Appellant provided no explanation for the failure to attach the actual contract sued upon or for the discrepancy in parties.

In *Kish v. Cleveland*, 112 Ohio App.3d 1, 6 (8th Dist. 1996), the court held that a plaintiff cannot vaguely refer to a contract without specifying its terms or attaching it. Here, the attached contract is between different parties and is not identified as the contract in force at the time of the alleged breach. The Appellees could not determine the obligations at issue from the document provided. Because the complaint failed to identify the contract sued upon and failed to explain the absence of the correct instrument, the trial court did not err in dismissing the claim under Civ.R. 12(B)(6). The defect goes to the very existence of the claim, rendering dismissal appropriate rather than a mere order for a more definite statement.

**C. Liability of Rick Case Automotive Group**

The Appellant contends that the complaint sufficiently stated a claim against Rick Case Automotive Group by alleging that the subsidiaries were "involved" and that all entities "benefited" from the services. The Appellant relies on *Sepulveda v. G & J Production Services, Inc.*, 10th Dist. No. 09AP-275, 2009-Ohio-6839, ¶ 18, to suggest a "unitary business enterprise" theory.

However, the trial court found that the complaint "generally grouped all defendants together without distinguishing what each defendant allegedly did" and contained "no allegations that would make defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable." Mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability on a parent company. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003). To hold a parent company liable, a complaint must allege facts establishing alter ego, agency, or veil-piercing. The Appellant's generalized allegations of benefit and involvement, without specific factual allegations of the parent company's conduct or liability, are insufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. *Union Savings & Loan Assn. v. Mortgage Corp. of Ohio*, 1991 WL 19345 (8th Dist. Feb. 14, 1991), does not override the binding precedent requiring specific factual allegations for parent-company liability.

**D. Fraud Claim and Particularity**

The Appellant argues that the fraud claim satisfied Civ.R. 9(B) by identifying the "who" (representatives) and the "what" (exclusivity representations), and that the trial court abused its discretion by dismissing the claim after a single opportunity to amend, citing *Maines v. MedMart*, 72 Ohio App.3d 653, 659 (8th Dist. 1991).

Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pled with particularity, including the "who, what, when, where, and how" of the alleged misrepresentation. *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995). The complaint alleged only that "representatives" made statements disproved by later events. It failed to allege that these representatives were agents or servants of the defendants, or that the defendants owed a duty to the plaintiff. The Appellant's assertion that the term "representatives" implies agency is insufficient to meet the particularity requirement; the complaint must allege the facts establishing the agency relationship.

The trial court granted the Appellant an opportunity to amend the fraud count to satisfy Civ.R. 9. The amended complaint still failed to allege the relationship facts necessary to support a claim for fraud. Under *Stroh v. Orkin Corp.*, 131 Ohio App.3d 328, 334 (8th Dist. 1999), a court may dismiss if amendment would be futile. Given that the Appellant failed to cure the deficiency regarding the agency of the representatives after being specifically ordered to do so, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that further amendment would be futile and dismissing the claim.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court properly applied the standards for Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motions. The complaint suffered from fatal defects regarding the identity of the plaintiff and the existence of a contractual relationship, failed to comply with Civ.R. 10(D), lacked sufficient allegations to hold the parent company liable, and failed to plead fraud with the required particularity. The Appellant's arguments regarding liberal construction and the availability of amendment cannot overcome the absence of essential factual allegations supporting the claims. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in granting the motion to dismiss.

**IT IS ORDERED that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.**

*In Support of Affirmance of the Trial Court's Dismissal*  

Your Honor, the appellant’s arguments in its "rebuttal" brief—mischaracterized as "defense" but actually the appellant’s own reply—rely on a fundamental misunderstanding of Ohio pleading standards. They seek to transform a motion to dismiss into a trial on the merits, demanding that this Court ignore the complaint’s fatal, incurable defects. But the law is clear: a complaint must contain sufficient factual allegations to state a plausible claim for relief. *Fancher v. Cincinnati Ins. Co.*, 2016-Ohio-3439, ¶ 17. Here, the trial court did not abuse its discretion—it correctly dismissed the complaint because it fails to meet even the most basic requirements of Civ.R. 12(B)(6). Let me address each of the appellant’s flawed arguments in turn.  

### I. THE COMPLAINT’S IDENTITY DEFECT IS NOT A "TECHNICAL ERROR"—IT IS A SUBSTANTIVE FAILURE TO ESTABLISH STANDING  
The appellant claims the discrepancy between "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C." in the caption and "Milton Hall" in the contract is merely a "technical error" curable by amendment, citing *Wolverine Fireproofing Co. v. Structural Metals Corp.* But *Wolverine* stands for the opposite proposition: amendments are for correcting *technical* deficiencies, not substantive failures to allege a legal right to sue. Here, the complaint contains no factual allegation linking Spit Shine to the contract. It labels the plaintiff as an LLC in the caption but then alleges in Paragraph 1 that "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual"—a contradiction that cannot be reconciled. Worse, it fails to allege *any* relationship between Spit Shine and Milton Hall: no successor-in-interest, no assignment, no "doing business as" status.  

This is not a pleading mistake; it is a failure to state a claim at all. *Smith v. Cleveland*, 108 Ohio App.3d 172, 175 (8th Dist. 1995), holds that standing requires clear identification of the parties and their roles. The Supreme Court of Ohio has long held that a complaint must "state the essential facts supporting a claim." *State ex rel. Berger v. McMonagle*, 6 Ohio St.3d 28, 30 (1983). Here, the complaint alleges no facts showing why Spit Shine—rather than Milton Hall—could enforce the contract. Standing is not established by vague assertions of "performance"; it requires a direct link between the plaintiff and the cause of action. The trial court was correct: without this link, the breach of contract claim collapses before it begins.  

### II. THE COMPLAINT VIOLATES CIV.R. 10(D)—AND THIS IS NOT A "MORE DEFINITE STATEMENT" ISSUE  
The appellant argues that attaching "a version" of the contract satisfies Civ.R. 10(D). But Civ.R. 10(D) is not a suggestion—it is a mandate. When a claim is "based upon a written instrument," the instrument must be attached *or* the pleader must explain the failure to attach it. Here, the appellant attached a 2013 contract between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda but admits it is merely "a version" of the purported 2011 contract. It offers no explanation for why the actual contract sued upon is missing, nor does it clarify how this document relates to the alleged breach.  

In *Kish v. Cleveland*, 112 Ohio App.3d 1, 6 (8th Dist. 1996), this Court squarely rejected the notion that vague references to a contract suffice. The appellant cannot rely on an instrument that is not between the parties it sued and that is not identified as the operative contract. This is not a deficiency that a Civ.R. 12(E) motion for a more definite statement would cure—it is a failure to plead the very foundation of the claim. The trial court did not err in dismissing this claim; it correctly held that without a proper contract attachment or explanation, the complaint fails to state a claim.  

### III. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO ALLEGE FACTS SUPPORTING LIABILITY FOR RICK CASE AUTOMOTIVE GROUP  
The appellant claims that alleging Rick Case Automotive Group "benefited" from services and was "involved" suffices to hold it liable as a parent company. But Ohio law is unequivocal: mere ownership of a subsidiary does not create liability for the parent. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003). To pierce the corporate veil, a complaint must allege specific facts showing alter ego, agency, or domination—such as commingled funds, undercapitalization, or use of the subsidiary as a "sham." The appellant’s complaint does none of this. It offers only conclusory statements like "all entities benefited," which are the very type of "conclusions of law without factual support" that *Berndt v. Department of Rehabilitation & Correction*, 92 Ohio App.3d 238, 243 (10th Dist. 1994), forbids.  

*Union Savings & Loan Assn. v. Mortgage Corp. of Ohio*, 1991 WL 19345 (8th Dist. Feb. 14, 1991), does not override this principle. It merely notes that parent companies may be liable under specific circumstances—but it does not excuse the failure to plead those circumstances. The trial court was right: without factual allegations of control, domination, or fraud, the claim against Rick Case Automotive Group must fail.  

### IV. THE FRAUD CLAIM LACKS PARTICULARITY—AND AMENDMENT WOULD BE FUTILE  
The appellant insists its fraud claim meets Civ.R. 9(B) because it alleges "representatives" made "exclusivity representations." But Civ.R. 9(B) requires more than a vague label like "representatives." It demands the "who, what, when, where, and how" of the misrepresentation. *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995). Here, the complaint does not identify *who* the representatives were, *how* they were authorized to bind the defendants, or *why* the plaintiff was entitled to rely on them. It does not allege an agency relationship or duty to speak truthfully.  

The trial court granted one opportunity to amend to cure this deficiency. When the amended complaint still failed to allege the agency relationship or specifics of the fraud, the court properly concluded that further amendment would be futile. *Stroh v. Orkin Corp.*, 131 Ohio App.3d 328, 334 (8th Dist. 1999). The appellant’s claim that the court should have granted "additional leave" ignores the reality: the complaint’s core deficiency—no allegations of agency—cannot be cured by more words. As *Meadows* holds, particularity is not a suggestion; it is a requirement for fraud claims.

Your Honor, the appellant’s brief asks this Court to rewrite Ohio pleading standards. It demands that we ignore the complaint’s fatal flaws—its failure to establish standing, its violation of Civ.R. 10(D), its lack of particularized allegations against the parent company, and its deficient fraud claim—and instead treat this as a case ripe for discovery. But that is not the law. *Fancher* requires only "a short and plain statement of the claim," but it does not excuse the absence of a claim altogether. The trial court acted within its discretion by dismissing a complaint that, as pled, has no legal foundation.  

The judgment below should be affirmed. This Court should not reward the appellant for pleading a case that does not exist.

The Appellant's allegations against Rick Case Automotive Group consist of conclusory statements that the subsidiaries were "involved" and that all entities "benefited" from the services. These allegations are insufficient as a matter of law.

Mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability on a parent company. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003). To hold a parent company liable, a complaint must allege facts establishing alter ego, agency, or veil-piercing—such as commingled funds, undercapitalization, or use of the subsidiary as a sham entity.

The Appellant's generalized allegations of benefit and involvement, without specific factual allegations of the parent company's conduct or liability, are insufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. *Berndt*, 92 Ohio App.3d at 243 (conclusory allegations without factual support are insufficient).

### E. The Fraud Claim Fails to Meet the Particularity Requirement

Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pled with particularity, including the "who, what, when, where, and how" of the alleged misrepresentation. *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995).

The complaint alleged only that "representatives" made statements that were "disproved by later events." It failed to allege that these representatives were agents or servants of the Appellees, or that the Appellees owed a duty to the Appellant. The mere use of the term "representatives" does not imply agency; the complaint must allege facts establishing the agency relationship.

The trial court granted the Appellant an opportunity to amend the fraud count to satisfy Civ.R. 9(B). The amended complaint still failed to allege the relationship facts necessary to support a claim for fraud. Under *Stroh v. Orkin Corp.*, 131 Ohio App.3d 328, 334 (8th Dist. 1999), a court may dismiss if amendment would be futile. Given the Appellant's failure to cure the deficiency after specific direction to do so, the trial court did not abuse its discretion.

## III. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S ARGUMENTS

The Appellant's appellate brief attempts to relitigate issues that were properly resolved below. We address each argument briefly:

**Regarding standing:** The Appellant argues that the discrepancy between the named plaintiff and the contracting party is a "technical error." This is incorrect. The failure to allege any relationship between Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C. and Milton Hall is a substantive deficiency, not a technical one. Standing cannot be established by vague assertions of "performance"—it requires a direct factual link between the plaintiff and the cause of action.

**Regarding Civ.R. 10(D):** The Appellant contends that attaching "a version" of the contract substantially complies with the rule. This argument fails. Civ.R. 10(D) requires the instrument sued upon or an explanation for its absence. The Appellant provided neither.

**Regarding parent company liability:** The Appellant relies on *Sepulveda v. G & J Production Services, Inc.*, 10th Dist. No. 09AP-275, 2009-Ohio-6839, ¶ 18. That case does not stand for the proposition that mere allegations of "benefit" suffice. To the contrary, veil-piercing requires specific factual allegations of domination and fraud, which are entirely absent here.

**Regarding the fraud claim:** The Appellant argues that the term "representatives" implies agency. This is insufficient. *Meadows* requires specific allegations establishing the agency relationship, not mere labels. The trial court gave the Appellant an opportunity to amend, which it failed to cure.

## IV. CONCLUSION

The trial court's decision to dismiss the complaint was correct. The Appellant failed to state claims upon which relief can be granted for multiple, independent reasons:

1. The Appellant lacks standing to enforce the alleged contract because it failed to allege any relationship between itself and the contracting party named in the attached document.

2. The complaint violates Civ.R. 10(D) by failing to attach the actual contract sued upon or explain the discrepancy.

3. The complaint fails to allege sufficient facts to hold Rick Case Automotive Group liable as a parent company.

4. The fraud claim fails to meet the particularity requirement of Civ.R. 9(B), and the Appellant failed to cure this deficiency even after specific leave to amend.

The Appellant asks this Court to reward pleading failures by allowing discovery to proceed. This Court should decline. The trial court correctly applied Ohio pleading standards and properly dismissed a complaint that, as pled, has no legal foundation.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

When reviewing a trial court's decision to grant a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim pursuant to Civ.R. 12(B)(6), an appellate court must accept as true all well-pleaded material allegations in the complaint and construe the complaint in favor of the non-moving party. *Berndt v. Department of Rehabilitation & Correction*, 92 Ohio App.3d 238, 243 (10th Dist. 1994). A motion to dismiss presents a question of law; therefore, the appellate court conducts a de novo review. *State ex rel. Edwards v. Toledo School Dist.*, 72 Ohio St.3d 106, 108 (1995). While a complaint must be given a liberal construction to ensure a plaintiff is not deprived of a day in court due to technical deficiencies, *Fancher v. Cincinnati Insurance Co.*, 2016-Ohio-3439, ¶ 17, a plaintiff must still provide sufficient factual allegations to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

**II. Analysis**

**A. Identity and Standing to Enforce the Contract**

The Appellees argue that the discrepancy between the named plaintiff, "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C.," and the party listed on the attached contract, "Milton Hall," constitutes a substantive failure to establish standing rather than a technical error. The record supports this position. The caption identifies the plaintiff as a limited liability company, while the body of the complaint alleges the plaintiff is an individual. More critically, the sole contract attached is between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda. The complaint contains no allegation that Milton Hall is the same entity as Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., nor does it allege a successor-in-interest, assignment, or "doing business as" relationship.

As held in *Smith v. Cleveland*, 108 Ohio App.3d 172, 175 (8th Dist. 1995), a complaint must clearly identify the parties and their roles to establish standing. Without a factual allegation linking the named plaintiff to the contract, the plaintiff lacks standing to enforce it. *State ex rel. Berger v. McMonagle*, 6 Ohio St.3d 28, 30 (1983), requires a complaint to state the essential facts supporting a claim. The failure to allege the plaintiff's relationship to the contracting party is a substantive deficiency regarding the plaintiff's capacity to sue. Therefore, the trial court properly dismissed the breach of contract claim.

**B. Compliance with Civ.R. 10(D)**

The Appellees correctly assert that the complaint failed to comply with Civ.R. 10(D). Civ.R. 10(D) mandates that when a claim is based upon a written instrument, the copy of the instrument must be included in the pleading, or the party must explain its failure to do so. The Appellant attached a 2013 contract between "Milton Hall" and Rick Case Honda but admitted it was merely "a version" of the pertinent contract, which allegedly commenced in 2011. The Appellant provided no explanation for the failure to attach the actual contract sued upon or for the discrepancy in parties.

In *Kish v. Cleveland*, 112 Ohio App.3d 1, 6 (8th Dist. 1996), the court held that a plaintiff cannot vaguely refer to a contract without specifying its terms or attaching it. Here, the attached contract is between different parties and is not identified as the contract in force at the time of the alleged breach. Because the complaint failed to identify the contract sued upon and failed to explain the absence of the correct instrument, the trial court did not err in dismissing the claim under Civ.R. 12(B)(6).

**C. Liability of Rick Case Automotive Group**

The Appellees contend that the complaint failed to state a claim against Rick Case Automotive Group. The trial court found that the complaint "generally grouped all defendants together without distinguishing what each defendant allegedly did" and contained "no allegations that would make defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable." Mere ownership of a subsidiary does not automatically impose liability on a parent company. *State ex rel. Buehrer v. Cuyahoga Cty. Bd. of Comm'rs*, 100 Ohio St.3d 128, 135 (2003). To hold a parent company liable, a complaint must allege facts establishing alter ego, agency, or veil-piercing. The Appellant's generalized allegations of benefit and involvement, without specific factual allegations of the parent company's conduct or liability, are insufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

**D. Fraud Claim and Particularity**

The Appellees argue that the fraud claim lacked the particularity required by Civ.R. 9(B). Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pled with particularity, including the "who, what, when, where, and how" of the alleged misrepresentation. *State ex rel. Meadows v. Ritter*, 72 Ohio St.3d 116, 119 (1995). The complaint alleged only that "representatives" made statements disproved by later events. It failed to allege that these representatives were agents or servants of the defendants, or that the defendants owed a duty to the plaintiff.

The trial court granted the Appellant an opportunity to amend the fraud count to satisfy Civ.R. 9. The amended complaint still failed to allege the relationship facts necessary to support a claim for fraud. Under *Stroh v. Orkin Corp.*, 131 Ohio App.3d 328, 334 (8th Dist. 1999), a court may dismiss if amendment would be futile. Given that the Appellant failed to cure the deficiency regarding the agency of the representatives after being specifically ordered to do so, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that further amendment would be futile and dismissing the claim.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court properly applied the standards for Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motions. The complaint suffered from fatal defects regarding the identity of the plaintiff and the existence of a contractual relationship, failed to comply with Civ.R. 10(D), lacked sufficient allegations to hold the parent company liable, and failed to plead fraud with the required particularity. The arguments presented by the Appellees confirm that these deficiencies are substantive and incurable on the record presented. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in granting the motion to dismiss.

**IT IS ORDERED that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.**
