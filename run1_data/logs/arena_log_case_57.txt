J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married when A.F.Y. gave birth to a son in 1994. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. later divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R. alleging that R.R. was the biological father of the child born during the marriage. J.M.Y. claimed that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R. for the child support payments he has made. In addition to reimbursement from R.R. for past child support payments, he sought reimbursement from A.F.Y. for past child support payments and from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for future child support payments.

R.R. filed exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. The trial court held a hearing on May 27, 2008, and granted the exceptions. A judgment in conformity with the trial court’s ruling was signed on May 29, 2008. J.M.Y. now appeals.

ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR

The plaintiff-appellant, J.M.Y., asserts one assignment of error:

The Court erred when it granted Defendant’s Exception of No Right of Action or Cause of Action.

| ¿DISCUSSION

The supreme court discussed the standard of review of an exception of no cause of action in Fink v. Bryant, 01-0987, pp. 3-4 (La.11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349 (citations omitted):

The function of the peremptory exception of no cause of action is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. The peremptory exception of no cause of action is designed to test the legal sufficiency of the petition by determining whether [the] plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged in the pleading. No evidence may be introduced to support or controvert the objection that the petition fails to state a cause of action. The exception is triable on the face of the papers and for the purposes of determining the issues raised by the exception, the well-pleaded facts in the petition must be accepted as true. In reviewing a trial court’s ruling sustaining an exception of no cause of action, the appellate court and this Court should subject the case to de novo review because the exception raises a question of law and the trial court’s decision is based only on the sufficiency of the petition. Simply stated, a petition should not be dismissed for failure to state a cause of action unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can *727prove no set of facts in support of any claim which would entitle him to relief.
This court discussed the standard of review of an exception of no right of action in Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc., 01-1623, pp. 2-3 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-03 (citations omitted):

An exception of no right of action has the function of determining whether the plaintiff has any interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. The function of this exception is to terminate the suit brought by one who has no judicial right to enforce the right asserted in the lawsuit. The determination of whether a plaintiff has a right of action is a question of law. Accordingly, we review exceptions of no right of action de novo.
Evidence can be admitted to support the allegations in a exception of no right of action, but if no evidence is offered the court must decide the exception solely on the basis of the plaintiffs allegations. Indus. Co., Inc. v. Durbin, 02-0665 (La.1/28/03), 837 So.2d 1207.

| ./‘The husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage.” Former La.Civ.Code art. 184. At the time of the child’s birth in 1994, J.M.Y. had 180 days from the time the husband “learned or should have learned” about the birth of the child to file a suit to disavow paternity. Former La.Civ.Code art. 189. He failed to do so, and his action for disavowal is per-empted. See Pounds v. Schori, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979). (Under current law, as amended in 2005, there is a one year liber-ative prescription period for filing a disavowal action.)

J.M.Y. does not seek to disavow his child. Instead, he seeks to be reimbursed for his child support obligation by R.R. He cites La.Civ.Code art. 227 to support his claim against R.R. Article 227 states:

Fathers and mothers, by the very act of marrying, contract together the obligation of supporting, maintaining, and educating their children.
In his brief, J.M.Y. argues that this article applies with equal force to biological fathers and presumed fathers. He further argues that nothing in the law prevents him from being reimbursed for child support payments from R.R., whom he claims is the biological father of the child.

Because J.M.Y. is presumed to be the father of the child, he has an obligation to support the child. While J.M.Y. alleges R.R. may be the biological father of the child, that fact, if established may not extinguish the obligation of J.M.Y. to support the child.

In Gallo v. Gallo, 03-0794 (La.12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, a man sued his ex-wife to recover money he paid as child support for a child born during the marriage |4that he learned years later was not his biological daughter. The supreme court denied his claim for reimbursement. In its opinion, the supreme court stated:

Proper analysis recognizes that it is not the adults but the child who is at the center of this unfortunate controversy. A father’s obligation to support his children is a “primary, continuous obligation,” based during marriage on parental authority and after divorce on tutorship. Blakesley, Louisiana Family Law § 16.02 at 16-5. The child is the veritable creditor of each parent’s unilateral obligation for the child’s upbringing with the special expenses it entails. Id. at 16-6. This father-child relationship between Mr. Gallo and M.L.G. did not end with blood testing that showed Mr. Gallo was not the biological father of M.L.G. He remained her legal presumptive father and was identified as such in the three-party acknowledgment. Fatherhood rests on something more than genes. See, T.D. v. M.M.M., 98-0167 at *7282 (concurring opinion), (La.2/2/99), 730 So.2d 873 at 878, citing Lehr v. Robertson, 463 U.S. 248, 103 S.Ct. 2985, 77 L.Ed.2d 614 (1983). Furthermore, Louisiana has recognized dual fatherhood. In Smith v. Cole, 553 So.2d 847, 854 (La.1989), this court held that the failure of the husband of the mother of the child to exercise his right to disavow paternity timely established the child as his legal and legitimate child. The legal tie of paternity would not be affected by subsequent proof of the child’s actual biological tie. This court specifically held that although the child was conclusively presumed to be the husband’s legitimate offspring, the biological father could not escape his support obligations. The question of whether the legal, presumed father also shared in the support obligation was not before the court, and this court declined to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother. Id. at 855.
Gallo, 861 So.2d at 178 (footnote omitted).

In Smith, 553 So.2d 847, the mother of a child brought a filiation action against her child’s biological father. The biological father claimed that the fact that there was a legal father extinguished his obligation to support the child. The supreme court held that even where there is dual paternity (a legal father and a biological father), the legal father’s obligation to support the child does not extinguish the biological father’s obligation to support the child. The court did not decide whether the opposite was true, i.e., whether the biological father’s obligation to support the child extinguishes the legal father’s support obligation. The court stated: “The question of | ^whether the ‘legal’ father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother.8” Id. at 855. The footnote states:

The best interest of the child should be considered in determining whether the court in a given case will impose the obligation of support on the person who, by virtue of Article 184, is conclusively presumed to be the father of the child. While LSA-C.C. art. 227 may provide the basis for such an imposition on the legal father, the fact that there is a biological father capable of providing support cannot equitably be ignored.
Id.

The matter before us, however, does not involve an issue of dual paternity. Although J.M.Y. alleges R.R. is the biological father, he never filed a disavowal action.  Therefore, J.M.Y. is the child’s legal father. Smith, 553 So.2d at 854-55. Louisiana Civil Code Article 197 states that a child may institute an action to prove paternity, yet A.F.Y. has never filed a filiation action on behalf of the child.

The trial court was correct in its determination that there is no cause of action under Louisiana law for a legal father to demand reimbursement for child support he has paid from an individual who has never been established to be the biological father. The trial court was correct in finding J.M.Y. has no right of action as well.

We affirm the ruling of the trial court in all respects. Costs of this appeal are assessed to the plaintiff-appellant.

AFFIRMED.

J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. later divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support for the child.

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The standards of review are well-established and support affirmance. Regarding the exception of no cause of action, this Court conducts **de novo review** because the exception raises a question of law based solely on the sufficiency of the petition. *Fink v. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. J.M.Y. Has No Cause of Action for Reimbursement

The trial court correctly determined that Louisiana law provides no cause of action for a legal father to demand reimbursement for child support from an individual who has never been established to be the biological father. This Court should affirm that ruling.

Louisiana recognizes that a father's obligation to support his children is a "primary, continuous obligation" owed directly to the child. *Gallo v. Gallo*, 03-0794 (La. 12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, 178. The child is the "veritable creditor of each parent's unilateral obligation for the child's upbringing." **Id.** This principle is fundamental: the support obligation runs to the child, not between parents as a matter of private debt.

In *Gallo*, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed precisely this situation—a man who had paid child support for a child born during his marriage later learned he was not the biological father, and sought reimbursement from his ex-wife. The Supreme Court denied the claim, holding that "the legal tie of paternity would not be affected by subsequent proof of the child's actual biological tie." **Id.** The Court emphasized that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes." **Id.** The father's obligation persisted because he remained the legal father regardless of biological reality.

The principle from *Gallo* applies with equal force here. J.M.Y. does not even allege that he has disavowed his paternity or that any court has made a finding that he is not the legal father. He remains the legal father by operation of law. His obligation to support the child is therefore fixed and continuing. He cannot now circumvent that obligation by seeking reimbursement from R.R., particularly when R.R.'s biological paternity has never been established.

### B. The Presumption of Paternity Bars J.M.Y.'s Claim

At the time of the child's birth in 1994, former Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 provided that "the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage." Under former Article 189, J.M.Y. had 180 days from the time he "learned or should have learned" about the birth of the child to file a suit to disavow paternity. He failed to do so, and his action for disavowal is preempted. *Pounds v. Schori*, 377 So.2d 1195 (La. 1979).

This Court in *Smith v. Cole*, 553 So.2d 847 (La. 1989), addressed the consequence of failing to timely disavow paternity. There, the Court held that the failure of the husband of the mother to exercise his right to disavow paternity timely established the child as his legal and legitimate child. The legal tie of paternity would not be affected by subsequent proof of the child's actual biological tie. **Id. at 854.** J.M.Y. is in exactly this position—he is the legal father, and no subsequent proof of biological paternity can undo that legal relationship.

### C. Dual Paternity Does Not Assist J.M.Y.

J.M.Y. attempts to rely on the concept of dual fatherhood to support his claim, but this reliance is misplaced. In *Smith v. Cole*, the Louisiana Supreme Court recognized that both a legal father and a biological father may owe support obligations to a child. However, the Court explicitly declined to hold that a biological father's obligation extinguishes the legal father's duty. The Court stated: "The question of whether the 'legal' father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." **Id. at 855.**

Critically, *Smith v. Cole* involved a situation where the biological father had been established through a filiation action. Here, R.R.'s biological paternity has never been established. No court has ever determined that R.R. is the biological father of the child. J.M.Y. merely alleges such biological paternity without any judicial finding to support it.

Moreover, the *Smith* Court emphasized that "the best interest of the child should be considered in determining whether the court in a given case will impose the obligation of support on the person who, by virtue of Article 184, is conclusively presumed to be the father of the child." **Id. at 855, n.8.** Allowing a legal father to seek reimbursement from an alleged biological father would directly conflict with the child's best interests by creating uncertainty about the source and stability of support.

### D. Article 227 Does Not Create a Reimbursement Right

J.M.Y. cites Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 in support of his claim, arguing that the article applies equally to biological fathers and presumed fathers. This argument fails.

Article 227 states: "Fathers and mothers, by the very act of marrying, contract together the obligation of supporting, maintaining, and educating their children." This article creates a mutual obligation between spouses to support their children—it does not create a right of reimbursement from third parties. The article imposes the support obligation on parents; it does not provide a mechanism for one parent to shift that obligation to another individual.

As the *Gallo* Court made clear, the child—not the parents—is the creditor of the support obligation. Reimbursement claims between parents would undermine this principle and create instability for the child. The article provides no basis for J.M.Y.'s claim against R.R.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff's opening argument fails for several reasons:

**First**, the plaintiff argues that Louisiana law establishes a presumed father's absolute duty of support. This is correct, but it undermines rather than supports his claim. Because J.M.Y. is the presumed father, his obligation to support the child is absolute and cannot be avoided by seeking reimbursement from another. The obligation runs to the child, and J.M.Y. cannot escape it by filing suit against R.R.

**Second**, the plaintiff cites *Gallo* for the proposition that fatherhood rests on more than genes. This is precisely the point. J.M.Y. is the legal father regardless of any biological connection. His legal fatherhood gives him no right to shift his support obligation to R.R., particularly when R.R.'s biological paternity has never been established.

**Third**, the plaintiff argues he never sought to disavow his paternity. This is true, but it is fatal to his claim. By failing to disavow, J.M.Y. remains the legal father with a fixed obligation. He cannot have it both ways—he cannot retain the legal benefits of fatherhood while attempting to avoid the financial responsibilities.

**Fourth**, the plaintiff argues Article 227 creates equal obligations. This misreads the statute. Article 227 imposes mutual obligations on married parents; it does not create a reimbursement mechanism. The plaintiff cannot use Article 227 to impose liability on R.R., who has never been found to be the biological father and who has no legal relationship to the child.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by J.M.Y., plaintiff-appellant, seeking reversal of a trial court judgment granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action in favor of R.R. and A.F.Y., defendants. **STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appropriate standard of review for an exception of no cause of action is de novo. The function of this exception is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. In reviewing such a ruling, the appellate court must determine whether the plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged in the pleading, accepting well-pleaded facts as true. *Fink v. Bryant*, 01-0987 (La.11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349.

Similarly, an exception of no right of action is reviewed de novo. This exception determines whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. *Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc.*, 01-1623 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-03.

**ANALYSIS**

J.M.Y. argues that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments made for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. He contends that R.R. is the biological father and that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 creates an obligation for biological fathers that allows for such reimbursement. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive based on established Louisiana jurisprudence and statutory law.

**1. Presumption of Paternity and Failure to Disavow**

Under Louisiana law, the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage. Former La. Civ. Code art. 184. J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. At that time, J.M.Y. had 180 days from the time he learned or should have learned of the birth to file a suit to disavow paternity. Former La. Civ. Code art. 189. J.M.Y. failed to file such an action. Consequently, his action for disavowal is preempted, and he is the conclusively presumed legal father of the child. *Pounds v. Schori*, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979).

**2. The Nature of the Support Obligation**

J.M.Y. seeks reimbursement for child support payments, treating the obligation as a private debt that can be recovered from a third party. However, Louisiana law establishes that a father's obligation to support his children is a primary, continuous obligation owed directly to the child, not to the other parent or a third party. In *Gallo v. Gallo*, 03-0794 (La.12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed a similar situation where a legal father sought reimbursement after discovering he was not the biological father. The Court denied the claim, stating that "the child is the veritable creditor of each parent’s unilateral obligation for the child’s upbringing." *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. The Court emphasized that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes" and that the legal tie of paternity is not affected by subsequent proof of biological ties. *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. Because J.M.Y. remains the legal father, his obligation to support the child persists, and the law provides no mechanism for him to recover these funds from R.R.

**3. Dual Paternity and Reimbursement**

J.M.Y. relies on the concept of dual fatherhood to support his claim for reimbursement. The Supreme Court addressed the issue of dual paternity in *Smith v. Cole*, 553 So.2d 847 (La.1989). In *Smith*, the Court held that the existence of a legal father does not extinguish the biological father's obligation to support the child. However, the Court explicitly declined to hold that the biological father's obligation extinguishes the legal father's duty or that the legal father is entitled to reimbursement or contribution from the biological father. The Court stated: "The question of whether the 'legal' father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." *Smith*, 553 So.2d at 855.

Furthermore, the present case is distinguishable from *Smith* because R.R. has never been established as the biological father through a filiation action. J.M.Y. merely alleges that R.R. is the biological father. Without a judicial determination of R.R.'s paternity, J.M.Y. has no basis upon which to assert a claim for reimbursement against him.

**4. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227**

J.M.Y. cites Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which states that fathers and mothers contract the obligation to support their children by the act of marrying. This article creates a mutual obligation between spouses to support their children; it does not create a right of reimbursement or contribution between a legal father and a third party. As established in *Gallo*, the support obligation is owed to the child, and Article 227 cannot be used to transform this obligation into a debt owed by the biological father to the legal father.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that J.M.Y., as the legal father of the child, has a continuous obligation to support the child that cannot be extinguished by the alleged biological paternity of R.R. Louisiana law does not provide a cause of action for a legal father to seek reimbursement from an alleged biological father, particularly where the legal father failed to disavow paternity and where the alleged biological father's paternity has not been established. The trial court correctly determined that J.M.Y. failed to state a cause of action and lacked a right of action.

**RULING**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

## II. RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S ASSERTIONS

### A. The Presumption of Paternity Cannot Be Ignored

Plaintiff-Appellant J.M.Y. fundamentally misapprehends the nature of Louisiana's paternity framework. Under former Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, "the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage." This presumption is conclusive when the husband fails to timely disavow paternity. As this Court correctly noted in *Pounds v. The facts establish that J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. ### B. The Support Obligation Runs to the Child, Not to the Legal Father

Plaintiff-Appellant J.M.Y. attempts to frame his claim as one for "reimbursement," suggesting that he is entitled to recover money he paid in satisfaction of a legal obligation. This framing ignores the fundamental nature of child support under Louisiana law.

In *Gallo v. Gallo*, 03-0794 (La. 12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, the Louisiana Supreme Court explicitly addressed precisely this type of claim. Mr. Gallo, like J.M.Y., was the legal father of a child born during his marriage. Like J.M.Y., he later discovered he was not the biological father. Like J.M.Y., he sought reimbursement for child support payments he had made. The Court further emphasized that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes." *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. The legal relationship between father and child creates obligations that are independent of biological ties. A legal father cannot treat child support as a debt owed to himself that he may recover from third parties.

### C. Louisiana Does Not Recognize a Right of Reimbursement Between Legal and Alleged Biological Fathers

Plaintiff-Appellant J.M.Y. cites Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 as creating some right of reimbursement. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, Article 227 states that "fathers and mothers, by the very act of marrying, contract together the obligation of supporting, maintaining, and educating their children." This article creates a mutual obligation between spouses—a duty owed to each other and to the child. It does not create a right of action for one spouse to recover support payments made from a third party.

Second, the Louisiana Supreme Court in *Smith v. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." *Smith*, 553 So.2d at 855.

The footnote to this holding is instructive: "The best interest of the child should be considered in determining whether the court in a given case will impose the obligation of support on the person who, by virtue of Article 184, is conclusively presumed to be the father of the child. This language demonstrates that the Supreme Court deliberately chose not to create a right of reimbursement. **FINAL RULING**

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by J.M.Y., plaintiff-appellant, seeking reversal of a trial court judgment granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action in favor of R.R. and A.F.Y., defendants. J.M.Y. filed suit seeking reimbursement for child support payments paid for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y., alleging that R.R. is the biological father. The trial court dismissed the suit, and J.M.Y. appeals, asserting that the court erred in granting the exceptions.

**STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appropriate standard of review for an exception of no cause of action is de novo. The function of this exception is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. In reviewing such a ruling, the appellate court must determine whether the plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged in the pleading, accepting well-pleaded facts as true. *Fink v. Bryant*, 01-0987 (La.11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349.

Similarly, an exception of no right of action is reviewed de novo. This exception determines whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. *Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc.*, 01-1623 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-03.

**ANALYSIS**

J.M.Y. argues that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments made for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. He contends that R.R. is the biological father and that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 creates an obligation for biological fathers that allows for such reimbursement. After reviewing the rebuttals of the parties, the Court finds these arguments unpersuasive based on established Louisiana jurisprudence and statutory law.

**1. Presumption of Paternity and Failure to Disavow**

Under Louisiana law, the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage. Former La. Civ. Code art. 184. J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. At that time, J.M.Y. had 180 days from the time he learned or should have learned of the birth to file a suit to disavow paternity. Former La. Civ. Code art. 189. J.M.Y. failed to file such an action. Consequently, his action for disavowal is preempted, and he is the conclusively presumed legal father of the child. *Pounds v. Schori*, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979). As noted in the defense rebuttal, J.M.Y. cannot now collaterally attack this established legal fatherhood through a suit for reimbursement.

**2. The Nature of the Support Obligation**

J.M.Y. seeks reimbursement for child support payments, treating the obligation as a private debt that can be recovered from a third party. However, Louisiana law establishes that a father's obligation to support his children is a primary, continuous obligation owed directly to the child, not to the other parent or a third party. In *Gallo v. Gallo*, 03-0794 (La.12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed a similar situation where a legal father sought reimbursement after discovering he was not the biological father. The Court denied the claim, stating that "the child is the veritable creditor of each parent’s unilateral obligation for the child’s upbringing." *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. The Court emphasized that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes" and that the legal tie of paternity is not affected by subsequent proof of biological ties. *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. Because J.M.Y. remains the legal father, his obligation to support the child persists, and the law provides no mechanism for him to recover these funds from R.R.

**3. Dual Paternity and Reimbursement**

J.M.Y. relies on the concept of dual fatherhood to support his claim for reimbursement. The Supreme Court addressed the issue of dual paternity in *Smith v. Cole*, 553 So.2d 847 (La.1989). J.M.Y. cites Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which states that fathers and mothers contract the obligation to support their children by the act of marrying. The function of this exception is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. In reviewing such a ruling, the appellate court must determine whether the plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged in the pleading, accepting well-pleaded facts as true. *Fink v. Bryant*, 01-0987 (La.11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349.

Similarly, an exception of no right of action is reviewed de novo. This exception determines whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. *Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc.*, 01-1623 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-03.

**ANALYSIS**

J.M.Y. argues that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments made for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. He contends that R.R. is the biological father and that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 creates an obligation for biological fathers that allows for such reimbursement. After reviewing the rebuttals of the parties, the Court finds these arguments unpersuasive based on established Louisiana jurisprudence and statutory law.

**1. Presumption of Paternity and Failure to Disavow**

Under Louisiana law, the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage. Former La. Civ. Code art. 184. J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. At that time, J.M.Y. had 180 days from the time he learned or should have learned of the birth to file a suit to disavow paternity. Former La. Civ. Code art. 189. J.M.Y. failed to file such an action. Consequently, his action for disavowal is preempted, and he is the conclusively presumed legal father of the child. *Pounds v. Schori*, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979). As noted in the defense rebuttal, J.M.Y. cannot now collaterally attack this established legal fatherhood through a suit for reimbursement.

**2. The Nature of the Support Obligation**

J.M.Y. seeks reimbursement for child support payments, treating the obligation as a private debt that can be recovered from a third party. However, Louisiana law establishes that a father's obligation to support his children is a primary, continuous obligation owed directly to the child, not to the other parent or a third party. In *Gallo v. Gallo*, 03-0794 (La.12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed a similar situation where a legal father sought reimbursement after discovering he was not the biological father. The Court denied the claim, stating that "the child is the veritable creditor of each parent’s unilateral obligation for the child’s upbringing." *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. The Court emphasized that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes" and that the legal tie of paternity is not affected by subsequent proof of biological ties. *Gallo*, 861 So.2d at 178. Because J.M.Y. remains the legal father, his obligation to support the child persists, and the law provides no mechanism for him to recover these funds from R.R.

**3. Dual Paternity and Reimbursement**

J.M.Y. relies on the concept of dual fatherhood to support his claim for reimbursement. The Supreme Court addressed the issue of dual paternity in *Smith v. Cole*, 553 So.2d 847 (La.1989). In *Smith*, the Court held that the existence of a legal father does not extinguish the biological father's obligation to support the child. However, the Court explicitly declined to hold that the biological father's obligation extinguishes the legal father's duty or that the legal father is entitled to reimbursement or contribution from the biological father. The Court stated: "The question of whether the 'legal' father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." *Smith*, 553 So.2d at 855.

Furthermore, the present case is distinguishable from *Smith* because R.R. has never been established as the biological father through a filiation action. J.M.Y. merely alleges that R.R. is the biological father. Louisiana Civil Code Article 197 provides that a child may institute an action to prove paternity, yet no such action has been filed. Without a judicial determination of R.R.'s paternity, J.M.Y. has no basis upon which to assert a claim for reimbursement against him. The defense correctly argues that J.M.Y. asks this Court to create a novel cause of action against a person who has never been adjudicated the biological father.

**4. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227**

J.M.Y. cites Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which states that fathers and mothers contract the obligation to support their children by the act of marrying. This article creates a mutual obligation between spouses to support their children; it does not create a right of reimbursement or contribution between a legal father and a third party. As established in *Gallo*, the support obligation is owed to the child, and Article 227 cannot be used to transform this obligation into a debt owed by the biological father to the legal father.

**5. Policy Considerations**

The Court agrees with the policy considerations raised in the defense rebuttal. Permitting J.M.Y. to pursue this claim would create perverse incentives, allowing a legal father who fails to timely disavow paternity to wait years and then attempt to recover support payments from an alleged biological father. This would destabilize child support arrangements and harm the children Louisiana law seeks to protect. As the Supreme Court noted in *Smith*, the "best interest of the child should be considered" in these matters. *Smith*, 553 So.2d at 855, n.8.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that J.M.Y., as the legal father of the child, has a continuous obligation to support the child that cannot be extinguished by the alleged biological paternity of R.R. Louisiana law does not provide a cause of action for a legal father to seek reimbursement from an alleged biological father, particularly where the legal father failed to disavow paternity and where the alleged biological father's paternity has not been established. The trial court correctly determined that J.M.Y. failed to state a cause of action and lacked a right of action.

**RULING**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.
