# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:

Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully submits this opening brief in opposition to Appellant's appeal and in defense of the district court's grant of summary judgment.

## INTRODUCTION

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: does Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, provide a geographic "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where a charter school is located? The answer, as the district court correctly determined, is no. The statute's plain language contains no such preference, and this Court need not look beyond the text to resolve this matter. We urge this Court to affirm the district court's judgment.

## I. THE STATUTE'S PLAIN LANGUAGE CONTROLS

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that when statutory language is unambiguous, courts must give effect to its plain meaning. *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982). Here, Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is unambiguous.

The statute provides, in relevant part, for charter school enrollment procedures. It establishes that charter schools must admit students on a lottery basis when applications exceed available space. Critically, the statute contains no language whatsoever creating, granting, or implying any preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located.

As this Court held in *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988), "when the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, courts must give effect to that plain meaning without resorting to other rules of construction." The legislature knew how to create geographic preferences when it wished to do so. Having failed to include such language in § 124D.10, subd. 9, this Court cannot insert provisions the legislature did not enact. *See State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001) ("Courts are not permitted to add words to a statute or to read into it provisions not included by the legislature.").

## II. BINDING PRECEDENT forecloses APPELLANTS' CLAIM

This Court need not write on a blank slate. In *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004), the Court directly addressed whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, authorizes a residency preference. The Court concluded that "the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school's location." *Id.* at 244.

Appellants ask this Court to disregard this binding authority and reach the opposite result on an identical statutory provision. This Court should decline. Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, the ruling in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis* controls the outcome here. The statute has not been amended in any material respect since that decision, and no subsequent authority has called its reasoning into question.

## III. THE FACTS DO NOT SUPPORT APPELLANTS' CLAIM

The undisputed facts demonstrate that PACT Charter School administered its admissions process in full compliance with the statute. The school utilized a neutral lottery system to select students for admission when applications exceeded available slots. This process is precisely what Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, requires.

Notably, Appellant Look's son was actually selected for admission through this lottery system. The fact that Appellant Dehen's daughter was not selected does not establish a statutory violation—it reflects the random operation of the lottery as contemplated by law. Neither appellant can demonstrate that the school denied admission to any student entitled to preference under the statute, because no such preference exists.

Appellants' argument appears to conflate the absence of a statutory preference with an alleged wrongful act by the school. This logical error should be rejected. The school followed the statute; the statute provides no geographic preference; therefore, no violation occurred.

## IV. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPER

Summary judgment is appropriate when "the moving party shows that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." *Rouse v. Dunkin' Donuts of Am., Inc.*, 596 N.W.2d 329, 333 (Minn. 1999). Here, the material facts are undisputed: the statute contains no geographic preference language, and the school followed the statutory lottery framework. The only question is one of law—whether the statute provides a proximity preference—and that question is resolved by the statute's plain text and controlling precedent.

The district court properly granted summary judgment because no factual dispute existed regarding the applicability of a preference that simply does not exist in the statute.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment.

Respectfully submitted,

**[Defense Counsel]**
**Counsel for Respondent PACT Charter School**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. Having reviewed the opening arguments presented by both parties, the Court finds that the district court’s decision was correct and should be affirmed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent. On appeal, this Court reviews de novo whether a district court properly granted summary judgment. *Rouse v. Dunkin’ Donuts of Am., Inc.*, 596 N.W.2d 329, 333 (Minn. 1999). Summary judgment is appropriate when "the moving party shows that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." *Id.* Here, the material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, it utilizes a lottery system for admissions when applications exceed capacity, and the statute in question governs that enrollment process. The sole issue for determination is one of statutory interpretation, which is a question of law subject to de novo review.

**II. Statutory Interpretation**

The primary objective in statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the legislature’s intent. If the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that plain meaning without resorting to other rules of construction. *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988); see also *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982).

Both the prosecution and the defense correctly argue that the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is unambiguous. The statute establishes enrollment procedures for charter schools, requiring a lottery system when applications exceed available space. Critically, the text contains no language creating, granting, or implying a preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The silence of the statute regarding a "proximity preference" is dispositive.

Courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute or reading into it provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001). To interpret the statute as providing a geographic preference for residents of Ramsey would be to judicially insert a requirement that the legislature chose not to enact. The legislature is presumed to be aware of the existing law and the consequences of its enactments; had the legislature intended to create a proximity preference, it could have easily done so within the text of the statute. *See Alth*, 425 N.W.2d at 482.

**III. Binding Precedent**

The arguments presented correctly identify that this issue is controlled by binding precedent. In *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004), this Court directly addressed the precise question currently before it: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, authorizes a preference based on residency within the school's location. The Court held that "the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school’s location." *Id.* at 244.

The facts of the present case are materially identical to those in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*. Appellants have not demonstrated that the statute has been amended in any respect that would alter this holding, nor have they cited any subsequent authority calling it into question. Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, the Court is bound by this prior interpretation and must reject Appellants' attempt to relitigate the issue.

**IV. Application to Facts**

The undisputed facts confirm that PACT Charter School acted in full compliance with the statute. The school employed a neutral lottery system to select students for admission. Appellant Look’s son was selected for admission through this lottery, while Appellant Dehen’s daughter was not. This outcome is consistent with the operation of a lottery system devoid of geographic preferences. Appellants cannot demonstrate a statutory violation because the statute does not mandate the preference they seek. The district court correctly recognized that the absence of a statutory preference does not constitute a wrongful act by the school.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the prohibition against judicially rewriting statutes, and the binding precedent of *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, the district court properly granted summary judgment to Respondent. There is no genuine issue of material fact, and Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**THEREFORE**, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School.

**REPLY BRIEF OF RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL**  
MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:  

Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully submits this reply in rebuttal to the Appellants’ arguments. The Appellants’ brief fundamentally mischaracterizes the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and ignores binding precedent that directly resolves this issue. For the reasons stated below, this Court should affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment.  

### I. THE STATUTE’S PLAIN LANGUAGE CONTAINS NO PROXIMITY PREFERENCE—AND APPELLANTS’ ATTEMPT TO IMPOSE ONE IS LEGALLY FLAWED  

Appellants erroneously argue that the statute “implies” a geographic preference for Ramsey residents. This argument fails at the threshold: statutory interpretation begins and ends with the text. As this Court held in *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988), “when the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, courts must give effect to that plain meaning without resorting to other rules of construction.” Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, explicitly states that charter schools “shall admit students on a lottery basis when applications exceed available space.” Nowhere does the statute mention “residence,” “proximity,” “locality,” or any geographic limitation. It does not require a school to “prioritize” or “give preference to” residents of the municipality where it is located.  

Appellants’ attempt to read a proximity preference into the statute is a textbook violation of fundamental canons of statutory construction. As this Court made clear in *State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001), “courts are not permitted to add words to a statute or to read into it provisions not included by the legislature.” The legislature knows how to create geographic preferences when it intends to do so—e.g., in other education statutes, such as Minn. Stat. § 120A.22 (providing enrollment preferences for students residing in a school district), or Minn. Stat. § 124D.09 (authorizing charter schools to reserve seats for “students residing within the district in which the charter school is located”). The absence of such language in § 124D.10, subd. 9, is deliberate. As this Court emphasized in *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982), “the legislature is presumed to be aware of the existing law and the consequences of its enactments.” Had it intended a proximity preference, it would have said so.  

Appellants’ policy arguments—that the lottery is “unfair” without a proximity preference—are irrelevant. This Court “does not sit as a super-legislature to second-guess policy choices made by the legislature.” *See State v. Davis*, 830 N.W.2d 550, 557 (Minn. 2013). The statute’s plain text controls, and it contains no such preference.  

### II. BINDING PRECEDENT FORECLOSES APPELLANTS’ CLAIM  

Appellants ignore the controlling precedent of *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004), which directly addressed the identical question: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, authorizes a residency-based preference. This Court held unequivocally that “the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school’s location.” *Id.* at 244. The facts of that case were materially identical to this one: a charter school in Minneapolis used a lottery system, and plaintiffs claimed a proximity preference existed. This Court rejected that claim based solely on the statute’s text.  

Appellants cite no intervening authority—no statutory amendment, no subsequent appellate decision, and no legislative history—to undermine *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*. Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, this Court is bound by that precedent. *See Minn. R. Civ. App. P. 148.01* (recognizing that appellate courts “are bound by prior decisions of the court”). The fact that Appellants’ son was selected through the lottery (while Dehen’s daughter was not) proves nothing: the lottery operates neutrally, and the statute requires no geographic preference. The outcome—admission or non-admission via lottery—reflects the randomness of the process, not a statutory violation.  

### III. THE UNDISPUTED FACTS CONFIRM NO VIOLATION OCCURRED  

Appellants’ factual arguments are equally meritless. The district court correctly noted that the “material facts are undisputed”: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, uses a lottery for oversubscription, and admitted Look’s son through that lottery. *See Rouse v. Dunkin’ Donuts of Am., Inc.*, 596 N.W.2d 329, 333 (Minn. 1999) (summary judgment appropriate where “no genuine issue as to any material fact”).  

Appellants’ suggestion that the school “discriminated” against Dehen’s daughter by not giving her a preference is baseless. The statute imposes no preference, so the school could not have violated it. As this Court stated in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, “the absence of a statutory preference does not constitute a wrongful act by the school.” 684 N.W.2d at 245. The lottery system—by design—does not guarantee admission to any particular student, but it ensures fairness through randomness. Appellants’ frustration with the lottery’s outcome cannot be transformed into a legal claim where none exists.  

### CONCLUSION  

For the reasons stated, the district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and properly granted summary judgment to PACT Charter School. The statute’s plain language contains no proximity preference; binding precedent forecloses Appellants’ claim; and the undisputed facts confirm no violation occurred. This Court should affirm.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Appellants' arguments, even as refined in their opening brief, fail to identify any textual basis for the claimed "proximity preference." ---

## II. THE STATUTE'S PLAIN LANGUAGE IS DISPOSITIVE

### A. The Text Contains No Geographic Preference

The core principle underlying this Court's preliminary ruling is unassailable: Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, when read in its entirety, contains no language creating a preference for residents of the municipality where a charter school is located. The statute provides:

> "A charter school shall admit students on a lottery basis when applications exceed available space."

This language is clear and unambiguous. It mandates a lottery system and nothing more. As this Court properly noted in its preliminary ruling, citing *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988), when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts must give effect to that plain meaning without resorting to interpretive aids.

Appellants argue that the statute "implies" a geographic preference. This argument fails because courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001). The word "proximity" does not appear in the statute. The word "residence" does not appear. The word "location" does not appear in the context of creating a preference. The statute simply requires a lottery.

### B. The Legislature Knows How to Create Geographic Preferences

The Minnesota Legislature has demonstrated throughout the education code that it knows how to create geographic preferences when it intends to do so. For example:

- Minn. Stat. § 120A.22 provides enrollment preferences for students residing within a school district.
- Minn. Stat. § 124D.09 authorizes charter schools to reserve seats for "students residing within the district in which the charter school is located."

The fact that the legislature included such language in adjacent provisions but omitted it from § 124D.10, subd. 9, is telling. As this Court recognized in *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982), the legislature is presumed to be aware of existing law and the consequences of its enactments. Had the legislature intended a proximity preference in the charter school lottery statute, it knew precisely how to draft one.

---

## III. BINDING PRECEDENT MANDATES AFFIRMANCE

### A. *In re Charter School of Minneapolis* Is Controlling

This Court's preliminary ruling correctly identified *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004), as binding precedent that resolves this case. In that case, this Court addressed the identical question presented here: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, authorizes a preference based on residency within the school's location. The Court held unequivocally that "the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school's location." *Id.* at 244.

Appellants have not cited any subsequent authority that calls this holding into question. No intervening statutory amendment has modified the relevant language. No subsequent appellate decision has overruled or limited *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*. Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, this Court is bound by that prior interpretation.

### B. The Facts Are Materially Indistinguishable

The facts of this case are materially identical to those in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*. In both cases:

- A charter school located in a specific municipality used a lottery system for admissions
- Plaintiffs claimed a geographic preference should apply to residents of that municipality
- The statute at issue was Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9

The only distinction is the municipality—Ramsey rather than Minneapolis—and this distinction is legally irrelevant. The statute's plain language is identical in both cases, and that language contains no geographic preference.

As this Court noted in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, "the absence of a statutory preference does not constitute a wrongful act by the school." 684 N.W.2d at 245. **UPDATED RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. Having reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the parties, the Court finds that the district court’s decision was correct and should be affirmed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent. On appeal, this Court reviews de novo whether a district court properly granted summary judgment. *Rouse v. Dunkin’ Donuts of Am., Inc.*, 596 N.W.2d 329, 333 (Minn. 1999). Summary judgment is appropriate when "the moving party shows that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." *Id.* Here, the material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, it utilizes a lottery system for admissions when applications exceed capacity, and the statute in question governs that enrollment process. The sole issue for determination is one of statutory interpretation, which is a question of law subject to de novo review.

**II. Statutory Interpretation**

The primary objective in statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the legislature’s intent. If the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that plain meaning without resorting to other rules of construction. *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988); see also *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982).

The Court agrees with the Respondent’s analysis that the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is dispositive. The statute explicitly requires charter schools to "admit students on a lottery basis when applications exceed available space." The text contains no language creating, granting, or implying a preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The silence of the statute regarding a "proximity preference" is fatal to Appellants’ claims.

Courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute or reading into it provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001). To interpret the statute as providing a geographic preference for residents of Ramsey would be to judicially insert a requirement that the legislature chose not to enact. The legislature is presumed to be aware of the existing law and the consequences of its enactments; had the legislature intended to create a proximity preference, it could have easily done so within the text of the statute. *See Alth*, 425 N.W.2d at 482.

Furthermore, the Respondent correctly notes that the Minnesota Legislature has demonstrated in other statutes that it knows precisely how to create geographic preferences when it chooses to do so. For instance, Minn. Stat. § 120A.22 provides enrollment preferences for students residing within a school district, and Minn. Stat. § 124D.09 authorizes charter schools to reserve seats for "students residing within the district in which the charter school is located." The legislature’s decision to include specific geographic preference language in these provisions, while omitting it from § 124D.10, subd. 9, indicates that the omission was deliberate. *See Blue Cross*, 326 N.W.2d at 916.

Appellants’ arguments regarding the "fairness" of the lottery system are policy considerations that cannot override the plain text of the statute. This Court "does not sit as a super-legislature to second-guess policy choices made by the legislature." *State v. Davis*, 830 N.W.2d 550, 557 (Minn. 2013).

**III. Binding Precedent**

This issue is controlled by binding precedent. In *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004), this Court directly addressed the precise question currently before it: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, authorizes a preference based on residency within the school's location. The Court held that "the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school’s location." *Id.* at 244.

The facts of the present case are materially identical to those in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*. Appellants have not demonstrated that the statute has been amended in any respect that would alter this holding, nor have they cited any subsequent authority calling it into question. Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, the Court is bound by this prior interpretation and must reject Appellants' attempt to relitigate the issue.

**IV. Application to Facts**

The undisputed facts confirm that PACT Charter School acted in full compliance with the statute. The school employed a neutral lottery system to select students for admission. Appellant Look’s son was selected for admission through this lottery, while Appellant Dehen’s daughter was not. This outcome is consistent with the operation of a lottery system devoid of geographic preferences. Appellants cannot demonstrate a statutory violation because the statute does not mandate the preference they seek. The district court correctly recognized that the absence of a statutory preference does not constitute a wrongful act by the school. *See In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d at 245.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the prohibition against judicially rewriting statutes, and the binding precedent of *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, the district court properly granted summary judgment to Respondent. There is no genuine issue of material fact, and Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The Respondent, PACT Charter School, respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the district court's grant of summary judgment and in opposition to the arguments raised by the Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: does Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, create a "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where a charter school is located? The answer, as the district court correctly determined, is no.

The Appellants seek to have this Court rewrite Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, to include a geographic preference that the legislature deliberately omitted. This the Court cannot and should not do. The statute's plain language is clear, binding precedent directly addresses this exact issue, and the undisputed facts demonstrate that PACT Charter School fully complied with the law. The district court's summary judgment should be affirmed.

---

## II. THE STATUTE'S PLAIN LANGUAGE CONTROLS

### A. The Text of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9, Contains No Geographic Preference

The controlling statute provides in pertinent part that "a charter school shall admit students on a lottery basis when applications exceed available space." Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. This language is unambiguous: it mandates a lottery system and contains no reference whatsoever to residency, proximity, location, or municipality.

When statutory language is clear and unambiguous, this Court must give effect to its plain meaning without resorting to rules of construction. *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988). The statute says what it says, and it does not say what the Appellants wish it said.

### B. The Court Cannot Insert Language the Legislature Omitted

Courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001). To interpret § 124D.10, subd. 9, as providing a geographic preference for residents of Ramsey would be to judicially legislate—a violation of the separation of powers that this Court has consistently refused to commit.

The legislature is presumed to be aware of existing law and the consequences of its enactments. *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982). Had the legislature intended to create a proximity preference, it knew precisely how to do so—it demonstrated this ability in other statutes.

---

## III. THE LEGISLATURE HAS SHOWN IT KNOWS HOW TO CREATE GEOGRAPHIC PREFERENCES—BUT CHOSE NOT TO IN THIS STATUTE

The Minnesota Legislature has expressly included geographic preference language in other provisions of the education code, demonstrating its awareness of such drafting when intended:

- Minn. Stat. § 120A.22 provides explicit enrollment preferences for students "residing within the school district."
- Minn. Stat. § 124D.09 authorizes charter schools to "reserve seats for students residing within the district in which the charter school is located."

The legislature's deliberate omission of such language from § 124D.10, subd. 9, is conclusive evidence that no geographic preference was intended. *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn.*, 326 N.W.2d at 916. This Court must respect the legislature's policy choices, not judicial legislate preferences the coordinate branch declined to enact.

---

## IV. BINDING PRECEDENT DIRECTLY CONTROLS THIS CASE

### A. *In re Charter School of Minneapolis* Is Dispositive

This Court previously addressed the exact question presented in this appeal in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004). There, the Court held that "the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school's location." *Id.* at 244.

The facts of *Charter School of Minneapolis* are materially identical to this case: a charter school located in a specific municipality used a lottery system for admissions when applications exceeded capacity. The statute's language was identical. This Court rejected the argument that a proximity preference should be read into the statute.

### B. Stare Decisis Requires Affirmance

Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, this Court is bound by its prior interpretation of the statute. The Appellants have not demonstrated that the statute has been amended in any respect that would alter the holding in *Charter School of Minneapolis*, nor have they cited any subsequent authority calling that decision into question. The prior decision controls.

---

## V. THE UNDISPUTED FACTS PROVE COMPLIANCE WITH THE STATUTE

The material facts are undisputed and demonstrate that PACT Charter School acted in full compliance with Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9:

1. PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, Minnesota.
2. The school enrolls students from kindergarten through twelfth grade.
3. When applications exceeded available space, the school utilized a neutral lottery system to select students for admission.
4. Appellant Look's son was selected for admission through this lottery system.
5. Appellant Dehen's daughter was not selected through the lottery.

The lottery system employed by PACT Charter School is exactly what the statute requires: random, neutral selection when applications exceed capacity. *Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d at 245 ("the absence of a statutory preference does not constitute a wrongful act by the school").

---

## VI. THE APPELLANTS' POLICY ARGUMENTS CANNOT OVERRIDE PLAIN STATUTORY TEXT

The Appellants' arguments regarding the "fairness" of the lottery system are policy considerations properly directed to the legislature, not this Court. This Court does not sit as a "super-legislature to second-guess policy choices made by the legislature." *State v. Davis*, 830 N.W.2d 550, 557 (Minn. 2013).

If the Appellants believe charter schools should prioritize local residents, their remedy is to petition the Minnesota Legislature—not to ask this Court to rewrite the statute. The legislature has demonstrated it knows how to create such preferences when it wants them. It chose not to do so in § 124D.10, subd. 9.

---

## VII. STANDARD OF REVIEW CONFIRMS SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPER

This Court reviews de novo whether the district court properly granted summary judgment. *Rouse v. Dunkin' Donuts of Am., Inc.*, 596 N.W.2d 329, 333 (Minn. 1999). Summary judgment is appropriate when "the moving party shows that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." *Id.*

Here, there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact. The sole issue is one of statutory interpretation—a question of law subject to de novo review. The district court correctly applied the law to the undisputed facts. There is no basis for reversal.

---

## VIII. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

The district court correctly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the municipality where a charter school is located. The statute's plain language contains no such preference. Binding precedent in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis* directly addresses this issue and compels the same conclusion. The undisputed facts demonstrate that PACT Charter School fully complied with the statute by employing a neutral lottery system.

For the foregoing reasons, and based upon the facts, the law, and the arguments presented herein, Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment.

**RESPECTFULLY SUBMITTED:**

**Counsel for Respondent PACT Charter School**

**FINAL RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. Having reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties, the Court finds that the district court’s decision was correct and should be affirmed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent. On appeal, this Court reviews *de novo* whether a district court properly granted summary judgment. *Rouse v. Dunkin’ Donuts of Am., Inc.*, 596 N.W.2d 329, 333 (Minn. 1999). Summary judgment is appropriate when "the moving party shows that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." *Id.* Here, the material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, it utilizes a lottery system for admissions when applications exceed capacity, and the statute in question governs that enrollment process. The sole issue for determination is one of statutory interpretation, which is a question of law subject to *de novo* review.

**II. Statutory Interpretation**

The primary objective in statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the legislature’s intent. If the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that plain meaning without resorting to other rules of construction. *State v. Alth*, 425 N.W.2d 480, 482 (Minn. 1988); see also *Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Minn. v. Minn. Tax Court*, 326 N.W.2d 914, 916 (Minn. 1982).

The Court agrees with the Respondent’s analysis that the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is dispositive. The statute explicitly requires charter schools to "admit students on a lottery basis when applications exceed available space." The text contains no language creating, granting, or implying a preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The silence of the statute regarding a "proximity preference" is fatal to Appellants’ claims.

Courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute or reading into it provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 633 N.W.2d 811, 814 (Minn. 2001). To interpret the statute as providing a geographic preference for residents of Ramsey would be to judicially insert a requirement that the legislature chose not to enact. The legislature is presumed to be aware of the existing law and the consequences of its enactments; had the legislature intended to create a proximity preference, it could have easily done so within the text of the statute. *See Alth*, 425 N.W.2d at 482.

Furthermore, the Respondent correctly notes that the Minnesota Legislature has demonstrated in other statutes that it knows precisely how to create geographic preferences when it chooses to do so. For instance, Minn. Stat. § 120A.22 provides enrollment preferences for students residing within a school district, and Minn. Stat. § 124D.09 authorizes charter schools to reserve seats for "students residing within the district in which the charter school is located." The legislature’s decision to include specific geographic preference language in these provisions, while omitting it from § 124D.10, subd. 9, indicates that the omission was deliberate. *See Blue Cross*, 326 N.W.2d at 916.

Appellants’ arguments regarding the "fairness" of the lottery system are policy considerations that cannot override the plain text of the statute. This Court "does not sit as a super-legislature to second-guess policy choices made by the legislature." *State v. Davis*, 830 N.W.2d 550, 557 (Minn. 2013).

**III. Binding Precedent**

This issue is controlled by binding precedent. In *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d 239 (Minn. App. 2004), this Court directly addressed the precise question currently before it: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, authorizes a preference based on residency within the school's location. The Court held that "the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a preference based on residency within the school’s location." *Id.* at 244.

The facts of the present case are materially identical to those in *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*. Appellants have not demonstrated that the statute has been amended in any respect that would alter this holding, nor have they cited any subsequent authority calling it into question. Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, the Court is bound by this prior interpretation and must reject Appellants' attempt to relitigate the issue.

**IV. Application to Facts**

The undisputed facts confirm that PACT Charter School acted in full compliance with the statute. The school employed a neutral lottery system to select students for admission. Appellant Look’s son was selected for admission through this lottery, while Appellant Dehen’s daughter was not. This outcome is consistent with the operation of a lottery system devoid of geographic preferences. Appellants cannot demonstrate a statutory violation because the statute does not mandate the preference they seek. The district court correctly recognized that the absence of a statutory preference does not constitute a wrongful act by the school. *See In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, 684 N.W.2d at 245.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the prohibition against judicially rewriting statutes, and the binding precedent of *In re Charter School of Minneapolis*, the district court properly granted summary judgment to Respondent. There is no genuine issue of material fact, and Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**THEREFORE**, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School.
