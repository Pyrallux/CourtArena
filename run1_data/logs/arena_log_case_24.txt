# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals, State of Arizona

---

**Introduction and Statement of Issues**

The defense respectfully submits that the family court's ruling should be affirmed. This case presents the question of whether Arizona's presumed parentage statute, A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), applies to a female spouse who intentionally participated in the creation of a child through artificial insemination, was named on the child's birth certificate, and was held out by the biological mother as the child's co-parent throughout the child's life. We contend that the family court correctly applied the statute in a gender-neutral manner consistent with *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), and that the doctrine of equitable estoppel properly bars Turner from now denying Oakley's parental rights.

---

## I. The Presumption Statute Must Be Applied Gender-Neutrally

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Supports a Gender-Neutral Interpretation

The plaintiff argues that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) contains explicit gendered language that confines its application to men. However, this argument mischaracterizes the statute's operative provisions. The relevant portion of the statute creates a presumption of parentage based on the marital relationship and the birth of a child during marriage. The statute does not turn on gendered pronouns but rather on the legal status of the parties as spouses and the birth of a child during that marital relationship.

Arizona law recognizes that statutes must be interpreted according to their plain meaning, and when the legislature intends gender-specific application, it knows how to craft such language explicitly. *See State v. Garcia*, 176 Ariz. 207, 211, 859 P.2d 1281, 1285 (App. 1993) ("We presume that the legislature intended the plain meaning of the statute's words, and we construe them according to their natural and ordinary meaning."). Here, the statute's purpose—establishing parentage for children born into a marital relationship—applies with equal force to same-sex marriages following *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), and Arizona's recognition of such unions.

### B. *McLaughlin v. Jones* Correctly Interpreted the Statute

The family court properly relied on *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), which held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. In *McLaughlin*, this Court reasoned that the statute's purpose is to establish parentage for children born to married couples, and extending that presumption to same-sex spouses fulfills the legislature's intent to ensure that children of married couples have two legal parents. Id. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *McLaughlin* by arguing it conflicts with *State v. Maldonado*, 223 Ariz. 309, 223 P.3d 661 (2010). However, this argument fails. *Maldonado* stands for the proposition that courts cannot amend statutes to reflect "contemporary values" when the legislature has clearly expressed a contrary intent. Here, however, the legislature's intent in enacting § 25-814(A)(1) was to provide a mechanism for establishing parentage for children born during marriage. There is no indication the legislature intended to exclude same-sex spouses from this protection, particularly given that the statute was amended after Arizona recognized same-sex marriage to address such situations.

The plaintiff further argues that *McLaughlin* improperly ignored the statute's gendered framework. To the contrary, *McLaughlin* properly recognized that the statute's essential mechanism—the presumption arising from marriage and birth during that marriage—applies equally to all married couples regardless of gender. The statute's use of gender-specific terms in some provisions does not overcome the fundamental purpose underlying the presumed parentage framework.

### C. The Plaintiff Misreads *State v. Superior Court (Henderson)* and *State v. Valenzuela*

The plaintiff relies heavily on *State v. Superior Court (Henderson)*, 132 Ariz. 169, 644 P.2d 860 (1982), and *State v. Valenzuela*, 154 Ariz. 236, 741 P.2d 1202 (App. 1987), for the proposition that gendered statutory language must be applied as written. These cases are distinguishable.

In *Henderson*, the Court considered a statute that explicitly used "he" and "him" to describe jurors, and the Court correctly applied the statute as written because the legislature had made a clear policy choice. However, the Court also recognized that when statutory language is ambiguous or when the statute's purpose suggests a broader application, courts may interpret the statute consistent with its purpose. Here, § 25-814(A)(1) is not ambiguous in its core function—establishing parentage for children of married couples—and applying it gender-neutrally serves rather than frustrates the statute's purpose.

Similarly, in *Valenzuela*, the Court declined to extend a statute referencing "husband and wife" to same-sex partners at a time when such partnerships were not recognized under Arizona law. That holding was necessarily premised on the legal non-existence of same-sex marriage at the time. Following *Obergefell* and Arizona's subsequent recognition of same-sex marriage, the reasoning of *Valenzuela* no longer applies. The statute must now be applied in a manner consistent with the legal reality of recognized same-sex marriages.

---

## II. Even If the Presumption Applies, Oakley Qualifies as a Presumed Parent Under the Statute's Terms

### A. Oakley Meets the Statutory Criteria

Under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), a person is presumed to be a parent if the person is married to the child's mother and the child is born during the marriage. Here, Oakley and Turner were married in October 2014, and C.T. was born in September 2015—during the marriage. The statute requires no more than this marital relationship and temporal nexus.

The plaintiff argues that Oakley cannot qualify because she is not biologically related to C.T. However, the statute contains no biological requirement for presumed parents. A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) creates a rebuttable presumption based on the marital relationship and birth during marriage, not on biological connection. The plaintiff's attempt to impose a biological requirement finds no support in the statute's text.

### B. Oakley's Intentional Participation in the Conception Process Strengthens Her Claim

The facts demonstrate that Oakley was not a passive bystander but an intentional participant in the conception of C.T. Oakley reviewed sperm donor profiles, accompanied Turner to medical appointments, and was present during the insemination procedures. This intentional participation distinguishes Oakley from a stranger and demonstrates her intent to be a parent to any resulting child.

The fertility clinic documents signed by Turner expressly contemplated that Oakley would be a parent, stating that any child born would be the legal child of "both recipient and recipient's husband or partner if applicable." This provision reflects the parties' understanding and the medical profession's recognition that Oakley would be a legal parent to any child conceived during their relationship.

---

## III. The Doctrine of Equitable Estoppel Properly Bars Turner

### A. The Elements of Equitable Estoppel Are Satisfied

Arizona law recognizes that a biological mother may be equitably estopped from denying another person's parental rights when the mother has represented that the other person is the child's parent and the other person has relied on that representation to their detriment. *See McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 565-67 ¶¶ 16-23, 382 P.3d at 123-24.

Here, the evidence overwhelmingly establishes the elements of equitable estoppel. Turner represented to Oakley, to the medical community, to friends and family, and to the world through social media that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent. Turner listed Oakley on the birth certificate, called Oakley "mom" in social media posts, and drafted a will naming Oakley as the child's sole custodian in the event of Turner's death. These representations were not incidental—they went to the heart of Oakley's parental status.

Oakley relied on these representations in forming the intent to participate in the conception process and in parenting C.T. from birth. Oakley invested significant emotional and physical resources in raising C.T., including being present at the birth and cutting the umbilical cord. To allow Turner to now deny Oakley's parental rights would work a profound injustice and enable Turner to benefit from her own prior representations.

### B. The Family Court Properly Applied Equitable Estoppel

The family court conducted an evidentiary hearing and heard testimony from both Turner and Oakley about Turner's representations. The court considered social media posts, the birth certificate, and other evidence. Based on this record, the court concluded that Turner was equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of Oakley's parentage.

This Court should defer to the family court's factual findings, which are supported by substantial evidence in the record. The court's conclusion that the parties intended to raise C.T. together as co-parents is amply supported by the evidence of Turner's consistent representations over C.T.'s entire life.

---

## IV. The Birth Certificate Is Evidence of Oakley's Parentage

The plaintiff argues that Oakley's listing on the birth certificate cannot override the legislature's intent. However, the birth certificate is not merely evidence of Oakley's parentage—it is itself a legal document establishing parentage under Arizona law.

Arizona law provides that a birth certificate showing a person as a parent creates a rebuttable presumption of parentage. *See* A.R.S. § 36-332 (creating presumptions based on birth certificate information). While the plaintiff is correct that the birth certificate alone does not establish immutable parentage, it is strong evidence of the parties' intent and the state's recognition of Oakley as C.T.'s parent.

Here, the hospital nurse specifically instructed Turner to list Oakley on the birth certificate in the section designated "father," demonstrating the hospital's understanding that Oakley would be recognized as a legal parent. Turner followed this instruction, listing both herself and Oakley on the birth certificate. Turner cannot now claim this was error when she affirmatively chose to list Oakley.

---

## V. Policy Considerations Support the Ruling

The plaintiff warns that applying the presumption statute to women "creates legal chaos" and would "destabilize Arizona's entire framework for establishing parentage." This argument is without merit.

Arizona law already distinguishes between biological parents, adoptive parents, and presumed parents. Applying the presumption statute gender-neutrally does not collapse these distinctions—it simply ensures that children of married couples have the same protection regardless of their parents' gender. This is the approach taken by every jurisdiction that has addressed the question since *Obergefell*, and there is no evidence of the "chaos" the plaintiff predicts.

To the contrary, denying Oakley parental rights would create chaos. C.T. has known Oakley as a parent from birth. Turner represented to C.T., to Oakley, and to the world that Oakley was C.T.'s parent. Denying Oakley parental status would deprive C.T. of a legal parent and all the rights that entails, including inheritance rights, support rights, and the right to know and be raised by both parents.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's ruling. The presumption of parentage under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies to Oakley as the spouse of C.T.'s biological mother, and Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental rights. The family court correctly applied Arizona law, and its ruling should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for special action review regarding the establishment of legal parentage. The central issue is whether A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), which creates a presumption of parentage, applies to the non-biological spouse in a same-sex marriage. The family court determined that the statute applies gender-neutrally and that the biological mother is equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of parentage. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties and the factual record, the Court finds that the family court’s ruling was correct and must be affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Presumption of Parentage Under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) Applies Gender-Neutrally**

The prosecution argues that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) should be applied according to its strict gendered text, relying on *State v. Superior Court (Henderson)*, 132 Ariz. 169, 644 P.2d 860 (1982), and *State v. Valenzuela*, 154 Ariz. 236, 741 P.2d 1202 (App. 1987), to contend that the legislature intended the presumption to apply only to men. However, this argument fails to account for the intervening legal landscape regarding marriage and the specific precedent guiding this case.

The facts indicate that the family court granted Oakley’s motion for reconsideration specifically because "another panel of this Court issued its decision in *McLaughlin*, holding that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally." *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016). As a panel of this Court, we are bound by the precedent established by our fellow judges unless and until it is overruled by a higher court or en banc review. The prosecution’s request to ignore *McLaughlin* in favor of older cases such as *Valenzuela* is unpersuasive. *Valenzuela* was decided at a time when same-sex marriage was not legally recognized, and its reasoning is necessarily outdated following the United States Supreme Court’s decision in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), which established the fundamental right to marry for same-sex couples.

The defense correctly argues that interpreting the statute gender-neutrally fulfills the legislature’s intent to ensure that children born into a marriage have two legal parents. The statute’s core mechanism—the marital relationship and the birth of a child during that marriage—applies with equal force to Oakley and Turner. The prosecution’s reliance on *State v. Maldonado*, 223 Ariz. 309, 223 P.3d 661 (2010), regarding "judicial legislation" is misplaced here; applying a statute to a class of individuals (same-sex spouses) that the legislature could not have explicitly contemplated when the statute was originally drafted, but who now fall squarely within the statutory definition of "spouse," is a matter of statutory interpretation, not legislation.

Therefore, the family court did not err in concluding that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1).

**B. The Doctrine of Equitable Estoppel Bars Turner from Rebutting the Presumption**

Even if the presumption were rebuttable, the record supports the family court’s finding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental rights. The prosecution contends that equitable factors cannot override statutory text, but where the statute applies, equitable estoppel serves to prevent a party from unfairly manipulating the parentage determination to the detriment of the child and the other parent.

The facts demonstrate that Turner consistently represented Oakley as C.T.’s co-parent. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as the child’s sole custodian in the event of Turner’s death. During the birth of C.T., Turner followed the hospital nurse’s instruction to list Oakley on the birth certificate in the section designated "father." Furthermore, the evidence included social media posts in which Turner referred to Oakley as C.T.’s mother.

These representations align with the elements of equitable estoppel recognized in *McLaughlin*, where the court held that the biological mother was estopped from disputing her wife’s legal parentage due to similar representations and the wife’s status as a de facto parent. *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 565-67 ¶¶ 16-23, 382 P.3d at 123-24. Oakley relied on these representations by investing emotional and physical resources in the conception process and the child's upbringing, including being present for the insemination procedures and cutting the umbilical cord at birth.

The prosecution’s argument that this creates "legal chaos" ignores the specific facts of this case and the established principle that the stability of the parent-child relationship is paramount. Allowing Turner to revoke Oakley’s parental status after holding her out as a parent for the child’s entire life would prejudice the child’s welfare.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The family court correctly applied the presumption of parentage under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) in a gender-neutral manner as required by *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016). Furthermore, the court’s finding that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting this presumption is supported by substantial evidence in the record, including the birth certificate, the will, and social media representations.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the family court’s ruling granting Oakley’s motion for reconsideration and recognizing her as a presumed parent is affirmed.

Arizona’s presumption statute defines parentage through gendered terms—terms the Arizona Legislature has used consistently for decades. *Obergefell* requires equal treatment *under the law*, not the rewriting of the law itself. If the Legislature intended for the presumption to apply to women, it would have amended the statute. As this Court held in *Valenzuela*, the statute’s "clear language limits the presumption to men," and "courts lack authority to expand statutory definitions based on policy preferences." *Valenzuela*, 154 Ariz. at 240, 741 P.2d at 1206. To hold otherwise would permit judges to act as legislators—a violation of separation of powers.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's ruling recognizing Oakley as a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1). ## II. MCLaughlin v. Jones Constitutes Binding Precedent

The prosecution argues that McLaughlin v. Jones was erroneously decided and requests that this Court disregard it in favor of older cases. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, under Arizona law, one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by the decision of another panel unless overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or the en banc Court of Appeals. State v. Brown, 125 Ariz. 160, 162, 608 P.2d 299, 301 (1980). The prosecution's argument that McLaughlin directly conflicts with State v. Superior Court (Henderson), 132 Ariz. 169, 644 P.2d 860 (1982), and State v. Superior Court (Valenzuela), 154 Ariz. 236, 741 P.2d 1202 (App. 1987), misunderstands the nature of these precedents. Henderson and Valenzuela were decided in contexts that predate the constitutional recognition of same-sex marriage. As the McLaughlin court itself recognized, the legal landscape fundamentally shifted with Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), which established that same-sex couples have the fundamental right to marry on equal terms with opposite-sex couples. McLaughlin, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on the "exception" to panel binding is misplaced. The exception cited applies when prior precedent directly conflicts with higher court authority—but here, McLaughlin itself accounted for Obergefell as higher authority that modified the analytical framework. The prosecution cannot distinguish McLaughlin by invoking precedents that did not address same-sex marriage because those cases were rendered obsolete on this specific issue by subsequent constitutional holdings.

Third, the prosecution's characterization of McLaughlin as "judicial legislation" misapprehends the nature of statutory interpretation. When the United States Supreme Court establishes constitutional principles that affect the meaning of statutes, courts are required to interpret those statutes consistently with the Constitution. This is not legislation—it is the fundamental duty of judicial review. The defense further notes that the prosecution's argument that "courts must enforce clear statutory language as written" (citing Henderson) ignores that the "clear language" must be read in light of constitutional constraints. A statute that discriminates against a protected class is not "clear" in the sense of being immune from constitutional interpretation.

The prosecution's request to follow Valenzuela rather than McLaughlin is contrary to the doctrine of stare decisis and the procedural rules governing this Court. McLaughlin directly addressed the precise issue before this Court—whether A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies to same-sex spouses—and constitutes binding authority that this panel must follow.

## III. The Presumption of Parentage Applies Gender-Neutrally Under Obergefell

The prosecution's argument that the plain language of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) limits the presumption to men fails to account for the constitutional framework that governs the interpretation of this statute.

The United States Supreme Court in Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), established the fundamental right to marry for same-sex couples and held that "there is no difference between same-sex and opposite-sex couples with respect to marriage." Id. at 672. This holding requires that statutes governing the rights and responsibilities of spouses be applied equally regardless of the sex of the spouses. As the McLaughlin court correctly held, the presumption of parentage under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) is triggered by the existence of a valid marriage and the birth of a child during that marriage. McLaughlin, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. Because same-sex marriages are now legally identical to opposite-sex marriages, applying the statute only to opposite-sex couples would result in unconstitutional discrimination.

The prosecution argues that Obergefell "does not require States to change the definition of marriage beyond the right to marry." However, this reading is overly narrow. The Obergefell Court specifically held that the Constitution protects "the dignity of marriage" for same-sex couples and that they must be treated "in respect of the marriage relation." Obergefell, 576 U.S. at 670. The rights associated with marriage—including the presumption of parentage for children born during the marriage—must be extended to same-sex spouses on equal terms. To hold otherwise would create a system where same-sex married couples have diminished parental rights compared to opposite-sex married couples, directly contradicting the equality principles of Obergefell.

The prosecution's reliance on Valenzuela is unpersuasive because Valenzuela was decided prior to the constitutionalization of same-sex marriage. As the McLaughlin court recognized, Valenzuela's strict interpretation of gendered statutory language cannot be applied in a manner that undermines the equal status of same-sex spouses recognized in Obergefell. McLaughlin, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The defense submits that this Court should follow McLaughlin's reasoned analysis and reject the prosecution's attempt to revive pre-Obergefell discrimination.

## IV. Equitable Estoppel Bars Turner from Rebutting the Presumption

The prosecution's closing argument devotes only brief attention to the family court's alternative holding that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of parentage. This omission is telling, because the estoppel analysis provides an independent and adequate basis for affirmance that does not require resolution of the statutory interpretation question.

Equitable estoppel applies when: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends that the other party rely on the conduct; (3) the other party is ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the other party relies on the representation to his or her detriment. Southwest Airline Co. v. Arizona Equipment Rental Co., Inc., 170 Ariz. 460, 464, 825 P.2d 938, 942 (App. 1991). Here, by contrast, the statute does recognize the relationship—the question is whether the presumption can be rebutted. Estoppel operates not to create parentage where none exists, but to prevent a party from renouncing representations upon which another party relied.

The defense further notes that the prosecution's argument that "a birth certificate listing a woman as 'father' is a legal contradiction" ignores the practical reality of how same-sex couples navigate bureaucratic processes. Allowing Turner to revoke Oakley's parental status after actively encouraging her to assume the role of a parent would prejudice the child's welfare and stability. The McLaughlin court recognized this consideration, noting that "the child's best interests are served by preserving the parental relationship." McLaughlin, 240 Ariz. at 567 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 124.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for special action review of the family court’s order recognizing Oakley as a presumed parent of C.T. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Court Is Bound by the Precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones***

The prosecution urges this Court to disregard the holding of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), arguing that it was erroneously decided and conflicts with earlier decisions such as *State v. Superior Court (Henderson)*, 132 Ariz. 169, 644 P.2d 860 (1982), and *State v. Superior Court (Valenzuela)*, 154 Ariz. 236, 741 P.2d 1202 (App. 1987). Specifically, the prosecution contends that *McLaughlin* constitutes "judicial legislation" by rewriting the gendered terms of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1).

This argument is procedurally barred. Under Arizona law, one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by the decision of another panel of the same court. *State v. Brown*, 125 Ariz. 160, 162, 608 P.2d 299, 301 (1980). The *McLaughlin* decision directly addressed the constitutionality and application of the presumption of parentage statute in the context of same-sex marriage. Unless overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or the en banc Court of Appeals, this panel lacks the authority to overturn that precedent. The prosecution’s reliance on *Henderson* and *Valenzuela* is misplaced because *McLaughlin* specifically distinguished those cases, noting that they were decided prior to the constitutional mandate established by *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015). Therefore, *McLaughlin* remains the controlling authority in this matter.

**B. The Presumption of Parentage Must Be Applied Gender-Neutrally**

The prosecution argues that the plain language of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)—which refers to a "man" and a "father"—limits the presumption of parentage to biological males. While the Court acknowledges the textualist arguments raised in *Henderson* and *Valenzuela*, the legal landscape regarding marriage has fundamentally changed.

As held in *McLaughlin*, the presumption of parentage is triggered by the existence of a valid marriage and the birth of a child during that marriage. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. To interpret A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to apply only to opposite-sex couples would effectively deny same-sex spouses a significant incident of marriage—the presumption of parentage for children born into the marriage. Such a result would be inconsistent with the constitutional mandate of equality. The defense correctly argues that interpreting the statute gender-neutrally is not "judicial legislation," but rather a necessary constitutional interpretation.

**C. Equitable Estoppel Bars Turner from Rebutting the Presumption**

The family court alternatively held that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of parentage. Equitable estoppel prevents a party from taking a position contrary to one they have previously taken if another party has relied on that position to their detriment. *Southwest Airline Co. v. The prosecution’s arguments, while grounded in principles of statutory construction, cannot override the constitutional requirements of *Obergefell* or the binding authority of *McLaughlin*.
