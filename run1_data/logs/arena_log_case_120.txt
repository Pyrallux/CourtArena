The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting Casey's recorded statement, and his claim that this violated his right to a fair trial under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 and Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 is without merit. Indiana appellate courts review evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion, and reversal is warranted only where the ruling is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Smith v. State, 744 N.E.2d 513, 515 (Ind. 2001). Indiana Evidence Rule 403 permits exclusion only if the probative value of evidence is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury. Here, Casey's statement was highly probative as direct evidence of his admission of ownership of the 16-gauge shotgun, which was central to the State's case for unlawful possession by a serious violent felon. As this Court held in Williams v. State, 712 N.E.2d 492, 496 (Ind. 1999), "evidence is not cumulative if it serves to corroborate other evidence or provide additional context." The statement was not cumulative because it constituted the only direct evidence of Casey's own admission of possession; his later testimony denying ownership and attributing the statement to fear and drug use merely created a credibility issue for the jury, which does not render the evidence inadmissible under Rule 403. The danger of unfair prejudice was minimal, as the statement was directly relevant to the charged offense and contained no inflammatory or extraneous details.

Regarding Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6, that statute governs evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove character or conduct in conformity therewith. However, the evidence here—Casey's statement about the shotgun in question—directly pertained to the charged offense itself, not prior bad acts. As confirmed in Wilson v. State, 753 N.E.2d 1054, 1058 (Ind. 2001), "evidence of the charged crime is not subject to the exclusion for other acts under Rule 404(b) or its statutory counterpart." Thus, Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 is inapplicable, as the statement was not "other acts" evidence but rather proof of the conduct underlying the indictment. The State properly moved to admit the statement as part of its case-in-chief, and the trial court correctly overruled the defense's objection after weighing the relevant factors. Under these circumstances, the trial court acted within its discretion, and no violation of Casey's right to a fair trial occurred.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE INDIANA COURT OF APPEALS

---

**DEFENDANT-APPELLANT'S OPENING BRIEF**

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Casey respectfully submits this opening brief in defense of his appeal from the Marion Superior Court, where he was convicted of Unlawful Possession of a Firearm by a Serious Violent Felon, a Level 4 felony, and found to be an Habitual Offender, resulting in an aggregate sentence of twenty-two years. The defense respectfully asserts that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting Casey's recorded statement over objection, and that this error violated Casey's rights under the Indiana Constitution and the United States Constitution. The State has failed to meet its burden of demonstrating that the evidentiary ruling was proper, and this Court should reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

On May 17, 2017, Indianapolis Metropolitan Police Officer Scott Emminger was dispatched to 44 North Euclid Avenue regarding a person who had been shot. Upon arrival, Officer Emminger observed two women leaving the neighboring porch at 48 North Euclid who appeared hurried and attempted to enter a blue Chevy Cavalier. Officer Emminger detained these women while he investigated the shooting at 44 North Euclid.

Detective Jason Leitze arrived at the scene and spoke with Officer Emminger and Tara Hamilton, one of the women who was subsequently arrested. Later that same day, Detective Leitze received an anonymous phone call suggesting potential evidence at 48 North Euclid, leading him to obtain a search warrant for that residence.

During the execution of the search warrant, Detective Leitze discovered a 16-gauge shotgun manufactured by Stevens hidden under a bed, along with ammunition and spent cartridges. Casey was present at the residence and was taken into custody. Detective Leitze transported Casey to the City County Building for an interview, where Casey made a recorded statement after being informed of his Miranda rights.

At trial, Casey testified that he lived at 8523 North Euclid Court, that Tara Hamilton was his girlfriend, and that the shotgun belonged to Hamilton. Casey denied firing the shotgun and stated that Hamilton had been selling it to another person. When asked why he had stated in the video that the shotgun was his and that he had fired it two days prior, Casey answered: "I was scared and confused, and I didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Casey further testified that he had just smoked "Katie," described as "blunts... spice," which rendered him scared and confused at the time of his statement.

On rebuttal, Detective Leitze testified that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of spice or any other narcotic during the interview.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting Casey's Recorded Statement

The State bears the burden of demonstrating that the trial court's admission of Casey's statement was proper. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that evidence "may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury." The Indiana Supreme Court has consistently held that appellate courts review evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion, reversing only where the ruling is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Taylor v. State, 818 N.E.2d 480, 485 (Ind. 2004).

The defense contends that the trial court erred in admitting Casey's statement because the probative value of the evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Although the State characterizes the statement as "highly probative," this characterization overlooks critical factors that render the statement's admission fundamentally unfair.

First, Casey's statement, in which he admitted ownership of the shotgun, was the centerpiece of the State's case. The admission of a defendant's own statement carries tremendous weight with a jury and creates a significant risk of unfair prejudice. As the Indiana Supreme Court recognized in Carter v. State, 846 N.E.2d 235, 242 (Ind. 2006), confessions "possess an enormous probative force" that can lead juries to convict based on the confession alone, regardless of other evidence. This Court must carefully scrutinize the admission of such statements to ensure the defendant receives a fair trial.

Second, the circumstances surrounding Casey's statement raise serious concerns about its reliability. Casey testified that at the time he made the statement, he had just smoked "spice," a synthetic cannabinoid that causes confusion and impaired judgment. He stated he was "scared and confused" and made the statement because he did not want his girlfriend to go to jail. These circumstances demonstrate that the statement was not the product of deliberate, reasoned decision-making but rather the result of impaired judgment and emotional distress.

The State's reliance on Williams v. State, 712 N.E.2d 492 (Ind. 1999), is misplaced. In Williams, the Court found that evidence was not cumulative because it served to corroborate other evidence. Here, however, the statement was not merely corroborative—it was the primary evidence of possession, and its admission created a substantial risk that the jury would credit the statement simply because it constituted an admission by the defendant, without adequately considering the circumstances under which it was made.

### B. The Trial Court Erred by Failing to Conduct a Voluntariness Hearing

The defense further asserts that the trial court erred by failing to conduct a voluntariness hearing regarding Casey's statement before admitting it into evidence. Due process requires that confessions be voluntary before they may be admitted at trial. Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157, 165 (1986); Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 602 (1975). Indiana law similarly requires that courts conduct an independent inquiry into the voluntariness of a defendant's statement when there is evidence suggesting the statement may have been coerced. Jackson v. State, 717 N.E.2d 609, 615 (Ind. 1999).

Here, there was ample evidence raising questions about the voluntariness of Casey's statement. Casey testified that he had recently consumed "spice," which impaired his judgment and caused confusion. He stated he was "scared" when he made the statement. These claims, if believed by the jury, would demonstrate that Casey's statement was not voluntarily given. Yet the trial court admitted the statement without conducting any inquiry into its voluntariness.

The Indiana Supreme Court has held that when a defendant presents evidence of coercion or impairment, the trial court must conduct a voluntariness hearing outside the presence of the jury. Townsend v. State, 793 N.E.2d 1092, 1098 (Ind. 2003). The failure to conduct such a hearing constitutes reversible error. Id. at 1099.

The trial court's failure to hold a voluntariness hearing was especially problematic given the conflicting evidence on this issue. While Detective Leitze testified on rebuttal that Casey "did not seem under the influence," this testimony does not negate the need for a hearing. The question of voluntariness is for the court, not the jury, to determine. Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 444 (1966). By admitting the statement without a hearing, the trial court effectively delegated this critical determination to the jury, which is impermissible.

### C. The Admission of the Statement Violated Casey's Right to Remain Silent

The defense also asserts that the admission of Casey's statement violated his right to remain silent under the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article 1, Section 14 of the Indiana Constitution. Although Casey was advised of his Miranda rights, the subsequent use of his statement against him raises concerns about whether his waiver of those rights was knowing and intelligent.

The Indiana Supreme Court has recognized that the right to remain silent is a fundamental constitutional protection. When a defendant makes a statement after invoking the right to remain silent, the statement is inadmissible. Neal v. State, 834 N.E.2d 484, 489 (Ind. 2005). Here, the record does not reflect whether Casey was specifically asked if he wished to waive his rights or whether he understood he could refuse to answer questions. The failure to ensure a knowing and intelligent waiver renders the statement inadmissible.

### D. The Cumulative Effect of the Errors Requires Reversal

Even if this Court finds that any single error was harmless, the cumulative effect of the errors requires reversal. The Indiana Supreme Court has held that under the cumulative error doctrine, "the accumulated effect of multiple errors may violate a defendant's right to a fair trial even when no single error would be sufficient." Fox v. State, 717 N.E.2d 957, 971 (Ind. 1999).

Here, the trial court committed multiple errors: (1) admitting the statement without conducting a voluntariness hearing; (2) failing to properly weigh the probative value against the danger of unfair prejudice; and (3) allowing the statement to be played for the jury without adequate safeguards. These errors, individually and cumulatively, denied Casey a fair trial.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant Casey respectfully requests that this Court reverse his conviction and remand this matter for a new trial. The trial court's admission of Casey's recorded statement constituted an abuse of discretion that violated Casey's rights under the Indiana and United States Constitutions. The statement was not properly subjected to a voluntariness inquiry, its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, and its admission deprived Casey of a fair trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense regarding the admissibility of Appellant Casey’s recorded statement. The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in admitting this statement, thereby violating Casey’s rights under Indiana Evidence Rule 403, Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6, and the Indiana and United States Constitutions.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard for reviewing the admission of evidence is well established. Appellate courts review evidentiary rulings for an abuse of discretion, reversing only if the ruling is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Smith v. State, 744 N.E.2d 513, 515 (Ind. 2001); Taylor v. State, 818 N.E.2d 480, 485 (Ind. 2004). The trial court is afforded broad discretion in balancing the probative value of evidence against its potential for unfair prejudice.

**II. Admissibility Under Indiana Evidence Rule 403**

The Defense argues that the trial court erred in admitting Casey’s statement because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The Defense relies on Carter v. State, 846 N.E.2d 235, 242 (Ind. 2006), to assert that confessions possess an "enormous probative force" that can lead juries to convict regardless of other evidence. While the Court acknowledges the weight given to confessions, the Prosecution correctly identifies that the statement was direct evidence of the charged offense: unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon.

Under Indiana Evidence Rule 403, evidence may be excluded only if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The statement in question contained Casey’s admission of ownership of the 16-gauge shotgun found at 48 North Euclid. This admission was central to the State’s burden of proving possession. As held in Williams v. State, 712 N.E.2d 492, 496 (Ind. 1999), evidence is not cumulative if it serves to corroborate other evidence or provide additional context. Here, the physical discovery of the gun was corroborated by Casey’s direct admission, providing the necessary link to the element of possession.

The Defense contends that the statement was unreliable due to Casey’s alleged use of "spice" and his emotional state. However, these factors go to the weight and credibility of the evidence, not its admissibility. The jury was made aware of Casey’s testimony that he was "scared and confused" and under the influence of drugs at the time of the statement. Furthermore, Detective Leitze testified on rebuttal that Casey did not appear to be under the influence. This conflict in evidence presented a classic credibility question for the jury to resolve. See Smith v. State, 744 N.E.2d at 515. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the probative value of the admission was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

**III. Application of Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6**

The Defense argues that the admission of the statement violated Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6, which governs the admissibility of evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts. The Prosecution correctly counters that this statute is inapplicable because the statement pertained to the charged crime itself, not prior bad acts.

As established in Wilson v. State, 753 N.E.2d 1054, 1058 (Ind. 2001), "evidence of the charged crime is not subject to the exclusion for other acts under Rule 404(b) or its statutory counterpart." Casey’s statement regarding the shotgun found at 48 North Euclid was inextricably linked to the specific conduct for which he was charged. It was not evidence of other crimes or character traits used to prove conformity therewith. Therefore, the trial court correctly determined that Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 did not bar the admission of the statement.

**IV. Voluntariness and Procedural Due Process**

The Defense asserts that the trial court erred by failing to conduct a voluntariness hearing pursuant to Townsend v. State, 793 N.E.2d 1092, 1098 (Ind. 2003). The Defense argues that Casey’s alleged impairment by "spice" and his fear necessitated a hearing outside the presence of the jury.

While due process requires that confessions be voluntary, see Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157, 165 (1986), the record indicates that Casey was informed of his Miranda rights prior to making the statement. The Defense’s claim of impairment relies on Casey’s subjective testimony that he had smoked "Katie" (spice). However, Detective Leitze testified on rebuttal that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of any narcotic. The trial court was presented with conflicting evidence regarding Casey’s condition. The decision to admit the statement, allowing the jury to assess the credibility of the claims regarding impairment, was within the court's discretion. The mere allegation of drug use does not automatically render a statement involuntary or require a separate hearing if the court determines the evidence of coercion is insufficient. See Jackson v. State, 717 N.E.2d 609, 615 (Ind. 1999). The trial court’s admission of the statement, followed by the Defense's ability to cross-examine and present contrary evidence, satisfied due process requirements.

**V. Conclusion**

Based on the facts and arguments presented, the Prosecution has demonstrated that the trial court acted within its discretion. The statement was highly probative of the charged offense and was not excluded by the "other acts" statute. The alleged prejudice and reliability issues raised by the Defense were matters of weight and credibility properly reserved for the jury. Consequently, the trial court’s admission of Casey’s statement does not constitute reversible error.

**THEREFORE, the Court preliminarily finds that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the evidence, and the Defense’s arguments for reversal are without merit.**

#### I. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 Was Properly Applied  
The Defense erroneously contends the trial court abused its discretion under Rule 403 by admitting Casey’s statement. Casey’s statement directly established his possession of the 16-gauge shotgun—the core element of the charged offense. As this Court held in Williams v. Casey’s statement pertained *exclusively* to the shotgun found at the scene of the charged offense—his admission of ownership was inextricably linked to the specific conduct for which he was prosecuted. **UPDATED RULING**

The Court has carefully reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The primary issue for determination is whether the trial court abused its discretion in admitting Casey's recorded statement in its entirety, specifically regarding the admission of "other acts" evidence and the failure to conduct a voluntariness hearing.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard of review remains unchanged. Appellate courts review evidentiary rulings for an abuse of discretion, reversing only if the ruling is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Smith v. State, 744 N.E.2d 513, 515 (Ind. 2001); Taylor v. State, 818 N.E.2d 480, 485 (Ind. 2004).

**II. Admission of "Other Acts" Evidence Under Rule 403 and Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6**

The Court finds merit in the Defense's argument regarding the specific content of Casey's statement. While the Prosecution correctly asserts that an admission of ownership is relevant to the charge of unlawful possession, the recorded statement contained more than just an admission of ownership. The record reflects that Casey stated he "fired it 2 days before" the incident. Transcript Volume II at 56.

The charge in this case was unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. The act of firing the shotgun two days prior is a separate act, distinct from the element of possession. Under Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and its statutory counterpart, Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6, evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show action in conformity therewith. Wilson v. State, 753 N.E.2d 1054, 1058 (Ind. 2001).

The Prosecution's rebuttal failed to address the admissibility of this specific portion of the statement. The Prosecution focused exclusively on the probative value of the ownership admission, ignoring the admission regarding the discharge of the weapon. State, 846 N.E.2d 235, 242 (Ind. 2006) (acknowledging the "enormous probative force" of confessions and the risk of unfair prejudice).

The trial court abused its discretion in admitting the unredacted statement. The portion of the statement regarding the discharge of the weapon should have been redacted or excluded, as its probative value to the charge of possession was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Ind. Evid. Rule 403. The admission of this "other acts" evidence was not harmless.

**III. Failure to Conduct a Voluntariness Hearing**

The Court further finds that the trial court erred in failing to conduct a voluntariness hearing outside the presence of the jury. Under Indiana law, a trial court must hold a voluntariness hearing when there is a substantial question of fact regarding the voluntariness of the statement. Townsend v. State, 793 N.E.2d 1092, 1098 (Ind. 2003).

Here, there was a direct conflict between Detective Leitze's observation that Casey did not appear impaired and Casey's testimony that he had smoked spice and was confused. The trial court erred by allowing the jury to resolve this factual dispute regarding the *voluntariness* of the waiver without first making a definitive ruling on the admissibility of the statement based on the defendant's capacity to waive his rights. Given the specific allegations of impairment and the conflicting evidence, the trial court should have held a hearing to determine if Casey was capable of making a knowing and intelligent waiver before the statement was admitted.

## II. THE ADMISSION OF IMPROPER "OTHER ACTS" EVIDENCE

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Evidence

The Prosecution contends that Casey's statement regarding firing the shotgun two days before the incident was "inextricably intertwined" with the charged offense of unlawful possession. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the charged crime and the evidence at issue.

The charge was unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. Under Indiana law, the State was required to prove that Casey possessed a firearm while having been convicted of a serious violent felony. The act of firing a shotgun is not an element of possession. One can possess a firearm without ever firing it, and firing a firearm is a separate criminal act that could form the basis of an entirely different charge.

### B. The Evidence Constitutes Prohibited "Other Acts" Evidence

As this Court correctly found in its ruling, Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and Indiana Code Section 34-1-21-6 prohibit the admission of evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove character and action in conformity therewith. In Wilson v. State, 753 N.E.2d 1054, 1058 (Ind. 2001), this Court held that such evidence is inadmissible unless it serves some purpose other than showing propensity to commit the charged crime.

The Prosecution's argument that the firing admission was "inextricably intertwined" with the charged offense fails because the two acts are legally and factually distinct. The Prosecution has not identified, nor can it identify, any legal principle requiring that evidence of firing a weapon must be admitted to establish possession. The narrative of how the gun came to be in Casey's possession could have been established through his admission of ownership without the inflammatory addition that he fired the weapon.

### C. The Danger of Unfair Prejudice Outweighed Any Probative Value

Even if the firing admission had some minimal probative value, Indiana Evidence Rule 403 required its exclusion. As this Court recognized in Carter v. State, 846 N.E.2d 235, 242 (Ind. 2006), confessions carry "enormous probative force," but that force cuts both ways—when a confession contains inflammatory evidence, the danger of unfair prejudice is correspondingly high.

Evidence that Casey fired the shotgun created a significant risk that the jury would convict him not for the charged offense of possession, but for the inflammatory act of firing a weapon. This is precisely the "action in conformity" that Rule 404(b) prohibits. The Prosecution's argument that any prejudice was "minimal" ignores the reality that evidence of discharging a firearm is highly inflammatory, particularly when the defendant is not charged with that act.

The Court correctly found that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting this evidence.

## III. THE FAILURE TO CONDUCT A VOLUNTARINESS HEARING

### A. The Prosecution Ignores the Substantial Question of Fact

The Prosecution argues that no voluntariness hearing was required because Detective Leitze testified that Casey did not appear impaired. This argument ignores the fundamental principle that when there is a substantial question of fact regarding voluntariness, the trial court must conduct a hearing outside the presence of the jury to resolve that question before admitting the statement.

In Townsend v. State, 793 N.E.2d 1092, 1098 (Ind. 2003), this Court held that a trial court must hold a voluntariness hearing when there is a substantial question of fact regarding the voluntariness of the statement. The existence of conflicting evidence—such as Detective Leitze's observation that Casey did not appear impaired versus Casey's testimony that he had smoked "spice" and was "scared and confused"—is precisely the circumstance that triggers the requirement for a hearing.

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies Jackson v. State

The Prosecution relies heavily on Jackson v. State, 717 N.E.2d 609, 615 (Ind. 1999), for the proposition that "the mere allegation of drug use does not automatically render a statement involuntary." This reliance is misplaced. Jackson stands for the proposition that drug use alone does not automatically render a statement involuntary—but it does not stand for the proposition that a trial court may skip the voluntariness hearing when there is conflicting evidence on the issue.

The Jackson court expressly noted that "the totality of circumstances must be considered." Here, the totality of circumstances included: (1) Casey's testimony that he had smoked spice and was confused; (2) his statement that he was "scared" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail"; and (3) the-detective's contradictory observation. These conflicting accounts created a substantial question of fact that could only be resolved through a proper hearing.

### C. The Trial Court Erred in Allowing the Jury to Resolve This Question

The Prosecution argues that the trial court properly "left the credibility determination to the jury." This argument inverts the proper procedure. The question of whether a defendant was capable of making a knowing and intelligent waiver of Miranda rights is a legal question for the court, not a factual question for the jury. Before the statement could be admitted, the trial court was required to determine that Casey validly waived his rights. By allowing the jury to hear the statement without first making this determination, the trial court committed reversible error.

As this Court correctly found, the trial court should have held a hearing to determine if Casey was capable of making a knowing and intelligent waiver before the statement was admitted.

Here, the combination of admitting highly prejudicial "other acts" evidence and failing to ensure the voluntariness of the statement through a proper hearing undermined the integrity of the proceedings. The Court correctly found that these errors, considered together, deprived Casey of a fair trial.

The Prosecution's arguments rest on a fundamental misreading of the record and misapplication of Indiana law. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting Casey's unredacted statement, which contained improper "other acts" evidence regarding the firing of the shotgun. The trial court further erred in failing to conduct a voluntariness hearing when substantial questions existed regarding Casey's capacity to waive his Miranda rights.

The Court's revised ruling correctly identified these errors and properly applied the cumulative error doctrine. The Defense respectfully requests that the Court affirm its ruling reversing the trial court's judgment and remanding for a new trial.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. Upon careful consideration of the additional arguments and the applicable law, the Court affirms its revised ruling reversing the trial court's judgment. The Prosecution's rebuttals do not overcome the trial court's errors regarding the admission of "other acts" evidence and the failure to conduct a voluntariness hearing.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard of review for evidentiary rulings remains abuse of discretion. A trial court's ruling is reversible only if it is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Smith v. State, 744 N.E.2d 513, 515 (Ind. 2001).

**II. Admission of "Other Acts" Evidence**

The Prosecution argues that Casey's statement regarding firing the shotgun was "inextricably intertwined" with the charged offense of possession and was necessary to complete the narrative of the crime. While the Prosecution correctly cites Wilson v. State, 753 N.E.2d 1054, 1058 (Ind. 2001), for the proposition that evidence may be admissible if it is necessary to tell the story, this principle does not save the admission in this context.

The charge was unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. The act of firing the shotgun two days prior is factually and legally distinct from the element of possession on the date of the search. As the Defense argues, possession can be established without evidence of discharge. The admission that Casey fired the weapon was not necessary to prove he possessed it; rather, it served to portray Casey as a dangerous individual who actively uses firearms, which is precisely the type of propensity evidence prohibited by Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b).

The Prosecution relies on the "enormous probative force" of confessions, citing Carter v. State, 846 N.E.2d 235, 242 (Ind. 2006). However, the probative value of the statement was limited to the admission of ownership. The specific detail regarding the discharge of the weapon was highly inflammatory and carried a significant danger of unfair prejudice, inviting the jury to convict Casey for being a person who fires guns, rather than for the specific offense of possession. The trial court abused its discretion in failing to redact this portion of the statement under Indiana Evidence Rule 403.

**III. Failure to Conduct a Voluntariness Hearing**

The Prosecution contends that no voluntariness hearing was required because "the mere allegation of drug use does not automatically render a statement involuntary," citing Jackson v. State, 717 N.E.2d 609, 615 (Ind. 1999). While this is a correct statement of law, it does not address the procedural error committed by the trial court.

The issue is not whether the drug use *automatically* rendered the statement involuntary, but whether the conflict in the evidence created a "substantial question of fact" requiring a hearing. Under Indiana law, when there is a substantial dispute regarding the voluntariness of a statement, the trial court must hold a hearing outside the presence of the jury to resolve the issue before admitting the statement. Townsend v. State, 793 N.E.2d 1092, 1098 (Ind. 2003).

Here, there was a direct conflict between Detective Leitze's testimony that Casey appeared normal and Casey's testimony that he was under the influence of "spice," scared, and confused. This conflict constituted a substantial question of fact regarding Casey's capacity to knowingly and intelligently waive his Miranda rights. The trial court erred by allowing the jury to resolve this factual dispute regarding the voluntariness of the waiver. The determination of whether a waiver was valid is a legal question for the court, not a factual question for the jury. The Prosecution's reliance on the detective's observation does not negate the trial court's duty to conduct the hearing mandated by Townsend.

**IV. Cumulative Error**

The Court finds that the cumulative effect of these errors deprived Casey of a fair trial. The trial court committed two distinct errors: (1) the admission of prejudicial "other acts" evidence in violation of Rule 403 and Rule 404(b); and (2) the failure to conduct a mandatory voluntariness hearing under Townsend. As held in Fox v. State, 717 N.E.2d 957, 971 (Ind. 1999), multiple errors which may not be reversible individually can constitute reversible error when considered together. The combination of these errors undermined the integrity of the judicial process.

**V. Conclusion**

The Prosecution's arguments fail to demonstrate that the trial court's rulings were within its discretion. The admission of the "other acts" evidence was erroneous, and the failure to hold a voluntariness hearing in the face of conflicting evidence was a procedural defect that requires reversal.
