## IN THE WASHINGTON STATE COURT OF APPEALS

### STATE OF WASHINGTON, Respondent v. TOMMY GEORGE and JOHN GEORGE, Appellants

## INTRODUCTION

Appellants Tommy George and John George respectfully submit that this Court should reverse the Court of Appeals' decision and dismiss the attempted first-degree theft conviction, or alternatively, reduce the conviction to a lesser included offense. The State has failed to meet its burden of proving the elements of attempted first-degree theft beyond a reasonable doubt. Specifically, the State presented no evidence establishing the actual market value of the truck at the time of the sale, which is essential for establishing the degree of theft under RCW 9A.56.030. The trial court erred in denying Appellants' motion to dismiss, and the Court of Appeals compounded this error by applying an incorrect legal standard that improperly shifts the burden of proof to Defendants.

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts established at trial are as follows:

In June 2003, Tommy George approached Jerome Potter regarding the purchase of Potter's 1974 Chevrolet half-ton pickup truck. Clerk's Papers at 7. At the time of this inquiry, the truck had a malfunctioning rear wheel differential that had rendered the vehicle inoperable for two years. Id. The truck had been sitting outside, uncovered, during this period. Id. Potter disclosed these mechanical problems to Tommy George and further informed him that he had replaced the original "350" engine with a "400" engine. Id. The Georges presented no evidence in their defense. Id. After the State rested, the Georges moved for dismissal, arguing that a prima facie case had not been established for first-degree theft because the State offered no evidence showing that the truck as sold was worth less than the sale price. ## ARGUMENT

### I. THE STATE FAILED TO ESTABLISH THE ELEMENTS OF ATTEMPTED FIRST-DEGREE THEFT

Under RCW 9A.56.030, first-degree theft requires that the property stolen have a value exceeding $1,500. The statute defines "value" as "the market value of the property at the time and place of the crime or, if such cannot be satisfactorily ascertained, the cost of replacement." RCW 9A.56.010(19).

The State's burden requires proving not only that the Defendants obtained property by deception but also that the property's actual market value exceeded $1,500 at the time of the crime. The record reveals that the State failed to carry this burden.

The prosecution presented no evidence whatsoever regarding the actual market value of the 1974 Chevrolet truck at the time of the sale. Detective Stokke testified that due to the vehicle's age, he could not find standardized pricing information. Clerk's Papers at 7. The State introduced no expert testimony, no comparable sales data, and no other evidence from which a jury could determine the truck's actual market value. The State relied entirely on the representation that the truck had 70,000 miles when it actually had 185,000 miles and was inoperable for two years prior to the Georges' purchase.

This Court should hold that when the State charges theft by deception, the State must prove the actual market value of the property as it existed at the time of the theft—not merely the misrepresentation. However, the State must still prove that actual market value through competent evidence. In State v. Johnson, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011), this Court stated that "the value of the property is determined by its actual market value at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim." Yet this principle assumes the State has presented evidence of that actual market value. Where, as here, the State presents no evidence of market value, the State has failed to establish the degree of theft.

### II. THE COURT OF APPLIED AN IMPROPER STANDARD THAT SHIFTS THE BURDEN TO DEFENDANTS

The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the conviction based on the argument that the Defendants could have presented evidence of the truck's value. This reasoning improperly shifts the burden of proof from the State to the defense.

It is fundamental that the State bears the burden of proving every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970). The degree of theft is an element that must be proven by the State. The Court of Appeals' suggestion that the Defendants should have presented evidence of the truck's value contradicts this basic principle.

The Defendants were under no obligation to present evidence regarding the truck's value. The burden remained on the State to prove that the truck's actual market value exceeded $1,500. The State failed to meet this burden.

### III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN DENYING THE MOTION TO DISMISS

The trial court should have granted the Defendants' motion to dismiss at the close of the State's case. A motion to dismiss should be granted when the State has failed to present prima facie evidence of all elements of the charged crime. State v. Gresham, 113 Wash. 2d 41, 46 (1989).

Here, the State failed to present any evidence regarding the actual market value of the truck. Without such evidence, the State could not establish the degree of theft. The trial court therefore erred in denying the motion.

### IV. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT FIRST-DEGREE THEFT

Even viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft. The State proved that the Defendants made misrepresentations about the truck's mileage and condition. However, the State presented no evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that the truck's actual market value exceeded $1,500.

The truck was a 1974 Chevrolet half-ton pickup with 185,000 miles (odometer rolled over), a replaced engine, a malfunctioning differential, and a history of sitting outside inoperable for two years. The State introduced no evidence regarding the value of this vehicle. Under these circumstances, no reasonable jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that the truck's actual market value exceeded $1,500.

### V. THE HOLDINGS IN STATE V. JOHNSON AND STATE V. BLEVINS DO NOT SUPPORT THE STATE'S POSITION

The State relies heavily on State v. Johnson, 161 Wash. App. 532 (2011), and State v. Blevins, 141 Wash. App. 323 (2007), for the proposition that actual market value, not the amount paid, determines the degree of theft. However, these cases are distinguishable and do not support the State's position in this case.

In both Johnson and Blevins, the State presented evidence of the property's actual market value. In Johnson, the court noted that the victim paid $5,400 for a vehicle that was actually worth only $1,800. 161 Wash. App. at 538. The court held that the actual market value ($1,800) determined the degree of theft, not the amount paid ($5,400). Id. However, the State in Johnson presented evidence of the actual market value—it did not simply argue that the value must be determined without any evidence of what that value actually was.

Similarly, in Blevins, the State presented evidence that the stolen property had an actual value of $3,300, not the $15,000 represented value. 141 Wash. App. at 330. The court held that the actual value controlled for statutory purposes. Id. Again, the State presented actual value evidence.

In this case, the State presented no evidence of the truck's actual market value. The State cannot rely on Johnson and Blevins for the proposition that actual market value controls when the State has failed to prove what that actual market value is.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellants respectfully request that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals' decision and dismiss the attempted first-degree theft conviction. Alternatively, this Court should reduce the conviction to attempted second-degree theft or attempted third-degree theft, as the evidence was insufficient to establish the degree of theft beyond a reasonable doubt.

The State bore the burden of proving that the truck's actual market value exceeded $1,500. The State failed to present any evidence of the truck's market value. Under these circumstances, the conviction for attempted first-degree theft cannot stand.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. ISSUE PRESENTED**

The central issue on appeal is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to sustain a conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. Specifically, the Court must determine if the State proved the "value" element of the offense—specifically, that the property's actual market value exceeded $1,500—given that the State presented no expert testimony, comparable sales data, or standardized pricing information regarding the 1974 Chevrolet truck.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, the relevant inquiry is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. See State v. Gresham, 113 Wash. 2d 41, 46 (1989). If the State fails to produce evidence establishing a prima facie case for each element of the crime charged, the defendant is entitled to a dismissal of that charge. Id.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Legal Definition of "Value" in Theft by Deception**

Both parties correctly identify the controlling legal standard for determining the value of stolen property. RCW 9A.56.010(19) defines "value" as "the market value of the property at the time and place of the crime or, if such cannot be satisfactorily ascertained, the cost of replacement." This Court has consistently held that "market value" is a constitutionally sufficient standard referring to the price a willing buyer would pay a willing seller in an arm's-length transaction. See State v. Smith, 143 Wash. 2d 477, 485 (2001); State v. Williams, 154 Wash. App. 137, 145 (2010).

Crucially, in cases of theft by deception, the value of the property is determined by its actual market value at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim or the misrepresented value. See State v. Johnson, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011); State v. Blevins, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007). The amount paid by the victim may be evidence of value, but it is not determinative where deception has induced the payment. Johnson, 161 Wash. App. at 538.

**B. Sufficiency of Evidence Regarding the Truck's Value**

While the State correctly articulates the legal standard, it fails to demonstrate that the evidence presented at trial meets this standard. To sustain a conviction for first-degree theft, the State was required to prove that the truck's actual market value exceeded $1,500. RCW 9A.56.030.

The record indicates that the State presented no evidence of the truck's actual market value. Detective Stokke testified that he could not locate standardized pricing information due to the vehicle's age. The State introduced no expert testimony, no comparable sales data, and no other evidence from which a jury could objectively determine the truck's actual market value at the time of the crime.

The State argues that the evidence of the truck's condition—specifically the high mileage (185,000 miles), the replaced engine, and the history of sitting inoperable—supports the jury's verdict. However, evidence of the truck's poor condition, without more, establishes only that the truck was likely worth *less* than the $5,500 asking price. It does not establish that the truck was worth *more* than $1,500. The State cannot rely on the $5,500 figure, as that was the misrepresented value induced by deception, which is explicitly excluded as the measure of value under Johnson and Blevins.

The Defense correctly distinguishes this case from the precedents cited by the State. In both State v. Johnson and State v. Blevins, the State presented affirmative evidence of the property's actual value (e.g., testimony establishing the vehicle was worth $1,800 in Johnson and $3,300 in Blevins). Here, the State presented a vacuum of evidence regarding value.

**C. Burden of Proof**

The State bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970). The degree of theft is an essential element of the offense. The State cannot meet this burden by relying on the absence of evidence from the defense. The Defense correctly argues that the Court of Appeals' suggestion that the Defendants should have presented evidence of the truck's value improperly shifts the burden of proof. The State's failure to provide any evidence of the truck's value exceeding $1,500 is fatal to the first-degree theft charge.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The State established that the Georges engaged in deception regarding the truck's condition, mileage, and history. However, the State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the "value" element necessary for a conviction of attempted first-degree theft. Because the State offered no evidence of the truck's actual market value, and cannot rely on the fraudulent asking price, no rational jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that the truck was worth more than $1,500.

Accordingly, the Defense's motion to dismiss the charge of attempted first-degree theft should have been granted. The conviction for attempted first-degree theft is unsupported by sufficient evidence. The case is remanded for entry of judgment on the lesser included offenses or further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. ISSUE PRESENTED**

The sole issue on appeal is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to sustain a conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. Specifically, the Court must determine if the State proved the "value" element of the offense—that the property's actual market value exceeded $1,500—given the absence of expert testimony, comparable sales data, or standardized pricing information.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, the relevant inquiry is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. See State v. Gresham, 113 Wash. 2d 41, 46 (1989). If the State fails to produce evidence establishing a prima facie case for each element of the crime charged, the defendant is entitled to a dismissal of that charge. Id.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Legal Framework and the Burden of Proof**

The parties agree that the controlling legal standard for determining the value of stolen property is found in RCW 9A.56.010(19), which defines "value" as "the market value of the property at the time and place of the crime or, if such cannot be satisfactorily ascertained, the cost of replacement." This Court has consistently held that "market value" refers to the price a willing buyer would pay a willing seller in an arm's-length transaction. See State v. Smith, 143 Wash. 2d 477, 485 (2001).

The prosecution correctly argues, citing State v. Williams, 154 Wash. App. 137, 145 (2010), that this statutory definition is not unconstitutionally vague. However, the clarity of the statute does not relieve the State of its burden to produce evidence proving that the facts of this case meet the statutory definition.

In cases of theft by deception, the value of the property is determined by its actual market value at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim or the misrepresented value. See State v. Johnson, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011); State v. Blevins, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007). The amount paid by the victim may be evidence of value, but it is not determinative where deception has induced the payment. Johnson, 161 Wash. App. at 538.

The State bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970). The degree of theft is an essential element of the offense. The State cannot meet this burden by relying on the absence of evidence from the defense.

**B. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The prosecution's closing argument correctly reiterates the legal principles established in *Smith*, *Johnson*, and *Blevins*. However, the prosecution's argument fails to address the core deficiency identified by the defense: the State presented no evidence whatsoever to satisfy the definition of value provided in those very cases.

The prosecution relies on *Johnson* and *Blevins* to argue that "value" is determined by actual market value rather than the transactional amount. While this legal proposition is correct, it underscores the State's failure to prove the necessary element. In both *Johnson* and *Blevins*, the State presented affirmative evidence of the property's actual value (e.g., testimony establishing the vehicle's worth). Here, the record reflects a complete vacuum of evidence regarding the truck's actual market value. Detective Stokke testified that he could not locate standardized pricing information due to the vehicle's age. The State introduced no expert testimony, no comparable sales data, and no evidence of replacement cost.

The defense correctly notes that the prosecution cannot rely on the $5,500 asking price to establish value, as that figure was the product of the Georges' deception and is legally irrelevant to the actual market value inquiry under *Johnson* and *Blevins*. Furthermore, evidence of the truck's poor condition—185,000 miles, a replaced engine, and a history of sitting inoperable—suggests the truck was worth less than the asking price, but it provides no basis for a rational juror to conclude the truck was worth *more* than $1,500.

The prosecution's rebuttal effectively concedes that no evidence of market value was introduced, choosing instead to argue the legal definition of value. However, a correct statement of the law cannot substitute for the evidentiary proof required to sustain a conviction. The State's failure to provide any evidence of the truck's value exceeding $1,500 is fatal to the first-degree theft charge.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The State established that the Georges engaged in deception regarding the truck's condition, mileage, and history. However, the State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the "value" element necessary for a conviction of attempted first-degree theft. Because the State offered no evidence of the truck's actual market value, and cannot rely on the fraudulent asking price, no rational jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that the truck was worth more than $1,500.

Accordingly, the Defense's motion to dismiss the charge of attempted first-degree theft should have been granted. The conviction for attempted first-degree theft is unsupported by sufficient evidence.
