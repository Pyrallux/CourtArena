COURT ARENA LOG - Case 187
FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic (""Argentina"") and YPF S.A. (""YPF"") (together, ""defendants"") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering (""IPO"") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the ""shares"").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts (""ADRs"") listed on the New York Stock Exchange (""NYSE""). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission (""SEC"") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer ""in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed."" App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that ""[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends."" App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, ""[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote."" App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that ""[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws."" App. 23. The prospectus further stated that ""any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired."" Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. (""Repsol"") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, ""Petersen"") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the ""governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights."" Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A ""classic example"" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a ""taking"" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) (""It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights.""). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) (""States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ....""); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., ""Indirect Expropriation"" and the ""Right to Regulate"" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the ""General Expropriation Law."" App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress ""shall particularly refer to specific property"" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that ""[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved."" App. 185-86. The law further declares that ""[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...."" App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive ""the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation,"" such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that ""[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator."" App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an ""Intervenor"" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as ""fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law."" App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as ""unfair"" and a ""bear trap."" Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the ""YPF Expropriation Law""). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving ""self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply,"" App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating ""public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons,"" App. 166.
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to ""exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation"" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

IV. Procedural History

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint, arguing, inter alia , that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. Â§ 1601 et seq. (the ""FSIA""), and that Petersen's claims were barred by the ""act of state doctrine."" As is relevant here, the district court denied defendants' motions to dismiss with respect to the FSIA and act of state issues. They timely appealed the FSIA ruling and the district court subsequently certified the act of state issue for our interlocutory review.


YPF's refusal to enforce section 7(h)'s penalties had a direct effect in the United States because (1) it enabled Argentina to cancel planned dividend payments, some of which would have been made to investors based in the United States, and (2) it precipitated Petersen's default on its loan obligations and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City."

**Opening Argument for Petersen Energía Inversora S.A.U. and Petersen Energía, S.A.U.**  

May it please the Court:  

This case arises from Argentina and YPF’s deliberate breach of binding contractual obligations they voluntarily undertook to secure U.S. investment in a critical energy company. When Argentina privatized YPF in 1993, it explicitly promised U.S. investors robust protections through YPF’s bylaws—protections central to the success of the New York Stock Exchange IPO. These bylaws mandated a cash tender offer for all shareholders if Argentina acquired controlling shares, and they strictly prohibited voting rights for shares acquired in violation of that requirement. Yet in 2012, Argentina expropriated 51% of Repsol’s shares in YPF without honoring the tender offer, and YPF actively enabled this breach by permitting Argentina to exercise voting rights and cancel dividend payments. The district court correctly denied Defendants’ motions to dismiss because: (1) these obligations are commercial in nature, triggering the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act’s exception to sovereign immunity; and (2) the act of state doctrine does not bar enforcement of private contractual duties.  

### I. Argentina and YPF’s Actions Were Commercial, Subjecting Them to U.S. Jurisdiction Under the FSIA  
The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) bars suits against foreign states unless an exception applies. Here, the commercial activity exception—28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)—clearly governs. This exception applies when the lawsuit is "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere" that has a "direct effect in the United States."  

Argentina and YPF created the tender offer provisions as part of a commercial transaction designed to attract U.S. investment. When YPF went public in 1993, it listed American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the NYSE and filed a prospectus with the SEC explicitly stating: "Under [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares]." This was not a sovereign mandate but a voluntary contractual safeguard for investors—a standard corporate governance mechanism any private company would adopt to reassure shareholders. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), "the commercial activity exception applies when the foreign state's activity is one that a private party could engage in." The Court emphasized that "the nature of the activity, not its purpose, determines commerciality." Here, the tender offer requirement is indistinguishable from provisions in the bylaws of any publicly traded corporation—commercial in every respect.  

Crucially, the breach had a direct effect in the United States. YPF’s refusal to enforce section 7(h) of the bylaws— which prohibits voting rights for shares acquired without a tender offer—allowed Argentina to cancel dividend payments that would have flowed to U.S. investors. It also precipitated Petersen’s default on loans secured by ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. In *Weltover*, the Court found a direct effect where a sovereign defaulted on bonds issued in New York. Here, the effects are even more concrete: dividends withheld from U.S. holders and loan defaults in New York City. This satisfies the FSIA’s direct effect requirement.  

### II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply to Contract Claims  
The act of state doctrine—which bars courts from questioning the validity of a foreign government’s official acts within its territory—has no place here. As the Supreme Court made clear in *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 405 (1990), "the act of state doctrine does not apply to a claim that a foreign government has breached a contractual obligation." Petersen’s claims are not about the legality of Argentina’s expropriation; they are about Argentina and YPF’s failure to honor their separate, pre-existing contractual duties under the bylaws. The bylaws created binding obligations that existed independently of the expropriation—obligations Argentina and YPF knowingly ignored.  

When Argentina acquired Repsol’s shares in 2012, it triggered the tender offer requirement under section 28(A) of the bylaws. Yet Argentina publicly dismissed these provisions as a "bear trap" and refused to make a tender offer. YPF then permitted Argentina to vote those shares and cancel dividend payments, violating section 7(h). These are not sovereign acts but breaches of private contractual duties. To apply the act of state doctrine would allow foreign governments to evade contractual responsibilities merely by labeling their conduct "sovereign"—a result the Supreme Court has consistently rejected.

This is a straightforward breach of contract case. Argentina and YPF promised U.S. investors specific protections to facilitate a successful IPO on the NYSE. They then broke those promises, causing real harm to U.S. investors and financial institutions. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies because the obligations are commercial, and the act of state doctrine does not shield private contractual breaches. The district court correctly denied dismissal. We respectfully request that this Court affirm its ruling.

### INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") respectfully submit this opening response to oppose the affirmance of the district court's denial of their motions to dismiss.

This case presents a fundamental question: whether a foreign sovereign may be haled into United States courts to answer for sovereign acts of expropriation simply because those acts were effectuated through corporate bylaws that were originally designed to attract private investment. The answer must be no.

The district court erred in denying Argentina's motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act because the claims at issue arise from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation—carried out pursuant to the Argentine National Constitution and the YPF Expropriation Law (Law 26,741)—not from ordinary commercial activity. Additionally, the act of state doctrine bars adjudication of these claims, which are fundamentally premised on challenging the validity and scope of Argentina's sovereign exercise of its expropriation power.

We respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss under the FSIA and dismiss the claims against Argentina.

---

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING ARGENTINA'S MOTION TO DISMISS UNDER THE FOREIGN SOVEREIGN IMMUNITIES ACT

#### A. The Applicable Legal Framework

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act establishes the baseline rule that foreign states are immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. This immunity may be overcome only if a claim falls within one of the statutory exceptions enumerated in 28 U.S.C. § 1605. The commercial activity exception, invoked by Plaintiffs, provides that a foreign state shall not be immune in any case:

> "in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act has a direct effect in the United States."

28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

Critically, the Supreme Court has held that "the commercial activity exception does not apply to a claim arising from expropriation." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). Expropriation of property is a sovereign act by definition—it is the exercise of the government's eminent domain power—and falls outside the commercial activity exception. As this Court observed in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006), "expropriation is quintessentially a sovereign activity."

#### B. The Claims Arise From Argentina's Sovereign Expropriation, Not From Commercial Activity

The essence of Plaintiffs' complaint is that Argentina, by enacting Law 26,741 and effectuating the expropriation of 51% of YPF's shares from Repsol, violated the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. This framing cannot obscure the fundamental nature of what occurred: Argentina exercised its sovereign power of eminent domain to expropriate property for a public purpose, as authorized by Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law (Law 21,499).

The Supreme Court's decision in *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400 (1990), is instructive. In *Kirkpatrick*, the Court held that the act of state doctrine did not bar a claim that a foreign government had breached a contractual obligation. However, the Court emphasized that the contract at issue was "an agreement between private parties" that did not "require the court to judge the legality of the sovereign's acts." *Id.* at 405-06. Here, by contrast, Plaintiffs' claims cannot be adjudicated without this Court necessarily passing upon the validity and scope of Argentina's sovereign expropriation under Argentine law.

The bylaws' tender offer provisions, moreover, cannot be divorced from their context as instruments of Argentine law. Argentina created YPF as a state-owned enterprise, decided to privatize it through specific statutory mechanisms, and embedded the takeover protections within the corporate governance structure as part of a coordinated governmental policy. As the Supreme Court held in *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618, "the commercial activity exception does not apply to actions arising from expropriation." The fact that Argentina utilized corporate bylaws as a mechanism does not transform the inherently sovereign act of expropriation into commercial activity.

#### C. The "Direct Effect" Analysis Does Not Override Sovereign Immunity for Expropriation Claims

Plaintiffs rely upon the "direct effect" prong of the commercial activity exception to argue that jurisdiction exists because Argentina's failure to make a tender offer affected U.S. investors and financial institutions. This argument fails.

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court recognized that the "direct effect" requirement demands a "direct effect in the United States," meaning "the foreign state has contracted to cause the plaintiff a direct injury." 504 U.S. at 618. However, the Court in *Weltover* was careful to distinguish between sovereign debt instruments—which are commercial in nature—and sovereign acts of expropriation. The Court explicitly held that "the commercial activity exception does not apply to actions arising from expropriation." *Id.*

Even assuming *arguendo* that the "direct effect" prong could otherwise apply, the injury alleged by Plaintiffs—loss of dividend payments and subsequent loan defaults—flows not from any purely commercial activity but from the sovereign act of expropriation. The chain of causation runs directly from Argentina's exercise of its eminent domain power. Without the expropriation, there would be no claim. The commercial activity exception cannot be triggered by collateral commercial effects of a sovereign act.

#### D. YPF Is Entitled to Sovereign Immunity as an Instrumentality of Argentina

YPF, as a corporation wholly owned by the Argentine government at the time of the events in question, is an "agency or instrumentality of a foreign state" within the meaning of the FSIA. 28 U.S.C. § 1603(a). Although YPF was partially privatized in 1993, Argentina retained significant control, including Class A shares entitling it to board representation and veto rights over acquisitions. At the time of the expropriation, Argentina exercised control over YPF through the Intervenor and subsequent governmental actions. Under these circumstances, YPF is entitled to sovereign immunity.

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS ADJUDICATION OF PLAINTIFFS' CLAIMS

#### A. The Applicable Legal Framework

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory are not subject to adjudication in United States courts." *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 404 (1990). The doctrine reflects "the sensitivity of the relations between foreign states and the concern that the United States courts become entangled in the validity of foreign sovereign acts." *Id.*

The doctrine applies unless "the relief requested would require the court to question the validity of the foreign state's act." *Id.* at 409. Where, as here, adjudication would require the court to "inquire into the validity of an act of a foreign state," the doctrine bars the claim. *Id.* at 405.

#### B. Adjudication of Plaintiffs' Claims Requires Questioning the Validity of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

Plaintiffs attempt to characterize this as a simple breach of contract case, arguing that the act of state doctrine does not apply because they are not challenging the legality of the expropriation itself. This characterization is incorrect.

The core of Plaintiffs' complaint is that Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares violated the tender offer provisions of the bylaws. But to determine whether those provisions were triggered—and whether Argentina breached its obligations—the Court would necessarily have to examine the nature, scope, and validity of Argentina's expropriation under Argentine law. Specifically, the Court would need to determine whether the expropriation, as carried out pursuant to Law 26,741 and the General Expropriation Law, was a valid exercise of Argentina's sovereign power.

In *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), the Supreme Court held that the act of state doctrine barred a claim challenging the validity of Cuba's expropriation of property from a U.S. company. The Court emphasized that "the doctrine serves to insulate from judicial inquiry the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory, even if those acts violate international law." *Id.* at 428. While subsequent cases have recognized exceptions for certain violations of international law, the doctrine remains robust where, as here, the claim would require the court to adjudicate the validity of a foreign sovereign's acts.

Plaintiffs' reliance on *Kirkpatrick* is misplaced. In *Kirkpatrick*, the Court held that the doctrine did not apply where the claim was "based upon a contract between [the foreign sovereign] and an American corporation," and where "adjudication of the claim would not require the court to pass on the validity of the foreign sovereign's acts." 493 U.S. at 405-06. Here, by contrast, the claims cannot be adjudicated without examining the validity and scope of Argentina's sovereign expropriation.

#### C. The Contractual Label Does Not Override the Act of State Doctrine

Plaintiffs argue that because the alleged obligations arise from YPF's bylaws—private corporate documents—the act of state doctrine is inapplicable. This argument fails.

The bylaws' tender offer provisions were activated only by Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The provisions themselves contemplate that Argentina might acquire shares through governmental action, and section 28(A) specifically addresses acquisitions by "the Government of Argentina." The question of whether Argentina triggered the tender offer requirement is therefore inseparable from the question of whether Argentina's acquisition was a sovereign expropriation.

Moreover, Argentina's decision to acquire YPF shares was effectuated through legislation (Law 26,741), a decree of the National Executive Office, and the exercise of governmental powers (appointment of an Intervenor, cancellation of dividend payments through governmental action). These are unmistakably sovereign acts. To hold that Argentina can be held liable for breaching corporate bylaws whenever it exercises its sovereign powers would eviscerate the act of state doctrine and discourage foreign investment in state-owned enterprises.

---

### III. THE SPECIFIC BYLAW PROVISIONS INVOLVED ARE INSEPARABLE FROM SOVEREIGN ACTS

The tender offer requirements in sections 7 and 28 of YPF's bylaws were designed to operate within the framework of Argentine governmental action. Section 28(A) specifically contemplates that "the Government of Argentina" might acquire shares "directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument." The provision was intended to address scenarios in which Argentina, acting in its sovereign capacity, might seek to regain control of YPF.

When Argentina enacted Law 26,741 and effectuated the expropriation, it acted as a sovereign exercising its constitutional power of eminent domain. The tender offer provisions cannot be read in isolation from this sovereign context. To enforce those provisions against Argentina would be to adjudicate the terms and conditions under which Argentina may exercise its expropriation power—a task that is barred by both the act of state doctrine and the FSIA's exclusion of expropriation claims from the commercial activity exception.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court is the appeal of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction and Rule 12(b)(6) based on the act of state doctrine.**

Having reviewed the facts provided, the opening arguments of the parties, and the applicable legal standards, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. Standard of Review**

We review de novo a district court's denial of a motion to dismiss based on foreign sovereign immunity under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). See *Jafari v. Islamic Republic of Iran*, 784 F.3d 997, 1001 (5th Cir. 2015). We also review de novo the denial of a motion to dismiss based on the act of state doctrine. See *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964).

**II. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

Argentina argues that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because the FSIA does not permit jurisdiction over its sovereign acts of expropriation. We disagree.

The FSIA establishes a presumption of immunity for foreign states, subject to specific exceptions. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The relevant exception here is the "commercial activity" exception, which provides that a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "in which the action is based upon... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act has a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), the Supreme Court held that the "commercial activity" exception should be governed by the "nature" of the activity rather than its "purpose." The Court stated that "when a foreign government acts in the manner of a private player within a market, the FSIA's commercial activity exception applies." *Id.* at 614. The Court further clarified that a foreign state's issuance of bonds is a commercial activity, even if the purpose is to raise funds for sovereign functions. *Id.*

Here, the action is based upon Argentina's and YPF's participation in the privatization of YPF and the subsequent management of its shares. The specific acts upon which Plaintiffs base their claims are the amendment of YPF's bylaws to include tender offer provisions, the issuance of ADRs on the NYSE, and the failure to honor those contractual provisions upon the reacquisition of shares. These acts—issuing securities, amending corporate governance documents, and promising a cash tender offer to shareholders—are "the sort of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. They are commercial in nature, regardless of Argentina's sovereign purpose in expropriating the company.

Furthermore, the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied. The complaint alleges that Argentina's refusal to comply with the bylaws caused the cancellation of dividend payments to U.S. investors and precipitated the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. In *Weltover*, the Court found a direct effect where Argentina's default on bonds caused a financial loss to bondholders in the United States. 504 U.S. at 619. Similarly, the financial injury to Petersen and the foreclosure of assets located in New York constitute a direct effect in the United States.

Argentina relies on *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), for the proposition that expropriation is a quintessentially sovereign activity. While we agree that the act of expropriation itself is sovereign, the claims here are not for the expropriation *per se*, but rather for the breach of the contractual obligations voluntarily undertaken by Argentina and YPF in connection with the commercial issuance of securities. The "commercial activity" exception encompasses the breach of those commercial obligations. Therefore, the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA.

**III. The Act of State Doctrine**

Argentina next argues that the act of state doctrine bars Plaintiffs' claims because adjudication would require this Court to question the validity of Argentina's expropriation under its own laws. We find this argument unpersuasive.

The act of state doctrine "prevents the judicial branch of our government from questioning the validity of public acts that a recognized foreign sovereign power has taken within its own territory." *Kirkpatrick*, 493 U.S. at 405. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly limited the application of this doctrine in the context of breach of contract claims.

In *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400 (1990), the plaintiffs alleged that a foreign government official had breached a contract by accepting a bribe. The Court held that the act of state doctrine did not bar the claim because "the issue presented does not require the Court to declare invalid the official act of a foreign sovereign." *Id.* at 405. The Court explained that "the fact that the contract was made with a foreign government does not necessarily mean that enforcing it would involve the courts in the validity of the sovereign's acts." *Id.* at 409.

Similarly, in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), the Court noted that the doctrine is not a rigid rule but a principle of decisionmaking that fosters respect for the executive branch's foreign policy. However, where a claim can be adjudicated without passing judgment on the validity of the sovereign's public acts, the doctrine does not apply.

Here, Plaintiffs allege a breach of the YPF bylaws. To adjudicate this claim, the Court need not determine whether the Argentine Congress had the constitutional authority to expropriate YPF's shares or whether Law 26,741 was a valid exercise of eminent domain. The Court need only determine whether the acquisition of shares triggered the contractual provisions of the bylaws—specifically, the tender offer requirements of sections 7 and 28—and whether the failure to comply with those provisions constitutes a breach. As in *Kirkpatrick*, enforcing the contract does not require us to declare the expropriation invalid; it merely requires us to hold the defendants to the terms they voluntarily agreed to when they sought U.S. investment.

Argentina's argument that the bylaws are inseparable from the expropriation ignores the distinction between the *act* of taking and the *obligations* undertaken regarding the method of taking. The bylaws specifically contemplated the Government of Argentina acquiring shares and imposed conditions on such acquisitions. Enforcing those conditions does not invalidate the expropriation; it enforces the contractual penalties associated with it.

Therefore, the district court correctly determined that the act of state doctrine does not bar Plaintiffs' breach of contract claims.

**IV. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we affirm the district court's order denying Defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine.

**SO ORDERED.**

Petersen attempts to obscure the sovereign nature of this dispute by focusing on the commercial packaging of the IPO rather than the substantive acts giving rise to the claim. This attempt fails.

The Supreme Court in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), established that the commercial activity exception applies only when the foreign state acts "in the manner of a private player within a market." However, the Court explicitly acknowledged that "the FSIA does not provide a basis for adjudication of the sovereign's public acts, such as expropriation." *Id.* at 618. The critical distinction is between commercial activity incidental to sovereign acts versus the sovereign acts themselves.

Petersen's injury flows directly from Argentina's sovereign exercise of eminent domain. Argentina enacted Law 26,741 to expropriate 51% of YPF's voting stock from Repsol for purposes of national public interest in hydrocarbon self-sufficiency. This is the quintessential sovereign act—an exercise of the government's power of eminent domain. See *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006) (holding that "expropriation is a quintessentially sovereign activity").

Petersen's argument that the commercial activity exception applies because Argentina "voluntarily amended bylaws" ignores the source of those bylaws. The bylaws were not a private commercial contract between arm's-length parties. They were amendments to YPF's corporate governance structure effected under Argentine law as part of a state-owned enterprise's privatization. Argentina enacted these bylaws in its sovereign capacity to structure the terms of a government-owned entity's transformation into a partially privatized company. The amendment of corporate bylaws by a sovereign state to govern its own property is a sovereign act, not a commercial one.

As this Court noted in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d at 595, "the FSIA's exceptions do not apply to a claim arising from an expropriation, even if the claim is styled as a breach of contract." The commercial activity exception cannot be used to circumvent sovereign immunity for the fundamental reason that the injury alleged—loss of ownership interest in YPF shares—results from the governmental taking of property.

#### B. The "Direct Effect" Requirement Is Not Satisfied

Petersen's reliance on the "direct effect" prong fails because the causal chain between the alleged commercial activity and the injury is too attenuated. The Supreme Court in *Weltover* found a direct effect where Argentina's default on bond payments caused immediate financial loss to bondholders in the United States. 504 U.S. at 619. The injury there flowed directly from the commercial activity—bond issuance—without an intervening sovereign act.

Here, the chain of causation is fundamentally different: Argentina's sovereign expropriation of YPF shares → loss of corporate control by Petersen → cancellation of dividends → default on loans → foreclosure. The proximate cause of Petersen's injury is the sovereign act of expropriation, not the commercial activity of issuing ADRs. The fact that Petersen held ADRs in New York and that dividends would have been paid to U.S. investors does not transform the nature of the taking.

Petersen's theory would permit any foreign sovereign that issues securities in the United States to be haled into U.S. court for any subsequent sovereign action affecting those securities. This would eviscerate the FSIA's protections. The commercial activity exception requires that the claim be "based upon" commercial activity, not merely that commercial activity preceded a sovereign act that caused injury.

#### C. YPF Retained Its Sovereign Immunity as an Instrumentality of Argentina

Petersen's argument that YPF lost sovereign immunity through its partial privatization ignores the statutory framework. Under the FSIA, an "instrumentality" of a foreign state is entitled to immunity unless it engages in commercial activity "in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

YPF remained under Argentina's control even after the IPO. Argentina held Class A shares, retained veto rights over third-party acquisitions, and maintained the power to elect board members. More critically, YPF's actions challenged here—amending bylaws, issuing securities, and failing to enforce bylaws against Argentina—occurred in connection with Argentina's sovereign ownership and control. YPF was not acting as a private commercial actor when it structured the IPO; it was implementing Argentina's sovereign policy of privatization.

The FSIA does not strip sovereign immunity from government instrumentalities merely because they engage in some commercial activity. Immunity is lost only for acts that are themselves commercial. See *Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 769 (2019) (holding that international organizations, like foreign states, are immune unless the claim is based upon commercial activity with a direct effect in the United States). YPF's core activities at issue—managing the expropriation and operating the company under government control—were sovereign, not commercial.

---

### III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS CLAIM

#### A. Adjudication Requires This Court to Judge the Validity of Argentina's Expropriation Under Argentine Law

Petersen's attempt to characterize this as a simple breach of contract claim is a transparent effort to evade the act of state doctrine. The doctrine provides that "the judicial branch of our government will not examine the validity of a taking of property by a foreign sovereign government within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964).

Petersen's claims require this Court to determine: (1) whether Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares triggered the tender offer requirements in sections 7 and 28 of the bylaws; (2) whether Argentina's failure to conduct a tender offer constituted a breach; and (3) whether YPF breached its obligation to enforce penalties against Argentina.

Each of these determinations requires interpreting Argentine law—the YPF bylaws, the General Expropriation Law, Law 26,741, and Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution—to determine whether Argentina's method of acquiring YPF shares complied with its own legal framework. This is precisely what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The bylaws were not a private commercial contract between equal parties. They were Argentine legal instruments that could only be amended and interpreted under Argentine law. When Petersen asks this Court to enforce the bylaws against Argentina, it asks this Court to sit in judgment of whether Argentina complied with its own laws in effectuating the expropriation.

Petersen's reliance on *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400 (1990), is misplaced. In *Kirkpatrick*, the plaintiff alleged that a foreign government official had breached a contract by accepting a bribe. The contract was with a private party, and the alleged breach did not require the Court to examine the validity of any sovereign act. 493 U.S. at 409.

Here, by contrast, the contract is with the sovereign itself. The bylaws impose obligations on Argentina as a shareholder and contemplate the government's acquisition of shares. Enforcing these provisions against Argentina necessarily requires this Court to evaluate whether Argentina's acquisition—its sovereign act—complied with the conditions Argentina itself had imposed. This is the "questioning of the validity of public acts" that the act of state doctrine prohibits. *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428.

#### B. The Bylaws Are Inseparable from Argentine Sovereign Law

Petersen's argument that the bylaws can be treated as a separate commercial contract ignores their legal character. The bylaws were adopted pursuant to Argentine law as part of the privatization of a government-owned enterprise. They derive their force from Argentine legal authority, not from private commercial agreement.

Section 28(A) of the bylaws specifically addresses acquisitions by "the Government of Argentina" and imposes tender offer requirements "if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the Government becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of YPF." This provision is part of Argentina's own legal framework governing the privatization. When Argentina later enacted Law 26,741 to expropriate YPF shares, it was acting within its sovereign capacity to modify the legal framework it had created.

To enforce the bylaws against Argentina, this Court would need to determine that Law 26,741's method of expropriation was invalid as to Petersen's interests because it did not comply with the tender offer provisions. This is exactly the type of judicial inquiry into the validity of a foreign sovereign's acts that the act of state doctrine bars.

#### C. The Public Policy Rationale Supports Application of the Doctrine

The act of state doctrine reflects "the strong sense of the judicial branch that its engagement in the task of passing on the validity of foreign acts of state may hinder" the executive branch's conduct of foreign relations. *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 423. This concern is particularly acute where, as here, the foreign sovereign has acted pursuant to its constitutional processes to expropriate property for public use.

Argentina enacted Law 26,741 pursuant to Article 17 of its National Constitution, which requires only a declaration of public use and compensation. Argentina has compensated Repsol $4.8 billion for the expropriation. To now allow Petersen to recover additional amounts based on the theory that Argentina violated its own bylaws in effectuating the taking would permit U.S. courts to sit in judgment of the adequacy and method of Argentine expropriation law.

This is precisely the interference with foreign sovereign authority that the act of state doctrine was designed to prevent. The executive branch, not the judicial branch, is best positioned to manage the diplomatic consequences of such disputes.

---

### IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's order denying Defendants' motions to dismiss. The FSIA's commercial activity exception does not apply because Petersen's claims arise from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation, not from independent commercial activity. The "direct effect" requirement is not satisfied where the injury flows from the sovereign taking. YPF retains sovereign immunity as an instrumentality of Argentina. Additionally, the act of state doctrine bars this claim because adjudication requires this Court to judge the validity of Argentina's expropriation under Argentine law.

**UPDATED RULING**

**Before the Court are the appeals of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction and Rule 12(b)(6) based on the act of state doctrine.**

Having reviewed the rebuttals of the parties and the applicable legal standards, we affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

We review de novo a district court's denial of a motion to dismiss based on foreign sovereign immunity under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). See *Jafari v. Islamic Republic of Iran*, 784 F.3d 997, 1001 (5th Cir. 2015).

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

Argentina argues that the FSIA's commercial activity exception does not apply because the "true nature" of the claim is sovereign expropriation, not commercial activity. Specifically, Argentina contends that the injury flows from the enactment of Law 26,741 and the seizure of YPF shares, which are quintessentially sovereign acts. See *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006). We are not persuaded.

The commercial activity exception provides that a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "in which the action is based upon... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act has a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), the Supreme Court held that the "commercial activity" exception is governed by the "nature" of the activity rather than its "purpose." The Court stated that "when a foreign government acts in the manner of a private player within a market, the FSIA's commercial activity exception applies." *Id.* at 614.

Here, the action is based upon Argentina's and YPF's failure to comply with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. The bylaws were amended as part of a privatization effort specifically designed to "entice investors" and "ensure [the IPO's] success." The acts of amending corporate bylaws to include investor protections, issuing ADRs on the NYSE, and filing prospectuses with the SEC are "the sort of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. While the *trigger* for the breach was Argentina's subsequent decision to expropriate shares, the *basis* for the lawsuit is the breach of the commercial obligations voluntarily undertaken to facilitate the IPO. The fact that the bylaws are "instruments of Argentine law" does not transform their commercial enforcement into a sovereign act. See *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 (holding that issuing bonds is commercial even if done for sovereign purposes).

Argentina's reliance on *Garb* is misplaced. *Garb* involved a direct claim for expropriation under international law. Here, Plaintiffs do not challenge the validity of the expropriation itself; they seek to enforce the contractual penalties associated with the acquisition of shares. The FSIA's commercial activity exception encompasses the breach of these commercial obligations.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

Argentina argues that the "direct effect" requirement is not satisfied because the chain of causation is attenuated: the expropriation led to a loss of control, which led to dividend cancellation, which led to default and foreclosure. We disagree.

The complaint alleges that Argentina's refusal to comply with the bylaws—specifically, the refusal to strip voting rights and the cancellation of dividend payments—had a direct effect in the United States. The cancellation of dividends precipitated Petersen's default on its loans and the subsequent foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. In *Weltover*, the Court found a direct effect where Argentina's default on bonds caused financial loss to bondholders in the United States. 504 U.S. at 619. Similarly, the financial injury to Petersen and the foreclosure of assets located in New York constitute a direct effect in the United States. The causal chain here is no more attenuated than the one in *Weltover*.

**C. YPF's Immunity**

YPF argues it retains immunity as an instrumentality of Argentina because it was implementing sovereign privatization policy. However, under the FSIA, an instrumentality is not immune for acts "based upon... a commercial activity of the foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). YPF's acts of issuing ADRs, amending bylaws to attract U.S. investors, and failing to honor those bylaws are commercial in nature. The fact that YPF was partially state-owned does not immunize its commercial activities. See *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Argentina next argues that the act of state doctrine bars Plaintiffs' claims because adjudication would require this Court to determine the validity of Argentina's expropriation under its own laws. We find this argument unpersuasive.

The act of state doctrine "prevents the judicial branch of our government from questioning the validity of public acts that a recognized foreign sovereign power has taken within its own territory." *Kirkpatrick*, 493 U.S. at 405. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly limited the application of this doctrine in the context of breach of contract claims.

In *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400 (1990), the plaintiffs alleged that a foreign government official had breached a contract by accepting a bribe. The Court held that the act of state doctrine did not bar the claim because "the issue presented does not require the Court to declare invalid the official act of a foreign sovereign." *Id.* at 405. The Court explained that "the fact that the contract was made with a foreign government does not necessarily mean that enforcing it would involve the courts in the validity of the sovereign's acts." *Id.* at 409.

Here, Plaintiffs allege a breach of the YPF bylaws. To adjudicate this claim, the Court need not determine whether Law 26,741 was a valid exercise of eminent domain or whether the Argentine Congress complied with its own constitution in declaring public use. The Court need only determine whether the acquisition of shares triggered the contractual provisions of the bylaws—specifically, the tender offer requirements of sections 7 and 28—and whether the failure to comply with those provisions constitutes a breach. As in *Kirkpatrick*, enforcing the contract does not require us to declare the expropriation invalid; it merely requires us to hold the defendants to the terms they voluntarily agreed to when they sought U.S. investment.

Argentina's argument that the bylaws are inseparable from the expropriation ignores the distinction between the *act* of taking and the *obligations* undertaken regarding the method of taking. The bylaws specifically contemplated the Government of Argentina acquiring shares and imposed conditions on such acquisitions. Enforcing those conditions does not invalidate the expropriation; it enforces the contractual penalties associated with it. The public policy concerns underlying the act of state doctrine, articulated in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), are not implicated because we are not sitting in judgment of Argentina's sovereign decision to expropriate, but rather enforcing a commercial contract regarding the *terms* of that expropriation.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we affirm the district court's order denying Defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine.

To hold otherwise would allow foreign sovereigns to evade contractual liability simply by labeling their breaches as “sovereign acts.”

Your Honor, this case is not about whether Argentina could expropriate YPF. It is about whether Argentina and YPF violated their own commercial promises to U.S. investors. The bylaws were not sovereign edicts—they were binding contracts designed to secure billions in U.S. capital. # CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENDANTS-APPELLANTS

## ARGENTINE REPUBLIC AND YPF S.A

---

Your Honor, with respect to the Court, the plaintiff's closing statement conflates the fundamental distinction between sovereign authority and commercial activity that lies at the heart of the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine. While the plaintiff attempts to characterize this as a simple breach of contract case, the reality is far more complex. At its core, this case concerns Argentina's sovereign exercise of eminent domain over a strategic national resource—a quintessentially governmental function that cannot be transformed into a "commercial activity" merely because Argentina previously chose to privatize YPF. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's denial of defendants' motions to dismiss.

---

## I. THE FSIA'S COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

### A. The True Nature of the Claim Is Sovereign Expropriation, Not Commercial Activity

The plaintiff erroneously conflates the *trigger* for the breach with the *basis* of the claim, but this Court must look beyond such superficial labeling. In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), the Supreme Court held that the commercial activity exception turns on the "nature" of the activity. However, the Court also recognized that some activities are inherently sovereign regardless of their form. The question this Court must ask is whether Argentina's conduct here—the enactment of Law 26,741, the declaration of public use, and the expropriation of YPF shares—constitutes the type of activity in which a private party could engage.

The answer is definitively no. Argentina enacted legislation declaring the expropriation of YPF shares to be in the national public interest for purposes of achieving self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply. This is the quintessential exercise of sovereign eminent domain power—the power to take private property for public use upon payment of just compensation. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "the sovereign's power to take property is inherent in sovereignty." *Hawaii v. Office of Thrift Supervision*, 489 U.S. 63, 70 (1989). No private party possesses this authority.

The plaintiff's argument that the *basis* for the lawsuit is the breach of commercial obligations arising from the bylaws fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the claims. The plaintiff does not allege that Argentina failed to pay for goods or services rendered in the ordinary course of commercial dealings. Rather, the plaintiff alleges that Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power to expropriate property triggered certain contractual penalties in the bylaws. The injury of which the plaintiff complains—loss of dividend payments and voting rights—flows directly from the sovereign act of expropriation. To hold that the FSIA's commercial activity exception applies merely because Argentina previously agreed to certain contractual terms in connection with a privatization would eviscerate the immunity afforded to foreign sovereigns under the statute.

In *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), this Court held that the commercial activity exception does not apply to claims "based upon" expropriation. The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Garb* by arguing that it involves a "direct claim for expropriation under international law," whereas here the plaintiff "seeks to enforce the contractual penalties associated with the acquisition of shares." This distinction is unavailing. The plaintiff cannot circumvent the FSIA by reframing an expropriation claim as a contract claim. The injury alleged—the loss of equity value in YPF shares—arises directly from the sovereign act of expropriation. The contractual provisions in the bylaws are merely the mechanism by which the plaintiff seeks to recover for that injury.

### B. The Direct Effect Requirement Is Not Satisfied

Even assuming *arguendo* that the commercial activity exception applies, the plaintiff's claims fail to satisfy the "direct effect" requirement of 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The statute requires that the action be based upon an act that "has a direct effect in the United States." The causal chain alleged by the plaintiff is attenuated and speculative:

1. Argentina enacted the expropriation law (sovereign act)
2. Argentina acquired control of YPF shares (sovereign act)
3. Argentina refused to comply with bylaws (alleged breach)
4. Argentina canceled dividend payments (consequence)
5. Petersen defaulted on loans (financial consequence)
6. Lenders foreclosed on ADRs in New York (financial consequence)

The plaintiff asks this Court to find a "direct effect" based on the ultimate financial harm suffered by a minority shareholder. However, the chain of causation is far too attenuated. The expropriation itself—the act upon which the entire claim depends—occurred in Argentina and was directed at the majority shareholder (Repsol), not the plaintiff. The plaintiff's injury is derivative of the harm suffered by the majority shareholder.

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court found a direct effect where Argentina's default on bonds "caused" financial loss to bondholders "in the United States." 504 U.S. at 619. But the bonds at issue in *Weltover* were issued directly to U.S. bondholders, and Argentina's default was the immediate cause of their injury. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff is a minority shareholder whose injury flows from the cancellation of dividend payments—a consequence that is two steps removed from the sovereign act of expropriation.

Moreover, the plaintiff's alleged injury is not "direct" within the meaning of the FSIA because it depends on the plaintiff's voluntary decision to leverage its shares as collateral for loans. The plaintiff chose to finance its acquisition of YPF shares through debt, and it chose to pledge its shares as collateral. The subsequent foreclosure was not a direct consequence of Argentina's conduct; it was a consequence of the plaintiff's own financial decisions.

### C. YPF Retains Immunity as an Instrumentality of Argentina

YPF separately argues that it retains immunity as an instrumentality of Argentina. The plaintiff argues that YPF's acts—amending bylaws, issuing ADRs, and filing prospectuses—were "commercial in nature." However, this argument ignores the context in which those acts occurred.

YPF was created as a state-owned enterprise and remained partially owned by the Argentine government even after the privatization. The amendments to the bylaws were made pursuant to Argentina's sovereign decision to privatize YPF as part of broader economic reform efforts. YPF was acting as an instrumentality of the Argentine government in implementing this privatization policy. The fact that certain corporate actions may have resembled commercial activity does not strip YPF of immunity for acts taken in its capacity as a sovereign instrumentality.

Under the FSIA, an instrumentality of a foreign state is immune unless the action is based upon "a commercial activity of the foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Here, the claims against YPF are not based upon ordinary commercial activity; they are based upon YPF's alleged failure to enforce bylaws in connection with a sovereign expropriation. This is not the type of commercial activity that the FSIA's exception was designed to address.

---

## II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THE PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

### A. Adjudication Requires Questioning the Validity of Argentina's Expropriation

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish this case from *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400 (1990), by arguing that the Court need only determine whether the acquisition of shares "triggered" the bylaws' tender offer requirements. This argument ignores the practical reality of the plaintiff's claims.

To determine whether Argentina "triggered" the tender offer requirements, this Court would necessarily have to determine: (1) whether Argentina's acquisition of shares through the expropriation law falls within the scope of section 28 of the bylaws; and (2) whether Argentina's failure to comply with the tender offer requirements constitutes a breach. Both inquiries require this Court to interpret Argentine law—the expropriation law, the General Expropriation Law, and the bylaws themselves.

Section 28 of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement to acquisitions by the Government of Argentina "whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument." The plaintiff argues that this provision applies to the expropriation. But determining whether the expropriation falls within the scope of section 28 requires this Court to interpret the bylaws—a contract governed by Argentine law—and to apply that interpretation to the sovereign act of expropriation.

This is precisely the type of inquiry that the act of state doctrine prohibits. In *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), the Supreme Court held that the act of state doctrine prevents judicial inquiry into the validity of foreign sovereign acts. The doctrine reflects "the capacity of the President to speak for the nation" in foreign affairs and "to avoid embarrassing the Executive Branch." *Id.* at 423. To adjudicate the plaintiff's claims, this Court would be required to determine whether Argentina's expropriation was valid under Argentine law—whether it satisfied the requirements of Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law. This is precisely the "judicial inquiry into the validity of an act of a foreign sovereign" that the doctrine bars.

### B. The Bylaws Are Inseparable from Argentine Sovereign Law

The plaintiff argues that the bylaws are "commercial contracts" rather than "sovereign edicts." But this characterization ignores the undisputed facts. The bylaws were amended as part of a sovereign privatization program, and they were designed to govern the relationship between the Argentine government (as a shareholder) and private investors. The provisions at issue—sections 7 and 28—specifically contemplate acquisitions by the Government of Argentina and impose conditions on such acquisitions.

These bylaws are not arm's-length commercial contracts between private parties. They are instruments of Argentine law that govern the privatization of a state-owned enterprise. The plaintiff cannot divorce the bylaws from the sovereign context in which they were created. To interpret and enforce the bylaws in the manner the plaintiff requests would necessarily involve this Court in judging the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts under Argentine law.

The act of state doctrine exists to prevent "the judicial branch of our government from questioning the validity of public acts that a recognized foreign sovereign power has taken within its own territory." *Kirkpatrick*, 493 U.S. at 405. The plaintiff asks this Court to do exactly that—to determine that Argentina's expropriation was invalid because it failed to comply with the bylaws, and to impose contractual penalties for that failure. This is precisely what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

---

## III. CONCLUSION

Your Honor, the plaintiff asks this Court to transform the FSIA and act of state doctrine into dead letters by permitting foreign sovereigns to be sued in U.S. courts for any act taken in connection with prior commercial activity. This Court should decline the invitation. Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain is precisely the type of act that the FSIA was designed to immunize, and the act of state doctrine was designed to protect from judicial scrutiny.

The plaintiff is not an innocent victim of commercial malfeasance. It is a sophisticated investor that chose to acquire shares in a company that remained subject to the sovereign authority of Argentina. When Argentina exercised its inherent power to expropriate property for public use—a power recognized by both Argentine and international law—the plaintiff suffered the same fate as any minority shareholder in a nationalized industry. The plaintiff's attempt to recover for that injury by invoking contractual provisions that Argentina allegedly violated in the exercise of its sovereign authority should be dismissed.

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's denial of their motions to dismiss.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court are the appeals of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction and Rule 12(b)(6) based on the act of state doctrine.**

Having carefully considered the closing arguments of both parties, the applicable legal standards, and the record provided, we affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

We review de novo a district court's denial of a motion to dismiss based on foreign sovereign immunity under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). See *Jafari v. Islamic Republic of Iran*, 784 F.3d 997, 1001 (5th Cir. 2015).

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

Argentina argues that the FSIA's commercial activity exception does not apply because the "true nature" of the claim is sovereign expropriation, not commercial activity. We disagree.

The commercial activity exception provides that a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "in which the action is based upon... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act has a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the Supreme Court held that the "commercial activity" exception is governed by the "nature" of the activity rather than its "purpose." The Court stated that "when a foreign government acts in the manner of a private player within a market, the FSIA's commercial activity exception applies." *Id.*

Here, the action is based upon Argentina's and YPF's failure to comply with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws—specifically sections 7 and 28—following Argentina's acquisition of a controlling interest in YPF. The bylaws were amended as part of a privatization effort specifically designed to "entice investors" and "ensure [the IPO's] success." The acts of amending corporate bylaws to include investor protections, issuing ADRs on the NYSE, and filing prospectuses with the SEC are "the sort of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

While the *trigger* for the breach was Argentina's subsequent decision to expropriate shares, the *basis* for the lawsuit is the breach of the commercial obligations voluntarily undertaken to facilitate the IPO. The fact that the bylaws are "instruments of Argentine law," as argued by the defense, does not transform their commercial enforcement into a sovereign act. As the Supreme Court noted in *Weltover*, "a foreign sovereign's issuance of bonds... is a commercial activity" even if the bonds are issued to raise funds for sovereign purposes. 504 U.S. at 614. Similarly, Argentina's entry into the commercial market to privatize YPF and its subsequent failure to honor the contractual terms of that privatization constitute commercial activity.

Argentina relies on *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006), to argue that the claim is "based upon" expropriation. However, *Garb* involved a direct claim for expropriation under international law. Here, Plaintiffs do not challenge the validity of the expropriation itself; they seek to enforce the contractual penalties associated with the acquisition of shares. The injury flows from the failure to make a tender offer and the cancellation of dividends, not from the taking itself. The FSIA's commercial activity exception encompasses the breach of these commercial obligations.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

Argentina argues that the "direct effect" requirement is not satisfied because the chain of causation is attenuated. We disagree.

The complaint alleges that Argentina's refusal to comply with the bylaws—specifically, the refusal to strip voting rights and the cancellation of dividend payments—had a direct effect in the United States. The cancellation of dividends precipitated Petersen's default on its loans and the subsequent foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. In *Weltover*, the Court found a direct effect where Argentina's default on bonds caused financial loss to bondholders in the United States. 504 U.S. at 619. Similarly, the financial injury to Petersen and the foreclosure of assets located in New York constitute a direct effect in the United States. The defense's argument that the chain is too attenuated because it involves Petersen's financial decisions ignores the direct causal link between the dividend cancellation and the default.

**C. YPF's Immunity**

YPF argues it retains immunity as an instrumentality of Argentina because it was implementing sovereign privatization policy. However, under the FSIA, an instrumentality is not immune for acts "based upon... a commercial activity of the foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). YPF's acts of issuing ADRs, amending bylaws to attract U.S. investors, and failing to honor those bylaws are commercial in nature. The fact that YPF was partially state-owned does not immunize its commercial activities. See *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Argentina next argues that the act of state doctrine bars Plaintiffs' claims because adjudication would require this Court to determine the validity of Argentina's expropriation under its own laws. We find this argument unpersuasive.

The act of state doctrine "prevents the judicial branch of our government from questioning the validity of public acts that a recognized foreign sovereign power has taken within its own territory." *Kirkpatrick*, 493 U.S. at 405. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly limited the application of this doctrine in the context of breach of contract claims.

In *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400 (1990), the plaintiffs alleged that a foreign government official had breached a contract by accepting a bribe. The Court held that the act of state doctrine did not bar the claim because "the issue presented does not require the Court to declare invalid the official act of a foreign sovereign." *Id.* at 405. The Court explained that "the fact that the contract was made with a foreign government does not necessarily mean that enforcing it would involve the courts in the validity of the sovereign's acts." *Id.* at 409.

Here, Plaintiffs allege a breach of the YPF bylaws. To adjudicate this claim, the Court need not determine whether Law 26,741 was a valid exercise of eminent domain or whether the Argentine Congress complied with its own constitution in declaring public use. The Court need only determine whether the acquisition of shares triggered the contractual provisions of the bylaws—specifically, the tender offer requirements of sections 7 and 28—and whether the failure to comply with those provisions constitutes a breach. As in *Kirkpatrick*, enforcing the contract does not require us to declare the expropriation invalid; it merely requires us to hold the defendants to the terms they voluntarily agreed to when they sought U.S. investment.

Argentina's argument that the bylaws are inseparable from the expropriation ignores the distinction between the *act* of taking and the *obligations* undertaken regarding the method of taking. The bylaws specifically contemplated the Government of Argentina acquiring shares and imposed conditions on such acquisitions. Enforcing those conditions does not invalidate the expropriation; it enforces the contractual penalties associated with it. The public policy concerns underlying the act of state doctrine, articulated in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), are not implicated because we are not sitting in judgment of Argentina's sovereign decision to expropriate, but rather enforcing a commercial contract regarding the *terms* of that expropriation.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we affirm the district court's order denying Defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine.

**SO ORDERED.**
