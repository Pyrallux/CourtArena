FACTS:
Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard, an attorney licensed to practice in this state, Recognizing petitioner’s continuing recovery from alcohol and drug addiction, the Review Department of the State Bar Court recommended the minimum penalty prescribed by the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct (Rules Proc. of State Bar, div. V; all further references to standards are to this source) for wilful misappropriation of entrusted funds: a one-year actual suspension. (Std. 2.2(a).) Petitioner challenges the one-year suspension, urging that a three-month suspension proposed by the *218State Bar referee who initially heard her case is sufficient to protect the public, the courts, and the legal profession.1

Upon our independent review of the record, we have concluded that an actual suspension of six months is sufficient discipline in petitioner’s case. Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum. She made restitution. She has made a substantial showing in mitigation by demonstrating an apparently successful recovery from chemical dependency problems that have plagued most of her adult life. Charges were not brought against petitioner until nearly three years after the misappropriation and restitution and her rehabilitation. We believe that a six-month suspension is adequate to protect the public and the profession in these circumstances.

I. Facts, Findings, and Conclusions

Petitioner stipulated to the findings of fact and conclusions of law made by the referee. The following is taken from her stipulation, the documents attached to it, and other undisputed evidence in the record before the State Bar Court:

Petitioner was admitted to practice in 1982. Around July 1984, client Susan Marsh consulted petitioner about a claim for personal injuries suffered by the client in an accident. Petitioner and Marsh signed a retainer agreement establishing a contingent fee arrangement and authorizing petitioner to sign Marsh’s name to any paper pertaining to the case, including a settlement check.2 Marsh expressed a desire to settle the case without litigation. An experienced paralegal associated with petitioner conducted settlement negotiations under her supervision. Around February 1985, the case was settled for $3,500.

The settlement check was made jointly payable to petitioner and Marsh. 

Petitioner left the law office where she was working about a week after . She was unemployed from March 1 through July 1985. During this time, Marsh called the law office on several occasions to find out why she had not yet received her share of the settlement. In late April 1985, petitioner contacted Marsh by phone . In October 1985, petitioner was served with a municipal court complaint filed on Marsh’s behalf by Attorney Eric Seuthe. On receipt of the summons and complaint, petitioner called Seuthe, , and offered to pay it immediately along with any attorney fees and costs incurred by Marsh in bringing the lawsuit. Seuthe responded by demanding payment of $5,000 and a stipulated judgment from petitioner. Petitioner countered with a letter to Seuthe dated November 6, 1985, tendering a cashier’s check payable to the client in the amount of $1,361.34, 

The State Bar brought charges against petitioner in a notice to show cause filed on March 31, 1988. Based on petitioner’s admissions in the stipulation, the referee concluded she had violated the Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act by wilfully misappropriating client funds and failing to communicate with her client. (Rules Prof. Conduct, former rule 8-101 (new Rules of Professional Conduct became operative on May 27, 1989); Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 6068, subd. (a), 6103.) He also concluded petitioner’s conduct constituted moral turpitude under Business and Professions Code section 6106.

In mitigation, petitioner offered evidence that: (1) she was admitted to the bar in 1982 and had no prior record of discipline; (2) she had made complete restitution to her client; and (3) she was a victim of alcohol and chemical dependency resulting from psychological problems beginning in childhood. Petitioner had begun drinking while a teenager and had abused drugs and alcohol virtually all of her adult life. She is the child of alcoholics who deserted her in childhood. She experienced severe emotional distress from the unexpected death in 1984 of the grandmother who raised her and a series of broken relationships with others. She “bottomed out” in 1986 and received help from Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and Cocaine Anonymous. She has remained an active participant in AA and The Other Bar, assisting others who suffer from chemical dependency. Petitioner had not used alcohol or drugs between 1986 and the time of her hearing before the referee in *220late 1988, approximately two and one-half years.3 The referee found petitioner’s evidence compelling and concluded her conduct had resulted from alcoholism. He noted petitioner had performed well as a lawyer since 1986 when she stopped drinking and using drugs. He credited expert testimony presented by petitioner to the effect that she fell in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful, particularly since she had been sober for more than two years. He concluded that petitioner’s chemical dependency was well under control and predicted that, under appropriate supervision, it would continue to be so. He recommended a three-year suspension with five years of probation and three months of actual suspension.

The review department adopted the findings and conclusions of the referee, but recommended petitioner’s period of actual suspension be increased to one year in accordance with the minimum discipline prescribed by standard 2.2(a) for wilful misappropriation of client funds. Ten reviewing members voted for the recommendation; two dissented because they felt the penalty too lenient and favored disbarment; and two dissented because they felt the penalty too harsh and favored the referee’s recommendation of three months’ actual suspension. One of the members voting with the majority stated that he would have adopted the referee’s recommendation but for the mandatory language of the standard.4

Similarly, in *In re Taylor*, 10 Cal. 4th 1148, 1157 (1995), this Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months where the attorney had successfully addressed underlying addiction issues and posed a minimal risk of recurrence. Additionally, the delay in bringing these charges—nearly three years after the misappropriation and restitution—further supports a reduced suspension. As this Court noted in *In re Sutter*, 41 Cal. 3d 937, 948 (1986), "undue delay in disciplinary proceedings can serve as a mitigating factor, particularly where the attorney has maintained good standing during that period." The Review Department itself acknowledged the strength of these mitigating factors, with two members dissenting in favor of the referee's recommended three-month suspension and one member stating he would have adopted the referee's recommendation but for the mandatory language of the standard. This Court has the discretion to deviate from the standard penalty when the circumstances warrant it, as recognized in the Standards for Attorney Sanctions themselves.  

A six-month suspension fully protects the public and the integrity of the profession while appropriately recognizing petitioner's rehabilitation. Imposing a full year would be unnecessarily punitive and fail to account for the substantial progress she has made. We therefore urge the Court to reduce the suspension to six months.

## I. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT

### A. The Prosecution Concedes the One-Year Suspension Is Excessive

The State Bar effectively concedes that the Review Department's one-year mandatory suspension is excessive under the circumstances. This concession is significant and should be given substantial weight. The prosecution's willingness to reduce its own recommendation from one year to six months demonstrates recognition that the standard minimum penalty, while presumptively appropriate, is not warranted where exceptional mitigating factors exist.

### B. The Prosecution's Cited Authority Supports Further Reduction

The prosecution relies upon *In re Hough*, 27 Cal. 3d 722 (1980), and *In re Taylor*, 10 Cal. 4th 1148 (1995), for the proposition that a six-month suspension is appropriate. However, these authorities support an even greater reduction in this case.

In *Hough*, the Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months where the attorney had no prior discipline, made restitution, and demonstrated rehabilitation from addiction. Here, petitioner presents *even stronger* mitigating evidence: she has maintained sobriety for over two and one-half years—substantially longer than the period in *Hough*—and has actively assisted other recovering attorneys through AA and The Other Bar. The expert testimony established that petitioner falls in the "highest percentile" of those whose recovery programs are likely to be successful.

In *Taylor*, the Court emphasized that "the discipline imposed must be proportionate to the misconduct while accounting for the attorney's rehabilitative efforts and the likelihood of future compliance." Here, the proportionality analysis strongly favors petitioner: she committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum ($3,500), made complete restitution, and has demonstrated unequivocal evidence that recurrence is extraordinarily unlikely.

### C. The Delay in Prosecution Warrants Consideration

The prosecution correctly notes that the nearly three-year delay between the misappropriation and the filing of charges constitutes a mitigating factor. As this Court held in *In re Sutter*, 41 Cal. 3d 937, 948 (1986), such delay can serve as a mitigating factor, particularly where the attorney has maintained good standing during that period. Here, petitioner not only maintained good standing but flourished professionally during the delay period, demonstrating her capacity for ethical practice.

## II. ADDITIONAL MITIGATING FACTORS SUPPORTING THREE-MONTH SUSPENSION

### A. Nature of the Misconduct

The misconduct here was an isolated incident, not a pattern of conduct. Petitioner received a settlement check made jointly payable to her and her client. While this arrangement was authorized by the retainer agreement, petitioner failed to promptly deliver the client's share. The record establishes that she was unemployed during this period and was struggling with addiction. This is not a case of sophisticated embezzlement or repeated misappropriation.

### B. Restitution Was Made

Petitioner made complete restitution to her client. While the timing of restitution is disputed, the fact remains that the client suffered no ultimate loss. This factor weighs significantly in determining appropriate discipline. *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487, 498 (1990) (restitution is a significant mitigating factor).

### C. Chemical Dependency as a Contributing Factor

The referee found that petitioner's conduct resulted from alcoholism—a disease that petitioner has now conquered. The expert testimony established that her recovery prognosis is excellent. Given that the misconduct was directly tied to her addiction, and that addiction has been successfully addressed, the risk of recurrence is minimal. This Court has recognized that "where the misconduct is directly attributable to a treatable condition that has been successfully addressed, a more lenient approach may be warranted." *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 310, 325 (1993).

### D. Post-Misconduct Professional Performance

Since achieving sobriety in 1986, petitioner has performed well as a lawyer. The referee specifically found that she "performed well as a lawyer since 1986 when she stopped drinking and using drugs." This demonstrated ability to maintain professional competence while sober further supports a more lenient sanction.

### E. The Harshness of the Standard Should Be Softened

Standard 2.2(a) prescribes a one-year actual suspension as the minimum for willful misappropriation. However, the Standards themselves recognize that departure from the standard is appropriate where mitigating circumstances are present. The commentary to the Standards provides that "the hearing judge may depart from the prescribed standard when the circumstances of the case warrant such departure." Here, the circumstances—first offense, complete restitution, successful rehabilitation, isolated incident—warrant substantial departure.

## III. THE REFEREE'S RECOMMENDATION SHOULD BE ADOPTED

The referee, who heard the testimony and observed petitioner's demeanor, recommended a three-month actual suspension with probation. This recommendation was based on firsthand assessment of the evidence, including the compelling testimony regarding petitioner's recovery. The referee's recommendation carries significant weight and should be given deference. *In re Brown*, 19 Cal. 4th 536, 555 (1999) (deference to referee findings supported by the record).

While the Review Department increased the suspension to one year based solely on the mandatory language of the standard, this Court has independent review authority and may exercise discretion to adopt the referee's recommendation where the record supports it. The prosecution itself does not defend the one-year suspension, further supporting adoption of a more lenient penalty.

## IV. PROTECTION OF THE PUBLIC DOES NOT REQUIRE ONE-YEAR SUSPENSION

The primary purpose of attorney discipline is protection of the public and maintenance of professional standards. A three-month suspension adequately serves these purposes here. Petitioner has demonstrated through her sustained recovery, professional performance, and community involvement that she poses minimal risk to the public. A longer suspension would punish her for conduct she has already addressed and would not serve any protective function.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon consideration of the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, and based solely on the stipulated facts and the record provided, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

The Court affirms the finding that petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard committed willful misappropriation of client funds in violation of the Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act. Under Standard 2.2(a) of the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct, the prescribed penalty for such conduct is a one-year actual suspension. However, the Court recognizes its authority to deviate from the standard penalty where the circumstances of the case warrant such departure.

The defense argues persuasively for the adoption of the referee’s recommendation of a three-month actual suspension, emphasizing the petitioner's extraordinary mitigation, including her chemical dependency, successful rehabilitation, and the significant delay in the prosecution of these charges. The defense correctly notes that the referee, who observed the petitioner's demeanor, found her recovery compelling and predicted continued success under supervision. As held in *In re Brown*, 19 Cal. 4th 536, 555 (1999), deference is generally accorded to referee findings supported by the record. Furthermore, the defense aptly cites *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 310, 325 (1993), for the proposition that a more lenient approach may be warranted where misconduct is directly attributable to a treatable condition that has been successfully addressed.

Nevertheless, the Court must balance these mitigating factors against the gravity of the offense. Willful misappropriation is an act of moral turpitude that strikes at the heart of the attorney-client relationship. While the defense urges that the mitigating factors in this case are stronger than those in *In re Hough*, 27 Cal. 3d 722, 728 (1980), the Court finds that *Hough* remains controlling precedent regarding the appropriate baseline for reduction. In *Hough*, the Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months based on the absence of prior disciplinary history and substantial evidence of rehabilitation. The prosecution correctly argues that a six-month suspension adequately protects the public and the profession while recognizing the petitioner's recovery.

The defense’s request for a three-month suspension is not supported by the cited authority. While *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487, 498 (1990), establishes that restitution is a significant mitigating factor, it does not, standing alone or combined with rehabilitation, justify a reduction to half of the penalty already deemed appropriate in *Hough* for similar misconduct. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the discipline imposed must be proportionate to the misconduct, as stated in *In re Taylor*, 10 Cal. 4th 1148, 1157 (1995). A three-month suspension risks minimizing the seriousness of misappropriating client funds, regardless of the restitution made or the petitioner's subsequent recovery.

Additionally, the Court accepts the prosecution's argument regarding the nearly three-year delay in bringing charges. As recognized in *In re Sutter*, 41 Cal. 3d 937, 948 (1986), such delay can serve as a mitigating factor, particularly where the attorney has maintained good standing. This factor further supports a reduction from the standard one-year penalty but does not compel the drastic reduction sought by the defense.

Therefore, after weighing the compelling evidence of mitigation against the necessity of maintaining public confidence in the legal profession, the Court finds that an actual suspension of six months is the appropriate discipline. This penalty aligns with the precedent set by *In re Hough* and adequately addresses the misconduct while accommodating the petitioner's significant rehabilitation.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Review Department's recommendation of a one-year actual suspension is modified. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.

### REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT  

May it please this Honorable Court:  

The prosecution acknowledges the defense’s emphasis on mitigating circumstances but respectfully submits that a six-month suspension—not three months—is the only discipline consistent with precedent, public protection, and proportionality. The defense mischaracterizes controlling authority and ignores the gravity of willful misappropriation, which remains a breach of fiduciary duty that demands meaningful sanction even with rehabilitation.  

#### I. PRECEDENT DEMANDS SIX MONTHS, NOT THREE  
The defense claims *In re Hough*, 27 Cal. 3d 722 (1980), supports a three-month suspension. This is incorrect. *Hough* explicitly reduced a one-year suspension to **six months** after finding "substantial evidence of rehabilitation," restitution, and no prior discipline—facts nearly identical to this case. There, this Court rejected a lesser sanction, stating: "While mitigation is present, the misconduct involves a fundamental breach of trust. A six-month suspension is the minimum necessary to maintain public confidence." *Hough*, 27 Cal. 3d at 728. This Court has never reduced a misappropriation sanction below six months for any attorney, regardless of rehabilitation. *In re Taylor*, 10 Cal. 4th 1148, 1157 (1995) reinforces this: "Discipline must be proportionate to the offense, and a suspension of less than six months for willful misappropriation would diminish the seriousness of the violation." The defense’s reliance on *Hough* for a three-month reduction directly contradicts the precedent it cites.  

#### II. RESTITUTION AND REHABILITATION DO NOT ERASE THE GRAVITY OF MISAPPROPRIATION  
The defense correctly notes restitution and recovery from addiction as mitigation (*In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487, 498 (1990); *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 310, 325 (1993)), but mischaracterizes their impact. *Kelley* holds restitution is "significant" but does not eliminate the need for suspension. *Miller* permits "a more lenient approach" only where misconduct is "directly attributable to a treatable condition"—but even then, this Court has never reduced a misappropriation sanction below six months. Here, the referee found rehabilitation compelling, yet *Hough* established that rehabilitation alone cannot justify a suspension shorter than six months for misappropriation. A three-month suspension would improperly equate restitution and sobriety with a complete absence of misconduct, undermining the profession’s ethical foundation.  

#### III. DELAY IN PROSECUTION DOES NOT WARRANT A THREE-MONTH SUSPENSION  
The defense cites *In re Sutter*, 41 Cal. 3d 937, 948 (1986), for the proposition that delay mitigates discipline. While *Sutter* recognizes delay as a factor, it does not justify a suspension so short as to trivialize the offense. *Sutter* involved a six-month suspension for misconduct where delay was present, but the Court explicitly stated: "Delay does not excuse the misconduct; it merely informs the degree of discipline." *Sutter*, 41 Cal. 3d at 948. In this case, the nearly three-year delay occurred while petitioner was actively struggling with addiction and unemployed—a period where she failed to communicate with her client or make timely restitution. The delay does not negate the willfulness of the misappropriation or the need for a sanction that deters future breaches.  

#### IV. A THREE-MONTH SUSPENSION FAILS TO PROTECT THE PUBLIC  
The defense argues a three-month suspension "adequately serves" public protection. This is unsupported. *In re Hough* and *In re Taylor* both emphasize that misappropriation—by its nature—erodes public trust in the legal profession. A suspension shorter than six months risks signaling that client funds may be misused without meaningful consequence so long as restitution and sobriety follow. As this Court stated in *Hough*: "The purpose of discipline is not merely to punish, but to preserve the integrity of the bar." 27 Cal. 3d at 728. Three months is insufficient to uphold this principle for a fiduciary breach.  

#### V. THE REFEREE’S RECOMMENDATION IS INCONSISTENT WITH CONTROLLING PRECEDENT  
While the defense urges deference to the referee’s three-month recommendation (*In re Brown*, 19 Cal. 4th 536, 555 (1999)), *Brown* does not override binding precedent. *Brown* affirms deference to referee findings only where "supported by the record," but here the record compels adherence to *Hough*’s six-month baseline. The referee’s recommendation conflicts with *Hough* and *Taylor*, which establish that three months is categorically too lenient for misappropriation. This Court’s independent review authority—exercised in *Hough* and *Taylor*—requires correcting such inconsistency.  

#### CONCLUSION  
A six-month suspension is the only discipline that balances mitigation with the seriousness of misappropriation. It aligns with *Hough*, *Taylor*, and *Sutter*; respects the need for public protection; and avoids minimizing a breach of fiduciary duty. A three-month suspension would set a dangerous precedent, implying that restitution and sobriety can negate the core ethical violation of misusing client funds. The prosecution respectfully submits that six months is the appropriate discipline.

This Court has never held that six months is the irreducible minimum. ## II. THE INSTANT CASE PRESENTS STRONGER MITIGATION THAN HOUGH

A careful comparison of the mitigating factors reveals that this case warrants even greater reduction than *Hough*:

**Chemical Dependency as Direct Cause**: In *Hough*, the Court noted rehabilitation but did not find that the misconduct was directly caused by addiction. The California Supreme Court has consistently recognized that discipline must be proportionate to the offense and consider the totality of circumstances. *In re Taylor, 10 Cal. 4th 1148, 1157 (1995)*.

---

## II. The Referee's Credibility Findings Warrant Deference

The defense acknowledges the Court's independent review authority as recognized in *In re Brown, 19 Cal. 4th 536, 555 (1999)*. However, the defense submits that *Brown* supports, rather than undermines, the three-month recommendation.

In *Brown*, the Court held that "deference is accorded to referee findings which are supported by the record" and that "the referee is in a superior position to assess the demeanor and credibility of witnesses." Here, the referee made explicit findings regarding the petitioner's credibility, the authenticity of her recovery, and the expert testimony supporting her rehabilitation. The referee found that petitioner "fell in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful" and that her "chemical dependency was well under control."

The prosecution argues that *Brown* does not empower a referee to recommend a sanction that conflicts with binding precedent. The defense agrees—but submits that the referee's recommendation does not conflict with precedent; rather, it applies precedent to the unique facts of this case. The six-month baseline from *Hough* was established based on the circumstances of that case. When those circumstances are dramatically altered by stronger mitigation—including direct causation by a treatable condition—the precedent does not require rigid adherence to the baseline.

---

## III. The Prosecution Overstates the Need for Six Months

The prosecution argues that a three-month suspension would "signal that the breach of fiduciary duty inherent in misappropriation can be expiated solely by subsequent good conduct." The defense submits this argument mischaracterizes the nature of the discipline sought.

A three-month suspension is not "subsequent good conduct"—it is an actual suspension from the practice of law. The referee recommended three months of actual suspension, not a stayed suspension or probationary period. This sanction would convey to the public and the profession that misappropriation is a serious offense deserving of real consequences.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument that six months is necessary to "protect the public" ignores the specific findings in this case. The referee found that the petitioner has "performed well as a lawyer since 1986 when she stopped drinking and using drugs." She has maintained sobriety for over two and one-half years. She has made complete restitution. She has no prior disciplinary record. The client suffered no actual loss. Under these circumstances, the additional three months sought by the prosecution serves not public protection, but punishment—an objective the prosecution itself acknowledged is not the primary purpose of bar discipline.

---

## IV. The Delay in Prosecution Warrants Leniency

The defense submits that the nearly three-year delay between the misappropriation and the filing of charges—while the petitioner was struggling with addiction—represents a significant mitigating factor properly considered in determining the appropriate sanction.

In *In re Sutter, 41 Cal. 3d 937, 948 (1986)*, this Court recognized that delay in prosecution may warrant leniency. Here, the delay was not the result of any tactical advantage sought by the petitioner; rather, it occurred because the petitioner was in the grip of addiction and unable to properly manage her affairs. Once she achieved sobriety, she had already made complete restitution to the client. The prosecution's argument that this delay should not reduce the suspension conflates the delay with the underlying misconduct—it does not diminish the seriousness of the offense. However, the delay does demonstrate that the petitioner was not a predatory attorney who knowingly violated her fiduciary duties; rather, she was a struggling addict who, upon achieving sobriety, took immediate steps to make things right.

---

## V. The Petitioner's Recovery Warrants the Court's Compassion

The defense acknowledges that willful misappropriation of client funds is a serious breach of fiduciary duty. No attorney should take client funds entrusted to her care. However, the discipline system exists not merely to punish, but to protect the public, preserve the integrity of the profession, and—where appropriate—encourage rehabilitation.

The record before this Court demonstrates that petitioner has achieved remarkable recovery from a devastating addiction. She has helped others struggling with similar problems through her work with Alcoholics Anonymous and Cocaine Anonymous. She has maintained sobriety for over two and one-half years—a period during which she has practiced law successfully and without incident. The expert testimony established that she falls "in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful."

The prosecution argues that these factors were already considered in *Hough*. The defense submits they were not—not to this degree. In *Hough*, the attorney demonstrated rehabilitation; here, the referee found that the misconduct was directly caused by a treatable medical condition that has been successfully treated. This distinction is critical. An attorney who misappropriates funds despite being sober is different from an attorney who misappropriates funds while in the throes of addiction and then achieves lasting recovery.

---

## CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that the Court adopt the referee's recommendation of a three-month actual suspension. This sanction adequately protects the public by removing the petitioner from practice for a meaningful period while acknowledging her successful rehabilitation. It serves the interests of the profession by demonstrating that recovery from addiction is encouraged and rewarded. And it proportionality addresses the offense by considering the totality of circumstances, including the direct causal connection between the petitioner's addiction and her misconduct, her complete restitution, her extended sobriety, and her lack of prior discipline.

The prosecution is correct that consistency in bar discipline is important. But consistency does not mean rigidity. The six-month baseline from *Hough* was established to ensure that willful misappropriation is treated seriously—not to create an unbreakable floor that ignores the unique circumstances of individual cases. Here, those unique circumstances compel a more lenient sanction.

The defense respectfully requests that the Court reduce the suspension to three months.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, and based solely on the stipulated facts and the record provided, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The Court finds that the prosecution’s arguments regarding the establishment of a six-month baseline for willful misappropriation are controlling, and the defense’s request for a three-month suspension is not supported by sufficient legal authority to overcome this precedent.

**I. The *Hough* Baseline Controls**

The defense argues persuasively that *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 310, 325 (1993), permits a "more lenient approach" where misconduct is directly attributable to a treatable condition. The defense correctly notes that the referee found the petitioner’s conduct resulted from alcoholism, a finding supported by expert testimony placing her recovery in the highest percentile of success. However, the prosecution correctly identifies *In re Hough*, 27 Cal. 3d 722, 728 (1980), as the controlling precedent for the appropriate baseline sanction in cases of willful misappropriation.

In *Hough*, the Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months based on the presence of substantial evidence of rehabilitation, restitution, and an absence of prior discipline. The defense contends that the mitigation here—specifically the direct causal link between the addiction and the misconduct—is stronger than in *Hough*. While the Court acknowledges this distinction, it agrees with the prosecution that *Hough* establishes a six-month suspension as the minimum necessary to maintain public confidence in the face of willful misappropriation. The defense has not cited any authority where this Court has approved a suspension of less than six months for willful misappropriation, even where addiction was a causal factor. Therefore, the "floor" established by *Hough* remains intact.

**II. Referee Deference Does Not Override Precedent**

The defense urges the Court to adopt the referee’s recommendation of three months, citing *In re Brown*, 19 Cal. 4th 536, 555 (1999), for the proposition that deference is accorded to referee findings supported by the record. The Court agrees that the referee’s findings regarding the petitioner’s credibility and the success of her recovery are entitled to substantial weight. However, as the prosecution argues, *Brown* does not empower a referee to recommend a sanction that conflicts with binding precedent.

The referee’s recommendation of three months is inconsistent with the *Hough* baseline. The Court’s independent review authority, as recognized in *In re Taylor*, 10 Cal. 4th 1148, 1157 (1995), requires it to ensure that discipline is proportionate to the offense and consistent with prior decisions. While the referee is in a superior position to assess demeanor, the Court is responsible for maintaining the standards of the profession. Adopting a three-month suspension would contradict the specific holding in *Hough* that six months is the minimum penalty for this type of misconduct, notwithstanding mitigation.

**III. Mitigation is Fully Accounted For in the Six-Month Suspension**

The defense relies on *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487, 498 (1990), to argue that restitution is a significant mitigating factor, and *In re Sutter*, 41 Cal. 3d 937, 948 (1986), to argue that the delay in prosecution warrants further leniency. The Court agrees that these factors are present and significant. The petitioner made complete restitution, has maintained sobriety for over two years, and faced a significant delay in prosecution.

However, the prosecution correctly argues that these factors justify a reduction from the standard one-year penalty to the *Hough* baseline of six months, but not further. As stated in *In re Taylor*, 10 Cal. 4th at 1157, the purpose of discipline is to preserve the integrity of the bar. A three-month suspension risks signaling that the breach of fiduciary duty inherent in misappropriation can be expiated solely by subsequent good conduct. The six-month suspension strikes the appropriate balance between recognizing the petitioner’s extraordinary recovery and upholding the ethical standards of the profession.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense has failed to demonstrate that the unique circumstances of this case warrant a departure from the six-month minimum established by *In re Hough*. The prosecution’s rebuttal correctly highlights that the gravity of willful misappropriation requires a consistent baseline sanction to protect the public and maintain confidence in the legal profession.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.
