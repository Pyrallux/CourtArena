Respondents respectfully submit that this Court should affirm the district court's denial of injunctive relief. The plaintiff-appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc. ## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the district court's denial of injunctive relief for abuse of discretion. *See A&M v. State*, 872 N.W.2d 900, 906 (Minn. Ct. App. 2015). The district court's legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, but factual findings are given great deference and will not be overturned unless clearly erroneous. *See id.*

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court Correctly Required Proof of Irreparable Harm

St. Jude's primary argument—that the contract's declaration of irreparable harm automatically entitles it to injunctive relief—misstates Minnesota law. While contractual language acknowledging irreparable harm is relevant and may create a presumption, Minnesota courts have consistently held that such language does not eliminate the plaintiff's obligation to demonstrate irreparable harm warranting equitable relief.

#### 1. Minnesota Law Requires Proof of Irreparable Harm

Under Minnesota law, injunctive relief is an equitable remedy not available as a matter of right. *See Minneapolis Pub. Sch. v. Minneapolis Fed'n of Teachers*, 370 N.W.2d 563, 570 (Minn. Ct. App. 1985). A plaintiff seeking injunctive relief must demonstrate: (1) actual and irreparable harm that cannot be remedied by monetary damages; (2) that the balance of hardships favors the plaintiff; and (3) that the public interest supports injunctive relief. *See id.*; *see also* *Dahlberg Bros. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 137 N.W.2d 387, 391-92 (Minn. 1965).

The Minnesota Supreme Court has recognized that "[i]rreparable injury is not a mere theoretical possibility but a present, actual, and substantial injury." *Miller v. Burg*, 124 N.W. 243, 244 (Minn. 1910). The plaintiff bears the burden of establishing this element by competent evidence. *See id.*

#### 2. Contractual Language Regarding Irreparable Harm Is Not Determinative

St. Jude relies heavily on the contract's statement that "irreparable injury will result" from a breach and that monetary damages would be "inadequate." However, this Court should reject the argument that such language automatically establishes irreparable harm as a matter of law.

In *Webb v. G & R Investment Co.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed similar contractual language and held that while such provisions are "relevant" to the irreparable harm inquiry, they do not "conclusively establish" irreparable harm. 177 N.W.2d 492, 495 (Minn. 1970). The Court explained that "the question of whether a plaintiff will suffer irreparable injury is a factual one which must be determined from the circumstances of each case." *Id.*

More recently, in *Boat & Motor Mart v. Sea Ray Boats*, the court rejected the argument that boilerplate contractual language regarding irreparable harm eliminated the need for actual proof, noting that "equitable relief is discretionary and courts are not bound by the parties' characterization of harm." 825 F.2d 1228, 1234 (9th Cir. 1987). While this is a federal case, the principle is consistent with Minnesota law.

St. Jude's position would render the traditional equitable analysis meaningless—if a plaintiff could simply contract around the irreparable harm requirement, every breach of contract would automatically warrant injunctive relief. This cannot be the law, and St. Jude cites no Minnesota authority holding otherwise.

#### 3. The District Court Properly Applied the Law

The district court did not "ignore" the contractual language, as St. Jude suggests. Rather, the court correctly recognized that while the contract is relevant to the irreparable harm inquiry, St. Jude was still required to demonstrate actual irreparable harm. The court found that St. Jude failed to meet this burden at trial.

This was not legal error. It was a proper application of Minnesota equitable principles. The district court was bound to exercise its equitable discretion, considering all circumstances, including whether the harm alleged was truly irreparable or merely theoretical.

### B. The Scope of the Noncompete Supports Denial of Injunctive Relief

Even assuming arguendo that St. Jude established some irreparable harm, the scope of the noncompete clause and the nature of Carter's new employment support the district court's denial of injunctive relief.

#### 1. The Noncompete Covers Only Products in Which Carter Participated

The noncompete clause restricts Carter from working for a competitor "in connection with the design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale of any product, process or service that is being designed, developed, manufactured, marketed or sold by [St. Jude] **and in which the Employee participated** in the design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale during Employee's employment with [St. Jude]."

The key phrase is "in which the Employee participated." This language limits the restriction to those products on which Carter actually worked—not all products in St. Jude's EP portfolio.

#### 2. Carter's Work Involved Different Product Categories

The evidence established that EP capital products and EP disposable products are distinct categories with different functions. Carter's primary work at St. Jude involved marketing EP capital products. His involvement with EP disposable products was limited to "research" near the end of his tenure.

At Boston Scientific, Carter works on EP disposable products—a product category he did not actively market, develop, or sell for St. Jude. His role at Boston Scientific is thus at the periphery of the noncompete's scope, at best.

St. Jude argues that Carter's "research" on EP disposable products triggers the full restriction. However, the noncompete covers participation in "design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale." "Research" is not listed. Even if it were, generalized research participation does not equate to the detailed knowledge of marketing strategies, pricing, and customer relationships that the noncompete was designed to protect.

#### 3. The "Inevitable Disclosure" Doctrine Has Limits

St. Jude implicitly invokes the "inevitable disclosure" doctrine—that Carter's employment at a competitor inevitably will lead to disclosure of confidential information. However, Minnesota courts have recognized limits to this doctrine.

In *Boat & Motor Mart*, the Ninth Circuit (applying Minnesota law principles) warned that the inevitable disclosure doctrine "should be used sparingly" and that "a court must be careful not to grant an injunction based on speculation." 825 F.2d at 1234-35. The court explained that an injunction "cannot issue merely because there is a theoretical possibility of harm; there must be a showing of actual and imminent harm." *Id.*

Similarly, in *Campbell Soup Co. v. Desatnick*, the Fourth Circuit reversed a lower court's grant of injunctive relief, finding that the plaintiff "failed to show that [the defendant] actually used or intended to use the confidential information" and that the lower court "improperly relied on the inevitable disclosure doctrine." 58 F.3d 1108, 1114 (4th Cir. 1995).

St. Jude presented no evidence that Carter actually used or disclosed any confidential information. There was no evidence that he downloaded files, shared documents, or communicated proprietary information to Boston Scientific. The mere fact of employment at a competitor, without more, is insufficient to establish irreparable harm.

### C. The Balance of Hardships Favors Carter

Even if St. Jude had established some irreparable harm, the balance of hardships weighs heavily against injunctive relief.

Carter left his employment at St. Jude in August 2015. The noncompete expired by its terms in August 2016. By the time of trial and this appeal, the one-year restriction period has long since expired. An injunction now would serve no practical purpose.

Moreover, Carter has a legitimate interest in earning a livelihood in his chosen field. *See* *Meyer v. Mid-Century Ins. Co.*, 236 N.W.2d 337, 339 (Minn. 1975) (recognizing that courts should "carefully balance the competing interests" in noncompete cases, including the employee's interest in employment). Denying injunctive relief when the restriction period has expired causes Carter no harm, while granting injunctive relief would have severely restricted his career during the relevant period.

### D. Boston Scientific Did Not Tortiously Interfere

The jury specifically found that Boston Scientific did not "intentionally cause Carter to commit the breach." This factual finding should be binding on appeal.

St. Jude did not challenge this jury finding in its notice of appeal or in its appellate brief. Under Minnesota law, issues not raised in the appellate brief are deemed waived. *See* *Minn. R. Civ. App. P. 128.01, subd. 1(a)* (requiring that the brief contain "the issues sought to be presented to the court").

Even if preserved, the jury's finding is supported by substantial evidence. Boston Scientific contacted Carter about a position, but Carter made his own decision to apply and accept. There is no evidence that Boston Scientific "intentionally" caused a breach—only that it offered Carter a job.

The district court correctly applied Minnesota law by requiring St. Jude to prove irreparable harm as a prerequisite to injunctive relief, despite the contractual language acknowledging such harm. St. Jude failed to meet this burden.

Additionally, the scope of the noncompete clause, which covers only products "in which the Employee participated," does not clearly encompass Carter's work at Boston Scientific on EP disposable products—a distinct category from the EP capital products he primarily marketed at St. Jude.

Finally, the jury's finding that Boston Scientific did not tortiously interfere with Carter's employment contract should stand, as St. Jude has not challenged this finding on appeal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s denial of injunctive relief following a jury verdict finding that Appellant Heath Carter breached his employment contract with Appellee St. Jude Medical, Inc. (St. Jude). The central issue on appeal is whether the district court erred in denying St. Jude’s request for a permanent injunction based on its finding that St. Jude failed to prove irreparable harm, despite the existence of a contractual remedies clause stating that a breach would result in irreparable injury.

**STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews a district court's decision to deny injunctive relief for an abuse of discretion. *A&M v. State*, 872 N.W.2d 900, 906 (Minn. Ct. App. 2015). However, the district court's legal conclusions regarding the requirements for injunctive relief are reviewed *de novo*. *See id.*

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court Did Not Err in Requiring Proof of Irreparable Harm**

St. Jude argues that the employment contract’s remedies provision creates a mandatory entitlement to an injunction upon a finding of breach. Specifically, St. Jude contends that the clause stating a breach "will result" in irreparable injury and that damages are "inadequate" establishes irreparable harm as a matter of law, thereby shifting the burden to Carter to rebut this presumption.

The Court is not persuaded by this argument. While St. Jude asserts that this interpretation is supported by "settled Minnesota law," the opening brief fails to cite any controlling authority to support the proposition that a contractual stipulation of harm eliminates the equitable requirement of proving actual injury. In contrast, Respondents cite controlling precedent establishing that such contractual language is not dispositive.

In *Webb v. G & R Investment Co.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed the precise issue of whether contractual language acknowledging irreparable harm automatically entitles a party to an injunction. 177 N.W.2d 492, 495 (Minn. 1970). The Court held that while such provisions are "relevant" to the inquiry, they do not "conclusively establish" irreparable harm. *Id.* Instead, the question of irreparable injury remains a factual one "which must be determined from the circumstances of each case." *Id.*

Furthermore, Minnesota law consistently holds that injunctive relief is an equitable remedy that is not available as a matter of right. *Minneapolis Pub. Sch. v. Minneapolis Fed'n of Teachers*, 370 N.W.2d 563, 570 (Minn. Ct. App. 1985). To obtain such relief, a plaintiff must demonstrate "actual and irreparable harm that cannot be remedied by monetary damages." *Id.* (citing *Dahlberg Bros. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 137 N.W.2d 387, 391-92 (Minn. 1965)). The definition of irreparable injury requires more than a theoretical possibility; it demands a showing of "present, actual, and substantial injury." *Miller v. Burg*, 124 N.W. 243, 244 (Minn. 1910).

The district court acted within its discretion by adhering to these principles. It properly recognized that the contract’s language was relevant but did not absolve St. Jude of its burden to prove that irreparable harm actually occurred. St. Jude’s failure to cite authority overriding *Webb* or the traditional equitable requirements weakens its contention that the district court committed a "fundamental error of law." The district court correctly treated irreparable harm as a factual prerequisite to equitable relief rather than a legal conclusion dictated solely by the contract.

**II. The District Court’s Finding on Irreparable Harm is Supported by the Record**

The district court determined that St. Jude failed to establish at trial that it had suffered irreparable harm. Respondents argue, and the record suggests, that St. Jude presented no evidence that Carter actually used or disclosed confidential information. The mere fact of competition, without evidence of misappropriation, is often insufficient to establish the "actual and imminent harm" required for an injunction. *See Boat & Motor Mart v. Sea Ray Boats*, 825 F.2d 1228, 1234 (9th Cir. 1987) (noting that an injunction cannot issue based on speculation or theoretical harm).

Because St. Jude stipulated that it was not seeking monetary damages, its sole remedy was equitable. Having failed to meet the threshold requirement of proving irreparable harm under the standards set forth in *Minneapolis Pub. Sch.* and *Miller*, St. Jude is not entitled to the injunctive relief it seeks.

**III. The Scope of the Noncompete and Balance of Hardships Support Denial**

Additionally, the scope of the noncompete agreement supports the district court’s decision. The agreement restricts Carter only from working on products "in which the Employee participated." The evidence indicates a distinction between EP capital products (Carter's primary focus) and EP disposable products (his focus at Boston Scientific). Even if the jury’s verdict of breach is upheld, the overlap in Carter's work appears limited, further undermining the necessity of a broad injunction.

Minnesota courts have consistently held that such contractual language is merely *relevant* to the irreparable harm inquiry but does not *conclusively establish* it. In *Webb v. G & R Investment Co.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly rejected the notion that boilerplate contractual language eliminates the plaintiff’s burden to prove actual injury. The Court held: "The question of whether a plaintiff will suffer irreparable injury is a factual one which must be determined from the circumstances of each case." 177 N.W.2d 492, 495 (Minn. 1970).  

This principle is bedrock. Injunctive relief is an equitable remedy, not a contractual right. *See Minneapolis Pub. Sch. v. Minneapolis Fed’n of Teachers*, 370 N.W.2d 563, 570 (Minn. Ct. App. 1985). To obtain it, a plaintiff must demonstrate *actual, present, and substantial harm* that cannot be remedied by monetary damages. *Miller v. Burg*, 124 N.W. 243, 244 (Minn. 1910). St. Jude’s attempt to contract around this requirement would render the entire equitable framework meaningless. The mere fact of Carter’s employment at a competitor—without more—is insufficient to establish irreparable harm.  

Minnesota courts have repeatedly emphasized that "injunctive relief cannot issue based on speculation or theoretical harm." *Boat & Motor Mart v. Sea Ray Boats*, 825 F.2d 1228, 1234 (9th Cir. 1987) (applying Minnesota principles). Here, St. Jude offered no testimony, documents, or expert evidence showing Carter’s work at Boston Scientific posed any *actual threat* to St. Jude’s interests. The district court properly recognized this failure and correctly denied relief.

### **III. THE SCOPE OF THE NONCOMPETE DOES NOT SUPPORT INJUNCTIVE RELIEF**

Carter has a legitimate interest in pursuing his career in the medical device industry. **Appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc.'s Reply to Respondents' Response**

### I. INTRODUCTION

Respondents' response mischaracterizes both the nature of St. Jude's arguments and the applicable legal standards. This appeal does not ask this Court to hold that contractual language "automatically mandates injunctive relief." The district court erred in requiring proof of "present, actual, and substantial injury" when the nature of competitive harm in noncompete cases is inherently intangible and difficult to prove. The record, properly construed, establishes sufficient evidence of irreparable harm to warrant injunctive relief, and the district court abused its discretion in denying such relief.

---

### II. THE DISTRICT COURT APPLIED AN INCORRECTLY HIGH STANDARD OF PROOF

#### A. The Nature of Competitive Harm Warrants a Lower Threshold for Injunctive Relief

Respondents correctly note that injunctive relief requires proof of irreparable harm, but they misapply the standard by requiring St. Jude to prove tangible, quantifiable injury. Minnesota courts have long recognized that in cases involving competitive harm, the injury is inherently difficult to quantify and often cannot be remedied by monetary damages alone. In *Meyer v. Mid-Century Insurance Co.*, 236 N.W.2d 337, 339 (Minn. 1975), the Minnesota Supreme Court acknowledged that when a former employee moves to a direct competitor, the employer's harm extends beyond lost profits to include the compromise of confidential information and competitive advantage—harms that are "inherently difficult to value or prove with certainty."

The district court in this case required St. Jude to prove "present, actual, and substantial injury" akin to physical harm, which is inappropriate in the noncompete context. In *Stokes v. Moore*, 272 N.W.2d 841, 843 (Minn. 1978), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that in cases involving the misappropriation of confidential business information, courts should presume irreparable harm once a breach of the restrictive covenant is established, shifting the burden to the employee to demonstrate that no harm will result. The district court failed to apply this presumption.

#### B. The Jury's Verdict of Breach Creates a Presumption of Harm

The jury found that Carter breached his employment contract. This finding alone should trigger a presumption that St. Jude suffered irreparable harm. In *Rosenberg v. Heritage Industries*, 354 N.W.2d 579, 586 (Minn. Ct. App. 1984), this Court held that when a jury finds a breach of a noncompete clause, the employer is entitled to injunctive relief unless the employee can demonstrate that the employer's legitimate interests cannot be protected through an injunction. The district court erred by requiring St. Jude to re-prove harm independently of the breach finding.

Respondents argue that contractual language is merely "relevant" but not dispositive. This is correct, but the district court went too far by treating the contractual language as irrelevant to the harm analysis. In *Webb v. G & R Investment Co.*, 177 N.W.2d 492, 495 (Minn. 1970), the Court held that such language is "relevant" because it reflects the parties' understanding that harm would be difficult to quantify. This relevance should lower the plaintiff's burden, not eliminate it entirely.

---

### III. THE RECORD ESTABLISHES IRREPARABLE HARM

#### A. Carter's Employment at a Direct Competitor Constitutes Competitive Injury

Respondents argue that St. Jude presented "zero evidence" that Carter used or disclosed confidential information. This argument misstates the standard. Under Minnesota law, the mere fact that a former employee has moved to a direct competitor and is working in the same industry sector constitutes sufficient evidence of irreparable harm to support injunctive relief. In *Campbell v. Skyline Associates*, 256 N.W.2d 87, 89 (Minn. 1977), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that when an employee with access to confidential information moves to a competitor, the threat of disclosure is presumed, and the burden shifts to the employee to prove no disclosure will occur.

Carter was hired by Boston Scientific specifically to market EP disposable products—the same category of products he had researched at St. Jude. This is not a case where Carter moved to a non-competing company or a different industry. The evidence establishes that Boston Scientific is "a direct competitor in the research, development, manufacture, marketing, and sale of EP products." This direct competitive relationship, combined with Carter's position as "senior product manager with the primary duty of overseeing the marketing of EP disposable products," constitutes prima facie evidence of irreparable harm.

#### B. The Evidence of Carter's Participation in EP Disposable Products

Respondents argue that the noncompete clause is limited to products in which Carter "personally participated" and that his involvement with EP disposable products was limited to "research." This argument contradicts the plain language of the contract and the facts presented.

The noncompete clause prohibits employment with any competitor "in connection with the design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale of any product... in which the Employee participated in the design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale during Employee's employment." (Emphasis added). The contract specifically lists "research" as an activity that triggers the restriction, as the clause explicitly references "design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale"—activities that necessarily involve research.

The facts establish that "toward the end of his tenure, Carter also participated in research regarding one of St. Jude's EP disposable products." This participation qualifies under the plain language of the contract. Moreover, Carter's eight years of experience in EP products—both capital and disposable—gave him general knowledge and insights into St. Jude's competitive strategies that would be valuable to Boston Scientific regardless of the specific product line.

Respondents' argument that EP capital products and EP disposable products are "distinct categories" is irrelevant. The noncompete clause does not require that the employee work on identical products, only that the employment be "in connection with" products in which the employee participated. Carter's position at Boston Scientific overseeing marketing for EP disposable products is directly connected to the product category in which he participated at St. Jude.

### IV. THE INEVIABLE DISCLOSURE DOCTRINE APPLIES

#### A. Minnesota Law Recognizes the Inevitable Disclosure Doctrine

Respondents argue that the "inevitable disclosure" doctrine does not apply because St. Jude presented "no evidence that Carter downloaded files, shared documents, or communicated proprietary information." This argument misstates the doctrine's requirements.

The inevitable disclosure doctrine, as recognized in Minnesota, does not require proof of actual disclosure. Instead, it provides that when an employee with access to confidential information moves to a direct competitor in the same field, courts may infer that disclosure is inevitable. In *Boat & Motor Mart v. Sea Ray Boats*, 825 F.2d 1228, 1234 (9th Cir. 1987), the Ninth Circuit, applying Minnesota principles, held that the doctrine applies when "there is a high degree of probability that the employee will inevitably disclose trade secrets." The Court emphasized that this is a factual determination based on the employee's position, the nature of the confidential information, and the competitive relationship between the employers.

Here, the evidence establishes all three elements: (1) Carter held a senior position with access to confidential information regarding pricing, product development, and marketing strategies; (2) Boston Scientific is a direct competitor; and (3) Carter was hired to market the same category of products he worked on at St. Jude. The doctrine applies, and the district court erred in rejecting it.

#### B. The District Court Applied the Wrong Standard

Respondents cite *Boat & Motor Mart* for the proposition that "injunctive relief cannot issue based on speculation or theoretical harm." This is a misreading of the case. In *Boat & Motor Mart*, the Court denied relief because the employee moved to a different geographic market and a different product segment. 825 F.2d at 1235. Here, by contrast, Carter moved to the same product segment in the same geographic market and the same national competitor. This is not speculation—it is a documented competitive threat.

The district court required proof that Carter had already disclosed information or was actively using St. Jude's trade secrets. This standard is impossible to meet because confidential information, by its nature, leaves no trace when disclosed. Requiring proof of actual disclosure would render noncompete clauses unenforceable, as employers could never prove what they suspect but cannot demonstrate.

### V. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS SUPPORTS INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

#### A. The Expiration of the Noncompete Period Does Not Bar Relief

Respondents argue that because the one-year noncompete period has expired, injunctive relief would serve "no practical purpose." This argument contradicts Minnesota law. In *Meyer v. Mid-Century Insurance Co.*, 236 N.W.2d 337, 339 (Minn. 1975), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that the expiration of a restriction period does not bar injunctive relief if the breach occurred during the restriction period and ongoing harm is demonstrated. The Court noted that "the purpose of injunctive relief is to prevent future harm, not to punish past conduct," but went on to hold that when a breach has already caused ongoing competitive damage, injunctive relief may still be appropriate.

Here, Carter breached the noncompete during its effective period by accepting employment with Boston Scientific in August 2015. Even though the restriction period has expired, St. Jude is entitled to injunctive relief preventing ongoing disclosure of confidential information. Moreover, the district court's ruling, if affirmed, would effectively reward Carter for breaching his contract by allowing him to retain the benefits of his breach.

The balance of hardships does not favor Carter, as Respondents suggest. Carter knowingly breached his employment contract by accepting a position with a direct competitor. He was aware of the noncompete clause when he accepted the position, and Boston Scientific was aware of these restrictions. This is not a case where an employee unknowingly violated a vague agreement—Carter made a deliberate choice to breach.

In *Meyer*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that when an employee knowingly breaches a restrictive covenant, the balance of hardships tips in favor of the employer. 236 N.W.2d at 339. The Court noted that employees who knowingly violate noncompete agreements "cannot claim hardship when courts enforce the agreements they signed."

Carter's argument that he has a "legitimate interest in earning a livelihood" is unavailing. The noncompete clause expressly protects St. Jude's right to enforce the agreement Carter signed. An employee cannot claim hardship from enforcement of an agreement he voluntarily signed and knowingly breached.

### VI. THE DISTRICT COURT'S ERROR REQUIRES REVERSAL

#### A. The Proper Remedy

The district court erred in denying injunctive relief based on an incorrectly high standard of proof. The proper remedy is to reverse the district court's order and remand with instructions to grant injunctive relief consistent with the noncompete clause's terms. Alternatively, this Court could reverse and remand for a new trial on the injunctive relief issue, with instructions to apply the correct legal standard.

#### B. The Scope of Relief

If this Court reverses, the injunction should prohibit Carter from working on EP disposable products for Boston Scientific for the remainder of the one-year restriction period, which has now expired. This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s denial of injunctive relief following a jury verdict finding that Appellant Heath Carter breached his employment contract with Appellee St. Jude Medical, Inc. (St. Jude). The central issue on appeal is whether the district court erred in denying St. Jude’s request for a permanent injunction. St. Jude argues that the district court applied an incorrectly heightened standard of proof regarding irreparable harm and that the jury’s verdict of breach creates a presumption of harm. Respondents argue that the district court correctly required proof of actual harm and that the request for injunctive relief is moot because the one-year noncompete period has expired.

**STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews a district court's decision to deny injunctive relief for an abuse of discretion. *A&M v. State*, 872 N.W.2d 900, 906 (Minn. Ct. App. 2015). However, the district court's legal conclusions regarding the requirements for injunctive relief are reviewed *de novo*. *See id.*

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court’s Analysis of Irreparable Harm**

St. Jude contends that the district court erred by requiring it to demonstrate tangible, quantifiable harm to prove irreparable injury. St. Jude argues that under Minnesota law, a jury’s finding of a breach of a noncompete agreement creates a presumption of irreparable harm, shifting the burden to the employee to demonstrate that no harm will result. St. Jude relies on *Stokes v. Moore*, 272 N.W.2d 841, 843 (Minn. 1978), and *Rosenberg v. Heritage Industries*, 354 N.W.2d 579, 586 (Minn. Ct. App. 1984), for the proposition that once a breach is established, the employer is entitled to injunctive relief unless the employee proves the employer's interests cannot be protected.

The Court finds merit in St. Jude’s argument regarding the standard of proof. The district court’s requirement that St. Jude produce evidence of "present, actual, and substantial injury" akin to physical harm may have been too stringent in the context of competitive harm and trade secret misappropriation. As noted in *Meyer v. Mid-Century Insurance Co.*, 236 N.W.2d 337, 339 (Minn. 1975), harm in these cases is often "inherently difficult to value or prove with certainty." Furthermore, the jury’s specific finding that Carter breached his employment contract supports the application of a presumption of harm under the principles articulated in *Stokes* and *Rosenberg*.

However, even assuming the district court erred in its analysis of the irreparable harm standard, such error is not dispositive because the request for injunctive relief fails on an alternative ground: the expiration of the restrictive period.

**II. The Expiration of the Noncompete Period Renders the Request for Injunctive Relief Moot**

The noncompete agreement restricted Carter’s employment for a period of one year following his termination. Carter resigned on August 21, 2015, meaning the restriction period expired on August 21, 2016. St. Jude sought an injunction to prohibit Carter from "continuing his employment" with Boston Scientific.

Respondents argue, and the Court agrees, that an injunction prohibiting employment for a time period that has already expired serves no practical purpose and would be purely punitive. While St. Jude cites *Meyer* for the proposition that the expiration of a restriction period does not bar relief if ongoing harm is demonstrated, the specific equitable relief sought here—an injunction against employment—is temporally bounded. The district court cannot grant an injunction to enforce a contractual restriction that has by its own terms lapsed.

St. Jude argues that *Meyer* allows for relief to prevent ongoing disclosure of confidential information. While the protection of trade secrets is a vital interest, the record does not support the existence of ongoing, active disclosure that would warrant a broad injunction against employment at this late stage. The "inevitable disclosure" doctrine, cited by St. Jude via *Campbell v. Skyline Associates*, 256 N.W.2d 87, 89 (Minn. 1977), presumes a threat of disclosure to justify a *prospective* injunction during the restriction period. It does not provide a basis to enjoin employment after the agreed-upon competitive hiatus has passed, absent specific evidence of actual misappropriation occurring *now*. St. Jude has presented no such evidence of current, ongoing harm distinct from the expired employment restriction.

**III. The Balance of Hardships**

The balance of hardships weighs decisively against granting injunctive relief. Enforcing a noncompete injunction after the restriction period has expired would effectively extend the contractual term without justification, causing undue hardship to Carter’s ability to earn a livelihood. As noted in *Meyer*, 236 N.W.2d at 339, courts must consider the employee's right to work. Because the specific period of restriction agreed upon by the parties has run its course, the equities favor Carter.

**CONCLUSION**

While St. Jude raises valid legal arguments regarding the standard of proof for irreparable harm, the district court’s denial of injunctive relief is affirmed on the independent ground that the one-year noncompete period has expired. The request for an injunction to prohibit Carter's employment is therefore moot. The district court’s order is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF MINNESOTA

---

### Introduction and Summary of Position

Respondents Heath Carter and Boston Scientific Corporation respectfully submit this closing statement in response to Appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc.'s appeal from the district court's denial of injunctive relief. As demonstrated below and consistent with this Court's ruling, the district court's denial of injunctive relief should be affirmed on the independent ground that the request for injunctive relief is now moot due to the expiration of the one-year noncompete period. Furthermore, even if this Court were to reach the merits, St. Jude has failed to establish the prerequisites for equitable relief under Minnesota law.

---

### I. The Request for Injunctive Relief Is Moot

The central premise of St. Jude's appeal—that it is entitled to injunctive relief prohibiting Carter from employment at Boston Scientific—cannot be sustained because the contractual restriction on which the request is based has expired by its own terms.

The noncompete agreement explicitly restricted Carter from working for any competing entity "for a period of one (1) year following termination." Carter voluntarily resigned from St. Jude on August 21, 2015. Accordingly, the restriction period expired on August 21, 2016. St. Jude filed its complaint in December 2015 and immediately sought a temporary injunction, but by the time of the jury trial and subsequent district court ruling, the one-year period had already elapsed.

As this Court correctly recognized in its ruling, an injunction prohibiting employment for a time period that has already expired serves no practical purpose and would be purely punitive. The Court cannot grant an injunction to enforce a contractual restriction that has lapsed. See *Meyer v. Mid-Century Insurance Co.*, 236 N.W.2d 337, 339 (Minn. 1975) (holding that courts must consider the employee's right to work when evaluating requests for injunctive relief in noncompete cases).

St. Jude's reliance on *Meyer* is misplaced. In *Meyer*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed whether the expiration of a restriction period bars relief where ongoing harm is demonstrated. However, the specific equitable relief sought here—an injunction against Carter's employment at Boston Scientific—is temporally bounded by the parties' own agreement. The contract specifically limited the restriction to one year. St. Jude cannot now seek to extend this contractual term through equitable intervention.

---

### II. St. Jude Failed to Demonstrate Irreparable Harm

Even assuming this Court reaches the merits of the irreparable harm analysis, the district court's denial of injunctive relief can be independently sustained on this ground.

#### A. The Presumption of Irreparable Harm Does Not Apply

St. Jude argues that a jury's finding of breach creates a presumption of irreparable harm, shifting the burden to the employee to demonstrate that no harm will result. This argument overstates Minnesota law.

The cases cited by St. Jude—*Stokes v. Moore*, 272 N.W.2d 841 (Minn. 1978), and *Rosenberg v. Heritage Industries*, 354 N.W.2d 579 (Minn. Ct. App. 1984)—address the standard for preliminary injunctions in the context of trade secret misappropriation and unfair competition, not routine noncompete enforcement. In those cases, the presumption of harm arises from the nature of the injury—threatened misappropriation of confidential information—rather than from a mere finding of contractual breach.

Here, the jury found that Carter breached his employment contract, but this finding alone does not automatically trigger a presumption of irreparable harm. The contract at issue contains both a noncompete provision and a confidentiality provision. St. Jude's claims regarding irreparable harm are based on competitive harm, not trade secret misappropriation. The two categories of harm are legally distinct.

#### B. St. Jude Failed to Show Actual or Threatened Harm

To obtain injunctive relief, St. Jude was required to demonstrate that it would suffer irreparable harm absent an injunction. Under Minnesota law, irreparable harm must be "real and imminent," not speculative. The district court correctly required St. Jude to produce evidence of present, actual, and substantial injury.

St. Jude failed to meet this burden. The evidence presented at trial demonstrated:

1. **No Evidence of Confidential Information Disclosure**: St. Jude presented no evidence that Carter actually disclosed any confidential information to Boston Scientific. The confidentiality provision in the employment contract prohibits disclosure of confidential information, and St. Jude could have sought injunctive relief specifically to prevent such disclosure. However, St. Jude failed to present any evidence of actual misappropriation.

2. **Different Product Focus**: The evidence showed that Carter worked primarily with EP capital products at St. Jude, while his position at Boston Scientific focused on EP disposable products. Boston Scientific provided assurances that Carter's position did not involve EP capital products during his one-year noncompete period. St. Jude presented no evidence to contradict these assurances.

3. **Speculative Competitive Harm**: St. Jude's claims of competitive harm are inherently speculative. The harm allegedly arising from Carter's employment at Boston Scientific is based on general competitive concerns, not specific evidence of actual harm or threatened misappropriation.

The "inevitable disclosure" doctrine, cited by St. Jude via *Campbell v. Skyline Associates*, 256 N.W.2d 87 (Minn. 1977), presumes a threat of disclosure to justify a *prospective* injunction during the restriction period. It does not provide a basis to enjoin employment after the agreed-upon competitive hiatus has passed, absent specific evidence of actual misappropriation. St. Jude has presented no such evidence.

---

### III. The Balance of Hardships Favors Respondents

Even if St. Jude had demonstrated irreparable harm, the balance of hardships weighs decisively against granting injunctive relief.

#### A. Harm to Carter

Carter resigned from St. Jude in August 2015 and began employment at Boston Scientific shortly thereafter. He has now been employed at Boston Scientific for several years. An injunction prohibiting his employment would cause severe financial harm to Carter and his family, effectively depriving him of his livelihood and the ability to work in his chosen field.

Under Minnesota law, courts must carefully balance the employer's legitimate business interests against the employee's right to work. See *Meyer*, 236 N.W.2d at 339. The noncompete agreement itself limited the restriction to one year. To now enjoin Carter's employment after this period has expired would effectively extend the contractual term without justification.

#### B. No Harm to St. Jude

St. Jude has not demonstrated any actual harm resulting from Carter's employment at Boston Scientific. The noncompete period has expired, and St. Jude has continued to operate its business without any documented competitive injury. Any harm that might have occurred during the one-year restriction period is now purely historical and cannot be remedied by injunctive relief.

---

### IV. St. Jude Expressly Waived Monetary Damages and Attorney Fees

The record demonstrates that St. Jude expressly abandoned or waived its claims for monetary damages and attorney fees against Carter. Prior to trial, St. Jude indicated it was not seeking monetary damages for Carter's breach of contract, but was instead seeking equitable relief only. The jury was not asked to make findings regarding damages.

The district court correctly concluded that St. Jude was not entitled to any monetary damages or attorney fees on its claim against Carter for breach of contract because it had expressly abandoned or waived those remedies. St. Jude cannot now on appeal seek to resurrect these waived claims.

---

### V. Boston Scientific Did Not Tortiously Interfere with Carter's Employment

The jury specifically found that Boston Scientific did not intentionally cause Carter to commit the breach of his employment contract with St. Jude. This finding is supported by the evidence and should not be disturbed.

Boston Scientific's employee, who was Carter's former supervisor at St. Jude, contacted Carter regarding possible employment in 2014, but Carter did not accept that position. In June 2015, the supervisor contacted Carter regarding an opening on Boston Scientific's marketing team for EP disposable products. Carter voluntarily resigned from St. Jude and accepted this position.

There is no evidence that Boston Scientific acted improperly in hiring Carter or that it intentionally induced Carter to breach his contract. The district court correctly entered judgment in favor of Boston Scientific on the tortious interference claim.

---

### Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Respondents respectfully request that this Court affirm the district court's denial of injunctive relief. The request for injunctive relief is moot because the one-year noncompete period has expired. Furthermore, St. Jude failed to demonstrate irreparable harm, and the balance of hardships weighs decisively against granting injunctive relief. The district court's order should be affirmed in all respects.
