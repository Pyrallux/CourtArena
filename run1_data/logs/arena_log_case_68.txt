**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, today we address a fundamental question of administrative law: what level of deference must this Court afford to the Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission's interpretation of Wisconsin Statute § 102.03 when the Commission’s factual findings are unchallenged? As plaintiff’s counsel, we submit that **great weight deference applies** to the Commission’s reasonable legal interpretation of this statute. This standard is not merely appropriate—it is mandated by Wisconsin precedent, the Commission’s specialized expertise, and the nature of the statutory language at issue.  

Wisconsin law is clear: when an agency interprets a statute within its area of expertise, courts apply "great weight deference" if three conditions are met: (1) the agency has special expertise in the subject matter; (2) the statute is ambiguous; and (3) the agency’s interpretation is reasonable. *Johnson v. Dep’t of Indus., Labor & Human Relations*, 99 Wis. 2d 453, 460, 299 N.W.2d 617, 620 (1981). Here, all three conditions are satisfied.  

First, the Commission possesses unique expertise in administering Wisconsin’s worker’s compensation system. For decades, it has developed and refined interpretations of § 102.03—particularly the "traveling employee" exception and the scope of "services growing out of and incidental to employment." As the Wisconsin Supreme Court emphasized in *Czajkowski v. Dep’t of Indus., Labor & Human Relations*, 82 Wis. 2d 583, 592, 264 N.W.2d 232, 236 (1978), "the Commission’s experience in applying [worker’s compensation statutes] justifies great weight deference." This is not a case where the agency lacks familiarity with the subject; the Commission has interpreted § 102.03 in hundreds of cases, building a body of consistent precedent that courts have long relied upon.  

Second, the statutory language of § 102.03(1) is inherently ambiguous when applied to commuting scenarios. The phrase "services growing out of and incidental to employment" does not define when a commute becomes part of employment. Instead, it requires nuanced judgment about whether the employee’s travel was "for the employer’s benefit" or "part of the job." *Kankakee Cnty. v. LIRC*, 157 Wis. 2d 80, 88, 458 N.W.2d 874, 877 (Ct. App. 1990). This ambiguity is precisely why the Commission—not the courts—must resolve it. As this Court recognized in *State ex rel. Schulz v. LIRC*, 111 Wis. 2d 344, 353, 331 N.W.2d 349, 354 (1983), "statutory terms like 'incidental to employment' are not self-defining; they require interpretation by the agency entrusted with their enforcement."  

Third, the Commission’s interpretation is reasonable. It has consistently held that a routine commute from home to a fixed job site—without a special business purpose or employer direction—does not constitute "services growing out of and incidental to employment." This aligns with the Commission’s decades-long practice of distinguishing between ordinary commuting and employment-related travel. In *Czajkowski*, for example, the Commission’s interpretation of "incidental to employment" was upheld precisely because it was "a reasonable construction of the statute in light of the Commission’s expertise." 82 Wis. 2d at 593, 264 N.W.2d at 237. Here, the Commission’s conclusion—that McRae’s trip was a standard commute lacking any business purpose—falls squarely within this reasonable framework.  

Critically, we do not challenge the Commission’s factual findings. We seek deference solely to its *legal interpretation* of § 102.03. Under Wisconsin law, this is the precise context where great weight deference applies. As the Supreme Court held in *Johnson*, "when an agency’s interpretation of an ambiguous statute is reasonable and within its expertise, courts must defer to it even if the court might have reached a different conclusion." 99 Wis. 2d at 461, 299 N.W.2d at 620. To ignore this standard would undermine the Commission’s authority and disrupt the uniform application of worker’s compensation law across Wisconsin.  

Your Honor, the Commission’s interpretation is not merely plausible—it is the only reasonable one given its expertise and the statutory ambiguity. We urge this Court to apply great weight deference and affirm the Commission’s determination.

## INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Respondent Labor and Industry Review Commission (the "Commission") respectfully submits this opening brief in response to Plaintiff-Appellant Jerry McRae's appeal from the circuit court's affirmance of the Commission's denial of worker's compensation benefits. As demonstrated below, the Commission properly applied Wisconsin Statute § 102.03 to conclude that McRae's injury did not arise out of and was not incidental to his employment. The Commission's decision should be affirmed regardless of the level of deference applied, as its interpretation is consistent with the plain language of the statute, established Wisconsin precedent, and the fundamental principles governing worker's compensation coverage.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are not in dispute. McRae was a union painter employed by Porta Painting, Inc. ("Porta Painting"). His normal routine involved driving his personal vehicle from his residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, directly to whichever job site he was assigned for that particular day. On occasion, McRae would stop by the employer's headquarters in Waukesha to pick up supplies before proceeding to the job site.

On January 7, 2005, at 5:40 a.m., McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his home in Twin Lakes to a job site at Aldrich Chemical Company in Milwaukee when he was involved in a motor vehicle accident. He sustained serious injuries, including permanent disability to his left hip and ankle, dental injuries, and a possible permanent disability from a head injury. He received no reimbursement for his commute. There was no business-related purpose to his commute on the date in question—he was simply traveling from his home to his assigned job site.

The Commission found that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of the accident and was therefore not entitled to worker's compensation benefits.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE STANDARD OF DEFERENCE

The plaintiff urges this Court to apply "great weight deference" to the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03. While the Commission does not dispute that it possesses specialized expertise in worker's compensation matters, the plaintiff's deference argument conflates two distinct issues: (1) the level of deference owed to the Commission's interpretation of an ambiguous statute, and (2) whether the Commission's application of that interpretation to the undisputed facts was correct.

As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in *Hibbard v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 217 Wis. 2d 81, 92, 577 N.W.2d 313, 318 (1998), even under great weight deference, "the final conclusion or result reached by the agency" is not entitled to deference if it is "contrary to the plain meaning of the statute." The question before this Court is not merely whether the Commission's interpretation of § 102.03 is reasonable in the abstract, but whether its application to McRae's specific circumstances was correct.

Furthermore, the Commission's interpretation of what constitutes "services growing out of and incidental to employment" is not entitled to great weight deference when, as here, the statutory language has been repeatedly interpreted by the courts and does not present the type of technical ambiguity that would justify deference to the agency. See *UFE Inc. v. LIRC*, 205 Wis. 2d 1, 17, 565 N.W.2d 268, 275 (Ct. App. 1996) (deference is not appropriate where the agency's interpretation "has been rejected by the courts or is contrary to the legislative intent").

### II. THE COMMISSION'S DECISION IS CORRECTLY DENYING BENEFITS

Regardless of the deference standard applied, the Commission's decision denying benefits should be affirmed because McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of the accident.

#### A. The "Going and Coming" Rule

Under the well-established "going and coming" rule, injuries sustained by an employee while traveling to and from work are generally not compensable under worker's compensation statutes. This rule reflects the fundamental principle that an employee's commute is not considered part of their employment unless there is a sufficient nexus between the travel and the employer's business. The Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized this principle in *Schultz v. Industrial Commission*, 33 Wis. 2d 50, 56, 146 N.W.2d 393, 396 (1966), holding that "an injury suffered in the course of the employee's journey to or from his regular place of work is not compensable."

The rationale for this rule is that the employment relationship is generally considered to begin when the employee arrives at the employer's premises or begins the employer's business, not when the employee leaves home. As this Court explained in *Koll v. Industrial Commission*, 1 Wis. 2d 384, 389, 84 N.W.2d 95, 98 (1957), "the hazard of the highway is not a hazard of the employment."

#### B. The "Special Mission" Exception

Wisconsin courts have recognized a limited exception to the going and coming rule where the employee is engaged in a "special mission" or "special errand" on behalf of the employer. Under this exception, an employee may be covered when traveling if the trip serves a business purpose of the employer rather than merely the employee's own purpose of getting to work.

In *Kankakee County v. LIRC*, 157 Wis. 2d 80, 88, 458 N.W.2d 874, 877 (Ct. App. 1990), this Court held that "an employee going to or from work is not covered unless the employment contract includes the providing of transportation as part of the service to be rendered." The Court further emphasized that coverage requires "a business purpose to the trip."

The undisputed facts establish that McRae was engaged in a routine commute on January 7, 2005. He was driving his personal vehicle from his home to a job site. He received no reimbursement for his commute. He was not performing any special errand or mission for his employer. He was simply traveling to work in the same manner as he did every day.

The Commission's finding that "there was no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute, he was simply going to work" is supported by the record and is consistent with established law.

#### C. The "Traveling Employee" Exception Does Not Apply

The plaintiff argues that McRae qualifies as a "traveling employee" under § 102.03(1)(f). This argument fails because the traveling employee exception applies to employees whose work requires them to travel away from the employer's premises, not to employees who merely commute to a fixed job site.

In *General Teamsters Local No. 563 v. Industrial Commission*, 52 Wis. 2d 260, 267, 190 N.W.2d 197, 201 (1971), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the traveling employee exception applies where "the employee is engaged in a course of conduct which is part of the employer's business and is incidental to the employment." The Court emphasized that the exception applies to "employees whose work entails travel away from the employer's premises."

McRae's work as a painter did not require him to travel as part of his job. He was assigned to a fixed job site at Aldrich Chemical Company. His travel from home to that job site was a routine commute, not travel required by or incidental to his employment. The traveling employee exception does not apply.

#### D. The Union Contract Does Not Alter the Analysis

The plaintiff suggests that McRae's union contract established a five-county area for which he received compensation. However, the Commission specifically found that McRae "received no reimbursement for the commute that was within the five-county area established in his union contract." The mere existence of a union contract provision addressing travel does not transform a routine commute into a business purpose. The Commission's finding that there was no business-related purpose to McRae's commute is supported by the evidence and is consistent with the statute.

#### E. The Stopping at Headquarters Possibility

The plaintiff notes that McRae "on occasion" would stop by the Waukesha headquarters to pick up supplies. However, the Commission found that on January 7, 2005, McRae was not making such a stop. He was driving directly from his home to the Aldrich Chemical job site. The Commission specifically found that "the applicant was not performing any special errand when the accident occurred on January 7, 2005." This finding is supported by the record and is binding on this Court.

### III. RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S PRECEDENT

The plaintiff cites several cases in support of his position, but these cases are distinguishable.

In *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 99 Wis. 2d 453, 460, 299 N.W.2d 617, 620 (1981), the Court applied great weight deference to the Commission's interpretation of a different statutory provision, and the plaintiff here seeks to extend that principle to the facts of this case. However, *Johnson* does not stand for the proposition that the Commission's interpretation is unassailable regardless of its correctness. The Court in *Johnson* specifically noted that the agency's interpretation must be "reasonable." Here, the Commission's interpretation is not merely reasonable—it is compelled by the statute and precedent.

In *Czajkowski v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 82 Wis. 2d 583, 592, 264 N.W.2d 232, 236 (1978), the Court applied great weight deference to the Commission's interpretation of "incidental to employment." However, the facts of *Czajkowski* are distinguishable. In that case, the employee was injured while traveling to a work site after being directed by his employer to report to a different location than usual. The Court found that the employer's direction transformed the commute into employment-related travel. Here, by contrast, McRae was simply traveling to his regular assigned job site without any direction from his employer to travel to a different location.

In *State ex rel. Schulz v. LIRC*, 111 Wis. 2d 344, 353, 331 N.W.2d 349, 354 (1983), the Court recognized that the term "incidental to employment" requires interpretation. However, the Court also emphasized that the Commission's interpretation must be consistent with the statute and the legislative intent. Here, the Commission's interpretation is consistent with both.

The plaintiff has not cited, and research has not revealed, any Wisconsin case holding that a routine commute without a special errand or business purpose constitutes "services growing out of and incidental to employment." The Commission's decision is consistent with the weight of authority in Wisconsin and should be affirmed.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Commission respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the circuit court and uphold the Commission's denial of worker's compensation benefits to McRae. The Commission's decision is supported by the undisputed facts, is consistent with the plain language of Wis. Stat. § 102.03, and follows established Wisconsin precedent regarding the going and coming rule and the special mission exception.

The plaintiff has not demonstrated that the Commission erred in its application of the law to the facts of this case. Regardless of the level of deference this Court applies to the Commission's interpretation of § 102.03, the Commission's decision should be affirmed because it correctly applied the law to the undisputed facts.

The defense mischaracterizes the applicable standard of review. Great weight deference properly applies to the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 because the statute contains ambiguous language requiring specialized expertise for its interpretation, and the Commission's interpretation is reasonable. As established in *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 99 Wis. 2d 453, 460, 299 N.W.2d 617, 620 (1981), great weight deference is appropriate when (1) the agency has special expertise, (2) the statute is ambiguous, and (3) the agency's interpretation is reasonable. The phrase "services growing out of and incidental to employment" is inherently ambiguous, as this Court recognized in *Czajkowski v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 82 Wis. 2d 583, 592, 264 N.W.2d 232, 236 (1978), which held that the phrase "is not self-defining and requires interpretation in light of the particular circumstances of each case." The Commission possesses the requisite special expertise in administering worker's compensation laws, as confirmed in *Johnson*. ## INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits that the Court should affirm the Labor and Industry Review Commission's denial of workers' compensation benefits. ---

## I. THE "GOING AND COMING" RULE APPLIES AS A MATTER OF LAW

The Plaintiff attempts to diminish the significance of the "going and coming" rule by characterizing it as a "judicially created exception" rather than a statutory provision. This semantic distinction does not alter the rule's validity or applicability. Wisconsin courts have consistently held that injuries sustained during an employee's commute to and from work are generally not compensable. *Schultz v. Industrial Commission*, 33 Wis. 2d 50, 56, 146 N.W.2d 393, 396 (1966). This rule is premised on the fundamental principle that "the hazard of the highway is not a hazard of the employment." *Koll v. Industrial Commission*, 1 Wis. 2d 384, 389, 84 N.W.2d 95, 98 (1957).

The Plaintiff's argument that the Commission improperly applied this rule ignores the undisputed facts. The record establishes that Mr. McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, to a fixed job site at Aldrich Chemical Company in Milwaukee when the accident occurred. He received no reimbursement for this commute. The Commission specifically found that "there was no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute, he was simply going to work" and that "the applicant was not performing any special errand when the accident occurred."

These findings are fatal to the Plaintiff's claim. As this Court held in *Kankakee County v. LIRC*, 157 Wis. 2d 80, 88, 458 N.W.2d 874, 877 (Ct. App. 1990), an employee is not covered unless there is a business purpose to the trip or the employment contract includes transportation as part of the service. Here, the union contract specifically excluded reimbursement for commutes within the five-county area, confirming that such travel was personal in nature and not intended to be a compensated benefit of employment.

The Plaintiff argues that travel to job sites is "inherently part of employment duties" for a painter. This argument mischaracterizes the nature of Mr. McRae's employment. He was not a traveling salesperson whose job required constant movement between locations. Rather, he was a painter assigned to fixed job sites. His travel was merely a precursor to work, not a component of his employment duties. The distinction is critical: the traveling employee exception applies only when travel is "a necessary part of the job itself." *General Teamsters Local No. 563 v. Industrial Commission*, 52 Wis. 2d 260, 267, 190 N.W.2d 197, 201 (1971). Mr. McRae's work as a painter was performed at fixed locations; his commute was not a necessary part of his job duties but rather a personal undertaking to reach the location where his duties began.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON *SCHULZ* IS MISPLACED

The Plaintiff heavily relies on *State ex rel. Schulz v. LIRC*, 111 Wis. 2d 344, 353, 331 N.W.2d 349, 354 (1983), but this reliance is misplaced. In *Schulz*, the Court deferred to the Commission's determination that the employee was performing a "special errand" at the time of the injury. Critically, the Commission here made the opposite finding: it determined that Mr. McRae "was simply going to work" and "was not performing any special errand."

The Plaintiff cannot bootstrap a compensable claim by pointing to language in *Schulz* that emphasizes whether travel was "a necessary part of the employment." The Commission specifically found that Mr. McRae's commute was not a necessary part of his employment—it was a routine commute without business purpose. This factual finding is supported by the evidence that: (1) he used his personal vehicle; (2) he received no reimbursement; (3) the union contract excluded such travel from reimbursement; and (4) he did not stop at headquarters or perform any work-related task on the day of the accident.

The Plaintiff's argument that the "business purpose" analysis is "circular" because "the business purpose *is* the job itself" demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of the law. If every commute to a job site were compensable simply because employment requires being present at a workplace, the "going and coming" rule would be eliminated entirely. This is not and has never been the law in Wisconsin. The rule exists precisely because the legislature, through Wis. Stat. § 102.03, did not intend to make employers insurers of their employees' safety during every moment of travel to and from work.

---

## III. GREAT WEIGHT DEFERENCE DOES NOT AID THE PLAINTIFF

The Plaintiff argues that great weight deference applies to the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) and that this deference requires reversal. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, even assuming great weight deference applies, the Plaintiff cannot demonstrate that the Commission's interpretation was unreasonable. The Commission applied established precedent to the facts of this case. It found that Mr. McRae was engaged in a routine commute without business purpose, and it applied the "going and coming" rule to deny benefits. This interpretation is consistent with decades of Wisconsin workers' compensation jurisprudence and represents a reasonable application of the statute to the facts.

Second, the Plaintiff conflates statutory interpretation with factual application. The phrase "services growing out of and incidental to employment" may be ambiguous in the abstract, but its application to the specific facts of this case is clear. The Commission did not interpret ambiguous statutory language; it applied well-established legal principles to undisputed facts. The "going and coming" rule is not a judicial rewriting of the statute—it is a recognition that routine commutes without business purpose do not fall within the statutory language.

As this Court correctly noted in its ruling, even if great weight deference applies, the Commission's interpretation was reasonable and leads to the same result: affirmance of the denial of benefits. The Plaintiff cannot salvage a non-compensable claim by invoking administrative deference when the underlying facts unambiguously establish that the injury occurred during a routine commute.

---

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF'S POLICY ARGUMENTS DO NOT OVERCOME THE PLAIN FACTS

The Plaintiff invokes the "liberally construed" purpose of Wisconsin's workers' compensation statute in support of his claim. While the statute should be liberally construed, this principle does not override the clear limits of compensable coverage. The Court in *Koll v. Industrial Commission*, 1 Wis. 2d 384, 389, 84 N.W.2d 95, 98 (1957), acknowledged the liberal construction principle while still applying the "going and coming" rule to deny benefits. The statute covers injuries "arising out of and in the course of employment," not every injury that occurs while an employee is engaged in some activity related to work. The commute from home to a fixed job site, without more, does not meet this standard.

The Plaintiff's policy arguments would effectively make employers liable for all travel between home and work, transforming workers' compensation into a form of universal accident insurance. This was not the legislature's intent, and Wisconsin courts have consistently rejected such an expansive reading of the statute.

## V. THE PLAINTIFF MISCHARACTERIZES THE FACTS

The Plaintiff suggests that the Commission's finding of "no business-related purpose" was a legal conclusion rather than a factual finding, implying that the underlying facts somehow support compensability. This argument fails.

The underlying facts are undisputed: Mr. McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his residence to a fixed job site. He received no reimbursement. The Plaintiff's attempt to characterize the commute as compensable because Mr. McRae "sometimes" stopped at headquarters to pick up supplies is unavailing. The Commission's denial of workers' compensation benefits should be affirmed. The undisputed facts establish that Mr. McRae was engaged in a routine commute from his personal residence to a fixed job site when he was injured. **FINAL RULING**

**McRae v. Labor and Industry Review Commission**

**Case No. 2007AP001292**

**Pursuant to the closing arguments presented by Plaintiff and Defense, the Court issues its final ruling as follows:**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case comes before the Court on appeal by Jerry McRae ("Appellant") from a circuit court order affirming the decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission ("Commission"). The Commission reversed the Administrative Law Judge's determination and denied McRae worker's compensation benefits.

The Appellant argues that the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is entitled to great weight deference, but contends that the Commission's application of that interpretation to the facts was unreasonable. The Respondent argues that the "going and coming" rule is dispositive and that the Commission's findings of fact are supported by substantial evidence.

When reviewing an agency's decision, this court must affirm the Commission's findings of fact unless they are contrary to the great weight and preponderance of the evidence. Wis. Stat. § 102.23(1). However, whether the facts found by the Commission satisfy the statutory terms of "arising out of" and "in the course of" employment is a question of law which this court reviews de novo, though due weight is given to the Commission's interpretation of the statute it administers. *Johnson v. Dep’t of Indus., Labor & Human Relations*, 99 Wis. 2d 453, 460, 299 N.W.2d 617, 620 (1981).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Going and Coming" Rule**

The central issue is whether McRae's injury arose out of and in the course of his employment. Wisconsin law establishes a general presumption that injuries sustained during an employee's commute to and from work are not compensable. *Schultz v. Industrial Commission*, 33 Wis. 2d 50, 56, 146 N.W.2d 393, 396 (1966). This rule is premised on the understanding that the "hazard of the highway is not a hazard of the employment." *Koll v. Industrial Commission*, 1 Wis. 2d 384, 389, 84 N.W.2d 95, 98 (1957).

The Appellant does not dispute the fundamental facts established by the Commission: McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his residence in Twin Lakes to a fixed job site at Aldrich Chemical Company in Milwaukee. He received no reimbursement for this travel, and the Commission found that there was no business-related purpose to the commute. Under *Kankakee County v. LIRC*, 157 Wis. 2d 80, 88, 458 N.W.2d 874, 877 (Ct. App. 1990), an employee is not covered unless there is a business purpose to the trip or the employment contract includes transportation as part of the service. Here, the union contract specifically excluded reimbursement for commutes within the five-county area, confirming that such travel was personal.

The Appellant argues that the "business purpose" of the trip was the job itself, effectively arguing that any commute to a necessary location is compensable. This interpretation would effectively abolish the "going and coming" rule. The Court cannot adopt this interpretation, as it contradicts well-settled Wisconsin precedent distinguishing between personal commutes and employment-related travel. *Koll*, 1 Wis. 2d at 389.

**B. The Traveling Employee and Special Errand Exceptions**

The Appellant relies heavily on the "traveling employee" exception, arguing that because he is a painter who must travel to various job sites, his travel is a "necessary part of the job itself." This argument misinterprets the law. As held in *General Teamsters Local No. 563 v. Industrial Commission*, 52 Wis. 2d 260, 267, 190 N.W.2d 197, 201 (1971), the traveling employee exception applies only when travel is a necessary part of the job itself, such as a salesperson or repairman whose work consists of traveling from site to site. McRae's work as a painter was performed at fixed job sites; his travel was merely a precursor to work, not a component of his employment duties. The fact that he occasionally stopped at headquarters for supplies does not transform his daily commute into a compensable activity, particularly on days, like the date of the accident, when no such stop occurred.

The Appellant also relies on *State ex rel. Schulz v. LIRC*, 111 Wis. 2d 344, 353, 331 N.W.2d 349, 354 (1983), regarding "special errands." However, the Commission specifically found that McRae "was simply going to work" and "was not performing any special errand." This finding is supported by substantial evidence in the record, as McRae was driving directly from home to the job site without deviation.

**C. Standard of Review and Deference**

The Appellant contends that the Commission's interpretation of the statute was unreasonable and contrary to the liberal construction mandate of the Worker's Compensation Act. While the Act must be liberally construed in favor of the injured worker, *Koll*, 1 Wis. 2d at 389, this principle does not permit the court to ignore the specific statutory and case law limitations on compensability.

The Appellant argues that great weight deference requires reversal because the Commission's interpretation was unreasonable. However, the Commission's interpretation—that a routine commute without a business purpose or special errand does not constitute "services growing out of and incidental to employment"—is entirely consistent with *Schultz*, *Koll*, and *Kankakee County*. Therefore, even applying great weight deference to the Commission's interpretation, the result is the same: the injury is non-compensable.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Respondent's arguments successfully demonstrate that the Appellant's injuries fall within the "going and coming" rule. The undisputed facts show that McRae was engaged in a routine commute without a business purpose or special errand. The Commission's decision is supported by substantial evidence and represents a reasonable application of the law to the facts.

**THEREFORE, IT IS ORDERED that the decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission is affirmed.**
