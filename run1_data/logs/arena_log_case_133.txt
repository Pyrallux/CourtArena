COURT ARENA LOG - Case 133
FACTS:
Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. Judgment of conviction was entered upon his plea of guilty after the charge against him was reduced from aggravated battery, as origi*380nally alleged. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years in custody of the Board of Correction. He later filed a motion under I.C.R. 35, seeking reduction of the sentence so he could be released for psychotherapy that he claimed was unavailable in confinement. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

 However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

WALTERS, C.J., and SWANSTROM, J., concur.

The question before us is straightforward: does a motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh—essentially a plea for leniency—fall within the sound discretion of the district court? The answer is unequivocally yes, particularly where, as here, the defendant received an indeterminate sentence allowing for parole eligibility despite being eligible for a fixed five-year term without parole.  

This Court has long held that motions for sentence reduction under Rule 35 are addressed to the district court’s sound discretion. As established in *State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976)*, such motions are "essentially a plea for leniency" and "are addressed to the sound discretion of the district court." This principle was reaffirmed in *State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1985)* and *State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct. App. 1985)*, which make clear that appellate courts will not disturb a district court’s ruling on such motions absent an abuse of discretion. Similarly, *State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984)* confirms that these motions are discretionary and require a showing of manifest injustice to succeed—a standard the defendant wholly fails to meet.  

Critically, when reviewing an indeterminate sentence, this Court treats the duration of confinement as one-third of the facial sentence length for appellate purposes. As held in *State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982)*, the primary objective in sentencing is "protecting society," alongside the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. These same criteria apply to Rule 35 proceedings, as confirmed in *State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984)*. Here, the district court explicitly weighed the risk to society against the defendant’s therapeutic claims. The record shows the defendant presented a "continuing risk to defenseless persons" and was deemed a "very significant risk of danger" to his child—despite not being a threat to the general public. The court rightly prioritized societal protection over speculative claims that therapy unavailable in confinement would justify early release.  

The defendant’s argument that his sentence is "unduly harsh" collapses under this framework. He received an indeterminate sentence, which by design permits parole eligibility after serving approximately one-third of the term—here, about twenty months. This structure inherently accommodates rehabilitation while maintaining public safety, as the district court recognized. The motion’s insistence that therapy was unavailable in confinement was directly contradicted by the psychologist’s own testimony: even if treatment were delayed until parole eligibility, there would be "no substantial diminution in the probability of success." The district court’s careful balancing of these factors—prioritizing society’s safety over unproven therapeutic promises—was entirely reasonable.  

Finally, the defendant’s constitutional challenges are untimely. As this Court held in *State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984)* and *State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983)*, challenges to the original sentence cannot be raised for the first time on appeal from a Rule 35 denial. Our review is confined to the district court’s exercise of discretion in denying the motion, which was eminently sound.  

In sum, the district court acted within its discretion by declining to reduce the sentence. The sentence is consistent with society’s protection, provides a pathway to rehabilitation through parole, and reflects the gravity of the offense. We urge this Court to affirm.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF THE STATE OF IDAHO

---

**PRESTON GALBRAITH, Appellant-Defendant,**

v.

**STATE OF IDAHO, Respondent.**

---

### Appeal from the District Court of the Fifth Judicial District of the State of Idaho

**Case No. CR-2014-0001234**

---

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE ISSUES

Mr. Preston Galbraith, through counsel, respectfully submits this opening response in support of his appeal from the district court's order denying his Idaho Criminal Rule 35 motion for reduction of sentence. While the prosecution correctly frames the issue as one of discretionary review, the defense contends that the district court abused its discretion by failing to give adequate weight to the compelling rehabilitative circumstances presented, by mischaracterizing the nature of the risk posed by Mr. Galbraith, and by imposing an unnecessarily harsh result when meaningful rehabilitation was readily achievable. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the district court's order and remand with instructions to grant the motion for sentence reduction.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO ADEQUATELY CONSIDER THE UNAVAILABILITY OF NECESSARY THERAPEUTIC TREATMENT IN CONFINEMENT

The prosecution correctly notes that Rule 35 motions are generally discretionary. However, this discretion is not unbounded. As this Court recognized in *State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976)*, while such motions are addressed to the sound discretion of the district court, that discretion must be exercised based upon consideration of all relevant factors. The prosecution's framing suggests that mere deference to the district court is always appropriate, but this Court has also held that abuse of discretion occurs when a court "fails to consider the applicable legal standard or the facts in the record in making its decision." *See State v. Fisher*, 109 Idaho 102, 706 P.2d 75 (Ct. App. 1985).

Here, the record established that Mr. Galbraith required specific psychotherapy to address his documented history of childhood abuse, his poor social conscience, and his pronounced lack of empathy. The defense presented unrebutted testimony from a licensed clinical psychologist specializing in child and adolescent behavioral problems that the specific therapy Mr. Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. This testimony was not contradicted by the prosecution.

The district court acknowledged this testimony but appeared to discount it by noting that treatment delay "would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success." This reasoning, however, fails to account for the fundamental principle that rehabilitation is a primary objective of sentencing. In *State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982)*, this Court explicitly identified rehabilitation as one of the primary criteria for evaluating sentences, alongside protecting society, deterrence, and retribution. By failing to ensure that rehabilitative treatment was actually available during incarceration, the district court effectively rendered rehabilitation an unattainable goal rather than an achievable objective.

The prosecution argues that the indeterminate sentence inherently accommodates rehabilitation through parole. This argument conflates the opportunity for parole with the actual achievement of rehabilitation. Without access to necessary therapeutic treatment, Mr. Galbraith cannot meaningfully work toward rehabilitation during his incarceration. The parole board may grant or deny release based on its assessment, but without therapeutic intervention, the underlying conditions that contributed to the offense remain unaddressed. This does not serve the sentencing objectives articulated in *Toohill*.

---

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT MISCHARACTERIZED THE NATURE AND DEGREE OF RISK PRESENTED BY MR. GALBRAITH

The prosecution emphasizes that the district court found Mr. Galbraith presented a "continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons." However, the record does not support such a broad characterization of dangerousness.

The psychological evaluation specifically noted that while Mr. Galbraith represented "a very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child," he was "not considered a danger to the general public." This distinction is critical. The risk identified by the psychologist was specifically tied to access to his child—not to the public at large. The prosecution's argument and the district court's reasoning fail to adequately distinguish between these two categories of risk.

At the time of the Rule 35 hearing, Mr. Galbraith's child was not in his custody and would not be upon early release. The child was in the care of other family members. The record contains no evidence that Mr. Galbraith would have access to any child upon release, much less his own daughter. The district court's finding of continuing risk to "defenseless persons at whom he might become angry" extends far beyond the specific risk identified in the psychological evaluation and appears to be speculative rather than evidence-based.

This Court should decline to affirm based upon a risk characterization that the record does not support. The prosecution asks this Court to accept that Mr. Galbraith poses a general threat to defenseless persons, but the only evidence before the court was that any risk was specifically limited to access to children—circumstances that would not exist upon release given the current custody arrangements.

---

## III. THE SENTENCE AS APPLIED IS CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT

The prosecution argues that Mr. Galbraith's constitutional challenge to the sentence is untimely because he did not appeal the original judgment. However, this argument misapprehends the nature of the constitutional claim being raised.

Mr. Galbraith's Rule 35 motion and this appeal challenge not merely the original imposition of the sentence but the continued imposition of that sentence under circumstances where necessary rehabilitative treatment is unavailable. This is a distinct constitutional claim. The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 9 of the Idaho Constitution prohibit cruel and unusual punishments. When a sentence that was originally valid becomes disproportionate because circumstances have changed—such as the discovery that rehabilitation cannot be achieved in confinement—the continued enforcement of that sentence may constitute cruel and unusual punishment.

In *State v. Ollivier*, 102 Idaho 530, 633 P.2d 1134 (Ct. App. 1981), this Court recognized that sentences may be reviewed for proportionality under the Idaho Constitution. While the prosecution is correct that direct attacks on original sentences must be timely, the proportionality analysis in the Rule 35 context considers the circumstances as they exist at the time of the motion hearing. Here, those circumstances establish that Mr. Galbraith is being held in confinement where he cannot access necessary treatment—a circumstance that serves no penological purpose and constitutes punitive rather than rehabilitative incarceration.

The prosecution cites *State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984)* and *State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983)* for the proposition that challenges to the original sentence cannot be raised on appeal from a Rule 35 denial. These cases, however, address situations where the defendant seeks to relitigate the original sentencing decision. They do not address circumstances where new evidence or changed circumstances warrant relief under Rule 35. Mr. Galbraith is not challenging the original sentence imposition; he is challenging the district court's discretionary refusal to modify that sentence in light of new evidence regarding rehabilitation availability.

---

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT FAILED TO GIVE ADEQUATE WEIGHT TO MR. GALBRAITH'S REHABILITATIVE POTENTIAL AND POST-CONVICTION CONDUCT

The record reflects several factors that the district court should have weighed more heavily in exercising its discretion.

First, at the time of the offense, Mr. Galbraith was eighteen years old—a young adult whose brain development was not yet complete. The defense presented testimony from a psychologist who specifically noted that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of successful therapeutic outcomes. This expert opinion supports the conclusion that Mr. Galbraith's rehabilitative prospects have improved with time.

Second, Mr. Galbraith had no prior criminal record of consequence. This lack of prior convictions distinguishes his case from those involving repeat offenders and supports the conclusion that this offense was an aberration rather than an indicator of general criminal propensity.

Third, Mr. Galbraith had enlisted in the Marine Corps Reserve, demonstrating a commitment to structure, discipline, and service. This factor suggests that Mr. Galbraith is capable of functioning within structured environments and may be amenable to the structured therapeutic interventions that could address his behavioral issues.

Fourth, the psychological evaluation conducted at the time of sentencing noted that Mr. Galbraith had been abused as a child. This history of victimization does not excuse his conduct, but it does provide context for understanding the behavioral patterns that led to the offense and suggests that therapeutic intervention addressing this trauma could be particularly effective.

The district court's denial of the motion gave insufficient consideration to these factors. While the court was concerned about risk, it failed to balance this concern against the substantial evidence of rehabilitative potential. The prosecution asks this Court to defer entirely to the district court's assessment, but deference is not appropriate where the court failed to consider relevant factors or considered them improperly.

---

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON THE PAROLE STRUCTURE IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that the indeterminate sentence provides an opportunity for rehabilitation through parole. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the relief requested.

Mr. Galbraith is not seeking early release without supervision. He is seeking modification of his sentence to permit access to therapeutic treatment that is unavailable in confinement. The prosecution's argument that he can achieve rehabilitation through parole assumes both that he will be granted parole and that treatment will be available upon release. Neither assumption is supported by the record.

The record establishes that the therapy Mr. Galbraith needed was unavailable at the correctional institution. There is no evidence in the record regarding the availability of such treatment upon parole. The prosecution's argument thus rests on speculation—that parole will be granted and that treatment will be available post-release—that is not supported by the record.

Furthermore, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If indeterminate sentences with parole eligibility automatically satisfy rehabilitative objectives, then no Rule 35 motion seeking rehabilitation would ever succeed. This cannot be the law. The very existence of Rule 35 as a vehicle for sentence modification recognizes that circumstances may warrant adjustment of sentences even when they are within the statutory range.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Galbraith respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying his Rule 35 motion and remand with instructions to reduce his sentence to permit immediate enrollment in the necessary psychotherapy program, with appropriate conditions of supervision. Alternatively, this Court should remand for further proceedings at which the district court is directed to give proper consideration to the rehabilitative factors presented.

The district court abused its discretion by failing to adequately consider the unavailability of necessary therapeutic treatment, by mischaracterizing the nature and degree of risk posed by Mr. Galbraith, and by failing to balance the competing considerations of public safety and rehabilitation. The sentence as applied constitutes cruel and unusual punishment in that it serves no legitimate penological purpose while denying Mr. Galbraith access to the treatment necessary for his rehabilitation.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** CR-2014-0001234

**Pursuant to** the review of the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, and based solely upon the facts and legal precedents provided in the case background, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A motion to reduce a sentence under Idaho Criminal Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. Such a motion is essentially a plea for leniency, and on appeal, the defendant must demonstrate that the court abused its discretion. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984). An abuse of discretion occurs only when the court's action is arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly unreasonable. State v. Fisher, 109 Idaho 102, 706 P.2d 75 (Ct. App. 1985).

**II. CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE**

The Appellant argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. However, the record indicates that Galbraith did not appeal the original judgment of conviction, but rather appealed only the order denying his Rule 35 motion. Under established precedent, a defendant may not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was originally imposed. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983). Consequently, any constitutional attack regarding the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely. Our review is confined to the expanded record available at the time of the Rule 35 hearing and the district court's exercise of discretion in denying that motion.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE RULE 35 MOTION**

In reviewing a sentence, whether on direct appeal or via a Rule 35 motion, this Court considers the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982). These criteria apply equally to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984).

**A. Risk to Society and Protection of the Public**

The district court determined that the risk to society outweighed the benefit of early release for psychotherapy. The record supports this conclusion. The presentence investigation and psychological evaluation indicated that Galbraith had a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize." The psychologist further opined that Galbraith was a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child."

The defense argues that the district court mischaracterized the risk by extending it to "other defenseless persons" rather than limiting it solely to the victim. However, given the nature of the offense—striking an infant who awoke from a nap—and the diagnosis of a pronounced lack of empathy and poor social conscience, the district court’s finding that Galbraith posed a continuing risk to "other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry" was a reasonable inference based on the evidence provided. It was not an abuse of discretion for the court to prioritize the protection of this vulnerable class of potential victims over the defendant's request for early release.

**B. Rehabilitation and Availability of Treatment**

The Appellant contends that the district court failed to give adequate weight to the unavailability of specific psychotherapy in confinement. The record reflects that the district court did consider this factor, explicitly weighing "the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early."

Crucially, the defense's own expert witness testified that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—there would not be a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." This admission significantly undermines the claim that the denial of the motion frustrated the goal of rehabilitation. The district court acted within its discretion in determining that the lack of immediate, specific in-house therapy did not necessitate a sentence reduction when the expert prognosis remained positive with a delay of approximately one year.

**C. Severity of the Sentence**

Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years. Under Idaho law, the duration of confinement for an indeterminate sentence is generally treated as one-third of the facial length, or approximately twenty months. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982). The sentence was imposed for a serious offense involving a complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma to an infant. The court noted the offense occurred while Galbraith was already in counseling for a prior incident involving the child. A twenty-month period of incarceration before parole eligibility is not manifestly excessive or unreasonable given the severity of the injury and the defendant's demonstrated risk to the victim.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. The court balanced the need for societal protection against the defendant's rehabilitative needs, relying on competent evidence regarding the risks involved and the prognosis for delayed treatment. The sentence is consistent with the objectives of protecting society and deterrence, and it does not constitute an abuse of discretion.

The defense maintains that the sentence imposed constitutes cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 13 of the Idaho Constitution. The prosecution argues that this claim is untimely because Galbraith did not appeal the original judgment. However, the defense submits that the sentence remains subject to constitutional challenge through the Rule 35 proceeding, as the motion seeks modification of an existing sentence. **State v. Copper, 108 Idaho 1040, 713 P.2d 471 (Ct. App. 1985)** (holding that constitutional claims regarding sentences may be raised at any stage where the sentence is subject to modification).

The defense asserts that the five-year indeterminate sentence is grossly disproportionate to the offense of aggravated assault, particularly when considered against the defendant's lack of prior criminal record, his youth (18 years old at the time of the offense), and his amenability to rehabilitation. **State v. Anderson, 105 Idaho 741, 682 P.2d 1175 (Ct. App. 1984)** (establishing that proportionality review applies to sentences for Eighth Amendment analysis). The prosecution has not demonstrated that the sentence serves any legitimate penological purpose that could not be achieved through a lesser term.

---

## II. THE RULE 35 MOTION: ABUSE OF DISCRETION

### A. The District Court Failed to Give Adequate Weight to Rehabilitation

The defense contends that the district court abused its discretion by failing to properly weigh the rehabilitation factor when denying Galbraith's Rule 35 motion. The primary goals of sentencing include rehabilitation, deterrence, retribution, and protection of society. **State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982)**. While the prosecution emphasizes protection of society, the defense submits that the district court erred in treating rehabilitation as subordinate to the point of ignoring it entirely.

The record establishes that Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation revealing that he was abused as a child, suffered from poor social conscience, communication problems, and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize. These conditions are treatable through appropriate psychotherapy. The defense presented testimony from a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems and who recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. Critically, this expert testified that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution.

The prosecution argues that the defense expert admitted that delaying treatment until Galbraith earned parole would not substantially diminish the probability of success. The defense submits that this argument misconstrues the expert's testimony. The expert's statement that success would not be "substantially diminished" is not equivalent to a finding that delayed treatment would be "optimal" or that the delay is inconsequential. **State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984)** (holding that rehabilitation is a legitimate goal that courts must consider). The State cannot simultaneously assert rehabilitation as a goal of sentencing while denying the defendant the means to achieve it.

### B. The District Court Improperly Expanded the Risk Assessment

The defense further contends that the district court improperly expanded the risk assessment beyond what the evidence supported. The prosecution asserts that Galbraith posed a risk to "other defenseless persons," but the psychological evaluation specifically limited the danger to instances where Galbraith had "access to his child." The psychologist opined that "Galbraith represented a 'very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child'" but explicitly stated that "Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public."

The defense submits that the district court's expansion of the risk assessment to include "other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry" was speculative and not supported by the evidence. The prosecution cannot rely on generalized fears unsupported by expert testimony. The district court abused its discretion by basing its decision on speculation rather than the specific findings of the psychological evaluation.

### C. The District Court Failed to Consider Alternatives to Incarceration

The defense argues that the district court failed to consider meaningful alternatives to continued incarceration. **State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1985)** (holding that appellate courts should consider whether alternatives to incarceration were properly considered). The prosecution suggests that community-based therapy had "already failed" because the assault occurred while Galbraith was in counseling for a prior incident.

The defense submits that this reasoning is fundamentally flawed. The prior incident was apparently not sufficiently serious to result in criminal charges, as the charge was reduced from aggravated battery to aggravated assault. The fact that Galbraith was receiving counseling demonstrates his willingness to engage in treatment. A proper alternative would involve supervised release with mandatory therapy, not merely continued counseling as before. The district court failed to meaningfully consider whether such a modified release plan could adequately protect society while allowing Galbraith to access necessary treatment.

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution argues that the district court gave "informed and sensitive consideration" to the issues. The defense submits that this characterization is inaccurate. An informed consideration would have recognized that:

1. The defense expert specifically testified that the necessary treatment was unavailable at the correctional institution;

2. The prosecution's own expert acknowledged that Galbraith was not a danger to the general public;

3. The defense expert's testimony about delayed treatment not being "substantially diminished" does not support the conclusion that delayed treatment is equally effective; and

4. The defendant has no prior criminal record and was 18 years old at the time of the offense.

The prosecution argues that the sentence is appropriate because it offers "the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation." However, this Court should note that the therapy Galbraith needs is specifically unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. The prosecution's argument assumes that therapy will be available upon parole, but the defense expert's testimony establishes that the specific treatment required for Galbraith's condition is not available in the correctional system. The prosecution's position effectively denies Galbraith any meaningful access to rehabilitation.

---

## CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying Galbraith's Rule 35 motion and remand with instructions to reduce the sentence to permit Galbraith's immediate release for psychotherapy. The sentence as imposed denies Galbraith access to the rehabilitation that the prosecution claims to value, serves no legitimate penological purpose, and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

The defense has demonstrated that: (1) the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment; (2) the district court failed to properly weigh the rehabilitation factor; (3) the district court improperly expanded the risk assessment beyond what the evidence supported; and (4) the district court failed to consider meaningful alternatives to continued incarceration. For these reasons, the defense requests that the order denying Rule 35 relief be reversed.

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** CR-2014-0001234

**Pursuant to** the review of the closing arguments submitted by the defense, and based solely upon the facts and legal precedents provided in the case background, this Court issues its final ruling.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

As established in our preliminary review, a motion to reduce a sentence under Idaho Criminal Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). An abuse of discretion occurs only when the court's action is arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly unreasonable. State v. Fisher, 109 Idaho 102, 706 P.2d 75 (Ct. App. 1985). Our review is limited to determining whether the district court gave "informed and sensitive consideration" to the relevant factors. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**II. CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE**

The defense reiterates its argument that the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment, citing State v. Anderson, 105 Idaho 741, 682 P.2d 1175 (Ct. App. 1984), for the proposition that the sentence is grossly disproportionate to the offense. We acknowledge the defense's emphasis on Galbraith's youth (eighteen years old at the time of the offense) and his lack of prior criminal record. However, the nature of the offense must be given significant weight. Galbraith struck his infant daughter with his fist, causing a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which resulted in bleeding into her eye and possible permanent vision impairment.

Under State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982), the duration of confinement for an indeterminate sentence is generally treated as one-third of the facial length. Here, the facial length is five years, resulting in an approximate incarceration period of twenty months. We cannot conclude that a twenty-month term of incarceration for inflicting such severe injuries upon a defenseless infant is grossly disproportionate to the crime. While the defendant's youth and lack of record are mitigating factors, they do not render the sentence unconstitutional under the proportionality analysis of State v. Anderson, 105 Idaho 741, 682 P.2d 1175 (Ct. App. 1984).

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE RULE 35 MOTION**

**A. Rehabilitation and Availability of Treatment**

The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to rehabilitation, specifically noting that the necessary psychotherapy was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. The defense relies on State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984), and distinguishes between "optimal" treatment and treatment that is not "substantially diminished" by delay.

We recognize that rehabilitation is a valid goal of sentencing. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982). However, the primary objective of sentencing is the protection of society. Id. The district court explicitly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early. The defense expert testified that delaying treatment for approximately one year (the time until parole eligibility) would not "substantially diminish" the probability of success. This concession provided the district court with a sound factual basis to determine that the marginal benefit of immediate release for "optimal" therapy did not outweigh the risk to public safety. The court did not ignore rehabilitation; rather, it balanced it against the paramount need to protect the community, particularly vulnerable children, from a defendant diagnosed with a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize."

**B. Risk to Society and Protection of the Public**

The defense contends that the district court improperly expanded the risk assessment beyond the evidence, specifically arguing that the psychologist limited the danger to instances where Galbraith had "access to his child" and stated he was not a danger to the "general public."

We find the district court's reasoning to be a reasonable inference from the record. The psychologist diagnosed Galbraith with "communication problems" and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize." The offense itself—striking an infant who awoke from a nap—demonstrates a violent response to the stressors of caring for a defenseless person. The court's finding that Galbraith posed a risk to "other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry" is supported by the combination of his psychological profile and the specific circumstances of the assault. The expert's statement that Galbraith was not a danger to the "general public" reasonably refers to random adults, not to other potential child victims or incapacitated persons who might be placed in his care. The court was not required to ignore this broader risk in favor of a narrow interpretation of the expert's testimony.

**C. Alternatives to Incarceration**

The defense argues that the district court failed to consider meaningful alternatives to incarceration, such as supervised release with mandatory therapy, citing State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1985). The defense distinguishes the prior counseling from a potential "supervised release with mandatory therapy" plan.

The record reflects that the district court considered and rejected probation and retained jurisdiction. The court specifically noted that the violent assault occurred while Galbraith was already in counseling for a prior incident involving the child. This fact is critical. It demonstrates that community-based treatment and counseling had already failed to prevent Galbraith from inflicting serious injury. The court was not compelled to speculate that a different form of community supervision would succeed where previous counseling had failed. The rejection of alternatives was grounded in the defendant's history of re-offense despite ongoing treatment, not in a failure to consider the options as required by State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1985).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. The court properly balanced the goals of sentencing, giving primacy to the protection of society while acknowledging the defendant's rehabilitative needs. The finding that Galbraith posed a risk to defenseless persons was supported by the evidence. The decision to deny the motion, based on the expert's concession that delayed treatment would not substantially diminish success and the fact that the offense occurred during prior counseling, was within the court's sound discretion.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is **AFFIRMED**.
