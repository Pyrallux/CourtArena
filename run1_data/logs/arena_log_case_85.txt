Trisha D. Nelson challenges the lawfulness of the traffic stop that led to her conviction for aggravated driving while under the influence. Specifically, she questions the authority of the officer who made the stop and claims that the trial court committed reversible error by denying her motion to suppress. We conclude that the trial court properly denied the motion to suppress and hold that an officer, employed by the governing body of an Indian tribe and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board, may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation, while engaged in the scope of employment. When the vehicle stopped, the officer approached the driver, Nelson, and requested her driver’s license and registration. Within minutes, officers from the Mesa Police Department arrived and took control of the investigation. Subsequent testing revealed that Nelson’s blood alcohol content was 0.184.

Nelson filed a motion to suppress arguing that the evidence seized was the result of an unlawful investigatory stop. See Ariz. R.Crim. P. 16.2. The State requested that the motion be denied because the officer was certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board (“AZ POST”). After a suppression hearing, the trial court denied the motion, finding that the AZ POST certification gave the officer statutory authority to initiate the stop.

Nelson renews her arguments on appeal. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Article 6, Section 9, of the Arizona Constitution and A.R.S. §§ 12-120.21(A)(1) (2003), 13-4031 and 13-4033(A) (2001). “We review the trial court’s ruling [on a motion to suppress] for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. Here, the relevant facts are undisputed. The parties agree that the stop occurred outside the reservation by a law enforcement officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by the AZ POST.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS
## STATE OF ARIZONA, DIVISION ONE

Your Honor, members of the Court:

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in denying Ms. Nelson's motion to suppress. The traffic stop that led to her conviction was unlawful because the tribal officer who initiated it lacked lawful authority to conduct a traffic stop on a public road outside tribal lands. The prosecution's reliance on Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-3874(A) is misplaced, and the judgment below must be reversed.

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are undisputed. On February 2, 2003, an officer employed by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community conducted a traffic stop on Horne Road in Mesa, Arizona—a public highway located entirely outside tribal lands. The officer was not in "fresh pursuit" of Ms. Nelson; rather, he initiated the stop based on a dispatcher alert regarding a possible drunk driver. The officer was not cross-deputized by any state or local law enforcement agency. Within minutes of the stop, officers from the Mesa Police Department arrived and assumed control of the investigation. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996).

Ms. Nelson was subsequently charged with and convicted of aggravated driving while under the influence.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Standard of Review

This Court reviews the trial court's ruling on a motion to suppress for clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While the evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court's ruling, the court's legal conclusions are reviewed de novo. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App. 2002).

### B. The Fourth Amendment Framework

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. An investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). Under Arizona Rule of Criminal Procedure 16.2, a defendant who establishes that evidence was seized pursuant to a warrantless search has satisfied the burden of going forward and has triggered the State's burden of proving the lawfulness of the acquisition of the challenged evidence. Rodriguez v. Arellano, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App. 1999).

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the warrantless seizure falls within a recognized exception to the warrant requirement. Here, the prosecution has failed to meet this burden.

### C. The Officer Lacked Jurisdictional Authority to Conduct the Stop

The central issue in this case is whether a tribal law enforcement officer, certified by AZ POST, may conduct a traffic stop on a public road outside tribal lands without satisfying additional jurisdictional requirements. The defense contends he could not.

**1. The Fresh Pursuit Doctrine**

Arizona law recognizes the "fresh pursuit" doctrine, which allows law enforcement officers to pursue suspects into jurisdictions where they would not otherwise have authority. However, this doctrine has specific requirements. The pursuit must be "fresh"—meaning continuous and uninterrupted—from the commission of the offense. Here, the officer did not witness Ms. Nelson commit any traffic violation. He initiated the stop based solely on a dispatcher alert regarding a possible drunk driver. This is not fresh pursuit. The officer was engaged in an independent investigatory stop of a vehicle he happened to observe, not pursuing a suspect from tribal lands.

**2. Cross-Deputization Requirements**

The defense argued below—and renews on appeal—that the officer lacked authority because he was not cross-deputized by the Arizona Department of Public Safety, the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office, or the Mesa Police Department. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). Cross-deputization is the mechanism by which law enforcement officers from one jurisdiction gain lawful authority to enforce laws in another jurisdiction. Without such authorization, a tribal officer's authority is generally limited to tribal lands.

The prosecution argues that AZ POST certification alone confers statewide authority. This argument fails for several reasons.

### D. The Prosecution's Statutory Argument Fails

The prosecution relies on Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-3874(A), arguing that AZ POST certification grants tribal officers full statewide law enforcement authority. The defense submits this interpretation is incorrect.

**1. Statutory Text and Legislative Intent**

Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-3874(A) must be read in conjunction with the entire statutory scheme governing law enforcement authority. The statute does not, by its terms, grant tribal officers unlimited authority to enforce state laws on any public road in Arizona. Rather, it must be interpreted in light of principles of tribal sovereignty and the specific jurisdictional frameworks governing tribal-state law enforcement cooperation.

The Arizona Legislature did not intend § 13-3874(A) to authorize tribal officers to conduct routine traffic stops anywhere in the state, independent of any cooperative agreement or cross-deputization arrangement. Such an interpretation would circumvent the carefully constructed system of jurisdictional boundaries that govern law enforcement authority in this state.

**2. The Statute Does Not Override Jurisdictional Limitations**

Even assumingarguendo that § 13-3874(A) grants certified officers authority to enforce state laws, this authority must be exercised in accordance with constitutional and common-law principles. The Fourth Amendment does not permit law enforcement officers to conduct investigatory stops without reasonable articulable suspicion of criminal activity. The officer here lacked such suspicion independent of the dispatcher alert, which itself was based on uncorroborated information about a possible drunk driver—insufficient grounds for a Terry stop. See Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968); State v. Rich, 184 Ariz. 311, 908 P.2d 1041 (App. 1995) (discussing the reasonable suspicion standard for investigatory stops).

**3. The Trial Court's Reasoning Was Flawed**

The trial court found that "the AZ POST certification gave the officer statutory authority to initiate the stop." This reasoning is flawed. AZ POST certification establishes that an officer has met minimum training standards; it does not, by itself, confer jurisdictional authority to enforce laws outside the officer's jurisdiction. Certification and jurisdiction are distinct concepts. An officer may be fully certified yet lack authority to act in a particular location or circumstance.

The prosecution's argument conflates these concepts. The statute cited by the prosecution addresses qualifications and standards, not the geographic scope of authority. The trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to suppress based on this erroneous reasoning.

### E. The Exclusionary Remedy

Because the traffic stop was unlawful, the evidence obtained as a result—the blood alcohol test showing a 0.184 BAC—must be suppressed. The exclusionary rule requires suppression of evidence obtained through unlawful governmental action. The officer's unlawful seizure of Ms. Nelson's person taints all subsequent evidence. The trial court erred in admitting this evidence.

## III. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's opening argument mischaracterizes the nature of this case and the applicable law.

First, the prosecution claims the stop was "entirely proper under Arizona law." This is incorrect. The prosecution has not identified any Arizona law that authorizes a tribal officer to conduct a routine traffic stop on a public highway outside tribal lands without cross-deputization or fresh pursuit.

Second, the prosecution argues that "certification alone confers full statewide law enforcement authority." This is an overstatement of the statute. Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-3874(A) does not contain such broad language. The prosecution asks this Court to read language into the statute that simply is not there.

Third, the prosecution dismisses the "fresh pursuit" and "cross-deputization" requirements as unnecessary. However, these requirements exist precisely to protect against the kind of overreach that occurred here. A tribal officer, acting without coordination with local authorities, initiated a stop on a public road in Mesa. This is exactly the scenario the jurisdictional requirements were designed to prevent.

Fourth, the prosecution invokes "public safety" to justify the stop. While removing impaired drivers from the road is unquestionably important, the Fourth Amendment does not permit shortcuts around constitutional protections. The ends do not justify the means. If the officer lacked authority to stop Ms. Nelson, the stop was unlawful regardless of the legitimate public safety goals it served.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to suppress and remand for further proceedings.

The traffic stop was unlawful because the tribal officer who initiated it lacked jurisdictional authority to conduct a traffic stop on a public road outside tribal lands. The officer was not in fresh pursuit, was not cross-deputized, and AZ POST certification does not confer unlimited statewide authority. Because the stop was unlawful, the evidence obtained as a result must be suppressed.

The judgment of conviction must be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review the trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While we view this evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court’s ruling, we review de novo the court’s legal conclusions. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App.2002). The relevant facts in this case are undisputed: the stop occurred outside the reservation by a law enforcement officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board (AZ POST).

**II. Analysis**

Nelson argues that the trial court erred in denying her motion to suppress because the tribal officer lacked the authority to initiate a traffic stop off-reservation. She contends that the stop was unlawful because the officer was neither in "fresh pursuit" nor cross-deputized by state or local authorities. We disagree.

**A. Statutory Authority of Tribal Officers**

The central issue is whether a tribal officer, certified by AZ POST, possesses the authority to conduct a traffic stop off-reservation. The State argues that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) grants such authority. The defense contends that AZ POST certification merely establishes training standards and does not confer jurisdiction, and that without "fresh pursuit" or cross-deputization, the officer’s authority is limited to tribal lands.

Based on the provided case background, the statute explicitly provides that an officer employed by the governing body of an Indian tribe and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation while engaged in the scope of employment. See Ariz.Rev.Stat. (“A.R.S.”) § 13-3874(A) (2001). The plain language of the statute supports the prosecution's position that certification confers the authority to enforce state laws, including traffic laws, throughout the state.

The defense’s argument conflates the concepts of certification and jurisdiction. While certification ensures an officer meets minimum training standards, A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) utilizes that certification as a prerequisite for extending statewide enforcement authority to tribal officers. The statute does not limit this authority to instances of "fresh pursuit" or require cross-deputization agreements. Therefore, the trial court correctly determined that the officer’s AZ POST certification provided the statutory authority to initiate the stop.

**B. Fourth Amendment Reasonableness**

Nelson further argues that the stop violated the Fourth Amendment because the officer lacked reasonable articulable suspicion independent of the dispatcher's alert. An investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). To justify such a seizure, an officer must have reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

Here, the officer acted based on a police dispatcher's alert regarding a possible drunk driver traveling northbound on Horne Road. The officer observed a vehicle fitting that description. The defense argues this was insufficient because the information was uncorroborated and the officer was not in "fresh pursuit." However, the officer's verification of the vehicle's description matching the dispatch report provided the requisite reasonable suspicion to justify the brief investigatory stop. See State v. Rich, 184 Ariz. 311, 908 P.2d 1041 (App. 1995) (discussing the reasonable suspicion standard for investigatory stops). The stop was lawful both under the statutory authority granted by A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) and under Fourth Amendment principles.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to suppress. The tribal officer possessed statutory authority under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) to conduct the stop, and the stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. The defense's arguments regarding the necessity of "fresh pursuit" or cross-deputization are unpersuasive in light of the plain language of the statute.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling denying the motion to suppress is affirmed.

The defense’s opening statement mischaracterizes the law, misinterprets the controlling statute, and ignores the undisputed facts. Its arguments rest on legal errors that cannot withstand scrutiny. The trial court correctly denied Ms. Nelson’s motion to suppress because the tribal officer possessed clear statutory authority to conduct the stop under Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-3874(A), and the stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. We respectfully urge this Court to reject the defense’s flawed arguments and affirm the judgment.  

### I. THE DEFENSE’S STATUTORY ARGUMENT IS FUNDAMENTALLY FLAWED  
The defense claims Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-3874(A) does not grant tribal officers statewide authority without cross-deputization or fresh pursuit. This is incorrect. The statute explicitly states:  
> *"An officer employed by the governing body of an Indian tribe and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation while engaged in the scope of employment."*  

This language is unambiguous. It requires **only two conditions**: (1) tribal employment and (2) AZ POST certification. It says **nothing** about "fresh pursuit," "cross-deputization," or jurisdictional limitations. The defense’s attempt to impose these requirements is pure judicial invention. The statute does not "circumvent jurisdictional boundaries"—it **defines** them. By certifying tribal officers, the Legislature intentionally extended their authority to enforce state laws statewide, including on public roads like Horne Road in Mesa.  

The defense cites *Gonzalez-Gutierrez* for the proposition that cross-deputization is required, but that case involved an officer acting **outside any statutory authorization**—not one operating under § 13-3874(A). That statute was not even in play there. *Gonzalez-Gutierrez* does not apply here because it addresses different factual circumstances and legal frameworks. The defense’s reliance on it is misplaced.  

### II. "FRESH PURSUIT" IS IRRELEVANT TO THIS STOP  
The defense wrongly frames this as a "fresh pursuit" case. It is not. Fresh pursuit doctrine applies only when an officer **chases a suspect across jurisdictional lines after witnessing a crime**. Here, the officer did not witness any traffic violation. He acted on a dispatcher alert and observed a vehicle matching the description of a reported drunk driver. This is a routine investigatory stop based on reasonable suspicion—not a pursuit. The statute requires **no fresh pursuit** because it authorizes tribal officers to act **independently** on state roads when performing duties within their scope of employment. The defense’s attempt to conflate these concepts is legally unsound.  

### III. AZ POST CERTIFICATION CONFERS STATUTORY AUTHORITY—NOT "TRAINING STANDARDS" ALONE  
The defense claims AZ POST certification "merely establishes training standards" and does not confer jurisdiction. This misstates the law. Section 13-3874(A) explicitly ties certification to enforcement authority. The Legislature did not create a separate "training" category; it made certification the **gateway** to statewide authority. By certifying tribal officers, AZ POST confirms they meet state standards for law enforcement conduct—**including the authority to act on public roads**. The defense’s artificial distinction between "training" and "jurisdiction" ignores the statute’s plain language. If certification were only about training, the Legislature would not have stated that certified officers "may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation."

**I. Standard of Review**

We review the trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While we view this evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court’s ruling, we review de novo the court’s legal conclusions. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App.2002). The relevant facts in this case are undisputed: the stop occurred outside the reservation by a law enforcement officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board (AZ POST).

**II. Analysis**

Nelson argues that the trial court erred in denying her motion to suppress because the tribal officer lacked the authority to initiate a traffic stop off-reservation. She renews her arguments that the stop was unlawful because the officer was neither in "fresh pursuit" nor cross-deputized, and that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) is ambiguous regarding the scope of a tribal officer’s jurisdiction. We disagree.

**A. Statutory Authority of Tribal Officers**

The central issue is whether A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) grants a tribal officer, certified by AZ POST, the authority to conduct a traffic stop off-reservation. The defense argues that the statute is ambiguous and that its interpretation must be guided by canons of construction favoring tribal sovereignty, citing Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959) and Arizona v. California, 530 U.S. 392, 417-18 (2000). The defense further contends that AZ POST certification establishes only training standards, not jurisdictional authority, and that tribal officers generally lack authority to enforce state law off-reservation absent express federal authorization, as suggested by United States v. Cooley, 919 F.3d 1135, 1143-44 (8th Cir. 2019).

We are not persuaded by these arguments. The plain language of A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) provides: "An officer employed by the governing body of an Indian tribe and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation while engaged in the scope of employment." The statute sets forth two explicit prerequisites for off-reservation authority: (1) employment by a tribal governing body, and (2) AZ POST certification. The statute does not condition this authority on "fresh pursuit," cross-deputization, or a nexus to tribal lands.

The defense’s assertion that the statute is ambiguous regarding "scope of employment" is unavailing. While the term "scope of employment" is not defined in the statute, the context suggests it refers to the officer acting in a law enforcement capacity. Here, the officer was responding to a dispatch regarding a drunk driver, which falls squarely within the general law enforcement duties of a certified peace officer. The defense’s reliance on *Williams v. Lee* and *Arizona v. California* is misplaced because those cases involve the interpretation of federal statutes and treaties in contexts where ambiguity exists. Here, the state legislature has clearly articulated its intent to grant statewide authority to tribal officers who meet state certification standards. The canons of construction cited by the defense do not require us to ignore the clear statutory text.

Furthermore, the defense’s argument that AZ POST certification is merely a "training standard" and cannot confer jurisdiction ignores the statutory scheme. The legislature explicitly chose AZ POST certification as the gateway for extending enforcement authority. By certifying tribal officers, the State ensures they meet the same minimum standards as other peace officers in Arizona. The legislature has the authority to define the jurisdiction of peace officers it certifies, and it has done so here. The holding in *United States v. Cooley*, regarding the inherent authority of tribal officers under federal law, does not limit the State’s power to grant authority to tribal officers under state law.

**B. "Fresh Pursuit" and Cross-Deputization**

The defense argues that the "fresh pursuit" doctrine remains relevant and that the officer’s lack of cross-deputization renders the stop unlawful. We agree with the prosecution that the fresh pursuit doctrine is inapplicable to the facts of this case. Fresh pursuit applies when an officer chases a suspect across jurisdictional lines after witnessing a crime. See State v. Williams, 144 Ariz. 487, 491, 698 P.2d 732, 736 (App. 1985). Here, the officer did not witness a violation prior to the stop; he acted on a dispatcher’s alert. Because A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) grants the officer independent authority to initiate a stop based on reasonable suspicion, the doctrine of fresh pursuit is unnecessary.

**C. Fourth Amendment Reasonableness**

Nelson argues that the stop violated the Fourth Amendment because the dispatcher’s alert was uncorroborated and insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. An investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). To justify such a seizure, an officer must have reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

The defense contends that the officer’s observation of a vehicle matching the description did not corroborate the allegation of intoxication. We disagree. Under *State v. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to suppress. The tribal officer possessed statutory authority under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) to conduct the stop, and the stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. The defense’s arguments regarding the ambiguity of the statute, the limitations of AZ POST certification, and the insufficiency of the evidence are unpersuasive.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Court of Appeals, State of Arizona

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the prosecution's submissions and in support of our position that the trial court erred in denying the motion to suppress. We ask this Court to reverse that ruling, suppress the evidence obtained from the unlawful stop, and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT CLEARLY GRANT OFF-RESERVATION AUTHORITY—AMBIGUITY EXISTS AND MUST BE RESOLVED IN FAVOR OF TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY

The prosecution insists that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) is unambiguous and grants tribal officers clear authority to conduct stops off-reservation. This Court should reject this interpretation.

### A. The Statute Is Ambiguous

While the prosecution quotes the statute's plain language, they ignore the critical ambiguity that pervades this legislation: the meaning of "while engaged in the scope of employment." The statute does not define this term, and the legislature provided no guidance as to what scope of employment means for a tribal officer acting outside reservation boundaries.

When a statute fails to define key terms, Arizona courts must interpret those terms according to their ordinary meaning or construing them in context. **State v. Korzec**, 194 Ariz. 176, 179, ¶ 13, 978 P.2d 51, 54 (App. 1999). The term "scope of employment" in the law enforcement context traditionally refers to actions taken within the officer's authorized duties and geographic jurisdiction. **See** **Yarborough v. Alvarado**, 541 U.S. 652, 667 (2004) (discussing scope of employment in the context of whether officer actions were within official duties).

Here, the ambiguity is profound: does "scope of employment" mean employment by the tribal government (which has jurisdiction limited to tribal lands), or does it mean any law enforcement activity statewide? The statute is silent. This ambiguity must be resolved in favor of tribal sovereignty.

### B. Canons of Construction Require Pro-Tribal Interpretation

The defense cited **Williams v. Lee**, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959), for the proposition that ambiguities in statutes affecting tribal rights must be resolved in favor of the tribe. This canon applies with particular force here because the statute fundamentally affects tribal sovereignty—the power of tribal nations to control law enforcement within their territories and the authority of their officers beyond those boundaries.

The prosecution dismisses this argument as "misplaced," but their position would allow tribal officers to exercise state law enforcement authority statewide—authority that tribal governments never consented to and that fundamentally alters the balance of jurisdiction between state, federal, and tribal authorities. The Arizona Legislature cannot be presumed to have intended such a significant encroachment on tribal sovereignty without clear language expressing that intent. **See** **Arizona v. California**, 530 U.S. 392, 417-18 (2000) (applying canons of construction favoring tribes when interpreting statutes affecting tribal interests).

---

## II. AZ POST CERTIFICATION CONFERS NO JURISDICTIONAL AUTHORITY

The prosecution argues that AZ POST certification is the statutory mechanism for conferring jurisdictional authority. This argument fails for several reasons.

### A. Certification Is a Training Standard, Not a Grant of Authority

The Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board establishes training standards for peace officers in this state. **A.R.S. § 41-1821** et seq. The purpose of AZ POST is to ensure that certified officers meet minimum training requirements—not to define the geographic scope of their authority.

The defense maintains, consistent with **United States v. Cooley**, 919 F.3d 1135, 1143-44 (8th Cir. 2019), that tribal officers possess inherent authority only on tribal lands, and that authority to enforce state law off-reservation must be expressly granted by federal or state law. AZ POST certification does not constitute such an express grant. It is a training credential, nothing more.

### B. The Statute's Structure Supports This Interpretation

If the legislature intended AZ POST certification to be the sole basis for off-reservation authority, it would have made that intent explicit. Instead, the statute uses certification as one of two prerequisites—employment by a tribal governing body *and* certification—without explaining how certification transforms a tribal officer's jurisdiction.

The prosecution's interpretation would render the employment requirement surplusage. If certification alone grants statewide authority, why does the statute require tribal employment? The more reasonable interpretation is that the statute was designed to allow tribal officers to act in limited circumstances—perhaps when pursuing suspects who fled from the reservation—while maintaining the general principle that tribal officers lack general jurisdiction off-reservation.

---

## III. THE "FRESH PURSUIT" DOCTRINE REMAINS RELEVANT

The prosecution argues that "fresh pursuit" is irrelevant because the officer was not chasing a suspect across jurisdictional lines. This argument reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the defense's position.

### A. Fresh Pursuit Defines the Scope of Off-Reservation Authority

The defense does not argue that fresh pursuit was required in the technical sense—that the officer must have witnessed a crime and immediately pursued the suspect. Rather, the defense argues that the fresh pursuit doctrine *defines the circumstances* under which tribal officers may exercise authority off-reservation.

Under common law and Arizona jurisprudence, a law enforcement officer's authority to pursue a suspect across jurisdictional lines is limited to situations involving fresh pursuit. **State v. Williams**, 144 Ariz. 487, 491, 698 P.2d 732, 736 (App. 1985). This doctrine exists because jurisdictions have inherent sovereignty within their boundaries, and one jurisdiction's officers cannot exercise authority within another without consent or express authorization.

### B. The Statute Should Be Read in Light of Fresh Pursuit Principles

Even accepting the prosecution's argument that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) grants independent authority, this Court should interpret the statute in a manner consistent with constitutional principles and the traditional limits on cross-jurisdictional law enforcement. To hold that any certified tribal officer may stop any vehicle anywhere in Arizona, based solely on a dispatcher tip, would extend tribal officers' authority far beyond what the legislature could have intended.

The defense respectfully suggests that the statute was intended to codify the fresh pursuit doctrine for tribal officers—allowing them to pursue suspects off-reservation when they have reasonable suspicion to believe a crime was committed on tribal land. The officer's response to a dispatch about a "possible drunk driver" does not fit this model.

---

## IV. REASONABLE SUSPICION WAS INSUFFICIENT

Even if this Court finds that the officer had statutory authority to conduct the stop, the stop must still satisfy Fourth Amendment requirements. It did not.

### A. The Dispatcher Tip Was Insufficient

The prosecution argues that the officer's observation of a vehicle matching the dispatcher's description constituted corroboration sufficient for reasonable suspicion. This argument misapplies the standard.

An anonymous tip that a vehicle is traveling in a particular direction, without more, does not provide reasonable suspicion. **Florida v. J.L.**, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000). The tip must be reliable, which typically requires corroboration of *identifying information* that could only be known if the tipster had genuine knowledge of the defendant's activities. **Alabama v. White**, 496 U.S. 325, 332 (1990).

Here, the dispatcher provided only a general description—vehicle traveling northbound on Horne Road—and the officer observed a vehicle at that location. This is minimal corroboration. The defense maintains that more is required to establish reasonable suspicion for a drunk driving stop.

### B. The Officer Observed No Criminal Activity

Reasonable suspicion requires specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity is afoot. **Terry v. Ohio**, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The officer did not observe the vehicle weaving, speeding, or committing any traffic violation. He did not observe signs of impairment. He simply saw a vehicle matching a general description.

Under **State v. Rich**, 184 Ariz. 311, 908 P.2d 1041 (App. 1995), the prosecution argues, corroboration of a dispatcher's tip is sufficient. But **Rich** is distinguishable. In that case, the officer observed the vehicle engaging in specific suspicious behavior—driving at an excessive rate of speed and failing to yield to the officer's signals. No such observations exist here.

The officer had no basis to believe the driver was impaired other than the uncorroborated dispatcher tip. This is insufficient under the Fourth Amendment.

## V. THE PROSECUTION FAILED TO MEET ITS BURDEN

Finally, the defense notes that under **Rodriguez v. Arellano**, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶ 12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App. 1999), once the defendant establishes that evidence was seized pursuant to a warrantless search, the burden shifts to the State to prove the lawfulness of the seizure.

The prosecution has not met this burden. They have not established that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) clearly grants tribal officers authority to conduct investigatory stops off-reservation based on dispatcher tips. They have not established that AZ POST certification confers jurisdictional authority. And they have not established that the officer had reasonable suspicion to believe Nelson was committing a crime.

1. **A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) is ambiguous** regarding the scope of a tribal officer's authority off-reservation, and this ambiguity must be resolved in favor of tribal sovereignty.

2. **AZ POST certification is a training standard**, not a grant of jurisdictional authority, and cannot serve as the basis for the officer's power to conduct the stop.

3. **The fresh pursuit doctrine remains relevant** as a limit on tribal officers' authority to act outside their jurisdiction.

4. **Reasonable suspicion was lacking** because the dispatcher tip was uncorroborated by specific observations of criminal activity.

5. **The prosecution failed to meet its burden** of proving the lawfulness of the stop.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review the trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While we view this evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court’s ruling, we review de novo the court’s legal conclusions. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App.2002). The relevant facts in this case are undisputed: the stop occurred outside the reservation by a law enforcement officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board (AZ POST).

**II. Analysis**

Nelson argues that the trial court erred in denying her motion to suppress. She renews her arguments that the tribal officer lacked authority to initiate a traffic stop off-reservation and that the stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion. We address each argument in turn.

**A. Statutory Authority Under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A)**

Nelson contends that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) is ambiguous regarding the scope of a tribal officer’s authority off-reservation, specifically arguing that the phrase "while engaged in the scope of employment" limits the officer’s authority to actions related to tribal interests. She asserts that this ambiguity must be resolved in favor of tribal sovereignty, citing Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959) and Arizona v. California, 530 U.S. 392, 417-18 (2000). We disagree.

The plain language of A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) provides: "An officer employed by the governing body of an Indian tribe and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation, while engaged in the scope of employment." The statute explicitly authorizes stops "off the reservation." The phrase "while engaged in the scope of employment" is a standard limitation on official acts, ensuring the officer is acting in a law enforcement capacity rather than for personal reasons. It does not act as a hidden jurisdictional bar that negates the express grant of off-reservation authority.

The defense’s argument that the statute is ambiguous ignores the clear legislative intent to extend authority to certified tribal officers beyond reservation boundaries. The legislature identified two objective prerequisites for this authority: tribal employment and AZ POST certification. By satisfying these conditions, the officer is empowered to act. The canons of construction cited by the defense do not require us to ignore the statute’s clear text or rewrite the statute to include limitations the legislature chose not to include. See State v. Korzec, 194 Ariz. 176, 179, ¶ 13, 978 P.2d 51, 54 (App. 1999) (statutes are interpreted based on their plain meaning).

Furthermore, the defense’s assertion that AZ POST certification is merely a training standard, and not a grant of jurisdictional authority, is unpersuasive. The legislature explicitly tied the power to conduct off-reservation stops to AZ POST certification. This certification ensures that tribal officers meet the same minimum standards as other peace officers in Arizona. The legislature has the authority to define the jurisdiction of peace officers it certifies, and it has done so here.

**B. The "Fresh Pursuit" Doctrine**

Nelson argues that the "fresh pursuit" doctrine defines the limits of a tribal officer’s off-reservation authority and that the officer’s lack of fresh pursuit renders the stop unlawful. See State v. Williams, 144 Ariz. 487, 491, 698 P.2d 732, 736 (App. 1985). We agree with the prosecution that this doctrine is inapplicable to the facts of this case.

Fresh pursuit is a common-law doctrine that allows officers to cross jurisdictional lines when chasing a suspect who has committed a crime within their original jurisdiction. Here, the officer was not pursuing a suspect from the reservation. He was responding to a dispatch regarding a drunk driver in Mesa. Because A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) grants the officer independent statutory authority to initiate a stop based on reasonable suspicion, the common-law doctrine of fresh pursuit is not required to validate the stop.

**C. Fourth Amendment Reasonableness**

Nelson argues that the stop violated the Fourth Amendment because the dispatcher’s alert was uncorroborated and insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. She relies on Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000) and Alabama v. White, 496 U.S. 325, 332 (1990) to argue that an anonymous tip regarding a vehicle's location, without more, cannot justify a seizure. We find these cases distinguishable.

An investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). To justify such a seizure, an officer must have reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

Under State v. Rich, 184 Ariz. 311, 908 P.2d 1041 (App. 1995), a dispatcher’s alert, when corroborated by an officer’s observation of specific details matching the description, provides reasonable suspicion to justify an investigatory stop. The defense attempts to distinguish *Rich* by noting that the officer in that case observed the vehicle speeding. However, the principle of *Rich* remains applicable: corroboration of the details provided in the tip elevates the tip's reliability.

Here, the officer received a dispatch of a possible drunk driver traveling northbound on Horne Road. He observed a vehicle matching that exact description at that exact location. This corroboration provided the officer with specific, articulable facts suggesting that the driver was the reported drunk driver. While the officer did not observe erratic driving prior to the stop, the totality of the circumstances—the report of an impaired driver and the immediate corroboration of the vehicle's identity and location—supported a reasonable suspicion that criminal activity was afoot. The defense’s argument that the officer needed to observe independent signs of intoxication before initiating the stop would impose a higher standard than required by the Fourth Amendment.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to suppress. The tribal officer possessed statutory authority under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) to conduct the stop, and the stop was supported by reasonable suspicion. The defense’s arguments regarding the ambiguity of the statute, the limitations of AZ POST certification, and the insufficiency of the evidence are unpersuasive.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling denying the motion to suppress is affirmed.
