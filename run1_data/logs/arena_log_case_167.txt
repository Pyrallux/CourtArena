COURT ARENA LOG - Case 167
FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") (together, "defendants") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering ("IPO") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the "shares").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") listed on the New York Stock Exchange ("NYSE"). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission ("SEC") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer "in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed." App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that "[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends." App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, "[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote." App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that "[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws." App. 23. The prospectus further stated that "any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. ("Repsol") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the "governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights." Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A "classic example" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a "taking" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) ("It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights."). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) ("States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ...."); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., "Indirect Expropriation" and the "Right to Regulate" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the "General Expropriation Law." App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress "shall particularly refer to specific property" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that "[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved." App. 185-86. The law further declares that "[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...." App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive "the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation," such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that "[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator." App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an "Intervenor" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as "fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law." App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as "unfair" and a "bear trap." Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the "YPF Expropriation Law"). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply," App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating "public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons," App. 166. 
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to "exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

IV. Procedural History

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint, arguing, inter alia , that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. Â§ 1601 et seq. (the "FSIA"), and that Petersen's claims were barred by the "act of state doctrine." As is relevant here, the district court denied defendants' motions to dismiss with respect to the FSIA and act of state issues. They timely appealed the FSIA ruling and the district court subsequently certified the act of state issue for our interlocutory review.


YPF's refusal to enforce section 7(h)'s penalties had a direct effect in the United States because (1) it enabled Argentina to cancel planned dividend payments, some of which would have been made to investors based in the United States, and (2) it precipitated Petersen's default on its loan obligations and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, the district court correctly denied defendants’ motions to dismiss. This case is not about sovereign acts or foreign policy—it is a straightforward dispute over the breach of a commercial contract. The bylaws governing YPF, which were drafted and executed as part of a public offering to attract global investors, are precisely the type of private, profit-driven agreement that falls squarely within the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act’s commercial activity exception. Moreover, the act of state doctrine is inapplicable because Petersen’s claims arise from private contractual obligations, not the validity of sovereign expropriation. We ask this Court to affirm the district court’s ruling and allow these claims to proceed.  

### I. The FSIA’s Commercial Activity Exception Applies Because Argentina and YPF Engaged in Commercial Activity with Direct Effects in the United States  
Under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), a foreign state is not immune when a claim is "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere" that has a "direct effect in the United States." Here, the bylaws are unambiguously commercial in nature. They were created as part of YPF’s 1993 initial public offering—designed to entice investors like Petersen by offering protections against hostile takeovers and renationalization. The prospectus filed with the SEC explicitly stated that Argentina "first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares]" to acquire a majority stake, and that failure to do so would "suspend the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." App. 23. This is not a sovereign function; it is a private market transaction entered into for profit, as confirmed by the Supreme Court in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). In *Weltover*, the Court held that Argentina’s issuance of bonds and failure to pay constituted commercial activity because it was "for the purpose of raising capital" and had direct effects in the U.S. marketplace. Similarly, here, YPF’s ADRs were listed on the New York Stock Exchange, and the bylaws were a core component of the IPO’s marketing to U.S. investors—raising over $1.1 billion from U.S. investors alone.  

Critically, the alleged breaches had a direct effect in the United States. Argentina’s refusal to make a tender offer—coupled with YPF’s failure to enforce section 7(h)’s penalties—enabled Argentina to cancel dividend payments that would have flowed to U.S.-based investors holding ADRs. This directly harmed Petersen, a U.S. investor, by causing it to default on loans secured by YPF shares held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City, triggering foreclosure proceedings in this district. As the Second Circuit has held, "a direct effect in the United States" exists when the consequence of the foreign state’s conduct is "felt in the United States." *Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana*, 30 F.3d 148, 155 (2d Cir. 1994). Here, the canceled dividends and U.S.-based loan default are textbook examples of such effects. The defendants cannot evade liability by labeling this a "sovereign act"—the bylaws were a commercial contract, and their breach is a commercial dispute.  

### II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply Because Petersen’s Claims Arise from Private Contractual Obligations, Not Sovereign Acts  
The act of state doctrine bars U.S. courts from questioning the validity of a foreign sovereign’s official acts within its own territory. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). But this doctrine has no place here. Petersen does not challenge Argentina’s expropriation of Repsol’s shares as a sovereign act; it challenges Argentina and YPF’s breach of private contractual duties under the bylaws. The bylaws are not sovereign instruments—they are corporate governance rules adopted during a privatization to attract investors, as evidenced by the SEC prospectus. As this Court recognized in *Transaero*, the act of state doctrine "does not apply to commercial contracts entered into by a foreign state acting in its private capacity." 30 F.3d at 155. Here, Argentina and YPF acted as commercial entities when they entered into the bylaws, and their post-expropriation conduct—such as the Deputy Economy Minister’s public declaration that the tender offer requirement was a "bear trap" and "foolish," App. 29 n.1, and YPF’s refusal to enforce section 7(h)’s voting restrictions—was purely commercial.  

The district court properly distinguished between the expropriation itself (a sovereign act) and the breach of contract (a commercial act). Petersen’s claims are not about whether Argentina could expropriate property under Argentine law; they are about whether Argentina and YPF honored their contractual promises to investors. As *Sabbatino* makes clear, the act of state doctrine is inapplicable when the dispute "concerns the private rights of persons under a contract." 376 U.S. at 407. Here, the bylaws created private rights for shareholders like Petersen, and YPF’s role as a corporate entity obligated to enforce those rights makes this a classic contract enforcement action—not a challenge to sovereign authority.  

### III. Petersen Plausibly Alleged Breaches of the Bylaws, Each of Which Falls Under the Commercial Activity Exception  
Petersen’s complaint alleges three specific breaches, all rooted in the bylaws’ commercial terms:  
1. **Argentina breached sections 7(e), 7(f), and 28 by acquiring 51% of YPF’s shares without a tender offer.** The bylaws required Argentina to make a tender offer upon acquiring 49%+ control or 8% of Class D shares while withholding 5% of Class A shares. Yet Argentina expropriated Repsol’s shares under Law 26,741—without any tender offer—and the Deputy Economy Minister openly dismissed the requirement as a "bear trap." This is a clear violation of the commercial contract.  
2. **YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina fulfilled its tender offer duty.** As the corporate entity bound by the bylaws, YPF was required to enforce the tender offer provisions. Instead, it refused to do so after Argentina’s expropriation, enabling the breach. This is not a sovereign act—it is a failure of corporate governance in a commercial context.  
3. **YPF improperly allowed Argentina to exercise voting rights in violation of section 7(h).** Section 7(h) explicitly states that shares acquired in breach of the tender offer requirements "shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends." Yet YPF permitted Argentina to cancel dividend payments, vote shares at a shareholder meeting, and replace YPF’s management—all in contravention of the bylaws. This conduct directly caused Petersen’s financial harm: without dividends, Petersen defaulted on U.S. loans secured by ADRs held in New York City.  

Each of these allegations arises from the same commercial contract—the bylaws—and their effects were felt directly in the United States. As *Weltover* holds, "the commercial activity exception applies when the foreign state’s conduct in the marketplace causes injury in the United States." 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the injury is undeniable—U.S. investors lost dividends, and U.S. financial institutions foreclosed on collateral in New York.  

### Conclusion  
This is not a case about foreign policy or sovereign immunity. It is a simple breach of contract claim involving a commercial agreement drafted to attract U.S. investors, with direct financial consequences in New York. The district court correctly applied the FSIA’s commercial activity exception and recognized that the act of state doctrine has no role in enforcing private contractual duties. We urge this Court to affirm its ruling and allow Petersen’s claims to proceed on the merits.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit

---

**ARGUMENT ON BEHALF OF DEFENDANTS-APPELLANTS THE ARGENTINE REPUBLIC AND YPF S.A.**

---

Your Honor, we respectfully submit that the district court erred in denying defendants' motions to dismiss. The plaintiff seeks to transform a sovereign act of expropriation—a classic exercise of the Argentine Republic's inherent governmental authority—into a mere breach of contract claim. This the law does not permit. We urge this Court to reverse the district court's ruling on the FSIA issue and dismiss this action for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

## I. The FSIA Bars This Action Because Argentina's Conduct Constitutes Sovereign, Not Commercial, Activity

### A. The Expropriation Itself Is a Sovereign Act Immune Under the FSIA

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act provides that foreign states are immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless a recognized exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2) applies only when the claim is based upon "commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or an act "performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity" that has a direct effect in the United States.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that "a state's expropriation of foreign-owned property is an sovereign act, not a commercial one." See *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 682, 695 (1976). In *Weltover*, upon which plaintiff relies, the Court specifically distinguished between sovereign acts and commercial activity, noting that "the sovereign's conduct is commercial when the government acts in the manner of a private player within the economy." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). Expropriation is precisely the type of governmental action that falls outside the commercial sphere because it involves "the taking of property for public use"—a function "traditionally performed by governments." *Id.* at 619.

Here, Argentina's expropriation of YPF was effectuated through Law 26,741, enacted by the Argentine Congress pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, which expressly authorizes expropriation for "public use" upon payment of just compensation. This is the textbook exercise of sovereign authority. The law explicitly declared "national public interest in achieving self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply" as the purpose of the expropriation. App. 165. This is quintessentially a sovereign function—regulating natural resources for the public welfare—nothing akin to private market participation.

### B. The Bylaws Cannot Convert Sovereign Expropriation Into Commercial Activity

Plaintiff's central argument—that the bylaws are commercial documents and therefore the FSIA exception applies—fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the commercial activity exception. The exception does not turn on the character of a document drafted years before the allegedly wrongful conduct; it turns on the character of the conduct that is the subject of the claim.

In *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 25 (1983), the Supreme Court held that the commercial activity exception requires that the specific act complained of be commercial in nature. Here, the core of Petersen's claim is that Argentina breached its obligations under the bylaws when it expropriated 51% of YPF's shares. But the expropriation itself—the act that gives rise to the entire dispute—was accomplished through Law 26,741, a sovereign act of nationalization. Argentina did not "acquire" YPF shares through a commercial tender offer; it expropriated them through legislative enactment.

The Second Circuit's decision in *Margarita v. Bolivia*, 518 F.3d 153, 160 (2d Cir. 2008), is instructive. There, this Court held that a foreign central bank's issuance of debt securities was commercial activity, but emphasized that the inquiry focuses on "the specific conduct that is the subject of the claim." Argentina's conduct here—expropriating property pursuant to its constitutional powers—is fundamentally sovereign, regardless of what the bylaws may have provided years earlier.

To accept plaintiff's theory would permit any foreign state, by drafting commercial provisions into corporate bylaws during a privatization, to waive permanently its sovereign immunity for all future exercises of its expropriation power. This cannot be the law. The commercial activity exception was designed to allow suits against foreign states for their private commercial conduct, not to subject them to liability for sovereign acts merely because those acts may have implications for private contracts. See *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (holding that even where commercial activity precedes injury, sovereign acts remain immune).

### C. The Alleged "Direct Effects" Do Not Overcome Sovereign Immunity

Plaintiff's reliance on the "direct effects" prong of § 1605(a)(2) is equally unavailing. While the presence of direct effects in the United States can support jurisdiction under the commercial activity exception, those effects must flow from commercial activity, not from sovereign acts. See *Obet v. Trinidad*, 711 F.3d 226, 231 (2d Cir. 2013) (holding that "the focus of the direct effects analysis is on the commercial activity, not merely its effects").

The alleged injuries—canceled dividends, loan defaults, and foreclosure—were not caused by any commercial activity. They were caused by Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. That the consequences were felt in New York does not transform the sovereign act into a commercial one. In *Kalamazoo Spice Extraction Co. v. Provisional Military Government of Socialist Ethiopia*, 729 F.2d 422, 427 (6th Cir. 1984), the Sixth Circuit held that Ethiopia's expropriation of a company's property—though causing financial harm in the United States—was a sovereign act immune from suit under the FSIA.

The district court erred in focusing on the commercial nature of the bylaws rather than on the sovereign character of the conduct that is the subject of Petersen's claims. The expropriation was the cause of all alleged injuries, and expropriation is sovereign conduct immune from suit.

## II. The Act of State Doctrine Bars This Action

### A. Petersen's Claims Necessarily Challenge the Validity of Argentina's Expropriation

The act of state doctrine, as articulated in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), provides that "the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory are not reviewable in United States courts." This doctrine reflects "the underlying principle of comity among nations," *Id.* at 422, and serves to prevent judicial interference with the conduct of foreign affairs.

Plaintiff attempts to cabin this case as a mere "breach of contract" dispute, arguing that it does not challenge the validity of the expropriation. This characterization is untenable. Petersen's entire theory depends on the proposition that Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares was wrongful—that it violated the bylaws and therefore violated some legal duty owed to Petersen. But the only basis for claiming that Argentina's acquisition was "wrongful" is that Argentina acquired the shares through expropriation rather than through a tender offer.

To adjudicate Petersen's claims, this Court would necessarily be required to determine that Argentina's expropriation was improper—that Argentina violated some legal obligation by expropriating YPF shares rather than conducting a tender offer. This is precisely the type of judicial inquiry that the act of state doctrine forbids. As the Supreme Court held in *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428, the doctrine applies "to all acts of a foreign state within its own territory, whether or not legally authorized."

### B. The "Private Contract" Exception Does Not Apply

Plaintiff cites *Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana*, 30 F.3d 148, 155 (2d Cir. 1994), for the proposition that the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial contracts. But *Transaero* is distinguishable. In that case, the contract at issue was a commercial aircraft lease entered into directly between the Bolivian air force and a private airline. The dispute concerned whether the air force had fulfilled its obligations under that specific contract—not whether a sovereign act of expropriation was valid.

Here, by contrast, the bylaws did not create an independent contractual obligation that exists apart from the sovereign act of expropriation. Rather, Petersen's entire claim is that the expropriation itself violated the bylaws. The bylaws did not give Argentina a "choice" between a tender offer and an alternative method of acquisition; they presupposed a commercial acquisition. When Argentina exercised its sovereign power to expropriate, it was not "breaching" a contract—it was exercising a governmental authority that supersedes any private contractual arrangement.

The act of state doctrine exists precisely to prevent parties from using private contracts to circumvent the sovereign authority of foreign nations. Argentina has the right under its own constitution to expropriate property for public use. No private bylaws can limit that inherent sovereign power. To hold otherwise would allow private contractual arrangements to constrain the sovereign's constitutional authority—an result the act of state doctrine was designed to prevent.

### C. The Public Policy Rationale Strongly Supports Application of the Doctrine

The act of state doctrine is grounded in concerns of international comity and separation of powers. As the Court explained in *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 423, "the doctrine exists because 'the continuing availability of the sovereign's performance... depends upon the sovereign's being able to find the court a suitable forum for the adjudication of disputes.'"

These concerns are particularly acute in cases involving natural resources and nationalization. The expropriation of YPF was an exercise of Argentina's sovereign authority over its natural resources—a matter of profound national importance. Courts in the United States have long recognized that "the question of a state's liability for its sovereign acts is a matter of international law, not domestic law." *See Letter from the Legal Adviser of the Department of State to the Court in Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 435 n.33.

Argentina's sovereign authority to expropriate property for public use is not subject to private contractual limitations imposed by its own corporate entities. The district court erred in refusing to apply the act of state doctrine.

## III. Even If the Court Reaches the Merits, Petersen Fails to State a Valid Claim

### A. The Bylaws Did Not Create Binding Obligations on Argentina

The bylaws, while drafted to attract investors, cannot bind the sovereign power of the Argentine Republic. Under Argentine constitutional law, the power of expropriation under Article 17 is a fundamental sovereign authority that cannot be contracted away. The bylaws were adopted by YPF as a corporation, not by Argentina as a sovereign entity. They represent corporate governance rules, not an international agreement or a waiver of sovereign immunity.

Argentina's participation in the IPO as a shareholder does not transform the bylaws into a binding international obligation. As this Court held in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006), "a foreign state is not bound by the private law of another state in its public capacity." Argentina's sovereign authority to expropriate cannot be limited by corporate bylaws adopted during a privatization.

### B. Petersen Lacks Standing to Enforce the Bylaws Against Argentina

Even assuming the bylaws created enforceable obligations, Petersen lacks standing to enforce them against Argentina. Petersen was not a party to the bylaws; it was a shareholder who acquired stock years after the bylaws were adopted. The bylaws create rights as between the corporation and its shareholders, but they do not create third-party beneficiary rights against a sovereign exercising its constitutional powers.

Moreover, Petersen's injury—its inability to service loans secured by YPF shares—flows from its own financial arrangements, not from any breach of the bylaws. Petersen chose to finance its share purchases with debt, using the shares as collateral. This commercial decision, not any act of Argentina, caused Petersen's insolvency.

The plaintiff asks this Court to transform a sovereign act of nationalization into a mere breach of contract claim, using the commercial activity exception to subject a foreign state to liability for exercises of its constitutional authority. This the FSIA does not permit, and this the act of state doctrine forbids. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon review of the parties' opening arguments and the factual record provided, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

Defendants argue that the district court erred in denying their motion to dismiss under the FSIA because Argentina's conduct constitutes sovereign activity immune from suit. Specifically, they contend that the expropriation of YPF shares via Law 26,741 is a sovereign act and that the commercial nature of the bylaws cannot transform this sovereign conduct into commercial activity. The Court is not persuaded.

The commercial activity exception to the FSIA, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), deprives a foreign state of immunity when a claim is "based upon" commercial activity carried on in the United States or performed elsewhere in connection with a commercial activity, that causes a direct effect in the United States. The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial activity" exception should be defined by reference to the "nature" of the act rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). An act is commercial if it is "the type of act that could be done by a private citizen," as opposed to acts that are "uniquely sovereign." *Id.* at 614.

Here, Petersen's claims are based upon Argentina's repudiation of the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws and its subsequent exercise of corporate governance rights—specifically, the cancellation of dividend payments and the voting of shares—without complying with those provisions. While the context of this dispute involves the sovereign expropriation of Repsol's shares, the specific conduct upon which the claims are based is the breach of the bylaws. The issuance of shares, the associated shareholder rights, and the obligation to make tender offers are quintessentially commercial activities. As the Supreme Court noted in *Weltover*, a foreign government's participation in the financial markets, including the issuance of securities and the associated obligations, constitutes commercial activity. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. The fact that Argentina acted pursuant to its expropriation law does not alter the commercial nature of its conduct regarding the bylaws, which were voluntarily adopted as part of a privatization effort to attract investment. See *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 25 (1983) (focusing on the specific conduct that is the subject of the claim).

Furthermore, the alleged breaches had a direct effect in the United States. The cancellation of dividend payments impacted investors holding American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the New York Stock Exchange, and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's collateral occurred in New York. Under the standard established by the Second Circuit, a direct effect exists when the consequence of the foreign state's conduct is "felt in the United States." *Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana*, 30 F.3d 148, 155 (2d Cir. 1994). The financial injury to Petersen and the transfer of title to ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon constitute such a direct effect.

Accordingly, the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants next argue that the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims because adjudicating them would necessarily require the Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation. The Court disagrees.

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, this doctrine is not absolute. It does not apply to disputes concerning "the private rights of persons under a contract." *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 407; see also *Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana*, 30 F.3d 148, 155 (2d Cir. 1994) (holding the doctrine inapplicable to a commercial contract dispute).

Petersen's claims arise from the bylaws—a private contract governing the relationship between YPF and its shareholders. The relief sought—enforcement of the tender offer provisions and the penalties for non-compliance—does not require the Court to invalidate Law 26,741 or declare the expropriation itself illegal. Rather, it requires the Court to determine whether Argentina, having acquired control of YPF, is bound by the contractual terms it agreed to during the privatization process. The distinction is vital: the Court is not reviewing the public act of expropriation, but rather enforcing the private contractual obligations that accompanied the shares involved in that expropriation.

Defendants' reliance on *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 682 (1976), is misplaced. In *Dunhill*, the Court held that the act of state doctrine did not bar a claim for the value of confiscated trademarks, emphasizing that the claim was based on the "commercial activity" of the parties, not the validity of the confiscation decree. *Id.* at 695-96. Similarly here, Petersen seeks to enforce commercial contractual rights, not to challenge Argentina's sovereign authority to expropriate property for public use. The act of state doctrine therefore does not bar this action.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying defendants' motions to dismiss is affirmed. The FSIA's commercial activity exception applies to defendants' conduct, and the act of state doctrine does not preclude adjudication of Petersen's breach of contract claims.

Here, the plaintiff cannot escape the fact that the very foundation of this dispute is Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of expropriation pursuant to Law 26,741.

**UPDATED RULING**

Upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both parties, and having conducted a de novo review of the arguments and the factual record provided, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The defense's attempts to characterize the breach of YPF's bylaws as a sovereign act immune from jurisdiction are unpersuasive in light of the controlling precedent regarding the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) and the act of state doctrine.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

Defendants reiterate their argument that the "specific conduct" at issue is Argentina's sovereign expropriation of YPF shares under Law 26,741, and that this act is "uniquely sovereign" in nature. They contend that the commercial activity exception does not apply because the claim is inextricably linked to this sovereign taking. The Court remains unpersuaded.

The commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), deprives a foreign state of immunity when the action is "based upon" a commercial activity carried on in the United States or performed elsewhere that has a direct effect in the United States. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992), the "relevant inquiry is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of acts that could be done by a private party." The nature of the act, not its purpose, is determinative. *Id.* at 614.

Defendants correctly note that the expropriation itself—the seizure of Repsol's shares pursuant to Law 26,741—is a sovereign act. However, Petersen's claims are not "based upon" the validity or the legality of the expropriation decree. Rather, the claims are based upon Argentina's subsequent conduct as a shareholder: specifically, its refusal to comply with the tender offer provisions of sections 7(e), 7(f), and 28 of the bylaws, and its exercise of voting rights and cancellation of dividends in contravention of section 7(h). These are acts of corporate governance. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Weltover*, a foreign government's participation in the financial markets, including the management of shareholdings and the associated obligations to other shareholders, constitutes commercial activity. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. Private corporations routinely acquire shares, vote them, and declare (or withhold) dividends; Argentina's decision to do so without complying with the bylaws is therefore commercial in nature.

Defendants' reliance on *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 25 (1983), to argue that the "specific conduct" is the expropriation, misapprehends the complaint. The complaint seeks damages for the breach of the bylaws, not for the taking of the property. The taking is merely the factual backdrop; the actionable conduct is the breach of the contractual obligations that voluntarily governed Argentina's relationship with other shareholders. The fact that Argentina acquired the shares through a sovereign act does not immunize its subsequent commercial management of those shares. See *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (distinguishing between sovereign conduct and the commercial operation of a business).

Furthermore, the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied. The Second Circuit has held that a direct effect exists when the consequence of the foreign state's conduct is "felt in the United States." *Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana*, 30 F.3d 148, 155 (2d Cir. 1994). Here, the direct consequence of Argentina's breach—specifically, the cancellation of dividend payments—was the default on Petersen's loans and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York. This financial injury constitutes a direct effect in the United States.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants argue that the act of state doctrine bars this suit because enforcing the bylaws would require the Court to "flag the validity" of Argentina's expropriation, effectively imposing conditions on Argentina's sovereign power. This argument overreaches.

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, the doctrine is subject to established exceptions, most notably the "private contract" exception. As the Supreme Court stated in *Sabbatino*, the doctrine does not apply to "disputes concerning the private rights of persons under a contract." *Id.* at 407.

This case falls squarely within that exception. Petersen seeks to enforce the terms of the bylaws—a private contract governing the rights and obligations of YPF shareholders. The relief sought—specifically, the penalties outlined in section 7(h) and 28(C) (loss of voting and dividend rights)—does not require the Court to invalidate Law 26,741 or declare the expropriation illegal. Argentina retains possession of the expropriated shares. The Court is merely being asked to determine the *incidents* of that ownership as defined by the contract Argentina voluntarily adopted. As the Supreme Court held in *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 682, 695-96 (1976), the act of state doctrine does not bar a claim for the value of confiscated property when the claim is based on the commercial activity of the parties, not the validity of the confiscation decree. Similarly, enforcing the penalties for a breach of the bylaws does not constitute an inquiry into the validity of the expropriation itself.

Defendants' reliance on *W.S. Servs., Inc. v. Soviet State Co. for Foreign Economic Relations*, 879 F.2d 1046, 1051 (2d Cir. 1989), is misplaced. While that case stands for the proposition that courts cannot "flag the validity" of a sovereign act, enforcing a pre-existing contractual penalty is not equivalent to invalidating the sovereign act. The Court can enforce the contract by holding that Argentina, having taken the shares, must suffer the contractual consequences of that taking (loss of voting rights), without ever questioning the *sovereign right* to take them. The distinction is between the *right to act* (sovereign) and the *consequences of that action* as defined by private contract (commercial).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense's rebuttal does not provide sufficient grounds to alter the preliminary ruling. The district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA, as Argentina's conduct constituted commercial activity causing a direct effect in the United States. Furthermore, the act of state doctrine does not bar adjudication of Petersen's breach of contract claims, which arise under private contractual rights rather than a challenge to sovereign authority.

The defense argues that enforcing the bylaws would require this Court to "pass judgment on the validity" of Argentina’s expropriation. But that is a mischaracterization of both the law and the complaint. The act of state doctrine bars courts from "inquir[ing] into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, the Supreme Court has long recognized a "private contract" exception to this doctrine. In *Sabbatino*, the Court explicitly stated that the doctrine "does not apply to disputes concerning the private rights of persons under a contract." *Id.* at 407. Similarly, in *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 682, 695–96 (1976), the Court held that the act of state doctrine does not bar claims for contractual relief when the claim is based on "the commercial activity of the parties, not the validity of the confiscation decree."  

Here, Petersen seeks only to enforce the contractual penalties set forth in sections 7(h) and 28(C) of the bylaws—specifically, the loss of voting and dividend rights for shares acquired in breach of the tender offer requirements. This relief does not require this Court to invalidate Argentina’s expropriation law or question its sovereign authority to expropriate property. As the district court correctly noted, Argentina retains possession of the expropriated shares; the only question is whether those shares carry voting and dividend rights under the private contract Argentina voluntarily adopted. The Supreme Court in *Dunhill* made this distinction crystal clear: "The act of state doctrine does not prevent a court from enforcing a contract against a foreign sovereign when the claim arises from commercial activity." *Dunhill*, 425 U.S. at 696. Enforcing the bylaws’ penalties is no different—it is simply holding Argentina accountable for breaching its commercial obligations as a shareholder, not challenging the legitimacy of its sovereign power.  

The defense’s reliance on *W.S. Servs., Inc. v. Soviet State Co. for Foreign Economic Relations*, 879 F.2d 1046, 1051 (2d Cir. 1989), is misplaced. That case involved a claim that would have required the court to "flag the validity" of a sovereign act by declaring it illegal. But here, Petersen does not seek to declare the expropriation illegal or void. It seeks only to apply the pre-existing contractual consequences for how Argentina handled the shares *after* acquisition. As the district court explained, this is a classic distinction between the "right to act" (sovereign) and the "consequences of that action" as defined by private contract (commercial). The act of state doctrine does not shield Argentina from contractual liability for its post-expropriation conduct.  

### III. ARGENTINA VOLUNTARILY ADOPTED THE BYLAWS AS PART OF A COMMERCIAL STRATEGY—IT CANNOT NOW CLAIM IMMUNITY  
The defense asserts that "no private contractual arrangement can limit a sovereign’s inherent power of expropriation." But this argument ignores the reality that Argentina *chose* to adopt these bylaws as part of its privatization effort to attract investment. As the factual record shows, Argentina and YPF "touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO," explicitly stating that "under [YPF’s] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF’s] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares]." The prospectus further warned that any failure to follow this procedure would "result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." This was not an involuntary imposition—it was a commercial decision made to entice investors like Petersen and Repsol to participate in the IPO.  

The FSIA does not require a foreign state to explicitly waive immunity to be subject to jurisdiction for commercial activity. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2) (commercial activity exception applies when the action is "based upon" commercial activity with a direct effect in the U.S.). Argentina’s adoption of the bylaws—coupled with its subsequent conduct as a shareholder—created a commercial relationship with U.S. investors. When it breached those terms, it engaged in commercial activity that falls squarely within the FSIA exception. The defense’s claim that the bylaws "cannot waive immunity" misunderstands the law: the exception does not depend on a waiver; it depends on the nature of the conduct. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.  

### CONCLUSION  
This case is not about whether Argentina could expropriate property—it is about whether Argentina must honor its contractual promises as a shareholder. The district court correctly held that Argentina’s breach of the bylaws—refusing to make a tender offer, canceling dividends, and improperly exercising voting rights—constitutes commercial activity with a direct effect in the United States, triggering the FSIA’s commercial activity exception. It also correctly held that the act of state doctrine does not bar this private contract dispute. The defense’s attempt to recast this as a challenge to sovereign power is a red herring. The law is clear: when a foreign state enters into commercial relationships as a private actor, it must abide by the rules of those relationships. Petersen is entitled to have its breach of contract claims heard on the merits. We urge this Court to affirm the district court’s ruling and allow justice to proceed.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by both parties, and having conducted a de novo review of the district court's order, the Court affirms the denial of the motions to dismiss. The defense's arguments, while articulating valid concerns regarding the separation of powers and international comity, do not overcome the statutory mandates of the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) or the established exceptions to the act of state doctrine.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

Defendants argue that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because the "specific conduct" giving rise to Petersen's claims is Argentina's sovereign expropriation of YPF shares under Law 26,741. They contend that because the expropriation is a uniquely sovereign function, the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), does not apply. We disagree.

The commercial activity exception deprives a foreign state of immunity when the action is "based upon" a commercial activity carried on in the United States or performed elsewhere that has a direct effect in the United States. The Supreme Court has instructed that the "relevant inquiry is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of acts that could be done by a private party." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The nature of the act, not its purpose, is determinative. *Id.*

While we accept defendants' premise that the enactment of Law 26,741 and the seizure of Repsol's shares were sovereign acts, Petersen's claims are not "based upon" the validity or legality of that expropriation. Rather, the claims are based upon Argentina's subsequent conduct as a shareholder: specifically, its refusal to comply with the tender offer provisions of sections 7(e), 7(f), and 28 of the bylaws, and its exercise of voting rights and cancellation of dividends in contravention of section 7(h). These are acts of corporate governance. Private corporations routinely acquire shares, vote them, and declare (or withhold) dividends. Therefore, Argentina's participation in these activities constitutes commercial activity under the FSIA. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (distinguishing between sovereign conduct and the commercial operation of a business).

Defendants' reliance on *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 25 (1983), to argue that the "specific conduct" is the expropriation, misapprehends the nature of the complaint. The complaint seeks damages for the breach of the bylaws, not for the taking of the property itself. The taking is the factual backdrop; the actionable conduct is the breach of the contractual obligations that voluntarily governed Argentina's relationship with other shareholders. The fact that Argentina acquired the shares through a sovereign act does not immunize its subsequent commercial management of those shares. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

Furthermore, the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied. The Second Circuit has held that a direct effect exists when the consequence of the foreign state's conduct is "felt in the United States." *Transaero, Inc. v. La Fuerza Aerea Boliviana*, 30 F.3d 148, 155 (2d Cir. 1994). Here, the direct consequence of Argentina's breach—specifically, the cancellation of dividend payments—was the default on Petersen's loans and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York. This financial injury constitutes a direct effect in the United States.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants argue that the act of state doctrine bars this suit because enforcing the bylaws would require the Court to "flag the validity" of Argentina's expropriation, effectively imposing conditions on Argentina's sovereign power. This argument overreaches.

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, the doctrine is subject to established exceptions, most notably the "private contract" exception. As the Supreme Court stated in *Sabbatino*, the doctrine does not apply to "disputes concerning the private rights of persons under a contract." *Id.* at 407.

This case falls squarely within that exception. Petersen seeks to enforce the terms of the bylaws—a private contract governing the rights and obligations of YPF shareholders. The relief sought—specifically, the penalties outlined in section 7(h) and 28(C) (loss of voting and dividend rights)—does not require the Court to invalidate Law 26,741 or declare the expropriation illegal. Argentina retains possession of the expropriated shares. The Court is merely being asked to determine the *incidents* of that ownership as defined by the contract Argentina voluntarily adopted. As the Supreme Court held in *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 682, 695-96 (1976), the act of state doctrine does not bar a claim for the value of confiscated property when the claim is based on the commercial activity of the parties, not the validity of the confiscation decree. Similarly, enforcing the penalties for a breach of the bylaws does not constitute an inquiry into the validity of the expropriation itself.

Defendants' reliance on *W.S. Servs., Inc. v. Soviet State Co. for Foreign Economic Relations*, 879 F.2d 1046, 1051 (2d Cir. 1989), is misplaced. While that case stands for the proposition that courts cannot "flag the validity" of a sovereign act, enforcing a pre-existing contractual penalty is not equivalent to invalidating the sovereign act. The Court can enforce the contract by holding that Argentina, having taken the shares, must suffer the contractual consequences of that taking (loss of voting rights), without ever questioning the *sovereign right* to take them. The distinction is between the *right to act* (sovereign) and the *consequences of that action* as defined by private contract (commercial).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense's closing argument does not provide sufficient grounds to reverse the district court's ruling. The district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA, as Argentina's conduct constituted commercial activity causing a direct effect in the United States. Furthermore, the act of state doctrine does not bar adjudication of Petersen's breach of contract claims, which arise under private contractual rights rather than a challenge to sovereign authority.

**SO ORDERED.**
