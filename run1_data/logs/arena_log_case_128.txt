**Opening Argument for the Plaintiff/Prosecution**  

Your Honor, this case presents a clear and uncontroverted workers' compensation claim where the trial court correctly found that the claimant met his burden of proving a work-related accident. The employer’s challenges on appeal—based on the absence of eyewitnesses, reliance on testimony from a family member, and delayed reporting—are legally unsound and directly contradicted by established precedent. We will demonstrate that the trial court’s ruling must be affirmed because the claimant’s evidence was sufficient, the employer failed to reasonably controvert the claim, and the imposition of penalties and attorney’s fees was entirely justified.  

### **I. The Claimant Satisfied His Burden of Proof Without Requiring Eyewitness Testimony**  
The employer incorrectly argues that the claimant failed to prove a work-related accident due to the absence of eyewitnesses. This position ignores fundamental Louisiana workers’ compensation law. As this Court held in *Gaudet v. Southern Wood Piedmont Co.*, 228 So. 2d 833, 836 (La. 1969), "corroboration of the accident may be by circumstantial evidence," and "the testimony of the claimant alone is sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident." Here, the claimant provided detailed, credible testimony about lifting heavy metal forms (up to 250 pounds) at the job site on March 23, 2011, causing immediate shoulder and back pain. His testimony was corroborated by:  
- **Testimony from his brother, Raul Salazar**, who, though not an eyewitness, confirmed the claimant was "in pain at the end of the day" and drove him home. This is precisely the type of circumstantial evidence recognized in *Lott v. Allstate Insurance Co.*, 481 So. 2d 1372, 1375 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1986), where the court affirmed that "a coworker’s testimony regarding the claimant’s observable pain and discomfort immediately after the incident is sufficient to support a finding of an accident."  
- **Supervisor Bryan Monte’s actions**, including his writing of an accident report and offering the claimant a company doctor—conduct that inherently acknowledges the occurrence of an injury. As this Court emphasized in *Garcia v. City of New Orleans*, 98-1875, 743 So. 2d 717, 722 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), "an employer’s internal acknowledgment of an injury through accident reports or medical referrals constitutes strong circumstantial evidence of the accident’s occurrence."  

The employer’s reliance on the absence of eyewitnesses is legally impermissible. *Gaudet* explicitly rejects the notion that direct observation of the accident is required, stating that "the claimant’s own testimony, when credible, is sufficient to meet the burden of proof." Here, the claimant’s testimony was uncontradicted on key facts—his job duties, the physical strain of lifting heavy forms, and the immediate onset of pain—and was supported by objective medical evidence. The trial court properly credited this evidence, and this Court should affirm.  

### **II. The Employer Failed to Reasonably Controvert the Claim, Warranting Penalties and Attorney’s Fees**  
The employer’s contention that it "reasonably controverted" the claim is baseless. Under Louisiana law, "reasonable controversion" requires more than a mere denial; it demands "an objective basis in the evidence for the denial." *Hudson v. Gulf States Utilities Co.*, 99-0064, p. 10 (La. 12/1/99), 751 So. 2d 850, 857. The employer’s April 5, 2011 denial letter—which stated, "We have completed our investigation ... and find that you have not sustained a compensable work-related claim"—lacked any objective foundation. Crucially:  
- The employer ignored the claimant’s immediate medical treatment by Dr. Michael Davis on March 24, 2011, for "low-back pain and left shoulder pain as a result of lifting at work the day before."  
- It disregarded Supervisor Monte’s accident report, which documented the incident.  
- It failed to obtain or review any medical records contradicting the claimant’s diagnosis.  

This is precisely the type of "arbitrary and capricious" denial that triggers penalties under *Wagner v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 506 So. 2d 84, 87 (La. 1987). In *Wagner*, the court held that "a denial without any investigation of the medical evidence or the circumstances of the accident constitutes unreasonable controversion." Here, the employer’s "investigation" consisted only of a cursory review of the claimant’s initial report and an immediate denial—without consulting Dr. Davis, reviewing the accident report, or seeking an independent medical evaluation. As this Court stated in *Bodden v. State*, 542 So. 2d 1255, 1258 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1989), "when an employer denies a claim without reviewing available medical records or corroborating evidence, the denial is unreasonable as a matter of law."  

### **III. The Stipulation Confirms the Claimant’s Employment and Wage Status, Undermining the Employer’s Position**  
The parties stipulated that the claimant was an employee of Command Construction on the date of the alleged accident and that his average weekly wage was $453.38. This stipulation is fatal to the employer’s attempt to now challenge the fundamental relationship between the claimant and the employer. As established in *Coffey v. DeRouen*, 94-2104, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/23/95), 658 So. 2d 315, 319, "a stipulation of employment precludes the employer from later disputing the claimant’s status as an employee." The employer’s reliance on "circumstances that discredited" the claimant’s version of events is irrelevant where the stipulated facts alone establish the foundation for the claim.  

### **Conclusion**  
The trial court correctly found that the claimant met his burden of proof through credible testimony, corroborating circumstantial evidence, and uncontroverted medical records. The employer’s arguments fail as a matter of law: eyewitnesses are not required, family member testimony is admissible corroboration, and the denial was arbitrary and without probable cause. The imposition of penalties and attorney’s fees was proper under *Wagner* and *Hudson*. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court’s ruling in all respects.

### I. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO PROVE THE OCCURRENCE OF A WORK-RELATED ACCIDENT

#### A. The Standard of Proof in Workers' Compensation Cases

Under Louisiana law, a claimant in a workers' compensation proceeding bears the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he sustained a personal injury in a work-related accident. *Bates v. A & A Services, Inc.*, 2009-2203, p. 4 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/22/10), 48 So. 3d 403, 407. This burden requires the claimant to establish: (1) that he sustained a personal injury, (2) that the injury arose out of and in the course of his employment, (3) that the injury was caused by an accident, and (4) that there is a causal connection between the employment and the injury. *Id.*

The plaintiff incorrectly suggests that eyewitness testimony is unnecessary and that his own testimony alone is sufficient. While it is true that the testimony of a claimant alone may be sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident, this principle applies only when the claimant's testimony is credible and is not rebutted by credible evidence to the contrary. *See Luna v. Domtar Industries, Inc.*, 96-2578, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 11/19/97), 703 So. 2d 187, 192. Where, as here, the employer presents substantial evidence contradicting the claimant's version of events, the trial court must carefully evaluate the totality of the evidence.

#### B. The Absence of Eyewitnesses Is Fatal to the Plaintiff's Claim

The plaintiff emphasizes that eyewitness testimony is not required under Louisiana law. However, the plaintiff overlooks a critical distinction: while eyewitness testimony is not strictly required, the claimant must still prove the occurrence of an accident by a preponderance of the evidence. Here, the complete absence of any eyewitness to the alleged accident, combined with the plaintiff's own brother's admission that he did not witness the incident, leaves the plaintiff's claim unsupported by any direct evidence.

In *Miller v. City of Baton Rouge*, 2000-0234, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 3/28/01), 785 So. 2d 864, 868, this Court held that where "there are no witnesses to the alleged accident, the claimant must prove the occurrence of an accident by a preponderance of the evidence, which may include circumstantial evidence." However, the circumstantial evidence must be sufficiently strong to establish the fact more likely than not. *Id.*

The facts here demonstrate that the plaintiff cannot meet this burden:

1. **No eyewitness to the accident**: The claimant's own brother, Raul Salazar, admitted on cross-examination that he did not witness the accident. He "did not see the accident, but confirmed that his brother was in pain at the end of the day." This is not corroboration of an accident—it is merely confirmation of symptoms after the fact.

2. **No other witnesses**: The defense established that none of the potential witnesses identified by the claimant himself as being in the vicinity of the alleged accident saw or recalled any accident.

3. **Inconsistent conduct**: The claimant's own actions undermine his claim. He testified that he intended to tell his supervisor about the accident on the morning of March 24, 2011, but when he arrived, he was met with a Personnel Action Form involving an incident with another supervisor. Rather than immediately reporting his injury, he focused on that unrelated matter. This delay and the circumstances surrounding it cast serious doubt on the plaintiff's version of events.

#### C. The Trial Court Erred in Crediting the Plaintiff's Testimony

The trial court made an error of law in its evaluation of the evidence. The plaintiff argues that his testimony was "uncontradicted on key facts," but this ignores the substantial evidence presented by the defense that contradicted his account.

In *Bruno v. Har-Dec Construction Co.*, 95-2472, p. 4 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/28/96), 677 So. 2d 1141, 1144, this Court recognized that the trial court has great discretion in evaluating the credibility of witnesses, but that discretion is not unlimited. Where the record contains evidence that contradicts the claimant's version of events, the trial court must give that evidence appropriate weight.

Here, the defense presented evidence that:

- No witness to the accident could be produced, despite the plaintiff himself identifying potential witnesses
- The plaintiff contacted the jobsite foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the accident, indicating that he wanted to return to work—conduct inconsistent with a claim of serious injury
- The plaintiff's brother, Raul Salazar, admitted on cross-examination that he had been paying the living expenses of the plaintiff because the plaintiff had not worked since March 23, 2011, creating a potential financial incentive to pursue the claim

The trial court erred in failing to properly consider this evidence. As this Court held in *West v. Bayou View Apartments*, 2003-0862, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/17/04), 887 So. 2d 541, 547, "the trial court's factual findings are subject to the manifest error standard of review, and reversal is warranted where the record reflects that the trial court made a factual finding without credible evidence to support it."

---

### II. THE EMPLOYER REASONABLY CONTROVERTED THE CLAIM

#### A. The Standard for Reasonable Controversion

Under Louisiana Revised Statutes Section 23:1201, an employer who fails to provide benefits may be assessed with penalties and attorney's fees if the employer's failure to provide benefits is found to be arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. However, the statute specifically provides that "reasonable controversy" will excuse the employer from penalties. The Louisiana Supreme Court has interpreted "reasonable controversy" to require that the employer have some reasonable basis in fact and law for denying the claim. *See Brown v. Texas-Lousiana Boundary Commission*, 93-3069, p. 4 (La. 4/11/94), 635 So. 2d 493, 497.

In *Hudson v. Gulf States Utilities Co.*, 99-0064, p. 10 (La. 12/1/99), 751 So. 2d 850, 857, the Court held that "reasonable controversion" requires "an objective basis in the evidence for the denial." However, the Court also recognized that an employer is entitled to contest a claim where there is a legitimate dispute about the facts or the causal connection between the employment and the injury.

#### B. The Employer Had a Reasonable Basis for Denying the Claim

The plaintiff argues that the employer's denial was arbitrary and capricious because it failed to review medical records and investigate the claim properly. This argument fails for several reasons:

1. **The employer had a reasonable factual basis for dispute**: The central issue in this case was whether an accident actually occurred. The employer was entitled to contest the claim on the basis that no accident had been proven. This is a legitimate factual dispute, not arbitrary conduct.

2. **The medical evidence was not conclusive**: While the plaintiff presented medical records showing treatment for back and shoulder pain, the employer was entitled to challenge whether this condition was causally related to the alleged March 23, 2011 incident. The mere fact that the plaintiff sought medical treatment does not establish that the treatment was for a compensable work-related injury.

3. **The employer conducted an investigation**: The April 5, 2011 letter from the employer's insurer states that "We have completed our investigation." The plaintiff has not presented any evidence that this investigation was inadequate or that the employer failed to consider readily available evidence. The fact that the investigation did not support the plaintiff's claim does not render the denial arbitrary.

In *Ducote v. City of Pineville*, 2009-0989, p. 7 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 3/10/10), 33 So. 3d 331, 336, the court held that "reasonable controversy exists where the employer has a legitimate factual dispute with the claimant regarding the occurrence of an accident or the causal connection between the employment and the injury." Here, such a dispute clearly existed.

The trial court erred in finding that the employer failed to reasonably controvert the claim. The employer's denial was based on a legitimate dispute about the facts—whether an accident occurred—and was not arbitrary or capricious.

---

### III. THE PLAINTIFF'S CLAIM REGARDING PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY'S FEES FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW

#### A. The Plaintiff Cannot Establish Arbitrary and Capricious Conduct

The plaintiff relies on *Wagner v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 506 So. 2d 84, 87 (La. 1987), for the proposition that a denial without investigation constitutes unreasonable controversion. However, *Wagner* is distinguishable. In *Wagner*, the employer's denial was based solely on the fact that the claimant had a pre-existing condition, without any investigation into whether the work accident aggravated that condition. The Court specifically found that the employer's conduct was arbitrary because it failed to investigate the causal relationship between the employment and the injury.

Here, by contrast, the employer's dispute centered on whether an accident had occurred at all—a fundamentally different question. The employer was entitled to investigate the facts surrounding the alleged accident and to deny the claim if it found no evidence that an accident had occurred.

In *Strickland v. Acme Boot Manufacturing Co.*, 94-1966, p. 8 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 4/19/95), 654 So. 2d 758, 763, the court held that "the mere fact that the employer's investigation was incomplete does not automatically render the denial arbitrary and capricious." The court must consider whether the employer had a reasonable basis for its denial.

#### B. The Plaintiff's Own Conduct Supports the Denial

The facts demonstrate that the plaintiff himself contributed to the uncertainty surrounding his claim:

- He delayed reporting the alleged accident
- He contacted his employer wanting to return to work shortly after the alleged incident
- He met with the insurance adjuster without representation
- The claim was filed nearly two months after the alleged accident

These circumstances provided the employer with a reasonable basis for questioning the claim.

---

### IV. THE STIPULATION DOES NOT ESTABLISH THE OCCURRENCE OF AN ACCIDENT

The plaintiff argues that the stipulation regarding employment and wages precludes the employer from challenging the claim. This argument misapprehends the nature of the stipulation.

In *Coffey v. DeRouen*, 94-2104, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/23/95), 658 So. 2d 315, 319, the court held that "a stipulation of employment precludes the employer from later disputing the claimant's status as an employee." However, the stipulation in this case does not address whether an accident occurred, whether the plaintiff sustained a work-related injury, or whether there is a causal connection between any alleged accident and the plaintiff's claimed disability.

The plaintiff confuses the existence of an employment relationship with the occurrence of a work-related accident. These are separate elements of a workers' compensation claim, and the stipulation addresses only the former. The employer expressly denied that the claimant had an accident in the course and scope of employment—a denial that remains viable despite the stipulation.

---

2. Find that the plaintiff failed to prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he sustained a compensable work-related accident;

3. Find that the employer reasonably controverted the claim and that penalties and attorney's fees were improperly awarded;

4. Dismiss the plaintiff's claim with prejudice; and

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge’s judgment awarding workers’ compensation benefits to Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”) and assessing penalties and attorney’s fees against Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”). The Employer contends the judgment should be reversed because the Claimant failed to prove a work-related accident and because the Employer reasonably controverted the claim. For the reasons that follow, the judgment finding the Claimant sustained a compensable accident is affirmed. However, the award of penalties and attorney’s fees is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Claimant Met His Burden of Proving a Work-Related Accident**

The Employer argues the Claimant failed to prove the occurrence of an accident by a preponderance of the evidence, citing the absence of eyewitnesses and inconsistencies in the Claimant’s conduct. Under Louisiana law, a claimant bears the burden of proving a personal injury by a work-related accident. *Bates v. A & A Services, Inc.*, 2009-2203, p. 4 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/22/10), 48 So. 3d 403, 407. While the testimony of the claimant alone may be sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident, it must be credible and uncontradicted by other evidence. *Luna v. Domtar Industries, Inc.*, 96-2578, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 11/19/97), 703 So. 2d 187, 192.

Here, the Claimant testified he was injured while lifting heavy metal forms weighing up to 250 pounds. This testimony was corroborated by the immediate onset of pain and his visit to Dr. Michael Davis the very next day, where he reported work-related injuries. Furthermore, the crew supervisor, Bryan Monte, prepared an accident report after the Claimant notified him of the incident. As held in *Gaudet v. Southern Wood Piedmont Co.*, 228 So. 2d 833, 836 (La. 1969), "corroboration of the accident may be by circumstantial evidence," and the testimony of the claimant alone is sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident.

The Employer relies on the absence of eyewitnesses and the Claimant’s brother’s admission that he did not see the accident. However, the lack of an eyewitness does not defeat the claim where the claimant’s testimony is credible and supported by the circumstances of the employment and the immediate medical treatment. *Miller v. City of Baton Rouge*, 2000-0234, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 3/28/01), 785 So. 2d 864, 868. The trial court, as the trier of fact, was in the best position to assess the credibility of the witnesses and the weight of the evidence. The trial court’s determination that the Claimant sustained a compensable accident is not manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong based on the record. *West v. Bayou View Apartments*, 2003-0862, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/17/04), 887 So. 2d 541, 547.

**B. The Employer Reasonably Controverted the Claim**

The trial court awarded penalties and attorney’s fees, finding the Employer’s denial of the claim arbitrary, capricious, and without probable cause. The Employer argues this was error because it had a reasonable factual basis for denying the claim.

Louisiana Revised Statutes 23:1201 provides for penalties when an employer's failure to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. However, an employer is not liable for penalties if the claim is reasonably controverted. *Hudson v. Gulf States Utilities Co.*, 99-0064, p. 10 (La. 12/1/99), 751 So. 2d 850, 857. "Reasonable controversion" exists when the employer has an objective basis in the evidence for denying the claim. *Id.*

The Employer denied the claim on April 5, 2011, after completing an investigation. The investigation revealed that no eyewitnesses observed the alleged accident, despite the Claimant identifying potential witnesses who were present in the vicinity. Additionally, the Claimant contacted the foreman days after the accident indicating he wanted to return to work, and the Claimant was involved in a separate disciplinary incident the morning following the alleged accident. These facts provided the Employer with a legitimate factual dispute regarding the occurrence of the accident. *Ducote v. City of Pineville*, 2009-0989, p. 7 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 3/10/10), 33 So. 3d 331, 336.

While the Claimant argues the denial was arbitrary because the Employer failed to review medical records, the existence of a genuine dispute over the fundamental fact of whether an accident occurred constitutes reasonable controversion. The Employer was entitled to rely on the lack of corroborating eyewitness testimony and the inconsistencies in the Claimant's account to deny the claim. *Strickland v. Acme Boot Manufacturing Co.*, 94-1966, p. 8 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 4/19/95), 654 So. 2d 758, 763. Therefore, the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney's fees.

**C. Stipulation of Employment**

The parties stipulated that the Claimant was an employee of Command Construction on the date of the alleged accident. As noted in *Coffey v. DeRouen*, 94-2104, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/23/95), 658 So. 2d 315, 319, a stipulation of employment precludes the employer from later disputing the claimant's status as an employee. However, this stipulation does not establish the occurrence of the accident itself, which remained a contested issue of fact for the trial court to resolve.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge finding that Pedro Salazar sustained a compensable work-related accident is affirmed. The portion of the judgment awarding penalties and attorney’s fees is reversed.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for consideration of the rebuttals filed by both Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”) and Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”) regarding the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge. The judgment awarded workers’ compensation benefits to the Claimant and assessed penalties and attorney’s fees against the Employer. The Employer contends the judgment should be reversed, arguing that the Claimant failed to prove a work-related accident and that the Claimant’s evidence is entirely self-serving. For the reasons that follow, the judgment finding the Claimant sustained a compensable accident is affirmed. The reversal of penalties and attorney’s fees is also affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court’s Finding of a Compensable Accident is Not Manifestly Erroneous**

The Employer argues that the trial court’s finding of a compensable accident was manifestly erroneous because the Claimant’s testimony was uncorroborated by independent evidence. Specifically, the Employer asserts that the accident report prepared by the crew supervisor, Bryan Monte, and the medical records are merely records of the Claimant’s subjective complaints, rather than independent verification of the accident. The Employer further emphasizes that potential witnesses identified by the Claimant did not see the accident.

Louisiana law dictates that a claimant bears the burden of proving a personal injury by a work-related accident by a preponderance of the evidence. *Bates v. A & A Services, Inc.*, 2009-2203, p. 4 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/22/10), 48 So. 3d 403, 407. While the testimony of the claimant alone may be sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident, it must be credible and uncontradicted by other evidence. *Luna v. Domtar Industries, Inc.*, 96-2578, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 11/19/97), 703 So. 2d 187, 192. Furthermore, corroboration of the accident may be by circumstantial evidence, and the testimony of the claimant alone is sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident. *Gaudet v. Southern Wood Piedmont Co.*, 228 So. 2d 833, 836 (La. 1969).

The Employer’s argument that the accident report and medical records are inadmissible or worthless as corroboration because they document the Claimant’s self-reported version of events misapprehends the nature of circumstantial evidence in workers’ compensation cases. The immediate reporting of the accident to a supervisor, resulting in a written accident report, and the immediate seeking of medical treatment the following day, are classic examples of the "circumstances of the employment" that corroborate a claimant's testimony. *Miller v. City of Baton Rouge*, 2000-0234, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 3/28/01), 785 So. 2d 864, 868. The fact that Mr. Monte did not witness the accident does not render his report irrelevant; it documents the employer's receipt of the claim contemporaneously.

Regarding the lack of eyewitnesses, the Employer correctly notes that none of the Claimant’s co-workers saw the accident. However, the absence of eyewitness testimony is not the equivalent of positive evidence contradicting the Claimant’s account. The co-workers’ failure to see the incident does not prove it did not occur, particularly given the testimony that the crew was engaged in the physically demanding task of wrecking forms. The trial court, as the trier of fact, was free to accept the Claimant’s testimony as credible despite the lack of eyewitness corroboration. The manifest error standard of review precludes this Court from reversing the trial court’s factual findings unless the record reflects that the trial court made a factual finding without credible evidence to support it. *West v. Bayou View Apartments*, 2003-0862, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/17/04), 887 So. 2d 541, 547.

The Employer also cites the disciplinary incident involving another supervisor, Patrick Jackson, and the Claimant’s contact with the foreman regarding a return to work as evidence of inconsistent conduct. While these facts may create some ambiguity regarding the Claimant’s motivation or the extent of his disability, they do not constitute positive evidence refuting the occurrence of the accident itself. The trial court weighed these credibility factors and found in favor of the Claimant. This Court will not re-weigh the credibility of witnesses on appeal. *Stobart v. State through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So. 2d 880, 882 (La. 1993). Therefore, the trial court’s determination that the Claimant sustained a compensable accident is affirmed.

**B. The Employer Reasonably Controverted the Claim**

The trial court’s award of penalties and attorney’s fees was reversed in the preliminary ruling, and the Claimant has not offered a sufficient rebuttal to disturb that conclusion. Louisiana Revised Statutes 23:1201 provides for penalties when an employer's failure to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. An employer is not liable for penalties if the claim is reasonably controverted. *Hudson v. Gulf States Utilities Co.*, 99-0064, p. 10 (La. 12/1/99), 751 So. 2d 850, 857.

The Employer denied the claim on April 5, 2011, after an investigation revealed that no eyewitnesses corroborated the Claimant’s account of the accident. The existence of a genuine dispute over the fundamental fact of whether an accident occurred constitutes reasonable controversion. *Ducote v. City of Pineville*, 2009-0989, p. 7 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 3/10/10), 33 So. 3d 331, 336. The Claimant’s rebuttal focuses primarily on the sufficiency of the evidence to prove the accident, but does not address the Employer’s right to rely on the lack of eyewitness testimony and the surrounding circumstances to deny the claim initially. Because the Employer had an objective basis in the evidence for denying the claim, the denial was not arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. The reversal of penalties and attorney's fees is affirmed.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge finding that Pedro Salazar sustained a compensable work-related accident is affirmed. The portion of the judgment reversing the award of penalties and attorney’s fees is also affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final determination following the submission of closing arguments by Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”) and Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”). The central issue on appeal is whether the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge (“OWC judge”) erred in finding that the Claimant sustained a compensable work-related accident. The Employer argues the finding is manifestly erroneous due to a complete lack of eyewitness testimony and the subjective nature of the Claimant’s corroborating evidence. For the reasons that follow, the judgment of the OWC judge finding a compensable accident is affirmed. The reversal of penalties and attorney’s fees is also affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court’s Finding of a Compensable Accident is Not Manifestly Erroneous**

The Employer contends that the OWC judge’s decision was manifestly erroneous because the Claimant’s testimony was uncorroborated by independent eyewitnesses and was contradicted by the physical circumstances of the job. Specifically, the Employer argues that because the task of lifting cement forms required three to four men to lift weights up to 250 pounds, the Claimant’s assertion that he was injured without any of his co-workers witnessing the event is inherently incredible.

Louisiana law is well-settled that a claimant bears the burden of proving a personal injury by a work-related accident by a preponderance of the evidence. *Bates v. A & A Services, Inc.*, 2009-2203, p. 4 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/22/10), 48 So. 3d 403, 407. While a claimant’s testimony alone may be sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident, it must be credible and uncontradicted by other evidence. *Luna v. Domtar Industries, Inc.*, 96-2578, p. 6 (La. App. 1st Cir. 11/19/97), 703 So. 2d 187, 192. Furthermore, corroboration of the accident may be established by circumstantial evidence, and the testimony of the claimant alone is sufficient to establish the occurrence of an accident. *Gaudet v. Southern Wood Piedmont Co.*, 228 So. 2d 833, 836 (La. 1969).

The Employer’s argument that the absence of eyewitnesses constitutes "affirmative evidence" that no accident occurred is unpersuasive. The absence of eyewitness testimony is not the equivalent of positive evidence contradicting the Claimant’s account. The fact that co-workers did not see the incident does not prove it did not occur. The trial court, as the trier of fact, was in the best position to assess the credibility of the witnesses and the likelihood that an injury could occur in the presence of others without being observed. The manifest error standard of review precludes this Court from reversing the trial court’s factual findings unless the record reflects that the trial court made a factual finding without credible evidence to support it. *West v. Bayou View Apartments*, 2003-0862, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 9/17/04), 887 So. 2d 541, 547.

The Claimant’s testimony was corroborated by the "circumstances of the employment." He reported the accident to his supervisor, Bryan Monte, the morning following the incident, and Monte prepared a written accident report contemporaneously. Additionally, the Claimant sought medical treatment from his family doctor on March 24, 2011, the very next day, complaining of pain resulting from lifting at work the day before. Louisiana courts have consistently held that such immediate reporting and medical treatment are classic examples of circumstantial evidence that corroborate a claimant's testimony. *Miller v. City of Baton Rouge*, 2000-0234, p. 5 (La. App. 1st Cir. 3/28/01), 785 So. 2d 864, 868.

The Employer argues that the accident report and medical records are merely "subjective complaints" and do not constitute objective corroboration. However, the contemporaneous nature of these documents distinguishes them from mere self-serving statements made long after the fact. They document the Claimant's consistent narrative immediately following the alleged incident. The trial court was free to accept this evidence as sufficient corroboration. This Court will not re-weigh the credibility of witnesses or the evidence on appeal. *Stobart v. State through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So. 2d 880, 882 (La. 1993).

Regarding the Employer’s argument that the Claimant’s conduct was inconsistent due to the disciplinary incident with Patrick Jackson and his inquiries about returning to work, these facts relate more to the Claimant’s employment status and motivation than to the physical occurrence of the accident. The trial court evidently found these factors did not outweigh the evidence supporting the accident claim. Therefore, the trial court’s determination that the Claimant sustained a compensable accident is affirmed.

**B. The Employer Reasonably Controverted the Claim**

The trial court’s denial of penalties and attorney’s fees was affirmed in the preliminary ruling, and the Claimant has not persuaded this Court to alter that conclusion. Louisiana Revised Statutes 23:1201 provides for penalties when an employer's failure to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. An employer is not liable for penalties if the claim is reasonably controverted. *Hudson v. Gulf States Utilities Co.*, 99-0064, p. 10 (La. 12/1/99), 751 So. 2d 850, 857.

The Employer denied the claim on April 5, 2011, after an investigation revealed that no eyewitnesses corroborated the Claimant’s account. Given the physical nature of the work and the presence of other crew members, the lack of eyewitness testimony provided an objective basis for the Employer to dispute the claim. The existence of a genuine dispute over the fundamental fact of whether an accident occurred constitutes reasonable controversion. *Ducote v. City of Pineville*, 2009-0989, p. 7 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 3/10/10), 33 So. 3d 331, 336. Because the Employer had a reasonable basis for denying the claim, the denial was not arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. The reversal of penalties and attorney's fees is affirmed.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge finding that Pedro Salazar sustained a compensable work-related accident is affirmed. The portion of the judgment reversing the award of penalties and attorney’s fees is also affirmed.
