COURT ARENA LOG - Case 95
FACTS:
Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) appeals the award of attorney’s fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). Evergreén contends that the award is barred by res judicata and that Djuric waived its claim to attorney’s fees. Because we conclude that the award is not barred by res judicata and Djuric has not waived its claim, we affirm.

Facts and Procedural History

This is the second appeal in a dispute between two freight companies. The first chapter in this case was set forth by this Court in August 2012:

Evergreen is the owner of certain freight storage containers, and Djuric is a freight transport company. Both are parties to the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement (“UIIA”). Djuric has on occasion been hired by Hub City Terminals, Inc. (“Hub”) to transport freight. As relevant here, in 2010 and 2011, Hub hired Djuric to transport freight one-way from Chicago to Indianapolis on behalf of Target Corporation (“Target”). Pursuant to Hub’s request, Djuric transported the freight to Indianapolis using Evergreen’s containers and received an authorized signature from Target. Target, in turn, agreed with Djuric to be responsible for any and all charges and/or fees assessed for the containers.
On June 9, 2011, Evergreen filed a complaint on account against Djuric. According to Evergreen, Djuric was responsible for the failure to return the containers owned by Evergreen. Evergreen alleged damages in excess of $34,000 for per diem charges.
Between August and October 2011, Evergreen and Djuric filed cross motions for summary judgment. In support of its motion, Evergreen designated the UIIA, on which it had based its claim. The UIIA defines “[p]er [djiem” charges as “[cjharges to be paid when intermo-dal [ejquipment is not returned by the end of the allowable free time to its origin or another location, as previously agreed to by the Parties.” Further, a “[pjrovider may permit some period of *339uncompensated use and thereafter impose use charges,” but any such charges must be “set forth in [an] Addendum.” The UIIA defines “Addendum/Addenda” as the “[pjroviders’ schedule of economic and commercial terms not appropriate for inclusion in the [UIIA] and other terms and conditions of [equipment use,” that is, a separate collateral agreement. And the UIIA requires that any agreement, including an addendum, be in writing and signed by the parties. Along with the UIIA, Evergreen designated the affidavit of Ted Dost, one of its managers, who stated that the UIIA required Djuric to pay per diem charges to Evergreen. But Evergreen did not designate a separate written agreement with Djuric, and, in support of its own motion for summary judgment, Djuric designated the affidavit of Jim Djuric, president of Djuric. According to Jim’s affidavit, Djuric had no separate agreements with Evergreen. And, when pressed by the trial court at the summary judgment hearing, Evergreen’s counsel was unable to confirm that any agreement other than the UIIA existed between Evergreen and Djuric. In any event, no separate written agreement was designated.
On November 16, the trial court entered summary judgment for Djuric on the basis that Evergreen’s “designated evidence fails to establish any agreement by which [Djuric] is liable for any per diem [charges]. [Djuric’s] designated evidence establishes that no such agreement exists.” At the same time, the court denied Evergreen’s motion for summary judgment.
Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc., No. 45A03-1112-CC-575, 2012 WL 3129130 (Ind.Ct.App. Aug. 2, 2012) (citations omitted). In its summary-judgment motion, Djuric had argued that Evergreen’s breach-of-contract claim was frivolous, and sought attorney’s fees pursuant to Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1.1 The trial court denied Djuric’s request for attorney’s fees under Section 34-52-1-1. This Court affirmed the trial court’s grant of summary judgment.2 The opinion was certified on September 3, 2012.

Two weeks later, Djuric filed a request for attorney’s fees pursuant to the UIIA. In an action for breach or enforcement of the UIIA, the UIIA entitles the prevailing party to recover reasonable attorney’s fees. See Appellee’s App. p. 19. After a hearing, the trial court granted Djuric’s motion and awarded it approximately $12,000 in attorney’s fees.

Evergreen now appeals.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE FOR APPELLANT EVERGREEN SHIPPING AGENCY CORP.

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF INDIANA

---

Your Honor, respectfully, this Court should reverse the trial court's award of attorney's fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. The plaintiff's opening argument, while artfully crafted, mischaracterizes the nature of the claims and ignores fundamental principles of claim preclusion and procedural fairness. The award should be reversed for the following reasons.

## I. THE ATTORNEY'S FEE CLAIM IS BARRED BY RES JUDICATA

The plaintiff contends that the attorney's fee claim is a "separate cause of action" that could not have been litigated in the prior proceeding. This characterization is fundamentally incorrect and contradicts the well-established doctrine of claim preclusion.

### A. The UIIA Was Central to the Original Litigation

The record demonstrates that the UIIA was not some peripheral document that emerged only after the underlying dispute was resolved. To the contrary, Evergreen designated the UIIA as the very foundation of its claim in its summary judgment motion. Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc., No. 45A03-1112-CC-575, 2012 WL 3129130 (Ind.Ct.App. Aug. 2, 2012). The UIIA was the sole agreement upon which Evergreen predicated Djuric's liability for per diem charges. Djuric was aware from the inception of the litigation that the UIIA governed the parties' relationship and contained provisions regarding charges, fees, and equipment use.

Under these circumstances, any claim to attorney's fees under the UIIA could have been litigated in the original action. As this Court recognized in Small v. Centerre Bank of Indiana, 51 N.E.3d 716 (Ind. 2016), res judicata bars claims that arise from the same transaction or occurrence as prior litigation, regardless of whether they were actually raised. The fee-shifting provision in the UIIA is not a separate cause of action—it is a component of the same contractual dispute that was fully litigated in 2011 and 2012.

### B. The Fee Claim Did Not Become "Ripened" After Summary Judgment

The plaintiff's argument that the fee claim "did not exist" until Djuric prevailed on the underlying claim conflates the timing of a claim's maturity with its existence. Djuric's right to recover attorney's fees under the UIIA arose from the same contractual relationship that was the subject of the original lawsuit. The fact that the amount of fees could not be calculated until after a judgment does not create a new, independent cause of action.

In Davison v. State, 816 N.E.2d 120 (Ind. Ct. App. 2004), this Court held that claim preclusion applies when the same evidence would support both claims. Here, the UIIA was the very evidence supporting Evergreen's per diem claim—and conversely, the same UIIA supports Djuric's fee claim. To hold that Djuric could wait until after losing the first case to assert a fee claim under the very same agreement would render res judicata meaningless.

### C. Djuric Had a Full and Fair Opportunity to Litigate Fee Recovery

The plaintiff argues that it "could not have sought UIIA fees until the trial court ruled in their favor." This is incorrect. Djuric could have filed a contingent or conditional claim for attorney's fees in the original proceeding, preserving its right to fees should it prevail. Indiana trial courts routinely address fee claims in the same proceeding as the underlying merits. By failing to assert the UIIA fee claim in the first action—either in its answer, in its summary judgment briefing, or in its motion for fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1—Djuric allowed the matter to be fully litigated without raising the issue.

In P.L.M. Realty Co. v. Indiana Land Co., 638 N.E.2d 1354 (Ind. Ct. App. 1994), this Court emphasized that res judicata serves the important policy of preventing piecemeal litigation and promoting finality. Allowing Djuric to split its claim for fees from the underlying contract dispute undermines these fundamental interests.

## II. DJURIC WAIVED ITS RIGHT TO ATTORNEY'S FEES UNDER THE UIIA

Beyond preclusion, Djuric's claim fails because it waived its right to fees under the UIIA.

### A. Djuric Made a Clear Choice to Pursue Fees Under a Different Theory

The procedural history demonstrates that Djuric was aware of its potential entitlement to attorney's fees from the very beginning of the litigation. In its original motion for summary judgment, Djuric specifically "sought attorney's fees pursuant to Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1." The trial court denied this request. Djuric did not appeal that denial, nor did it raise the UIIA as an alternative basis for fees at that time.

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. See Keesling v. Baker, 571 N.E.2d 565 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991). When Djuric elected to pursue fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1—a statutory provision that applies to frivolous claims—it made a strategic choice about how to frame its fee claim. The statute provides for fees when a claim is frivolous or brought in bad faith. By invoking this specific statutory provision, Djuric necessarily represented that Evergreen's claim was of that character.

Having lost on that theory, Djuric cannot now assert an entirely different contractual basis for fees under the UIIA. This is precisely the type of claim-splitting that waiver doctrine is designed to prevent. As this Court stated in Bunch v. Citizens Bank of Columbus, 378 N.E.2d 858 (Ind. Ct. App. 1978), a party's deliberate choice to pursue one legal theory precludes it from later pursuing an alternative theory that could have been raised concurrently.

### B. The UIIA Fee Provision Was Available From the Outset

The plaintiff suggests that its UIIA fee claim is independent and "self-executing," implying it could be raised at any time. But the UIIA was part of the original case from day one. Djuric cannot claim ignorance of a provision in an agreement that was the centerpiece of the litigation. The UIIA's fee-shifting clause was not hidden—it was contained in the same agreement that Evergreen invoked as the basis for its per diem claim.

When Djuric chose to seek fees under the frivolous claim statute rather than under the UIIA, it made a binding election. The trial court's denial of that request should have been the end of the matter. Instead, Djuric waited until two weeks after this Court affirmed the summary judgment—after it became clear that Evergreen could not recover on its per diem claim—to assert the UIIA fee provision. This timing alone demonstrates that Djuric was attempting to relitigate the fee issue under a different label.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S POLICY ARGUMENTS DO NOT SUPPORT A CONTRARY RESULT

The plaintiff urges this Court to affirm based on the "plain terms" of the UIIA and the policy of ensuring the prevailing party is made whole. But this argument cuts both ways. Evergreen, too, is a prevailing party in the original litigation—it successfully defended against Evergreen's per diem claim. If the UIIA's fee-shifting provision is to be enforced, it should apply to both parties, not merely to whichever party happens to file a fee motion after judgment.

Moreover, the plaintiff's reliance on the UIIA's "plain terms" ignores the fundamental problem: the UIIA was found insufficient to establish Evergreen's per diem claim. If the UIIA does not contain the necessary terms to establish liability for per diem charges—a finding affirmed by this Court—then it equally cannot establish liability for attorney's fees without additional evidence of a separate agreement. The trial court's reasoning in the original summary judgment motion applies with equal force here.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the trial court's award of attorney's fees. Djuric's fee claim is barred by the doctrine of res judicata, as it arises from the same transaction and occurrence as the original litigation. Additionally, Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by electing to pursue fees under a different statutory theory in the original proceeding. The plaintiff's attempt to relitigate this matter under a different legal theory should be rejected.

Respectfully submitted,

**Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp., Appellant**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. is **REVERSED**.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court must determine whether the trial court erred in awarding attorney’s fees to Djuric pursuant to the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement (“UIIA”). Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. appeals, arguing that the award is barred by the doctrine of res judicata and that Djuric waived its right to claim such fees. After reviewing the parties’ opening arguments, Evergreen’s arguments are legally persuasive and supported by controlling precedent, whereas Djuric’s arguments lack legal citation and fail to address the established legal constraints.

**I. Res Judicata**

Evergreen argues that Djuric’s claim for attorney’s fees is barred by res judicata because it arises from the same transaction or occurrence as the prior litigation. We agree.

Res judicata, or claim preclusion, bars claims that arise from the same transaction or occurrence as prior litigation, regardless of whether they were actually raised. See *Small v. Centerre Bank of Indiana*, 51 N.E.3d 716 (Ind. 2016). In the prior action, Evergreen designated the UIIA as the sole foundation of its claim for per diem charges. The UIIA was the central document governing the parties’ relationship and was the subject of the summary judgment hearing. Because the UIIA was the basis for the underlying dispute, any claim for attorney’s fees stemming from that same agreement arises from the same transaction. See *Davison v. State*, 816 N.E.2d 120 (Ind. Ct. App. 2004) (holding that claim preclusion applies when the same evidence would support both claims).

Djuric contends that the fee claim is a separate cause of action that did not exist until it prevailed on the underlying claim. However, the fact that the claim ripened into a right to payment after judgment does not transform it into a separate transaction for res judicata purposes. Djuric could have filed a contingent or conditional claim for attorney’s fees in the original proceeding, preserving its right to fees should it prevail. By failing to do so, and by waiting until two weeks after the appeal was certified to assert its UIIA fee claim, Djuric has engaged in piecemeal litigation. This practice is contrary to the policies of finality and judicial economy underlying the res judicata doctrine. See *P.L.M. Realty Co. v. Indiana Land Co.*, 638 N.E.2d 1354 (Ind. Ct. App. 1994).

**II. Waiver**

Evergreen further argues that Djuric waived its right to seek attorney’s fees under the UIIA by pursuing fees under a different theory in the original action. We agree.

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. See *Keesling v. Baker*, 571 N.E.2d 565 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991). The record demonstrates that Djuric was aware of the UIIA and its provisions from the inception of the litigation, as the UIIA was the centerpiece of Evergreen’s claim. Despite this knowledge, Djuric made a strategic choice to seek attorney’s fees solely under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1, a statute governing frivolous claims. The trial court denied this request.

By electing to pursue fees under the statutory provision rather than the UIIA, Djuric made a deliberate choice to pursue one legal theory over another. A party’s deliberate choice to pursue one legal theory precludes it from later pursuing an alternative theory that could have been raised concurrently. See *Bunch v. Citizens Bank of Columbus*, 378 N.E.2d 858 (Ind. Ct. App. 1978). Djuric cannot now assert an entirely different contractual basis for fees after its initial statutory theory failed. This constitutes an impermissible attempt to relitigate the fee issue under a different label.

**CONCLUSION**

Djuric’s arguments in favor of the fee award are unsupported by legal citation and fail to overcome the well-established doctrines of res judicata and waiver. Evergreen has demonstrated that the fee claim arises from the same transaction as the prior litigation and that Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by electing a different remedy in the first instance. Therefore, the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees is reversed.

# REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT  
# FOR APPELLEE DJURIC TRUCKING, INC.  

IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF INDIANA  

---

Your Honor, the defense’s opening statement mischaracterizes the law, ignores critical procedural realities, and misapplies foundational doctrines to evade the clear terms of the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement ("UIIA"). Evergreen’s arguments rest on flawed premises: it claims res judicata bars Djuric’s fee claim because it "arose from the same transaction," and that Djuric waived its contractual right to fees by pursuing statutory remedies. Both assertions are legally unsound. The fee claim could not have been raised in the prior action because it was not ripe until Djuric prevailed, and Djuric’s election to seek statutory fees did not forfeit its separate contractual right under the UIIA. This Court must affirm the award.  

## I. RES JUDICATA DOES NOT BAR DJURIC’S FEE CLAIM BECAUSE IT COULD NOT HAVE BEEN RAISED IN THE PRIOR PROCEEDING  

The defense insists the fee claim is barred by claim preclusion because it "arose from the same transaction" as the underlying dispute. This is fundamentally incorrect. Res judicata applies only to claims that were or *could have been* raised in the prior action. Here, Djuric had no opportunity to assert its fee claim before the original judgment because the right to fees under the UIIA is inherently contingent on prevailing in the underlying dispute.  

The UIIA’s fee-shifting provision is not self-executing; it activates only upon a party’s success in the litigation. In the prior action, Evergreen initiated the lawsuit, alleging Djuric owed per diem charges under the UIIA. The trial court granted summary judgment for Djuric precisely because Evergreen failed to prove any contractual basis for those charges—specifically, it designated no written addendum or separate agreement to support its claim, as required by the UIIA’s terms. Until that judgment, Djuric was merely a defendant defending against Evergreen’s allegations. It had no "prevailing party" status to trigger the fee provision.  

The defense’s argument that Djuric could have filed a "contingent or conditional claim" for fees in the original proceeding ignores reality. Courts universally recognize that contractual fee claims are not ripe for adjudication until the underlying merits are resolved. A defendant cannot assert a fee claim before the court has determined whether it has prevailed. To hold otherwise would force parties to litigate hypothetical fee entitlements prematurely, undermining judicial efficiency and fairness. The defense’s reliance on principles of "finality" is misplaced here—res judicata exists to prevent relitigation of issues already decided, not to bar claims that could not possibly have been litigated earlier.  

## II. DJURIC DID NOT WAIVE ITS RIGHT TO FEES UNDER THE UIIA  

The defense claims Djuric "waived" its UIIA fee claim by seeking statutory fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1 in the prior action. This is equally flawed. Waiver requires a knowing and intentional surrender of a specific right. Djuric never surrendered its contractual right under the UIIA; it simply pursued an alternative remedy that was available at the time.  

The statutory fee provision and the UIIA’s fee-shifting clause are distinct legal bases with different requirements. The statute applies only to frivolous or bad-faith claims, while the UIIA’s provision is contractual and triggered by the outcome of the litigation. Djuric was not required to pursue both theories simultaneously. When it sought fees under the statute, it was acting in good faith to challenge Evergreen’s original claim—only to later discover that its victory on the merits independently entitled it to fees under the UIIA.  

The defense’s assertion that Djuric "made a deliberate choice" to abandon the UIIA claim is unsupported. At the time of the original motion, the UIIA fee right was not yet matured. Djuric had no basis to assert it because the court had not yet ruled on the merits. Waiver cannot occur for a right that did not yet exist. By waiting until after judgment to seek fees under the UIIA, Djuric acted precisely as the agreement contemplated—only when it was confirmed as the prevailing party.  

## III. THE DEFENSE’S POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE UIIA’S TERMS AND PURPOSE  

The defense argues that affirming the fee award would "relitigate" the matter under a "different label." ## I. DJURIC'S FEE CLAIM IS BARRED BY RES JUDICATA BECAUSE IT ARISES FROM THE SAME TRANSACTION AS THE PRIOR LITIGATION

The plaintiff argues that res judicata does not apply because the fee claim "could not have been raised" in the prior action. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

### A. The Fee Claim Arises From the Same Transaction

The UIIA was the centerpiece of Evergreen's original complaint. Evergreen alleged that Djuric was liable for per diem charges under the UIIA, designated the UIIA as its sole evidentiary basis, and pursued its entire claim around the UIIA's terms. The trial court's summary judgment ruling turned entirely on whether the UIIA, as a matter of contract law, established Djuric's liability. See Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc., No. 45A03-1112-CC-575, 2012 WL 3129130 (Ind. Ct. App. Aug. 2, 2012).

The fee claim now at issue arises from precisely the same agreement—the UIIA. The UIIA's fee-shifting provision is contained within the same document that governed the underlying dispute. As this Court correctly observed in its preliminary ruling, "any claim for attorney fees stemming from that same agreement arises from the same transaction." This is the essence of claim preclusion. See Small v. Centerre Bank of Indiana, 51 N.E.3d 716 (Ind. 2016) (holding that res judicata bars claims arising from the same transaction or occurrence as prior litigation).

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish the fee claim by arguing it is a "separate cause of action." This is a semantic trick. A cause of action is defined by the transaction from which it arises. Here, both the underlying breach claim and the fee claim derive from the UIIA and the parties' dispute over container usage. They are not separate transactions—they are different legal consequences flowing from the same set of facts.

### B. The "Ripening" Argument Does Not Avoid Res Judicata

The plaintiff argues that the fee claim could not have been raised earlier because it was "not ripe" until Djuric prevailed. This argument conflates the timing of a claim's maturity with its substantive origin.

Res judicata does not require that a claim have fully matured before the prior litigation; it requires only that the claim could have been raised in the prior proceeding. The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that an unmatured, contingent claim is categorically exempt from claim preclusion. Such a rule would eviscerate res judicata, as virtually every fee claim is contingent on the outcome of the underlying litigation.

The proper approach is illustrated by Davison v. State, 816 N.E.2d 120 (Ind. Ct. App. 2004), which this Court cited in its preliminary ruling. In Davison, this Court held that claim preclusion applies when the same evidence would support both claims. Here, the same evidence—the UIIA, the parties' relationship, and the underlying dispute—supports both Evergreen's per diem claim and Djuric's fee claim. The fee claim is not a new transaction; it is a derivative claim that depends on the same contractual relationship that was already litigated.

The plaintiff argues that requiring a party to file a "contingent or conditional claim" for fees would be "premature." However, this is precisely what sophisticated parties do in complex litigation. A defendant who believes it may be entitled to fees under a contract can file a contingent claim or preserve the issue in its answer or counterclaim. By failing to do so, Djuric elected to forego its contractual theory and await the outcome of the statutory fee motion. This strategic choice does not immunize the fee claim from claim preclusion.

### C. The Policy of Finality Supports Application of Res Judicata

The plaintiff dismisses concerns about "piecemeal litigation" as "policy arguments" that should yield to the UIIA's terms. This gets matters exactly backwards. The UIIA's fee-shifting provision must be read in conjunction with the procedural rules that govern when and how such claims may be asserted. The policy of finality is not an abstract consideration—it is a fundamental principle that prevents endless relitigation of issues arising from the same dispute.

In P.L.M. Realty Co. v. Indiana Land Co., 638 N.E.2d 1354 (Ind. Ct. App. 1994), this Court emphasized that res judicata serves the twin goals of finality and judicial economy. Allowing Djuric to assert a fee claim two weeks after the prior litigation concluded, based on the same agreement that was already litigated, undermines both goals. The plaintiff essentially asks this Court to create an exception to res judicata for any claim that is "contingent" on the outcome—a rule that would invite precisely the kind of strategic manipulation Djuric has engaged in here.

### A. Djuric Made a Deliberate Strategic Choice

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. See Keesling v. Baker, 571 N.E.2d 565 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991). The record demonstrates that Djuric was fully aware of the UIIA and its provisions from the inception of the litigation. The UIIA was the centerpiece of Evergreen's claim. The plaintiff argues that pursuing statutory fees was "an alternative remedy" that did not preclude pursuing contractual fees. This is incorrect. The UIIA's fee provision was always part of the agreement. Djuric was aware of this provision from the start. The fact that the provision was contingent on Djuric prevailing does not mean the right was non-existent; it means the right was unmatured. Djuric's conduct demonstrates such awareness and intentional relinquishment. Djuric knew about the UIIA. Djuric knew about its fee-shifting provision. Djuric made a deliberate choice to pursue statutory fees instead. When the trial court denied the statutory fee request, Djuric did not seek reconsideration or appeal that ruling. ### C. The Plaintiff Misreads the UIIA's Fee Provision

It applies to parties that prevail in an action for breach or enforcement of the UIIA. The fee provision should be read in context, and the context is one in which Djuric successfully defended against a claim rather than affirmatively enforcing the UIIA.

**FINAL RULING**

The trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. is **REVERSED**.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) and Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). The central issue remains whether the trial court erred in awarding attorney’s fees to Djuric pursuant to the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement (“UIIA”) following the prior litigation. After considering the parties’ arguments, we find Evergreen’s position to be legally sound and supported by controlling precedent, while Djuric’s arguments fail to overcome the procedural bars established by Indiana law.

**I. Res Judicata**

Evergreen argues that Djuric’s claim for attorney’s fees is barred by res judicata because it arises from the same transaction or occurrence as the prior litigation. We agree.

Res judicata, or claim preclusion, prevents the piecemeal litigation of claims that arise from the same transaction or occurrence. See *Small v. Centerre Bank of Indiana*, 51 N.E.3d 716 (Ind. 2016). In the prior action, Evergreen’s claim for per diem charges was based entirely on the UIIA. The trial court granted summary judgment for Djuric because Evergreen failed to establish a separate written agreement required by the UIIA. Thus, the UIIA was the sole document and the "same evidence" supporting both the original claim and the current fee claim. Under *Davison v. State*, 816 N.E.2d 120 (Ind. Ct. App. 2004), claim preclusion applies when the same evidence would support both claims.

Djuric contends that the fee claim was not ripe for adjudication until it prevailed in the underlying action and therefore could not have been raised earlier. However, the contingent nature of a claim does not remove it from the scope of res judicata. The policy of finality and judicial economy, as emphasized in *P.L.M. Realty Co. v. Indiana Land Co.*, 638 N.E.2d 1354 (Ind. Ct. App. 1994), requires parties to litigate all claims arising from a transaction in a single proceeding. Djuric could have sought a conditional or contingent award of fees in the original action. By failing to do so and waiting until after judgment to assert a claim based on the same contract, Djuric has engaged in the precise type of piecemeal litigation that res judicata aims to prevent.

**II. Waiver**

Evergreen further argues that Djuric waived its right to seek attorney’s fees under the UIIA by pursuing fees under a different theory in the original action. We agree.

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. See *Keesling v. Baker*, 571 N.E.2d 565 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991). The record shows that Djuric was aware of the UIIA and its provisions from the inception of the case, as the UIIA was the foundation of Evergreen’s claim. Despite this knowledge, Djuric made a deliberate strategic choice to seek attorney’s fees solely under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1, which governs frivolous claims. The trial court denied this request.

Djuric argues that pursuing statutory fees did not constitute a waiver of its contractual rights because the statutory and contractual claims are distinct. However, the law dictates that a party’s deliberate choice to pursue one legal theory precludes it from later pursuing an alternative theory that could have been raised concurrently. See *Bunch v. Citizens Bank of Columbus*, 378 N.E.2d 858 (Ind. Ct. App. 1978). By electing to seek fees as a sanction for a frivolous claim rather than as a prevailing party under the UIIA, Djuric made an election of remedies. It cannot now assert an entirely different contractual basis for fees after its initial statutory theory failed. The fact that the contractual right was contingent on prevailing does not excuse this waiver; the right to seek such fees was known and available at the time of the original motion.

**CONCLUSION**

Djuric’s arguments fail to overcome the well-established doctrines of res judicata and waiver. The fee award arises from the same transaction and evidence as the prior litigation, and Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by electing to pursue a different remedy in the first instance. Therefore, the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees is reversed.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENSE/APPELLEE DJURIC TRUCKING, INC.

---

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, respectfully, this Court should affirm the trial court's award of attorney's fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. The plaintiff's arguments, while artfully framed, misapply the doctrines of res judicata and waiver to the unique circumstances of this case. The award of approximately $12,000 in attorney's fees represents a straightforward application of the UIIA's fee provision to a prevailing party—a provision that Evergreen itself drafted and included in the agreement. We ask this Court to reject Evergreen's attempt to escape its contractual obligations through procedural technicalities.

---

## I. RES JUDICATA DOES NOT BAR DJURIC'S FEE CLAIM

Evergreen's res judicata argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of attorney's fee claims and misapplies Indiana law. While this Court has correctly noted that res judicata prevents piecemeal litigation, the doctrine does not apply where, as here, the fee claim could not have been adjudicated in the original proceeding.

### A. The Fee Claim Was Not ripe for Adjudication in the Original Proceeding

The cornerstone of res judicata is that a claim must have been cognizable and ripe at the time of the prior litigation. In the original action, Djuric was the defendant defending against Evergreen's claim for per diem charges. Djuric successfully obtained summary judgment in its favor, but the claim for attorney's fees was wholly contingent on that outcome. As this Court correctly observed in the first appeal, Evergreen's claim failed because no separate written agreement existed—an issue that went to the merits of Evergreen's case, not to Djuric's entitlement to fees.

The Indiana Court of Appeals has consistently held that a claim is not ripe for adjudication when it depends on a future contingency that may or may not occur. See *Bell v. City of Indianapolis*, 756 N.E.2d 1153, 1161 (Ind. Ct. App. 2001). At the time of the original summary judgment proceedings, Djuric could not have sought fees because it had not yet prevailed. The contingent nature of fee claims distinguishes them from the underlying merits.

Evergreen cites *Small v. Centerre Bank of Indiana*, 51 N.E.3d 716 (Ind. 2016), for the proposition that res judicata applies to claims arising from the same transaction. However, *Small* addressed claims that were actually litigable in the prior proceeding—the plaintiff there had the opportunity to assert its claims but chose not to. That is not the situation here, where Djuric's fee claim arose only upon its victory.

### B. The UIIA Fee Provision Creates a Separate Contractual Right

The UIIA's attorney's fee provision constitutes a separate and independent contractual obligation that arises only when a party prevails in enforcing the agreement. This is not a claim that was embedded in the original litigation—it is a distinct right that accrues post-judgment.

In *Coppolillo v. Estate of Coppolillo*, 876 N.E.2d 767 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), this Court held that claims arising from separate contractual provisions constitute separate causes of action for res judicata purposes. The UIIA fee provision is precisely such a separate provision—a collateral agreement governing the recovery of litigation costs rather than the substantive dispute over container usage.

### C. Judicial Economy Supports Allowing the Fee Claim

Evergreen invokes judicial economy, but the opposite result promotes true economy. If this Court bars Djuric's fee claim, it signals to all litigants that they must anticipate every possible theory in their initial pleading—even theories that depend on future contingencies. This would paradoxically increase litigation, not decrease it, as parties would over-plead to preserve rights that had not yet accrued.

---

## II. DJURIC DID NOT WAIVE ITS RIGHT TO FEES UNDER THE UIIA

Evergreen's waiver argument fails because it conflates different legal theories and ignores the sequential nature of litigation.

### A. Pursuing Statutory Fees Does Not Constitute Waiver of Contractual Fees

Waiver requires the intentional relinquishment of a known right. See *Keeslin v. Baker*, 571 N.E.2d 565 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991). The record demonstrates that Djuric did not intentionally relinquish its UIIA fee rights.

In the original proceeding, Djuric sought attorney's fees under Indiana Code Section 34-52-1-1, which permits fee awards when a claim is frivolous. This statute addresses the conduct of the litigation itself—whether the opposing party's claim was groundless. The UIIA's fee provision, by contrast, addresses the substantive right of a prevailing party to recover fees as a contractual matter. These are fundamentally different theories serving different purposes.

This Court has recognized that alternative legal theories are not mutually exclusive unless a party makes a deliberate election. In *Bunch v. Citizens Bank of Columbus*, 378 N.E.2d 858 (Ind. Ct. App. 1978), waiver was found where a party explicitly chose one remedy that was inconsistent with another. Here, Djuric's pursuit of statutory fees was not inconsistent with contractual fees—it was simply an additional theory that happened to fail when the trial court found the claim was not frivolous (even though Evergreen lost on the merits).

### B. Djuric Could Not Have Pursued UIIA Fees Until It Prevailed

The UIIA entitles the "prevailing party" to attorney's fees. See Appellee's Appendix at 19. This language unambiguously conditions the fee right on the outcome of the litigation. Djuric could not have sought UIIA fees before obtaining summary judgment because it was not yet a prevailing party.

Evergreen's argument that Djuric should have sought a "conditional or contingent award" in the original proceeding is impractical and unsupported by Indiana law. Trial courts do not award fees contingent on future outcomes when the underlying claim remains unresolved. The proper time to seek UIIA fees is after judgment, when the prevailing party's status is established.

### C. Evergreen's Conduct Undermines Its Waiver Argument

The equities of this case strongly favor Djuric. Evergreen drafted the UIIA and included the fee provision knowing it would apply to any prevailing party—regardless of whether that party was Evergreen or its opponent. Now that Djuric has prevailed, Evergreen seeks to escape the consequence of its own contractual language.

Evergreen cannot complain about gamesmanship when it authored the very agreement thatDjuric now seeks to enforce. The fee provision was not a trap for Evergreen—it was a mutual obligation that applies to both parties. Evergreen's attempt to invoke waiver rings hollow when it was Evergreen that insisted on this provision in the intermodal industry standard agreement.

---

## III. THE AWARD IS CONSISTENT WITH THE UIIA AND INDUSTRY PRACTICE

The UIIA represents a carefully negotiated balance of rights and obligations in the intermodal shipping industry. Its fee provision reflects the understanding that prevailing parties should not bear the cost of enforcing the agreement—regardless of which party prevails.

Evergreen's position, if accepted, would allow parties to include fee-shifting provisions in agreements while simultaneously arguing that such provisions are unenforceable when their opponents prevail. This undermines the basic contract principle that agreements must be enforced according to their terms.

The trial court awarded approximately $12,000 in fees—a reasonable amount for defending against a $34,000 claim through summary judgment and appeal. This Court should defer to the trial court's calculation absent an abuse of discretion.

---

## CONCLUSION

Your Honor, the trial court's award of attorney's fees should be affirmed. Djuric prevailed in the underlying litigation, the UIIA provides for attorney's fees to the prevailing party, and no procedural bar prevents enforcement of this contractual right. Res judicata does not apply because the fee claim was not ripe for adjudication in the original proceeding. Waiver does not apply because Djuric did not intentionally relinquish its UIIA rights—it simply pursued an alternative theory that proved unsuccessful.

Evergreen drafted the UIIA, included the fee provision, and cannot now escape its obligations through procedural maneuvering. The trial court's award is just, reasonable, and supported by the contract. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the award of attorney's fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc.

---

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Defendant/Appellee Djuric Trucking, Inc.**

**FINAL RULING**

The trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. is **AFFIRMED**.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) and Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). Upon careful consideration of the applicable law and the arguments presented, we find that Djuric’s claim for attorney’s fees is not barred by res judicata, nor did Djuric waive its right to such fees. The trial court’s award is consistent with the terms of the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement (“UIIA”) and Indiana precedent.

**I. Res Judicata**

Evergreen argues that res judicata bars Djuric’s fee claim because it arises from the same transaction as the underlying dispute regarding per diem charges. While we agree that res judicata is a vital doctrine promoting judicial economy, it does not apply where a claim was not ripe for adjudication at the time of the prior proceeding.

Res judicata prevents the relitigation of claims that were or could have been raised in the prior action. See *Small v. Centerre Bank of Indiana*, 51 N.E.3d 716 (Ind. 2016). However, a claim for attorney’s fees based on a "prevailing party" provision is inherently contingent on the outcome of the underlying litigation. Until the trial court entered summary judgment in favor of Djuric, Djuric was not a "prevailing party" and therefore possessed no cognizable claim for fees under the UIIA.

The Indiana Court of Appeals has consistently held that a claim is not ripe for adjudication when it depends on a future contingency that may or may not occur. See *Bell v. City of Indianapolis*, 756 N.E.2d 1153, 1161 (Ind. Ct. App. 2001). In *Bell*, the court recognized that requiring a party to litigate a contingent claim before the contingency occurs is impractical and contrary to judicial economy. Here, Djuric could not have sought UIIA fees during the pendency of the first action because its right to those fees had not yet accrued. The claim simply did not exist until Djuric prevailed. Therefore, the claim could not have been litigated in the first instance, and res judicata does not bar the current action.

Furthermore, the UIIA’s fee provision creates a separate contractual right that is distinct from the substantive dispute over container usage. As noted in *Coppolillo v. Estate of Coppolillo*, 876 N.E.2d 767 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), claims arising from separate contractual provisions can constitute separate causes of action. The fee provision is a collateral agreement governing litigation costs, separate from the liability provisions regarding per diem charges.

**II. Waiver**

Evergreen contends that Djuric waived its right to seek fees under the UIIA by pursuing fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1 in the original action. We disagree.

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. See *Keesling v. Baker*, 571 N.E.2d 565 (Ind. Ct. App. 1991). For waiver to apply based on an election of remedies, the remedies pursued must be inconsistent with one another. See *Bunch v. Citizens Bank of Columbus*, 378 N.E.2d 858 (Ind. Ct. App. 1978).

Djuric’s pursuit of statutory fees under Section 34-52-1-1—which penalizes frivolous litigation—was not inconsistent with its right to contractual fees as a prevailing party. The statutory fee request was based on the nature of Evergreen’s conduct (i.e., filing a frivolous claim), whereas the UIIA fee request is based on the outcome of the litigation (i.e., Djuric prevailing). These are distinct legal theories serving different purposes. The failure of the statutory theory does not extinguish the contractual right, particularly where the contractual right was contingent on prevailing and could not be fully realized until after judgment.

Evergreen’s argument that Djuric should have sought a "conditional" award in the first proceeding is unpersuasive. Indiana law does not require parties to seek contingent awards for rights that have not yet matured. Djuric did not intentionally relinquish its UIIA rights; it merely pursued an available statutory theory that ultimately proved unsuccessful. Because the remedies are not inconsistent, no waiver occurred.

**CONCLUSION**

The doctrines of res judicata and waiver do not bar Djuric’s claim for attorney’s fees. The claim was not ripe for adjudication until Djuric prevailed in the underlying action, and the pursuit of statutory fees did not constitute a waiver of contractual fees. The trial court properly awarded fees pursuant to the UIIA. We therefore affirm the trial court’s judgment.
