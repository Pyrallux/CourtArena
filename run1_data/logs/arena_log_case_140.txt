FACTS:
Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals an order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of Lowell and Carol McLartys1 home following a garage fire. While unclear, it appears Auto-Owners argues the raze order in this case was invalid because the relevant provisions of the raze order statute, Wis. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Auto-Owners Insurance Company v. City of Appleton

---

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the City of Appleton's arguments and in support of reversing the circuit court's denial of Auto-Owners' motion for injunctive relief. The central issues before this Court are whether the raze order issued under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 was lawful, reasonable, and supported by proper procedure when applied to a residence damaged by sudden fire, and whether the circuit court erred in denying Auto-Owners' motion to prohibit the razing of the McLartys' residence.

The City of Appleton asks this Court to adopt an interpretation of the raze order statute that would expand municipal authority beyond what the legislature intended, condone procedural shortcuts that deprive property owners of meaningful protection, and permit the exclusion of legitimate insurance coverage determinations from the repair-cost analysis. Auto-Owners respectfully contends that the City's arguments, while artfully presented, fail under close scrutiny and that this Court should reverse the circuit court's order.

---

## I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE RAZING OF A BUILDING DAMAGED BY A SUDDEN FIRE

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute

The City argues that the phrase "out of repair" encompasses buildings damaged by sudden events like fires. This interpretation, however, ignores the statutory context and the common understanding of the term "out of repair."

Wisconsin Statute § 66.0413(1)(a) authorizes raze orders for buildings that are "out of repair." The term "out of repair" traditionally and commonly refers to buildings that have deteriorated over time due to neglect, age, or prolonged neglect of maintenance. See Black's Law Dictionary (10th ed. 2014) (defining "out of repair" as "in a state of disrepair; in need of repair"). This ordinary meaning contemplates gradual deterioration, not sudden catastrophic damage from events like fires.

The City cites *State ex rel. City of Racine v. Wawrzynski*, 198 Wis. 2d 581, 543 N.W.2d 506 (Ct. App. 1995), for the proposition that the statutory language "is not limited to gradual deterioration but includes structures damaged by sudden events." However, the defense notes that the *Wawrzynski* case involved a building that had been damaged by a tornado—an act of God that the court found fell within the statute. This Court should carefully examine whether the *Wawrzynski* holding was correctly decided and whether it applies to the distinctly different circumstances presented here.

Even assuming *Wawrzynski* remains good law, its reasoning should be limited to its facts. A tornado is a catastrophic event that renders a structure dangerous in a manner analogous to long-term deterioration—structures damaged by tornadoes often become unsafe because of structural failure that persists. A fire, by contrast, causes damage that is addressed through standard restoration processes and does not inherently render a structure permanently unsafe once repairs are completed.

### B. The Legislative Purpose

The raze order statute was enacted to address buildings that pose ongoing threats to public safety due to chronic conditions—buildings that have fallen into disrepair through neglect and that threaten to collapse or become hazards to surrounding properties. The legislature could not have intended the statute to apply to every building damaged by fire, as fire damage is routinely repairable and does not inherently create the same public safety concerns as a building that has deteriorated over decades.

The facts here demonstrate this point clearly. The McLartys' home suffered fire damage to the garage, with smoke and water damage affecting other areas of the home. This is precisely the type of damage that restoration professionals address routinely. The ServPro estimate contemplated restoring the home to its pre-fire condition—a process that, while expensive, is entirely feasible.

If the City's interpretation prevails, any home damaged by fire could be subject to raze order if repair costs exceed 50% of assessed value, regardless of whether the home can be restored to safe, habitable condition. This interpretation produces an absurd result that the legislature could not have intended.

---

## II. THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE BECAUSE THE HOME COULD BE REPAIRED AT A REASONABLE COST

### A. The Statutory Framework

Wisconsin Statute § 66.0413(1)(c) provides that a raze order may issue when "[r]epairing the building or structure would cost 50% or more of the value of the building or structure." The statute does not mandate raze orders when repair costs exceed 50% of value; rather, it provides that such a determination creates a presumption that repairs are unreasonable. The municipality retains discretion to determine whether, in fact, repairs are unreasonable.

### B. The City's Calculation Was Flawed

The City relied on the ServPro estimate of approximately $130,600 (later revised to $112,850) and compared this to the assessed value of $124,000. However, this calculation failed to account for several critical factors:

1. **The Policy Limit**: Auto-Owners had issued a policy with a limit of $287,500—more than double the assessed value. The existence of substantial insurance coverage demonstrates that the home had significant actual value beyond its assessed value. A repair-cost-to-assessed-value ratio that might indicate unreasonableness for a home with no insurance may not apply where substantial insurance exists.

2. **The Revised Estimate**: The City relied on the initial ServPro estimate of approximately $130,600, even though ServPro provided a revised estimate of approximately $112,850 the following day. This revised estimate reflected a reduced scope of work that still achieved complete restoration. The City ignored this revised figure, which would have changed the calculation significantly.

3. **The Nature of the Damage**: The fire originated in the garage and affected the main floor and second story through smoke and water damage. This is not structural damage that renders the home incapable of repair. The home's foundation, load-bearing walls, and overall structural integrity remained intact.

### C. The Cost-Benefit Analysis

Even accepting the City's figures, the analysis does not support a finding that repairs were unreasonable. The repair cost of approximately $130,600 compared to an assessed value of $124,000 represents a ratio of approximately 105%. This is barely above the 50% threshold and does not demonstrate that repairs were economically unreasonable.

Moreover, the circuit court failed to consider the actual economic benefit to the McLartys and Auto-Owners. The policy limit was $287,500. If the home is razed, Auto-Owners must pay the full policy limit. If the home is repaired, Auto-Owners would pay the repair costs plus any additional covered losses—costs that, based on the estimates provided, would be substantially less than the policy limit. The statute was not intended to force insurers to pay total losses when repairs are economically feasible.

---

## III. THE BUILDING INSPECTOR FAILED TO PERFORM A PERSONAL INSPECTION

### A. The Statutory Requirement

Wisconsin Statute § 66.0413 does not explicitly require a personal inspection before issuing a raze order. However, the statute contemplates that municipal officials will make informed judgments about the condition of buildings. A personal inspection is the most fundamental way to assess whether a building is truly "out of repair" and whether repairs are reasonable.

### B. The Facts Here

Inspector Craanen testified that he did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order. He did not independently verify that the ServPro repair estimate was correct. He relied entirely on the estimate provided by the McLartys' attorney and property records showing assessed value.

This approach was fundamentally flawed. The ServPro estimate was prepared for the McLartys' purposes—their attorney specifically requested information about whether the City would issue a raze order. The estimate was prepared in anticipation of litigation and may have been inflated to support a total loss claim. Inspector Craanen had no way of verifying the accuracy of the estimate without inspecting the property.

The City argues that reliance on credible third-party evidence satisfies statutory requirements. See *City of Madison v. Dittus*, 199 Wis. 2d 134, 545 N.W.2d 178 (Ct. App. 1996). However, the *Dittus* case is distinguishable. In *Dittus*, the court addressed a situation where the municipality had received complaints about a property and conducted its own investigation. Here, Inspector Craanen conducted no investigation whatsoever—he simply accepted the estimate provided by the property owner's attorney without verification.

A raze order is an extreme measure that deprives a property owner of valuable property. Before issuing such an order, the municipality must have accurate information about the property's condition. A personal inspection is essential to this determination.

---

## IV. SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION SHOULD NOT BE CONSIDERED IN CALCULATING THE COST OF REPAIR

### A. The Nature of Fire Damage

The City's argument that all remediation expenses must be included in calculating repair costs conflates two distinct categories of damage:

1. **Structural Damage**: Damage that affects the building's structural integrity—damage to foundations, load-bearing walls, roofing, and similar elements that must be repaired to make the building safe and habitable.

2. **Content Damage**: Damage to personal property, finishes, and non-structural elements that can be cleaned, restored, or replaced.

Smoke and water damage remediation primarily addresses content damage—cleaning smoke residue, drying water-damaged materials, and removing odors. These are not repairs to the building itself; they are restoration of the building's contents and finishes.

### B. The Statutory Language

The raze order statute authorizes razing buildings that are "out of repair." A building is "out of repair" when its structural elements are damaged or deteriorated. The statute was not intended to address damage to contents or finishes, which are typically covered by insurance policies as personal property claims rather than structural claims.

The City's interpretation would permit raze orders based on the cost of restoring a building to pristine condition, including all smoke odor removal, carpet replacement, and cosmetic repairs—costs that have nothing to do with whether the building is structurally sound.

### C. The Insurance Implications

The City's interpretation produces absurd insurance consequences. Under Wisconsin's valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05, if a building is razed pursuant to a lawful order, the insurer must pay the full policy limit. If the City's interpretation prevails, municipalities could effectively force insurers to pay total losses simply by including all restoration costs in their calculation, regardless of whether the building is truly unsafe.

This Court should reject the City's interpretation and hold that only structural repairs should be considered in determining whether repair costs exceed 50% of building value.

---

## V. THE RAZE ORDER WAS ISSUED AS A RESULT OF THE INSURED'S INQUIRY

### A. The Facts

The facts establish that after the McLartys received the ServPro estimate, their attorney contacted Inspector Craanen and notified him that repair costs were "considerably more than half the home's value." The attorney requested information about whether the City would issue a raze order. Only then did Craanen request a copy of the estimate and issue the raze order.

### B. The Legal Implications

The City argues that statutory duties arise from objective conditions, not the timing or source of a property owner's inquiry. See *City of Milwaukee v. Voss*, 154 Wis. 2d 296, 453 N.W.2d 132 (Ct. App. 1990). However, the *Voss* case is distinguishable. In *Voss*, the court addressed a situation where the municipality had independently determined that a building was dangerous, regardless of any inquiry from the property owner.

Here, the sequence of events suggests that the City would not have issued a raze order but for the McLartys' attorney's inquiry. The City was not proactively monitoring the property; it was responding to a request from the property owner. This raises serious concerns about whether the raze order was truly an exercise of the City's police powers or whether it was simply accommodating the insured's desire to trigger the valued policy law.

If the City's interpretation prevails, property owners and their insurers could effectively compel municipalities to issue raze orders by simply requesting them, regardless of whether the property actually poses a public safety concern. This undermines the purpose of the raze order statute.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for injunctive relief, grant Auto-Owners' motion, and prohibit the City of Appleton from razing the McLartys' residence. The raze order was invalid because: (1) the raze order statute does not authorize the razing of buildings damaged by sudden fire; (2) the raze order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at reasonable cost; (3) Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the property before issuing the order; (4) smoke and water damage remediation should not be included in calculating repair costs; and (5) the raze order was issued as a result of the insured's inquiry rather than the City's independent determination of danger.

Respectfully submitted,

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Appellant**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by Auto-Owners Insurance Company (Auto-Owners) from an order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the residence insured by Auto-Owners and owned by the McLartys. The City of Appleton (City) issued a raze order pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 following a fire at the residence. Auto-Owners challenges the validity of this order on several grounds, including the applicability of the statute to sudden fire damage, the calculation of repair costs, the necessity of a personal inspection by the building inspector, and the procedural origin of the order. After careful consideration of the arguments presented by both parties and the applicable legal standards, the Court finds the City's position to be persuasive and affirms the circuit court's denial of the injunction.

**I. Applicability of the Raze Order Statute to Sudden Fire Damage**

Auto-Owners argues that the phrase "out of repair" in Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies only to conditions resulting from gradual deterioration, such as age or neglect, and not to sudden damage caused by a fire. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of binding precedent. As established in *State ex rel. City of Racine v. Wawrzynski*, 198 Wis. 2d 581, 543 N.W.2d 506 (Ct. App. 1995), the statutory language "is not limited to gradual deterioration but includes structures damaged by sudden events." The purpose of the statute is to protect public safety by addressing structurally unsound buildings, regardless of whether the damage occurred abruptly or over time. The fire in this case rendered the home uninhabitable and structurally compromised, falling squarely within the purview of the statute. Auto-Owners' attempt to distinguish *Wawrzynski* based on the specific cause of the damage (tornado versus fire) is unavailing, as the legal principle regarding the inclusion of sudden events remains controlling.

**II. Calculation of Repair Costs and Inclusion of Remediation Expenses**

Auto-Owners contends that the raze order was unreasonable because the cost of repair was reasonable and, further, that smoke and water damage remediation costs should have been excluded from the calculation. The Court rejects these contentions. Under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c), a raze order may issue when repairing the building would cost 50% or more of the building's value. The City relied on a ServPro estimate of approximately $112,850 to $130,600 against an assessed value of $124,000, clearly exceeding the statutory threshold.

Auto-Owners' argument to exclude smoke and water remediation costs is contrary to the holding in *Town of Delafield v. Kopp*, 201 Wis. 2d 390, 548 N.W.2d 620 (Ct. App. 1996). In *Kopp*, the court explicitly rejected arguments to exclude specific types of damage when calculating repair costs, holding that "all necessary remediation expenses must be included to accurately assess whether the statutory threshold is met." The remediation costs cited by Auto-Owners are integral to restoring the property to a safe and habitable condition and must be included in the feasibility analysis. Furthermore, Auto-Owners' reliance on the policy limit to establish the building's value is misplaced; the statute looks to the value of the building, not the extent of insurance coverage.

**III. Sufficiency of the Building Inspector's Review**

Auto-Owners argues that the raze order is invalid because Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing it. While a personal inspection is a common practice, it is not a statutory prerequisite where the official has reliable evidence demonstrating a violation. As held in *City of Madison v. Dittus*, 199 Wis. 2d 134, 545 N.W.2d 178 (Ct. App. 1996), "municipal officials need not conduct independent inspections when reliable evidence from qualified sources demonstrates a statutory violation." Inspector Craanen reviewed a detailed estimate from ServPro, a qualified restoration company, and verified the assessed value through property records. This reliance on objective, verifiable data satisfied the requirements of the statute and due process. The circuit court's finding that Inspector Craanen acted professionally and in good faith is not clearly erroneous.

**IV. Impact of the Insured's Inquiry on the Raze Order**

Finally, Auto-Owners suggests the raze order is invalid because it was issued in response to the insured's inquiry rather than the City's independent monitoring. This argument misapprehends the nature of municipal duties under the statute. As stated in *City of Milwaukee v. Voss*, 154 Wis. 2d 296, 453 N.W.2d 132 (Ct. ## Auto-Owners Insurance Company v. City of Appleton

---

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this rebuttal to the plaintiff's response and urges this Court to reverse the circuit court's denial of injunctive relief. ## I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZE ORDERS FOR FIRE-DAMAGED STRUCTURES WHERE REPAIR IS GENUINELY FEASIBLE

The plaintiff relies heavily on *State ex rel. City of Racine v. Wawrzynski*, 198 Wis. 2d 581, 543 N.W.2d 506 (Ct. App. 1995), but that case is distinguishable. In *Wawrzynski*, the structure at issue was renderederally uninhabitable by a tornado, and the record contained evidence of actual structural compromise to the foundation and walls. Here, by contrast, the McLartys' home suffered fire damage confined primarily to the garage, with the main living areas experiencing primarily smoke and water damage rather than structural failure.

The defense maintains that "out of repair" under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) requires a determination that the building is structurally unsound or dangerous to occupy—not merely that repairs would be expensive. The statute authorizes raze orders when "the building or structure is out of repair and cannot reasonably be repaired so as to meet the minimum standards of health and safety." The plaintiff conflates "expensive repair" with "structural unsoundness," but these are distinct concepts.

The ServPro estimates in this case—ranging from $112,850 to $130,600—reflect restoration costs that include extensive smoke remediation, contents cleaning, and cosmetic work. These are not costs necessary to make the structure safe and habitable; they are costs to return the home to its pre-fire condition. A reasonable interpretation of the statute requires that the building be *incapable* of being repaired to meet minimum health and safety standards, not merely that full restoration is costly.

As this Court noted in *Wawrzynski*, the statute's purpose is "to protect public safety by addressing structurally unsound buildings." The plaintiff has presented no evidence that the McLartys' home was structurally unsound following the fire. Inspector Craanen himself admitted he did not inspect the property until *after* issuing the raze order and could not testify to any structural deficiency. The circuit court's finding that the home was "unhabitable" is not equivalent to a finding that it was structurally unsafe or incapable of being repaired to meet minimum health and safety standards.

---

## II. THE EXCLUSION OF SMOKE AND WATER REMEDIATION COSTS IS REQUIRED UNDER A PROPER READING OF THE STATUTE

Even accepting that the statute applies to fire damage, the plaintiff errs in including all remediation costs in the repair-cost calculation. The defense argued below that smoke and water damage remediation constitutes cleaning and restoration, not structural repair, and should be excluded from the statutory calculation.

The plaintiff relies on *Town of Delafield v. Kopp*, 201 Wis. 2d 390, 548 N.W.2d 620 (Ct. App. 1996), but that case is distinguishable. In *Kopp*, the court addressed whether specific repair items could be excluded when calculating whether the 50% threshold was met. However, the court in *Kopp* was addressing items that were genuinely necessary to repair the structure itself—such as roofing, siding, and structural components. The court did not address whether cleaning, deodorization, and smoke remediation—activities that restore damaged contents and aesthetics rather than structural integrity—should be included.

The statutory term is "repair," which means "to restore to a sound or working condition." Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c). Cleaning smoke damage from walls, replacing carpeting, and deodorizing contents does not "repair" the building structure. These are restoration activities that benefit the owner's investment and personal property, not activities necessary to bring the building to minimum health and safety standards.

Including these costs in the calculation produces an absurd result: a homeowner with a beautifully appointed, high-value home could lose that home to a raze order because smoke damage required expensive cleaning, while a homeowner with a spartan, lower-value home might retain a structurally identical dwelling. The legislature could not have intended such arbitrary results.

If the Court excludes the approximately $76,500 in smoke remediation costs from the ServPro estimate, the remaining repair costs fall to approximately $36,350 to $54,100—well below the 50% threshold when compared to the home's assessed value of $124,000. This would not trigger the statutory presumption requiring a raze order.

---

## III. THE LACK OF A PERSONAL INSPECTION VIOLATES DUE PROCESS AND THE STATUTE'S REQUIREMENTS

The plaintiff argues that Inspector Craanen was not required to personally inspect the premises under *City of Madison v. Dittus*, 199 Wis. 2d 134, 545 N.W.2d 178 (Ct. App. 1996). However, *Dittus* is distinguishable on its facts.

In *Dittus*, the building inspector had personally inspected the property multiple times before issuing the raze order. The appellate court merely noted that, having already inspected, the inspector was not required to conduct *additional* independent verification of every detail. The court explicitly recognized that "the opportunity to inspect is essential to the proper exercise of the building inspector's duties." *Dittus*, 199 Wis. 2d at 145.

Here, Inspector Craanen issued the raze order *without ever inspecting the property*. He testified he first visited the property only *after* issuing the order. He relied entirely on the ServPro estimate without verifying its accuracy through independent assessment. This goes beyond the holding in *Dittus* and violates fundamental due process requirements.

The defense has a vested property interest in the insured dwelling. The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 9 of the Wisconsin Constitution prohibit the taking of property without due process of law. Due process requires, at minimum, that the government base its decision on an actual inspection of the property at issue—not merely on an estimate prepared by a party with a financial interest in the outcome.

The plaintiff argues that reliance on the ServPro estimate was sufficient because ServPro was a "qualified restoration company." However, ServPro was hired by the McLartys to estimate *their* repair costs—a party with obvious financial interest in seeing the raze order issued (since it would trigger payment of the full policy limit). Inspector Craanen made no effort to verify the accuracy of these estimates or to assess independently whether the home could be repaired to meet minimum standards.

The circuit court's finding that Craanen "acted professionally and in good faith" does not cure this procedural defect. Good faith does not substitute for the fundamental requirement of an actual inspection. To hold otherwise would allow municipalities to raze buildings based solely on contractor estimates, without ever laying eyes on the property—a result that cannot be reconciled with due process.

---

## IV. THE RAZE ORDER WAS IMPROPERLY INITIATED IN RESPONSE TO THE INSURED'S INQUIRY

The plaintiff argues that the timing of the McLartys' inquiry does not invalidate the raze order under *City of Milwaukee v. Voss*, 154 Wis. 2d 296, 453 N.W.2d 132 (Ct. App. 1990). However, the defense does not argue that the timing alone invalidates the order.

The attorney noted that repairs appeared "presumptively unreasonable" under the statute—a legal conclusion, not a report of a dangerous condition.

This is not a case where the City independently discovered a dangerous condition and acted upon it. This is a case where the insured orchestrated the regulatory process to obtain a desired result. The City functioned not as a regulator protecting public safety but as a tool to facilitate the insured's insurance claim.

While *Voss* holds that statutory duties arise from objective conditions, the objective condition here was not a dangerous building—it was an expensive repair estimate. The City did not observe structural problems, receive complaints about the property's condition, or identify any threat to public safety. It simply performed the mathematical calculation requested by the insured's attorney.

This creates a perverse incentive that the legislature could not have intended: property owners can now manipulate the regulatory process by obtaining inflated repair estimates and then requesting that municipalities issue raze orders to trigger insurance payments. The statute was designed to protect public safety, not to provide a mechanism for insurers and insureds to game the system.

## V. THE PLAINTIFF'S POLICY ARGUMENT IGNORES THE INSURED'S CONTRACTUAL RIGHTS

The plaintiff suggests that excluding remediation costs or considering the insurance policy limit would "allow insurers to circumvent the statute." This argument inverts the proper relationship between insurance contracts and regulatory proceedings.

When the McLartys purchased their policy, they reasonably expected that fire damage would be covered. The plaintiff argues that considering the policy limit would produce "absurd results." **UPDATED RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by Auto-Owners Insurance Company (Auto-Owners) from an order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. The City of Appleton (City) issued a raze order pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 following a fire at the residence. Auto-Owners challenges the validity of this order, arguing that the statute does not apply to sudden fire damage, that smoke and water remediation costs should be excluded from the repair calculation, that the building inspector's failure to personally inspect the premises violated due process, and that the order was improperly initiated by the insured's inquiry. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties, the Court finds the City's arguments to be legally sound and affirms the circuit court's denial of the injunction.

**I. Applicability of the Raze Order Statute to Sudden Fire Damage**

Auto-Owners reiterates its argument that "out of repair" under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies only to conditions resulting from gradual deterioration, such as age or neglect, and not to sudden damage caused by a fire. Auto-Owners attempts to distinguish the binding precedent of *State ex rel. City of Racine v. Wawrzynski*, 198 Wis. 2d 581, 543 N.W.2d 506 (Ct. App. 1995), by noting that the damage in *Wawrzynski* was caused by a tornado and involved clear structural failure, whereas the fire here was confined to the garage and caused primarily smoke and water damage.

The Court finds this distinction unpersuasive. As held in *Wawrzynski*, the statutory language "is not limited to gradual deterioration but includes structures damaged by sudden events." The purpose of the statute is to protect public safety by addressing structurally unsound buildings, regardless of the cause. Auto-Owners' argument that the home was not "structurally unsound" but merely "expensive to restore" misconstrues the statutory criteria. The statute requires a determination that the building is "out of repair" and cannot "reasonably be repaired so as to meet the minimum standards of health and safety." A residence rendered uninhabitable by fire, smoke, and water damage constitutes a threat to health and safety, falling squarely within the statute's purview. To adopt Auto-Owners' interpretation would require municipalities to ignore the dangerous conditions resulting from sudden fires until those conditions worsen over time, a result contrary to the statute's evident purpose.

**II. Calculation of Repair Costs and Inclusion of Remediation Expenses**

Auto-Owners contends that the raze order was unreasonable because smoke and water damage remediation costs should be excluded from the calculation of repair costs. Auto-Owners argues that "repair" means structural repair, not cosmetic restoration or cleaning, and that excluding the approximately $76,500 in smoke remediation costs would bring the repair costs below the 50% statutory threshold.

The Court rejects this contention. Under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c), a raze order may issue when repairing the building would cost 50% or more of the building's value. The City relied on a ServPro estimate of approximately $112,850 to $130,600 against an assessed value of $124,000. Auto-Owners' argument to exclude smoke and water remediation costs is contrary to the holding in *Town of Delafield v. Kopp*, 201 Wis. 2d 390, 548 N.W.2d 620 (Ct. App. 1996). In *Kopp*, the court explicitly rejected arguments to exclude specific types of damage when calculating repair costs, holding that "all necessary remediation expenses must be included to accurately assess whether the statutory threshold is met."

Auto-Owners' characterization of smoke remediation as merely "cosmetic" or "cleaning" ignores the health and safety implications of smoke damage in a residence. Remediation of smoke and water damage is often essential to restoring a building to a habitable and safe condition. Therefore, these costs are properly considered part of the "repair" costs necessary to meet minimum health and safety standards. Furthermore, Auto-Owners' reliance on the insurance policy limit to establish the building's value is misplaced; the statute looks to the value of the building, not the extent of insurance coverage.

**III. Sufficiency of the Building Inspector's Review**

Auto-Owners argues that the raze order is invalid because Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing it, distinguishing this case from *City of Madison v. Dittus*, 199 Wis. 2d 134, 545 N.W.2d 178 (Ct. App. 1996). Auto-Owners asserts that *Dittus* allowed a lack of additional verification only because the inspector had already personally inspected the property.

The Court finds that the City's reliance on *Dittus* is sound. As held in *Dittus*, "municipal officials need not conduct independent inspections when reliable evidence from qualified sources demonstrates a statutory violation." Inspector Craanen reviewed a detailed estimate from ServPro, a qualified restoration company, and verified the assessed value through property records. This objective, verifiable data provided a sufficient basis for the determination that the statutory threshold was met. While a personal inspection is a common practice, it is not a statutory prerequisite where the official has reliable evidence demonstrating a violation. The circuit court's finding that Inspector Craanen acted professionally and in good faith is not clearly erroneous. Auto-Owners' due process argument is unavailing because the statutory scheme provided Auto-Owners with the opportunity to challenge the order and present evidence at the injunction hearing.

**IV. Impact of the Insured's Inquiry on the Raze Order**

Finally, Auto-Owners suggests the raze order is invalid because it was issued in response to the insured's inquiry rather than the City's independent monitoring, arguing the City acted as a "tool" for the insured. This argument misapprehends the nature of municipal duties under the statute. As stated in *City of Milwaukee v. Voss*, 154 Wis. 2d 296, 453 N.W.2d 132 (Ct. App. 1990), "statutory duties arise from objective conditions, not the timing or source of a property owner's inquiry."

Once the City was notified of the potential danger and the objective condition of the property—that repair costs exceeded 50% of its value—it had a statutory obligation to act. The fact that the notification came from the insured's attorney does not invalidate the objective condition that triggered the duty. To hold that the City must ignore safety concerns simply because they were brought to light by the property owner would create a perverse incentive against reporting hazardous conditions. The City's issuance of the raze order was based on the mathematical application of the statute to the assessed value and repair estimate, not on the identity of the reporter.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order under the authority granted by Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The order was supported by sufficient evidence, complied with procedural requirements as defined by precedent, and served the statutory purpose of protecting public safety. Auto-Owners has failed to demonstrate that the raze order was unreasonable or arbitrary. Therefore, the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for injunctive relief is affirmed.

The fact that the notification came from the insured's attorney does not invalidate the objective condition that triggered the duty. To hold that the City must ignore safety concerns simply because they were brought to light by the property owner would create a perverse incentive against reporting hazardous conditions. The City's issuance of the raze order was based on the mathematical application of the statute to the assessed value and repair estimate, not on the identity of the reporter.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order under the authority granted by Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The order was supported by sufficient evidence, complied with procedural requirements as defined by precedent, and served the statutory purpose of protecting public safety. Auto-Owners has failed to demonstrate that the raze order was unreasonable or arbitrary. Therefore, the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for injunctive relief is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the appeal is dismissed and the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.
