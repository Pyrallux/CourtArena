COURT ARENA LOG - Case 32
FACTS:
DECISION AND JOURNAL ENTRY
HENSAL, Presiding Judge.
{¶ 1} Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, appeals her conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs from the Summit County Court of Common Pleas. This Court affirms.
I.
{¶ 2} This case involves the discovery of a methamphetamine lab and Ms. Kittle's involvement with same. Officer Lemonier from the Akron Police Department testified that he and another officer conducted surveillance on an apartment where they believed illegal activity was occurring. The officers observed Ms. Kittle and another woman, J.S., leave the apartment and get into Ms. Kittle's car, with J.S. taking the driver's seat. Shortly thereafter, the officers performed a traffic stop on the vehicle. During the stop, the officers discovered that J.S. did not have a driver's license and that she had an outstanding felony warrant. As a result, they placed her under arrest and searched her, finding three bags of methamphetamine in her purse. The officers did not arrest or search Ms. Kittle, but did request that she empty her pockets, which revealed no illegal substances.
{¶ 3} The officers then returned to the apartment and knocked on the door. After about twenty minutes of waiting outside, one of the occupants allowed the officers inside. Upon entering, Officer Lemonier immediately smelled the distinct odor associated with the production of methamphetamine. The occupant then consented to a search of the apartment, which revealed a number of items associated with the production of methamphetamine, as well as several small bags containing the finished product.
{¶ 4} Through his investigation of the matter, including an interview with J.S., Officer Lemonier discovered information that implicated Ms. Kittle. Another officer located Ms. Kittle and brought her to the Akron Police Department for questioning. Officer Lemonier conducted the interview, wherein Ms. Kittle admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.
*1243{¶ 5} A Grand Jury indicted Ms. Kittle on the following three counts: (1) illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs in violation of Revised Code Section 2925.041(A) ; (2) illegal manufacture of drugs in violation of Section 2925.04(A); and (3) aggravated possession of drugs in violation of 2925.11(A),(C)(1). The State dismissed the latter two counts prior to trial.
{¶ 6} The case proceeded to a bench trial. The State presented testimony from one witness: Officer Lemonier. The State also played portions of Officer Lemonier's interview with Ms. Kittle, and introduced records from The National Precursor Log Exchange ("NPLEX"), which is a system that electronically tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine. Ms. Kittle did not present any evidence.
{¶ 7} The trial court found Ms. Kittle guilty, and subsequently sentenced her to five years of imprisonment. She now appeals, raising three assignments of error for our review.
II.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ADMITTED THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE AND ALLOWED A POLICE OFFICER WHO WAS NOT A QUALIFIED WITNESS TO LAY THE FOUNDATION FOR INTRODUCING THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE, IN VIOLATION OF MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS PURSUANT TO ARTICLE I, SECTION 10, OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE OF THE SIXTH AMENDMENT OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 8} In her first assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records because he was not the custodian of those records, nor was he otherwise qualified to do so. Ms. Kittle asserts that a de novo standard of review applies because the trial court's error in this regard implicated her rights under the Confrontation Clause.
{¶ 9} We will begin by addressing the standard of review. While Ms. Kittle's assignment of error suggests that she believes NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause, she does not develop an argument in that regard. See App.R. 16(A)(7). Rather, she cites Ohio Supreme Court precedent for the proposition that business records are nontestimonial, and states: "[a]ssuming that the NPLEX records of the pharmacy admitted into evidence in [this] case were truly nontestimonial in nature, the foundation for their proper authentication as 'business records' under the meaning of Evid.R. 803(6) could not have been established by [Officer Lemonier]." Ms. Kittle does, however, develop such an argument in her reply brief, arguing that NPLEX records are testimonial because the primary purpose of that system is to "assist in the interdiction of methamphetamine creation and use[.]"
{¶ 10} As an initial matter, we note that "[a]ppellate courts generally will not consider a new issue presented for the first time in a reply brief." State v. Quarterman , 140 Ohio St.3d 464, 2014-Ohio-4034, 19 N.E.3d 900, ¶ 18. Notwithstanding, we are unpersuaded by Ms. Kittle's argument that NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause. This is because "[t]estimonial statements are those created or *1244given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." State v. Garcia , 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, quoting Ohio v. Clark , --- U.S. ----, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015). The purpose of the NPLEX system, however, is to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. See R.C. 3715.05 ; State v. Coleman , 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 ("The purpose of the NPLEx system is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets."). We cannot say that NPLEX records are "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." Garcia at id. ; United States v. Collins , 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir.2015) (holding that a similar "MethCheck" record was "not clearly testimonial in nature," and stating that "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial."). We, therefore, reject Ms. Kittle's contentions that her rights under the Confrontation Clause were implicated, and that a de novo standard of review applies.
{¶ 11} We now turn to Ms. Kittle's argument that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records. This Court reviews a trial court's decision to admit a business record into evidence for an abuse of discretion. State v. Baker , 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 9 ; Coleman at ¶ 35-42 (acknowledging that a NPLEX record is a business record). "A trial court is deemed to abuse its discretion where it admits a business record when the party lays an inadequate foundation to establish its admissibility in accordance with Evid.R. 803(6)." Baker at ¶ 9.
{¶ 12} Evidence Rule 803(6) provides that a business record is excepted from the hearsay rule if it is "kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity, and if it was the regular practice of that business activity to make the * * * record * * *, all as shown by the testimony of the custodian or other qualified witness or as provided by Rule 901(B)(10)[.]" "A 'qualified witness' for this purpose would be someone with 'enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business.' " (Citation omitted.) State v. Hood , 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, quoting 5 McLaughlin, Weinstein's Federal Evidence , Section 803.08[8][a] (2d Ed.2009). Notably, "[t]he witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." Baker at ¶ 11, citing Evid.R. 803(6). The witness, however, must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." Id. , quoting Keeva J. Kekst Architects, Inc. v. George Dev. Group , 8th Dist. Cuyahoga No. 70835, 1997 WL 253171, *5 (May 15, 1997).
{¶ 13} Here, Officer Lemonier testified that, through his training as a law enforcement officer, he has personal knowledge as to how pharmacies use the NPLEX system to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. He testified that he applied for-and was granted-a license to access the system, which requires a username and password. He indicated that he accesses the NPLEX system every day, and that he *1245receives real-time alerts for pseudoephedrine purchases.
{¶ 14} Regarding the process for electronically tracking purchases, Officer Lemonier explained that a person attempting to buy pseudoephedrine must provide an ID, which is then swiped. As soon as the ID is swiped, the information contained thereon is immediately transmitted to the NPLEX database. Within a few minutes, more specific information is transmitted, including the brand name, pill count, grams of pseudoephedrine contained in the package, and whether the purchase was accepted or denied. He stated that a purchase will be denied if the individual attempts to purchase more than nine grams of pseudoephedrine during a 30-day period.
{¶ 15} Officer Lemonier testified that he personally obtained the NPLEX record for Ms. Kittle, which was introduced as an exhibit, and that the record is kept in the ordinary course of business. To obtain the record, he entered Ms. Kittle's name, date of birth, and driver's license number into the NPLEX system, which then generated a list of pseudoephedrine purchases associated with that information. Officer Lemonier then testified as to the purchases of pseudoephedrine reflected in the NPLEX record.
{¶ 16} While not every police officer may have familiarity with the NPLEX system or the ability to explain how those records came into existence in the ordinary course of business, the record reflects that Officer Lemonier did, in fact, have this knowledge. He, therefore, was a "qualified witness" for purposes of Evidence Rule 803(6). Thus, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion when it admitted the NPLEX records into evidence. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ABDICATED ITS DUTIES AS A NEUTRAL AND IMPARTIAL FINDER OF FACT BY DIRECTING THE PROSECUTION TO SUPPLEMENT ITS EVIDENCE AGAINST MS. KITTLE MID-TRIAL, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO A FAIR TRIAL AND DUE PROCESS UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS TO THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 17} In her second assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its duties as a neutral and impartial finder of fact by directing the State to supplement its evidence against her at trial. Similar to her first assignment of error, she contends that a de novo standard of review applies, arguing that the trial court's actions deprived her of a fair trial. We disagree that a de novo standard of review applies, and otherwise find no merit in her argument.
{¶ 18} Evidence Rule 614(B) provides that "[t]he court may interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." As this Court has stated, " '[t]he evident purpose of Evid. R. 614(B) is to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness.' " State v. Grad , 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, quoting State v. Armstrong , 2d Dist. Montgomery No. 13498, 1993 WL 294834, *5 (Aug. 6, 1993). When, as is the case here, the matter is proceeding as a bench trial, the trial judge is afforded greater flexibility in questioning a witness because there is no risk of prejudicially *1246influencing a jury. Id. " 'In absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony, it will be presumed that the trial court acted with impartiality [in propounding to the witness questions from the bench] in attempting to ascertain a material fact or to develop the truth.' " (Alterations sic.) State v. Baston , 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), quoting Jenkins v. Clark , 7 Ohio App.3d 93, 98, 454 N.E.2d 541 (2d Dist.1982). "In reviewing any alleged prejudicial errors resulting from the court's own interrogation of a witness, a reviewing court will examine the questions in light of the entire record and, based upon the totality of the circumstances, determine whether there has been a manifest abuse of discretion." Grad at ¶ 43, quoting State v. Hoover , 9th Dist. Medina No. 1549, 1987 WL 12247, *2 (June 3, 1987).
{¶ 19} Here, when cross-examining Officer Lemonier, Ms. Kittle's counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record did not contain certain information, such as the form of identification used to purchase the pseudoephedrine. Officer Lemonier indicated that if he logged into the NPLEX system and clicked on a certain field, that information would populate onto the record, which he could then print. He further indicated that it was not his typical practice to print that information, and that it had never been an issue in prior trials that he has been involved in.
{¶ 20} The trial court noted that it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth[,]" and did not fault the State for failing to provide an expanded version of the NPLEX record. The trial court then allowed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version, which included the missing information. Despite the trial court's willingness to allow Ms. Kittle's counsel a few days to review the record and prepare accordingly, her counsel elected to proceed as scheduled and chose not to cross-examine Officer Lemonier on the expanded record.
{¶ 21} Based upon the totality of the circumstances present in this case, we cannot say that the trial court's actions resulted in a manifest abuse of discretion. Id. at ¶ 43. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III
THE TRIAL COURT COMMITTED REVERSIBLE ERROR WHEN IT AMENDED THE INDICTMENT ON THE MORNING OF TRIAL TO EXPAND THE SINGLE DATE DETERMINED BY THE GRAND JURY TO INCLUDE THE ENTIRE PREVIOUS WEEK, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO DUE PROCESS AND INDICTMENT BY A GRAND JURY UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH, AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 22} In her third assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing the State to amend the timeframe of the indictment to include the previous week. We disagree.
{¶ 23} "We review a trial court's decision to allow the amendment of an indictment for an abuse of discretion." State v. Sauto , 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. An abuse of discretion implies that the court's attitude is unreasonable, arbitrary or unconscionable. Blakemore v. Blakemore , 5 Ohio St.3d 217, 219, 450 N.E.2d 1140 (1983). To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show that: (1) the trial court abused its discretion; and (2) the amendment prejudiced her defense.
*1247State v. Dudukovich , 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.
{¶ 24} Here, the indictment listed the date of the illegal-assembly-or-possession-of-chemicals-for-the-manufacture-of-drugs offense as on or about December 2, 2014 (the date the officers discovered the methamphetamine lab). On the morning of trial, the trial court allowed the State to extend the timeframe of the indictment to include the preceding seven days. Despite allowing the State to amend the indictment, the trial court ultimately determined that the evidence indicated that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, that she gave those pills to J.S. so that J.S. could make methamphetamine, and that methamphetamine was made on December 2, 2014. The trial court's journal entry indicates that it found Ms. Kittle guilty based upon the December 2, 2014, date only. Thus, even assuming without deciding that the trial court abused its discretion, Ms. Kittle cannot establish that she suffered prejudice and, consequently, cannot establish reversible error. Id. at ¶ 16. Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error is overruled.
III.
{¶ 25} Ms. Kittle's assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
Judgment affirmed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Dana Kittle respectfully submits this response in opposition to the State's request to affirm her conviction. The prosecution's argument fails to address the substantial errors committed by the trial court that deprived Ms. Kittle of a fair trial and violated her constitutional rights. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas and remand for a new trial.

## I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: THE ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS VIOLATED MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS

The prosecution erroneously contends that Officer Lemonier was a qualified witness to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records under Evidence Rule 803(6). This Court should reject this argument and sustain Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error.

### The Prosecution's Argument Regarding the Confrontation Clause Fails

The prosecution correctly notes that testimonial statements are those created with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony. Ohio v. Clark, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015). However, the prosecution's reliance on State v. Garcia, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, and United States v. Collins, 799 F.3d 554 (6th Cir. 2015), is misplaced.

The defense maintains that NPLEX records are testimonial in nature because the primary purpose of the system is to assist law enforcement in the interdiction of methamphetamine creation and use. The NPLEX system was established under Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05 specifically to track pseudoephedrine purchases and prevent the manufacture of methamphetamine. Unlike ordinary business records created for purely commercial purposes, NPLEX records are generated precisely because the state and federal governments have mandated their creation to combat illegal drug activity. The system exists at the intersection of regulatory compliance and criminal law enforcement, with the explicit goal of providing evidence to police.

As the Sixth Circuit recognized in United States v. Collins, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), the question is whether it was "improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial." In the context of NPLEX, however, pharmacy employees are well aware that these records are maintained specifically for law enforcement purposes and will be used in criminal prosecutions. The system is designed to generate real-time alerts to law enforcement officers like Officer Lemonier. This is fundamentally different from a routine business record.

### Officer Lemonier Was Not a Qualified Witness Under Evidence Rule 803(6)

Even assuming the NPLEX records are business records, the trial court erred in allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for their admission.

Evidence Rule 803(6) requires testimony from a custodian or "other qualified witness" who has sufficient familiarity with the record-keeping system to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business. State v. Hood, 135 Ohio St. 3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40. The witness must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." State v. Baker, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11.

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier's daily use of the NPLEX system qualified him as a witness. However, the fact that a police officer accesses a database does not make him familiar with how that database is maintained in the ordinary course of business. Officer Lemonier is not an employee of the pharmacies that create these records, nor is he a custodian of the NPLEX system itself. His testimony regarding the system's operation was based on his training as a law enforcement officer and his personal use of the system—not on personal knowledge of how the pharmacies input data or how the NPLEX system maintains records.

As this Court noted in State v. Hood, 135 Ohio St. 3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, a qualified witness must have enough familiarity with the record-keeping system to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business. Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he knows how to access and retrieve records from the system—he did not demonstrate familiarity with the business practices of the pharmacies that input data, the technical infrastructure of the NPLEX system, or the procedures by which records are created and maintained. His testimony regarding the process of ID swiping and data transmission was based on inference and assumption, not personal knowledge.

The trial court abused its discretion in admitting these records without proper foundation. State v. Baker, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 9.

## II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: THE TRIAL COURT ABDICATED ITS ROLE AS A NEUTRAL FINDER OF FACT

The prosecution defends the trial court's actions in directing the State to supplement its evidence mid-trial. This Court should reject this argument and sustain Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error.

### The Trial Court's Intervention Was Prejudicial

Evidence Rule 614(B) permits the court to interrogate witnesses in an impartial manner. However, as this Court recognized in State v. Grad, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, the purpose of this rule "is to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness."

While the prosecution notes that a bench trial affords greater flexibility, the trial court's role remains that of a neutral finder of fact. The court may not become an advocate for either party. Here, the trial court did not merely ask clarifying questions—it actively directed the prosecution to supplement its evidence by obtaining an expanded version of the NPLEX record. This went beyond passive adjudication into active case-building for the prosecution.

The prosecution argues that Ms. Kittle cannot show prejudice because her counsel elected not to cross-examine Officer Lemonier on the expanded record. This argument is unavailing. The prejudice occurred when the trial court directed the prosecution to obtain additional evidence—the very act of supplementing the record altered the evidentiary landscape and deprived Ms. Kittle of the opportunity to prepare her defense against the complete record that would ultimately be used against her. Ms. Kittle's counsel was placed in the untenable position of having to either request a continuance (which would have delayed the proceedings) or proceed without adequate time to review the newly-supplemented evidence.

As this Court stated in State v. Baston, 85 Ohio St. 3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), there is a presumption of impartiality only "in absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony." Here, the trial court's actions constituted prodding to elicit additional evidence for the prosecution. The trial court manifestly abused its discretion. State v. Grad, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 43.

## III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: THE AMENDMENT OF THE INDICTMENT VIOLATED MS. KITTLE'S RIGHTS

The prosecution argues that any error in amending the indictment was harmless because the trial court relied only on the original date. This Court should reject this argument and sustain Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error.

### The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Amending the Indictment

The prosecution correctly notes that this Court reviews a trial court's decision to allow amendment of an indictment for an abuse of discretion. State v. Sauto, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. However, the prosecution ignores the fundamental issue: the amendment expanded the timeframe of the indictment from a single specific date to an entire week, fundamentally altering the nature of the charge.

Article I, Section 10 of the Ohio Constitution and the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution guarantee the right to indictment by a grand jury. A defendant has the right to know the specific charges against her so that she may prepare a defense. By allowing the State to amend the indictment to cover an entire week instead of the specific date returned by the grand jury, the trial court deprived Ms. Kittle of this fundamental right.

### The Prosecution's Harmlessness Argument Fails

The prosecution argues that no prejudice resulted because the trial court ultimately relied only on the December 2, 2014 date. However, this argument ignores the reality of the proceedings. The trial court allowed the amendment specifically to permit the prosecution to present evidence of conduct occurring during the entire week preceding December 2, 2014. The trial court's willingness to consider evidence outside the original timeframe necessarily affected its evaluation of the case, even if the journal entry ultimately referenced only one date.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument conflates the trial court's stated rationale with the actual impact of the amendment. When a court expands the timeframe of an indictment, the defense must prepare to defend against conduct occurring during that expanded period. This requires different evidence, different witnesses, and a different defense strategy. The harm is in the expansion itself, not merely in what the trial court ultimately credited.

Ms. Kittle was prejudiced by the amendment. State v. Dudukovich, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant Dana Kittle respectfully requests that this Court sustain all three assignments of error, reverse the judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas, and remand this matter for a new trial.

The trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records through an unqualified witness, thereby implicating Ms. Kittle's Confrontation Clause rights. The trial court further erred by actively directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence mid-trial, abdicating its role as a neutral finder of fact. Finally, the trial court erred by allowing the amendment of the indictment to expand the timeframe, violating Ms. Kittle's constitutional right to indictment by a grand jury.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the record, the prosecution's opening argument, and the defense's opening argument. Based on the facts provided and the legal arguments presented, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling.

The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS**

The Defendant argues that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records, claiming they are testimonial and that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness to lay the foundation. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive.

**A. Confrontation Clause**
The defense contends that NPLEX records are testimonial because their primary purpose is law enforcement interdiction. However, testimonial statements are those created with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony. *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015). The NPLEX system is designed to track pseudoephedrine purchases for regulatory compliance. As noted in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), it is improbable that a pharmacy employee conducting a standard identification check would anticipate the records being used against a specific defendant at trial. The system's function of tracking sales does not transform it into a testimonial instrument. Therefore, the Confrontation Clause was not implicated.

**B. Qualified Witness**
The defense argues that Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6) because he is not a pharmacy employee or system custodian. This argument misinterprets the rule. Evidence Rule 803(6) permits the admission of business records through the testimony of a "custodian or other qualified witness." A qualified witness is defined as someone with sufficient familiarity with the record-keeping system to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business. *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St. 3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40.

The record reflects that Officer Lemonier possesses a license to access the NPLEX system, uses it daily, and receives real-time alerts. He testified specifically regarding the process: an ID is swiped, information is transmitted to the database, and details such as pill count and grams are generated. This testimony demonstrates his familiarity with the operation of the system and the circumstances of the record's retrieval. As held in *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, a witness need not have personal knowledge of the exact preparation of the document but must understand the system's operation. Officer Lemonier met this standard. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: SUPPLEMENTATION OF EVIDENCE**

The Defendant argues that the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the State to supplement its evidence mid-trial. The Court finds no reversible error.

Evidence Rule 614(B) authorizes a trial court to interrogate witnesses impartially to ascertain the truth. *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. In a bench trial, the court has greater flexibility to clarify facts without the risk of prejudicing a jury.

Here, the trial court acted to ensure the complete record was before the court after the defense noted missing information. The court allowed the officer to print an expanded version of the NPLEX record. Crucially, the trial court offered the defense time to review the new information, but defense counsel elected to proceed without delay. The defense claims prejudice resulted from the court's intervention. However, where the defense voluntarily chooses to proceed without a continuance after being offered the opportunity, it is difficult to establish manifest abuse of discretion or prejudice. *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St. 3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999). The trial court's actions were consistent with its duty to find the truth and did not constitute advocacy for the prosecution.

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: AMENDMENT OF INDICTMENT**

The Defendant argues that the trial court erred by amending the indictment to expand the timeframe of the offense. The Court finds that any error was harmless.

A trial court's decision to amend an indictment is reviewed for abuse of discretion. To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.

The trial court permitted the State to amend the indictment to include the preceding seven days. However, the trial court's journal entry explicitly states that the guilty verdict was based solely on the original date, December 2, 2014. The defense argues that the amendment itself caused prejudice by requiring preparation for a wider timeframe. While the expansion of the indictment is a significant procedural step, the lack of prejudice is dispositive. Because the conviction was founded on conduct occurring on the specific date originally charged, the defense was not prejudiced by the inclusion of the additional dates in the indictment. The prosecution's argument that the error was harmless is sustained.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the prosecution's arguments are accepted. The Defendant's three assignments of error are overruled. The conviction is affirmed.

## Introduction

## I. Assignment of Error I: The Trial Court Erred in Admitting NPLEX Records

### A. The Prosecution's Mischaracterization of the Confrontation Clause Issue

The prosecution contends that NPLEX records are not testimonial because they serve a regulatory purpose. However, this argument ignores the specific statutory framework governing NPLEX in Ohio and the primary purpose analysis required under *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015).

The defense acknowledges that the appellate court previously addressed this issue, but the prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015) is misplaced. In *Collins*, the Sixth Circuit merely noted that the MethCheck records were "not clearly testimonial in nature," using language that indicates uncertainty rather than a definitive holding. The court stated it was "improbable" that pharmacy employees would anticipate trial use, but this probabilistic analysis does not control the situation where, as here, the system is specifically designed for law enforcement purposes.

Under Ohio law, the NPLEX system was established pursuant to Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05 specifically to combat methamphetamine production. The system electronically tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine precisely because that ingredient is used in manufacturing methamphetamine. The primary purpose of this system is not merely regulatory compliance but rather law enforcement interdiction. When records are created specifically for the purpose of aiding criminal investigations and prosecutions, they bear the requisite "testimonial" character to trigger Sixth Amendment protections. *See State v. Steele*, 95 Ohio St. 3d 202, 2002-Ohio-1608, 767 N.E.2d 116 (2002) (holding that business records created for law enforcement purposes may be testimonial).

### B. Officer Lemonier Was Not a Qualified Witness

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier qualified as a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6). This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution confuses general familiarity with a system with the specific knowledge required to authenticate business records. In *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St. 3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, the Ohio Supreme Court defined a qualified witness as someone with "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." The key phrase is "record-keeping system of the business." Officer Lemonier is not employed by the pharmacy that created the records, nor by the NPLEX system operator. He has no knowledge of how the pharmacy inputs data, how the NPLEX system maintains and processes records, or whether the specific records at issue were made in the regular course of business.

The prosecution cites *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, but that case is distinguishable. In *Baker*, the witness was a hospital employee who personally knew the record-keeping practices of the hospital. Here, Officer Lemonier's knowledge comes from his law enforcement use of the system, not from any personal involvement in the business's record-keeping practices.

Second, Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he knows how to access and retrieve records from NPLEX—he did not establish that the records were made in the regular course of business at the time they were created. The foundational requirement under Evidence Rule 803(6) requires testimony that the record "was kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity, and if it was the regular practice of that business activity to make the memorandum, report, record, or data compilation." Officer Lemonier never testified that it was the regular practice of the pharmacy to make these records. He testified only that the NPLEX system generates records when purchases occur, but this does not establish the pharmacy's regular practice.

The prosecution's argument that "general familiarity" suffices is contrary to the plain language of Evidence Rule 803(6) and the holdings in *Hood* and *Baker*. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting these records without proper foundation.

---

## II. Assignment of Error II: The Trial Court Abdicated Its Role as Neutral Factfinder

The prosecution argues that the trial court's intervention was proper under Evidence Rule 614(B) and that no prejudice resulted because the defense was offered time to review the expanded records. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, Evidence Rule 614(B) authorizes the court to "interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner." However, the trial court's actions went beyond impartial questioning. The court actively directed the prosecution to "supplement its evidence" by requesting that Officer Lemonier print an expanded version of the NPLEX record. This was not interrogation of a witness—it was advocacy for the prosecution. The court essentially performed the function of the prosecution by identifying a gap in the evidence and directing that it be filled.

In *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, this Court recognized that the purpose of Evidence Rule 614(B) is "to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness." While this case involved a bench trial rather than a jury trial, the principle remains important: the trial judge must remain neutral. By actively directing the prosecution to supplement its case, the trial court crossed the line from impartial factfinder to advocate.

Second, the prosecution's claim that no prejudice resulted is incorrect. The defense was put in the position of having to cross-examine a witness about evidence that had not been disclosed prior to trial. While the trial court offered time to review the new evidence, this offer did not cure the fundamental unfairness of the situation. The defense had prepared its cross-examination based on the original NPLEX record and was forced to either proceed without adequate preparation or request a continuance—itself a prejudice because it would have signaled to the court that the defense needed more time to address the prosecution's new evidence.

The prosecution cites *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St. 3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999) for the presumption of impartiality. However, *Baston* involved situations where the trial court's questions were designed to clarify testimony, not to supplement the prosecution's case. The court in *Baston* specifically noted that there must be "no showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony." Here, the trial court's actions were precisely the "prodding" that *Baston* warns against.

The trial court's actions violated Ms. Kittle's constitutional right to a fair trial before an impartial judge. This assignment of error should be sustained.

## III. Assignment of Error III: The Trial Court Erred in Amending the Indictment

The prosecution argues that any error in amending the indictment was harmless because the trial court's journal entry indicated the conviction was based only on the original date. This argument ignores the prejudice that results from an improperly amended indictment regardless of the evidence actually relied upon.

Article I, Section 10 of the Ohio Constitution guarantees the right to indictment by a grand jury. This right is fundamental and protects against prosecution for conduct not properly charged. When the prosecution seeks to amend an indictment to expand the timeframe of alleged criminal conduct, it fundamentally alters the nature of the charges.

In *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16, this Court held that to constitute reversible error, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. The prosecution's argument that no prejudice resulted because the conviction was based on the original date misconstrues the nature of the prejudice.

Ms. Kittle was charged with illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. The original indictment charged conduct "on or about December 2, 2014." The amendment expanded this to include "the preceding seven days." This expansion required the defense to prepare for evidence regarding conduct spanning approximately one week rather than a single day. The defense was forced to guess what specific conduct the prosecution was alleging and could not properly prepare its defense.

The trial court's journal entry stating it relied only on the December 2, 2014 date does not cure the prejudice. The defense was required to defend against the expanded timeframe during trial, not knowing which date or dates the prosecution would rely upon. This uncertainty inherently prejudices the defense's ability to prepare and present its case.

Furthermore, the prosecution's argument that the amendment was harmless assumes that the evidence supports the conviction on the original date. However, the defense maintains that the evidence was insufficient to establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt on any date, and the improper amendment compounded the error by forcing the defense to respond to a moving target.

**UPDATED RULING**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. Upon consideration of the arguments presented regarding the three assignments of error, the Court affirms the judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas.

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS**

The Defendant reiterates her argument that the NPLEX records are testimonial and that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness to authenticate them. The prosecution counters that the records are non-testimonial business records and that Officer Lemonier possessed sufficient familiarity with the system to lay the proper foundation. The Court agrees with the prosecution.

**A. Confrontation Clause**
The defense argues that the NPLEX records are testimonial because the system was designed for law enforcement interdiction under Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05, citing *State v. Steele*, 95 Ohio St. 3d 202, 2002-Ohio-1608, 767 N.E.2d 116 (2002). However, the prosecution correctly identifies the controlling standard set forth in *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), which holds that testimonial statements are those created with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony.

The defense attempts to distinguish *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), arguing that the Sixth Circuit's use of the term "improbable" indicates uncertainty. The Court finds this distinction unpersuasive. *Collins* directly addressed a similar methamphetamine-tracking system and held that such records are "not clearly testimonial in nature" because it is improbable that a pharmacy employee would anticipate the records being used against a specific defendant at trial. The primary purpose of the NPLEX system is to track pseudoephedrine purchases for regulatory compliance, not to create evidence for a specific prosecution. Therefore, the Confrontation Clause was not implicated.

**B. Qualified Witness**
The defense argues that Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6) because he is not a pharmacy employee and lacks knowledge of the "record-keeping system of the business" regarding data input. The defense distinguishes *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, by noting that the witness in *Baker* was a hospital employee.

The prosecution's argument is sustained. Evidence Rule 803(6) allows a business record to be authenticated by the testimony of the "custodian or other qualified witness." *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St. 3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40. A qualified witness is one with enough familiarity to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business. Officer Lemonier testified that he holds a license to access the NPLEX system, uses it daily, and explained the process by which an ID is swiped and data is transmitted to the database. This testimony established his familiarity with the operation of the system and the retrieval of the records. As held in *State v. Baker*, a witness need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of the document's preparation but must demonstrate familiarity with the system's operation. Officer Lemonier met this standard. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: SUPPLEMENTATION OF EVIDENCE**

The defense argues that the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence, characterizing this as "prodding" under *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St. 3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999). The prosecution did not address this assignment of error in its rebuttal.

The Court finds no reversible error. Evidence Rule 614(B) permits a trial court to interrogate witnesses impartially to ascertain the truth. *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. While the defense argues the court crossed the line into advocacy, the record shows the court acted to clarify the record after the defense pointed out missing information. The court offered the defense a continuance to review the expanded record, but defense counsel elected to proceed without delay. In a bench trial, where there is no jury to influence, the court has greater flexibility to ensure a complete record. The defense cannot establish manifest abuse of discretion or prejudice where it voluntarily chose to proceed without the offered continuance. *State v. Baston* at 426.

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: AMENDMENT OF INDICTMENT**

The defense argues that the trial court's amendment of the indictment to include the preceding seven days prejudiced the defense by forcing it to prepare for a wider timeframe, regardless of the final verdict date. The prosecution did not address this assignment of error.

The Court finds that any error was harmless. A trial court's decision to amend an indictment is reviewed for abuse of discretion. To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.

The defense claims prejudice in the preparation phase. However, the trial court's journal entry explicitly states that the guilty verdict was based solely on the original date, December 2, 2014. Because the conviction was founded on conduct occurring on the specific date originally charged, the defense was not prejudiced by the inclusion of the additional dates in the indictment. The defense's argument that it was forced to defend against a "moving target" does not overcome the fact that the court's finding of guilt was limited to the original date. Therefore, the second prong of *Dudukovich* is not met.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the prosecution's arguments regarding Assignment of Error I are persuasive. The defense's arguments regarding Assignments of Error II and III, while raising valid procedural concerns, fail to demonstrate reversible error under the applicable standards of review. The Defendant's three assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense. Upon careful consideration of the rebuttals regarding the three assignments of error, the Court affirms the judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas.

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS**

The Defendant argues that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records, asserting they are testimonial in nature and that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness to authenticate them. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive.

**A. Confrontation Clause**
The defense contends that the NPLEX records are testimonial because the system was designed for law enforcement interdiction. The standard for determining whether a statement is testimonial is whether it was created "with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." Ohio v. Clark, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015). The defense attempts to distinguish United States v. Collins, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), by focusing on the Sixth Circuit's use of the term "improbable." However, the holding in Collins is clear: records from similar methamphetamine-tracking systems are "not clearly testimonial in nature" because it is improbable that a pharmacy employee would anticipate the records being used against a specific defendant at trial. The primary purpose of the NPLEX system is to track pseudoephedrine purchases for regulatory compliance, not to create evidence for a specific prosecution. The defense's argument that the statutory purpose of interdiction transforms these routine business records into testimonial statements is not supported by the primary purpose test articulated in Clark. Therefore, the Confrontation Clause was not implicated.

**B. Qualified Witness**
The defense argues that Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6) because he lacked familiarity with the pharmacies' internal record-keeping practices. This argument misinterprets the legal standard. Under State v. Hood, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, a qualified witness is one with "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." The business in question here is the tracking of pseudoephedrine sales via the NPLEX system.

As held in State v. Baker, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, "the witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." The witness need only demonstrate familiarity with the operation of the system. Officer Lemonier testified that he holds a license to access NPLEX, uses it daily, and explained the technical process by which an ID is swiped and data is transmitted to the database. This testimony established his sufficient familiarity with the NPLEX system to authenticate the records. The defense's assertion that Officer Lemonier was required to testify specifically about the pharmacies' internal manual of operations is a hyper-technical reading of the rule not supported by precedent. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: SUPPLEMENTATION OF EVIDENCE**

The defense argues that the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence, characterizing this as "prodding" or advocacy. The Court finds no reversible error.

Evidence Rule 614(B) permits a trial court to interrogate witnesses impartially to ascertain the truth. State v. Grad, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. In a bench trial, the trial judge is afforded greater flexibility in questioning witnesses because there is no jury to influence. State v. Baston, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999).

Here, the trial court acted to clarify the record after the defense pointed out missing information in the NPLEX exhibit. The court offered the defense a continuance to review the expanded record, but defense counsel elected to proceed without delay. The defense cannot establish manifest abuse of discretion or prejudice where it voluntarily chose to proceed without the offered continuance. The court's actions were an attempt to ensure a complete factual record, not an advocacy for the prosecution.

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: AMENDMENT OF INDICTMENT**

The defense argues that the trial court's amendment of the indictment to include the preceding seven days prejudiced the defense by forcing it to prepare for a wider timeframe. The Court finds that any error was harmless.

A trial court's decision to amend an indictment is reviewed for abuse of discretion. To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. State v. Dudukovich, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.

The defense claims prejudice in the preparation phase, arguing it faced a "moving target." However, the trial court's journal entry explicitly states that the guilty verdict was based solely on the original date, December 2, 2014. Because the conviction was founded on conduct occurring on the specific date originally charged, the defense was not prejudiced by the inclusion of the additional dates in the indictment. The defense's argument that it was forced to defend against a wider timeframe does not overcome the fact that the court's finding of guilt was limited to the original date. Therefore, the second prong of Dudukovich is not met.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Defendant's three assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
