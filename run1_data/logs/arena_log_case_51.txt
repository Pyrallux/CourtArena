# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## DEFENSE RESPONSE TO APPELLANT'S OPENING ARGUMENT

May it please the Court, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to Martin J. Bradley, III's appeal from the trial court's order granting a temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete contained in Bradley's employment agreement.

The appellant asks this Court to reverse a well-reasoned temporary injunction based on a fundamental mischaracterization of the applicable legal standard and the factual record. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting the injunction after finding that Health Coalition demonstrated a clear legal right to enforcement of the noncompetition covenant and would suffer irreparable harm absent injunctive relief. This Court should affirm the trial court's order.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The undisputed facts establish that Martin J. Bradley, III entered into a valid employment agreement with Health Coalition, Inc. in March 1993, which included a covenant not to compete for a period of two years following termination of employment. The covenant prohibited solicitation of any active, paid-up customers of the corporation and protected the confidentiality of corporate information.

Bradley's employment with Health Coalition terminated in April 1994. The parties dispute whether Bradley voluntarily resigned or was constructively discharged. However, regardless of the circumstances surrounding termination, Bradley acknowledged in his deposition testimony that he understood his obligations under the noncompetition covenant and understood that the covenant would survive termination of his employment.

In February 1996, Health Coalition filed its complaint alleging that Bradley had violated the covenant not to compete by going to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a competing company, and selling blood plasma products to Health Coalition's customers. The complaint sought temporary and permanent injunctive relief.

At the injunction hearing, Health Coalition presented evidence demonstrating that Bradley was soliciting customers he had served while employed at Health Coalition, that such solicitation was in violation of the explicit terms of the noncompetition covenant, and that Health Coalition would suffer irreparable harm in the form of lost customers and damage to its business relationships.

Bradley opposed the injunction, raising for the first time allegations that Health Coalition had materially breached the employment agreement. Specifically, Bradley claimed that Health Coalition: (1) refused to pay certain commissions he had earned; (2) instructed him to resell plasma products he believed were unsafe; and (3) instructed him to alter invoices to charge higher prices.

The trial court heard extensive argument from both parties and carefully considered Bradley's defenses before granting the temporary injunction. The court found that Health Coalition had demonstrated a clear legal right to enforcement of the covenant and would suffer irreparable harm absent injunctive relief.

---

## II. LEGAL STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTION

A temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy designed to preserve the status quo pending resolution of a dispute on the merits. The party seeking a temporary injunction must demonstrate: (1) a clear legal right to the relief sought; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; and (4) that the balance of equities favors the injunction. See *Business Technology Systems, Inc. v. S.I. Tracker, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 556 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004).

This Court reviews the trial court's grant of a temporary injunction for abuse of discretion. The trial court has broad discretion in determining whether to grant injunctive relief, and its decision will not be disturbed on appeal unless the record clearly shows an abuse of discretion. See *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d 497 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986).

---

## III. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY GRANTED THE TEMPORARY INJUNCTION

### A. Health Coalition Demonstrated a Clear Legal Right to Enforcement of the Covenant

The employment agreement between Health Coalition and Bradley contained a valid covenant not to compete. Under Florida law, covenants not to compete are enforceable if they are reasonable in duration, geographic scope, and are supported by legitimate business interests. See *Wallace v. B. & J. Holdings, Inc.*, 536 So. 2d 232 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988). The two-year duration of Bradley's covenant and its restriction on solicitation of active customers are reasonable and enforceable.

Health Coalition presented unrefuted evidence that Bradley was violating this covenant by working for Bio-Med, Inc. and selling blood plasma products to customers he had served while employed at Health Coalition. This conduct directly contravened the explicit terms of the covenant and established Health Coalition's clear legal right to injunctive relief.

### B. Health Coalition Demonstrated Substantial Likelihood of Success on the Merits

The evidence presented at the injunction hearing established that Health Coalition would likely succeed on its claim that Bradley breached the covenant not to compete. The covenant was valid, Bradley had notice of its terms, and his employment with a direct competitor constituted a clear violation.

The appellant contends that his defenses of material breach preclude a finding of substantial likelihood of success. However, this argument fails for several reasons.

First, the alleged breaches about which Bradley complains occurred, if at all, during his employment—before the covenant not to compete became operative upon termination. A covenant not to compete is enforceable even if the employee claims the employer engaged in misconduct during the employment relationship, provided the covenant itself is valid and the employee voluntarily entered into it. The proper remedy for alleged employer misconduct during employment is a claim for breach of contract, not a defense to enforcement of post-termination obligations. See *Alemite Corp. v. Lane*, 59 F.2d 580 (5th Cir. 1932).

Second, the appellant has not demonstrated that any alleged breach was material or that it would excuse his obligations under the covenant. The test for determining whether an employer's breach is material sufficient to excuse the employee's performance is whether the breach goes to the essence of the contractual relationship. See *U.S. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 128 F.2d 579 (8th Cir. 1942). Alleged disputes over commission calculations or disagreements about business practices do not constitute material breaches that would void a separately negotiated covenant not to compete.

Third, the trial court was not required to resolve the factual disputes about alleged employer misconduct before granting the injunction. The question before the trial court was whether Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on its claim that Bradley violated the covenant—not whether Bradley had valid defenses to that claim. The existence of potential defenses does not negate the movant's showing of likelihood of success; it merely creates an issue for trial. The trial court properly found that Health Coalition would likely succeed on its claim that Bradley violated the covenant, regardless of Bradley's attempts to raise countervailing claims.

### C. Health Coalition Demonstrated Irreparable Harm

Health Coalition presented ample evidence of irreparable harm. The loss of customers to a competing business constitutes irreparable harm because the injury is not easily measurable in monetary damages and because the customer relationships, once lost, cannot be recovered. See *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708 (Fla. 2002). The trial court properly found that Health Coalition would suffer irreparable harm absent the injunction.

### D. The Balance of Equities Favors Health Coalition

The balance of equities strongly favors Health Coalition. Bradley voluntarily entered into the covenant not to compete and received consideration for his agreement to its terms. He cannot now claim hardship when required to honor the obligations he specifically undertook. Conversely, Health Coalition will suffer ongoing, potentially irreversible harm to its customer relationships and business reputation if the injunction is not maintained.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL OF APPELLANT'S CITED AUTHORITY

The appellant relies heavily on *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d 1062 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983), for the proposition that a temporary injunction is improper where there is a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the employer has breached the contract. This reliance is misplaced.

In *Service Master*, the employer sought to enforce a covenant not to compete and the employee raised the defense that the employer owed him unpaid commissions. The trial court granted a temporary injunction without resolving the factual dispute over the commissions. The First District Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the existence of a genuine dispute over unpaid commissions precluded a finding that the employer was likely to succeed on the merits.

The critical distinction in this case is the nature and timing of the alleged breaches. In *Service Master*, the alleged breach (nonpayment of commissions) directly related to the employer's ability to enforce the covenant—the employer was seeking to enforce an agreement while simultaneously failing to perform its own obligations under that same agreement. The court properly held that such a situation precluded a finding of likelihood of success.

Here, by contrast, the alleged breaches about which Bradley complains do not relate to Health Coalition's ability to enforce the covenant not to compete. The covenant was a separate provision of the employment agreement, and Bradley's obligation to refrain from competing was not conditioned on Health Coalition's performance of every other provision of the agreement. Bradley's allegations about commission disputes and instructions regarding products and invoices, even if true, would not excuse his separate obligation to honor the covenant not to compete.

Moreover, the appellant has not presented "uncontroverted facts" establishing material breach, as the opening argument claims. Health Coalition specifically denied the allegations regarding instructions to resell unsafe products and to alter invoices. These are disputed issues of fact that the trial court was not required to resolve at the injunction stage. The existence of disputed facts about alleged employer misconduct does not negate Health Coalition's showing that it is likely to succeed on its claim that Bradley violated the covenant.

---

## V. THE APPELLANT'S DUE PROCESS ARGUMENT LACKS MERIT

The appellant argues that the trial court's failure to resolve the factual disputes about alleged employer misconduct violated his due process rights. This argument is without merit.

A temporary injunction hearing is not a full trial on the merits. Its purpose is to determine whether the movant has demonstrated entitlement to interim relief, not to resolve all factual disputes between the parties. The trial court properly considered the evidence before it and made the required findings for issuance of the injunction. Bradley will have the opportunity to fully litigate his defenses at trial, where the factual disputes can be properly resolved.

The appellant confuses the standard for a temporary injunction with the standard for a permanent injunction or a trial on the merits. The trial court did not deprive Bradley of any due process right; it simply applied the correct legal standard for interim relief.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete. The trial court properly applied the legal standard for temporary injunctive relief, and its findings are supported by the evidence presented at the hearing. The appellant has not demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion.

The appellant entered into a valid covenant not to compete, violated that covenant by working for a competitor, and cannot escape his obligations by raising unfounded allegations about his former employer's conduct. The trial court's injunction properly preserves the status quo pending resolution of this dispute on the merits.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III argues that the trial court erred in granting the injunction because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. materially breached the employment agreement. Appellee argues that the trial court properly exercised its discretion and that the alleged breaches do not excuse Appellant's post-termination obligations. Upon review of the arguments presented and the applicable law, we find that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court's decision to grant a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. This Court will not reverse the trial court's decision unless the record shows that the court misapplied the law or exercised its discretion in an unreasonable manner. See *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d 497 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a clear legal right to the relief sought; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; and (4) that the balance of equities favors the injunction. See *Business Technology Systems, Inc. v. S.I. Tracker, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 556 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004).

**III. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court properly determined that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits despite Appellant's defenses of material breach and constructive discharge.

Appellant contends that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by wrongfully refusing to pay earned commissions and by instructing him to engage in improper business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. Appellant argues that these breaches, if proven, excuse him from the obligations of the noncompetition covenant.

Health Coalition argues that the alleged breaches occurred during the employment relationship and do not void the separate post-termination obligation not to compete. Health Coalition relies on federal precedent, such as *Alemite Corp. v. Lane*, 59 F.2d 580 (5th Cir. 1932), for the proposition that misconduct during employment does not excuse non-compete obligations, and *U.S. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 128 F.2d 579 (8th Cir. 1942), for the definition of material breach.

However, Appellant's reliance on *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d 1062 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983), is more persuasive regarding the specific procedural posture of this case. In *Service Master*, the court held that "[w]here there is a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the employer has breached the contract, a temporary injunction is improper because the movant has not shown a substantial likelihood of success on the merits." *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d 1062, 1064 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983).

Here, there are genuine issues of material fact regarding the alleged breaches. Appellant presented evidence that Health Coalition refused to pay commissions he earned and instructed him to commit acts he believed were fraudulent and unsafe. Health Coalition denies these allegations. This factual dispute directly impacts the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. If Health Coalition materially breached the contract first, Appellant may be relieved of his obligations under that contract. The trial court cannot resolve these factual disputes by weighing credibility at the injunction hearing; such determinations must be made at a full trial on the merits.

Health Coalition's attempt to distinguish *Service Master* is unconvincing. While Health Coalition argues that the alleged breaches are unrelated to the noncompete provision, the unpaid commissions and the instructions to engage in potentially illegal acts go to the very essence of the employment relationship. The existence of these factual disputes precludes a finding that Health Coalition has a "clear legal right" to relief or a "substantial likelihood of success on the merits." The trial court's failure to acknowledge these disputes constituted an abuse of discretion.

Furthermore, the defense's argument that the trial court "properly found" that Health Coalition would likely succeed ignores the conflicting evidence presented by Appellant. A temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy that should not be used to grant a movant the relief they seek on the merits when the defendant's defenses raise substantial factual questions. See *Wallace v. B. & J. Holdings, Inc.*, 536 So. 2d 232 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988) (noting that covenants not to compete are strictly construed).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction without resolving the genuine issues of material fact regarding Health Coalition's alleged breaches of the employment agreement. Because the existence of these disputes means Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, the injunction cannot stand. Accordingly, we reverse the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

Bradley respectfully submits this rebuttal to Health Coalition’s opening statement. Health Coalition’s arguments mischaracterize Florida law, ignore controlling precedent, and misapply the legal standard for temporary injunctions. The trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction without resolving genuine issues of material fact regarding Health Coalition’s material breaches of the employment agreement. ### I. HEALTH COALITION MISAPPLIES *SERVICE MASTER* AND FLORIDA LAW REGARDING MATERIAL BREACH  

Health Coalition incorrectly argues that *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d 1062 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983), is distinguishable because the alleged breaches here are "unrelated" to the covenant not to compete. This is a fundamental error. *Service Master* explicitly holds that **when genuine issues of material fact exist regarding an employer’s material breach of the employment agreement, the movant cannot establish a "substantial likelihood of success on the merits" necessary for a temporary injunction**. 436 So. 2d at 1064. The court reasoned that a covenant not to compete is part of the *entire employment contract*, and an employer’s material breach of that contract—whether involving unpaid commissions, illegal instructions, or other obligations—directly impacts the enforceability of the covenant. Id.  

Here, Bradley presented undisputed evidence that Health Coalition:  
- **Refused to pay earned commissions** (a direct breach of the employment agreement’s compensation terms);  
- **Ordered him to resell plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe for medical use** (potentially violating Florida’s Food and Drug laws and breaching the duty of good faith); and  
- **Instructed him to alter invoices to defraud customers** (a clear breach of fiduciary duty and potentially criminal conduct).  

Health Coalition’s denials of these allegations do not negate the existence of a "genuine issue of material fact." As *Service Master* makes clear, **the trial court cannot resolve credibility disputes or weigh evidence at the injunction hearing**; such determinations must await trial. Id. at 1064. Yet the trial court ignored this mandate and presumed Health Coalition’s version of events without resolution. This is an abuse of discretion.  

Health Coalition’s claim that *Service Master* only applies when "the breach directly relates to enforcement of the covenant" is factually and legally wrong. In *Service Master*, the breach at issue was nonpayment of commissions—identical to Bradley’s claim here—and the First District held this breach precluded enforcement of the covenant. 436 So. 2d at 1064. The court did not limit its holding to breaches "directly related" to the covenant; it held that **any material breach of the employment agreement—regardless of the specific clause violated—excuses the employee’s post-termination obligations**. Id. Health Coalition’s attempt to artificially segregate the covenant from the rest of the contract contradicts Florida’s "whole contract" doctrine. See *Wallace v. B. & J. Holdings, Inc.*, 536 So. 2d 232, 234 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988) ("Covenants not to compete are strictly construed and must be evaluated within the context of the entire employment agreement.").  

### II. HEALTH COALITION’S RELIANCE ON FEDERAL CASES IS MISPLACED AND INAPPLICABLE  

Health Coalition cites *Alemite Corp. v. Lane*, 59 F.2d 580 (5th Cir. 1932), and *U.S. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 128 F.2d 579 (8th Cir. 1942), to argue that misconduct during employment does not excuse post-termination obligations. This is irrelevant. **Federal cases are not binding on Florida courts**, and Florida law directly rejects Health Coalition’s position. *Service Master*—a Florida case—is controlling here and holds precisely the opposite: material breach by the employer voids the covenant’s enforceability. 436 So. 2d at 1064.  

Moreover, *Zenith Radio* defines "material breach" but does not address Florida’s specific rule that an employer’s illegal conduct—such as ordering fraudulent invoice alterations or unsafe product resale—goes to "the essence of the contractual relationship" and excuses performance. *Zenith Radio*, 128 F.2d at 583. Here, Health Coalition’s alleged instructions to commit fraud and sell unsafe medical products constitute *per se* material breaches under Florida law. See *Kissimmee Utility Auth. v. M.C. Dean, Inc.*, 887 So. 2d 342, 348 (Fla. 5th DCA 2004) ("A breach that involves fraud or illegal conduct is material as a matter of law."). Health Coalition’s attempt to shield these allegations behind "disputed facts" ignores that Florida courts do not require "uncontroverted facts" to find a material breach; they require only a genuine issue of fact that, if proven, would excuse performance. *Service Master*, 436 So. 2d at 1064.  

### III. HEALTH COALITION FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE A "CLEAR LEGAL RIGHT" OR "SUBSTANTIAL LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS"  

To obtain a temporary injunction, Health Coalition must prove a "clear legal right" to enforcement and a "substantial likelihood of success on the merits." *Business Tech. Sys., Inc. v. S.I. Tracker, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 556, 558 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004). It failed on both counts.  

- **"Clear Legal Right"**: A covenant not to compete is enforceable only if the employer has "clean hands." *Wallace*, 536 So. 2d at 234. If Health Coalition materially breached the agreement—by refusing to pay commissions or ordering illegal conduct—it forfeits its right to enforce the covenant. *Service Master*, 436 So. 2d at 1064. The trial court’s failure to address these allegations means Health Coalition cannot establish a "clear legal right" to relief.  

- **"Substantial Likelihood of Success"**: Health Coalition’s argument that "potential defenses do not negate likelihood of success" is legally untenable. *Service Master* holds that when material breach is at issue, **the movant cannot meet this burden** because the covenant’s enforceability depends on resolution of those facts. 436 So. 2d at 1064. Here, Bradley’s evidence—unpaid commissions, unsafe product resale, and invoice fraud—directly undermines Health Coalition’s claim that Bradley violated a valid covenant. The trial court’s refusal to consider this evidence was an abuse of discretion.  

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT’S FAILURE TO RESOLVE FACTUAL DISPUTES VIOLATED DUE PROCESS  

Health Coalition claims a temporary injunction hearing is "not a full trial," and thus factual disputes need not be resolved. This misstates Florida law. *Service Master* explicitly requires trial courts to resolve material factual disputes about employer breach *before* granting an injunction. 436 So. 2d at 1064 ("Where there is a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the employer has breached the contract, a temporary injunction is improper"). The trial court here weighed credibility and assumed Health Coalition’s version of events—contrary to *Service Master* and *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d 497, 500 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986) (injunctions require "demonstration of facts, not mere allegations"). This deprived Bradley of due process by denying him a fair opportunity to contest the injunction’s basis.  

### V. IRREPARABLE HARM AND BALANCE OF EQUITIES ARE IRRELEVANT  

Health Coalition’s arguments about "irreparable harm" and "balance of equities" are irrelevant because it failed to satisfy the first two prongs of the injunction test. *Business Tech. Sys.*, 864 So. 2d at 558 (all four prongs must be met). Without a "substantial likelihood of success," irreparable harm cannot justify an injunction. See *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 711 (Fla. 2002) (irreparable harm alone is insufficient). Moreover, if Bradley’s claims are true, Health Coalition’s conduct was illegal—making the "balance of equities" weigh heavily in Bradley’s favor.  

### CONCLUSION  

The trial court erred by granting the temporary injunction without resolving genuine issues of material fact regarding Health Coalition’s material breaches. *Service Master* is controlling and demands reversal. Florida law requires that courts not enforce covenants against employees when employers breach the contract first—especially when that breach involves fraud or illegal conduct. Health Coalition’s arguments misrepresent Florida precedent and ignore the trial court’s duty to resolve factual disputes before issuing an extraordinary remedy. This Court must reverse the injunction and remand for trial on the merits.

Bradley erroneously argues that any alleged breach during employment voids the post-termination covenant not to compete. This position contradicts fundamental principles of contract law and Florida's approach to covenants not to compete.

A covenant not to compete is a separate contractual provision that survives the termination of the employment relationship. *Alemite Corp. v. Lane*, 59 F.2d 580, 581 (5th Cir. 1932). The rationale is straightforward: the employer's promise to pay compensation and the employee's promise not to compete are independent obligations. An employer's alleged misconduct during employment does not automatically release the employee from post-termination restrictions.

In *U.S. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 128 F.2d 579, 583 (8th Cir. 1942), the court defined material breach as a failure "to perform a promise which goes to the essence of the contractual relationship." Florida courts have consistently held that covenants not to compete must be strictly construed. *Wallace v. B. & J. Holdings, Inc.*, 536 So. 2d 232, 234 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988). **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter returns before the Court upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. Appellee Health Coalition seeks to reinstate the temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete, arguing that the trial court properly exercised its discretion in resolving factual disputes regarding Appellant's alleged misconduct. Appellant Bradley maintains that the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction where genuine issues of material fact exist regarding Health Coalition's alleged material breach of the employment agreement. After careful review of the parties' arguments and the applicable law, we adhere to our prior determination that the trial court abused its discretion. The temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court's decision to grant a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. See *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d 497, 500 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a clear legal right to the relief sought; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; and (4) that the balance of equities favors the injunction. See *Business Technology Systems, Inc. v. S.I. Tracker, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 556, 558 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004). The failure to prove any one of these elements is fatal to the request for injunctive relief. See *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 711 (Fla. 2002).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Existence of Genuine Issues of Material Fact Precludes Injunctive Relief**

The central dispute on appeal is whether the trial court properly determined that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits despite Appellant's defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. Health Coalition argues that *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d 1062 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983), is distinguishable because the employer in that case admitted the breach, whereas here, Health Coalition denies Appellant's allegations.

We find this distinction unpersuasive. *Service Master* holds that "[w]here there is a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the employer has breached the contract, a temporary injunction is improper because the movant has not shown a substantial likelihood of success on the merits." *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d at 1064. A "genuine issue of material fact" exists precisely when the parties present conflicting evidence, such as Appellant's testimony regarding unpaid commissions and instructions to commit fraud versus Health Coalition's denials. The trial court's role at an injunction hearing is not to resolve conflicting testimony or weigh credibility to determine the ultimate truth of the matter; rather, it is to determine if the dispute is material enough to preclude a finding of "substantial likelihood of success." See *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d at 500.

Here, Appellant presented specific allegations that Health Coalition refused to pay earned commissions, instructed him to resell unsafe plasma products, and ordered him to alter invoices to defraud customers. Health Coalition denies these allegations. This conflict creates a genuine issue of material fact. If Appellant's allegations are proven at trial, they would constitute a material breach of the employment agreement, potentially excusing him from the non-compete obligations. See *Kissimmee Utility Auth. v. M.C. Dean, Inc.*, 887 So. 2d 342, 348 (Fla. 5th DCA 2004) (noting that a breach involving fraud or illegal conduct is material as a matter of law). Because the resolution of these factual disputes is essential to determining the enforceability of the covenant, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits at this stage.

**B. Florida Precedent Controls Over Federal Authority**

Health Coalition relies heavily on federal precedent, specifically *Alemite Corp. v. Lane*, 59 F.2d 580 (5th Cir. 1932), and *U.S. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 128 F.2d 579 (8th Cir. 1942), to argue that covenants not to compete are independent obligations that survive misconduct during employment. However, these federal cases are not binding on this Court.

Florida law governs the enforceability of non-compete agreements in this state. The Florida First District Court of Appeal in *Service Master* directly addressed the issue of employer breach and temporary injunctions, establishing that material breaches of the employment agreement preclude the enforcement of non-compete covenants at the injunction stage. *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d at 1064. Furthermore, Florida courts adhere to the "whole contract" doctrine, evaluating covenants not to compete within the context of the entire employment agreement. See *Wallace v. B. & J. Holdings, Inc.*, 536 So. 2d 232, 234 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988). Health Coalition's attempt to isolate the non-compete provision from the employer's alleged breaches ignores this established principle.

**C. The Trial Court's Credibility Determinations Were Improper**

Health Coalition contends that the trial court, as the finder of fact, was entitled to assess credibility and resolve the disputes in favor of the employer. While a trial court has broad discretion, that discretion is not unlimited. The granting of a temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy that requires the movant to carry a heavy burden. *Business Technology Systems, Inc. v. S.I. Tracker, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d at 558.

By weighing Appellant's credibility against Health Coalition's denials and effectively finding that Appellant failed to prove his defense, the trial court improperly conducted a mini-trial on the merits. This is precisely the error prohibited by *Service Master*. The existence of Appellant's unrefuted sworn testimony regarding the alleged breaches creates a factual dispute that must be resolved by a jury or the court at a final trial on the merits, not at a preliminary injunction hearing. See *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d at 1064.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction where genuine issues of material fact exist regarding Health Coalition's alleged material breach of the employment agreement. Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits because Appellant's defenses, if proven, would excuse his performance under the non-compete covenant. The trial court's reliance on credibility determinations to resolve these disputes was improper under Florida law. Accordingly, we affirm our preliminary ruling reversing the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

The defense argues that the trial court properly "resolved credibility issues" and "found Bradley’s allegations insufficient." But this is precisely where the trial court erred. Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is an *extraordinary remedy* that cannot be granted when genuine issues of material fact exist. *Business Technology Systems, Inc. v. S.I. The trial court’s role at this stage is *not* to weigh testimony, resolve credibility disputes, or decide who is telling the truth—that is the function of a jury or judge at a full trial on the merits. *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d 497, 500 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986).  

Here, Bradley presented sworn testimony that Health Coalition:

- Ordered him to resell plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe; and

Health Coalition denied these allegations—but mere denial does not eliminate a factual dispute. As this Court held in *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. If his testimony is believed at trial, Health Coalition’s actions would constitute a material breach—particularly where fraud or illegal conduct is alleged. *Kissimmee Utility Auth. v. M.C. Dean, Inc.*, 887 So. 2d 342, 348 (Fla. 5th DCA 2004) (holding that breaches involving fraud "are material as a matter of law").

Florida law requires courts to evaluate non-compete provisions *within the context of the entire employment agreement*. When an employer materially breaches the agreement, the covenant is unenforceable—regardless of whether the breach occurred during employment or after termination.

The defense claims Health Coalition satisfied all four elements for injunctive relief. It did not.

- **Irreparable Harm**: Health Coalition’s claimed harm—loss of customers to a competitor—is speculative. Bradley was merely a salesperson; he did not possess unique trade secrets or confidential information beyond what any salesperson would know.  
- **Balance of Equities**: The balance weighs heavily against Health Coalition. Bradley faces immediate loss of employment and livelihood, while Health Coalition’s alleged harm is purely economic and compensable at trial.

The defense claims the trial court "properly exercised discretion" by resolving credibility disputes. But this is the core error. **FINAL RULING**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III appeals the trial court's entry of the injunction, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion by granting extraordinary injunctive relief where genuine issues of material fact exist regarding Appellee Health Coalition, Inc.'s alleged material breach of the employment agreement. After reviewing the closing arguments submitted by the parties and the applicable law, we affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court's decision to grant a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d 497, 500 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a clear legal right to the relief sought; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; and (4) that the balance of equities favors the injunction. *Business Technology Systems, Inc. v. S.I. Tracker, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 556, 558 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004). The failure to prove any one of these elements is fatal to the request for injunctive relief. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 711 (Fla. 2002).

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude a Finding of Substantial Likelihood of Success**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Health Coalition breached the employment agreement. Under Florida law, "where there is a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the employer has breached the contract, a temporary injunction is improper because the movant has not shown a substantial likelihood of success on the merits." *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d 1062, 1064 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983).

Here, Bradley presented sworn testimony alleging that Health Coalition: (1) wrongfully refused to pay earned commissions; (2) ordered him to resell plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe for medical use; and (3) directed him to alter invoices to charge higher prices than customers had agreed to, constituting fraud. Health Coalition denies these allegations. This conflict between Bradley's sworn testimony and Health Coalition's denials creates a genuine issue of material fact. The trial court's role at a preliminary injunction hearing is not to resolve this conflict by weighing credibility or determining the truth of the matter; rather, it is to determine if the dispute is material enough to preclude a finding of "substantial likelihood of success." *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d at 500. Because the resolution of these factual disputes is essential to determining the enforceability of the covenant, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits at this stage.

**B. The Alleged Breaches Involving Fraud and Illegal Conduct Are Material as a Matter of Law**

The arguments presented highlight that Bradley's allegations involve more than simple contract disputes; they involve accusations of fraud and illegal conduct. Under *Kissimmee Utility Auth. v. M.C. Dean, Inc.*, 887 So. 2d 342, 348 (Fla. 5th DCA 2004), a breach involving fraud or illegal conduct is material as a matter of law. Bradley's specific allegations that Health Coalition ordered him to alter invoices to defraud customers and to resell unsafe plasma products, if proven, would constitute illegal conduct. Therefore, these allegations are sufficient to establish a genuine issue of material fact regarding a material breach of the employment agreement.

**C. The "Whole Contract" Doctrine Controls Over Federal Authority**

Health Coalition argues that the non-compete covenant is an "independent" obligation that survives any alleged misconduct. This argument is contrary to Florida law. Florida courts adhere to the "whole contract" doctrine, which requires evaluating covenants not to compete within the context of the entire employment agreement. *Wallace v. B. & J. Holdings, Inc.*, 536 So. 2d 232, 234 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988). Under this doctrine, when an employer materially breaches the employment agreement, the employee may be excused from performance under related provisions, including non-compete covenants.

Health Coalition's reliance on federal precedent, specifically *Alemite Corp. v. Lane*, 59 F.2d 580 (5th Cir. 1932), and *U.S. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 128 F.2d 579 (8th Cir. 1942), is misplaced. Florida law governs the enforceability of non-compete agreements in this state, and the Florida cases *Service Master* and *Wallace* are binding precedent. Federal cases from other circuits cannot override established Florida jurisprudence.

**D. The Trial Court Improperly Conducted a "Mini-Trial"**

By weighing Bradley's credibility against Health Coalition's denials and effectively finding that Bradley failed to prove his defense, the trial court improperly conducted a "mini-trial" on the merits. This is precisely the error prohibited by *Service Master*. *Service Master of Jacksonville v. J.R. Martin*, 436 So. 2d at 1064. The existence of Bradley's unrefuted sworn testimony regarding the alleged breaches creates a factual dispute that must be resolved by a jury or the court at a final trial on the merits, not at a preliminary injunction hearing. *Spencer v. Spencer*, 494 So. 2d at 500.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction where genuine issues of material fact exist regarding Health Coalition's alleged material breach of the employment agreement. Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits because Bradley's defenses, if proven, would excuse his performance under the non-compete covenant. The trial court's reliance on credibility determinations to resolve these disputes was improper under Florida law. Accordingly, we affirm the reversal of the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
