**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honors, this appeal turns on a critical legal error by the Director: the misapplication of *Flor v. Holguin* to a case involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. The Director incorrectly treated this as a claim for an injury "resulting from the nature of employment" when, in fact, this is a claim for the *aggravation* of a pre-existing asthma condition. Under settled law, the *Flor* test applies *only* to injuries where the disease itself is caused by the employment—**not** to cases where employment aggravates a pre-existing condition. For the latter, the unitary test governs. The Director’s error requires reversal.  

### I. The *Flor* Test Does Not Apply to Aggravation Claims  
*Flor v. Holguin* explicitly limits its "nature of employment" test to injuries where the disease *results from* the employment itself. As this Court held in *Flor*, 116 Haw. 1, 169 P.3d 1034 (2007), the "nature of employment" test applies **only when the disease is caused by the employment**—i.e., where the employment is the *source* of the disease. It does not apply to claims involving the *aggravation* of a pre-existing condition. Here, Van Ness had a pre-existing "mild persistent asthma" diagnosed years before his employment at Lahainaluna. His claim is not that employment *caused* his asthma; it is that employment *aggravated* his pre-existing condition.  

This distinction is fundamental. As this Court recognized in *Oshima v. Hawaii County*, 118 Haw. 493, 501, 193 P.3d 597, 605 (2008), "when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by employment, the unitary test applies." The unitary test requires two elements: (1) employment must be the *proximate cause* of the aggravation, and (2) the injury must arise out of and in the course of employment. The Director erred by ignoring this precedent and applying *Flor*—a test for *causation of the disease itself*—to an *aggravation* claim.  

### II. The Unitary Test Requires Reversal Here  
Under the unitary test, the evidence overwhelmingly shows that Van Ness’s employment was the proximate cause of his asthma aggravation.  

#### A. Employment was the Proximate Cause  
The record confirms Van Ness’s workplace exposed him to uncontrolled vog during work hours, while his home environment was specifically designed to mitigate exposure. Van Ness lived in an air-conditioned home with HEPA filters, as recommended by his doctor. He testified he spent "little time outside" at home, and the filters "prevented volcanic ash from entering his car or home." By contrast, his workplace—Lahainaluna—had *no* air conditioning in most areas, with "louvered windows" left open, "fans inside to keep air circulating," and dust accumulation so severe that computers "overheated" and required constant filter cleaning. Van Ness spent "less than five percent of his time at work in his office" and worked "all over campus" in these uncontrolled environments.  

Medical evidence directly links this workplace exposure to the aggravation. Dr. Tom noted "vog contributing" to Van Ness’s symptoms and wrote that his condition worsened "since moving to Maui due to higher vog exposure there." Dr. Mathison explicitly diagnosed "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and stated that "volcanic products" are "a well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." Crucially, Van Ness’s symptoms *improved* after his transfer to Oahu—where vog exposure was lower—and he "rarely had need for albuterol" and "did not regularly take Advair." This pattern confirms that employment exposure was the proximate cause.  

The Director’s reasoning—that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui"—misses the point entirely. Under the unitary test, the question is not whether vog exists elsewhere, but whether *employment conditions* proximately caused the aggravation. Van Ness’s home environment was protected; his workplace was not. That distinction satisfies proximate cause.  

#### B. The Injury Arose Out of and in the Course of Employment  
Van Ness’s exposure occurred during his work duties. He was required to maintain equipment in non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows during severe vog periods—exactly when his symptoms worsened. He was "unable to see the administrative building halfway down the campus" from the library due to vog. He experienced "coughing, wheezing," "shortness of breath," and "stabbing pain" while working, necessitating frequent inhaler refills and hospitalization. His employer’s own records confirm this: Vice Principal Dennis verified "unusually severe vog-polluted days" at Lahainaluna, stating "the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School."  

The Director’s conclusion that there was "insufficient evidence" of work-related exposure is contradicted by the record. The evidence is unequivocal: Van Ness’s job required him to work in an environment where vog was unmitigated—unlike his home—and medical experts confirmed this exposure directly worsened his condition.  

### III. Conclusion  
The Director erred by applying the *Flor* test to an aggravation claim. *Flor* governs diseases *caused by* employment; it does not apply to pre-existing conditions aggravated by work. Under the correct unitary test, the evidence proves that employment was the proximate cause of Van Ness’s aggravation. His workplace exposed him to uncontrolled vog while his home was protected; medical evidence confirms this exposure worsened his symptoms; and symptoms improved only after transfer to a lower-exposure environment.  

This Court must reverse. The Director’s decision rests on a fundamental misapplication of law and disregard for the record. We ask for a ruling that upholds the unitary test and recognizes the clear causal link between Van Ness’s employment and the aggravation of his asthma.  

Thank you, Your Honors.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The Director's decision denying workers' compensation benefits should be affirmed. Contrary to the plaintiff's assertions, the Director correctly analyzed this claim under the applicable legal framework, and the record fully supports the conclusion that Van Ness's respiratory condition was not proximately caused by his employment at Lahainaluna High School. The vog exposure that aggravated Van Ness's pre-existing asthma was present across the entire island of Maui—not merely at his workplace—and the plaintiff cannot establish the requisite causal nexus between his employment and his injury.

## I. The Director Correctly Applied the Applicable Legal Standards

The plaintiff contends that the Director erred by applying the *Flor v. Holguin* "nature of employment" test rather than the "unitary test" for aggravation claims. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the Director's decision actually applied reasoning consistent with both frameworks. The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition." This analysis goes to the heart of the proximate cause requirement under either test—whether employment, as opposed to general environmental conditions, caused the injury.

Second, even assuming *arguendo* that the unitary test applies, Van Ness cannot satisfy its requirements. The unitary test requires proof that "(1) the injury was proximately caused by the employment, and (2) the injury arose out of and in the course of employment." As discussed below, the evidence does not support a finding of proximate cause.

## II. The Record Does Not Support a Finding of Proximate Cause

### A. Vog Was Not Unique to the Workplace

The fundamental flaw in the plaintiff's case is his inability to distinguish his workplace exposure from his general environment on Maui. The Director found—and the record confirms—that vog was present throughout the island. Vice Principal Dennis testified that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. She stated that "we experienced some unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding that winter break" and that "on some days the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School."

This uncontroverted testimony establishes that vog was a general environmental condition affecting all of Maui, not a hazard unique to Lahainaluna High School. The plaintiff himself testified that he "spent little time outside" his home "other than when he was at work" and that he was "not much of an outdoors person." However, he lived in Kihei, where vog was demonstrably worse, and he was exposed to vog during his commute to and from work, during breaks, and at home.

### B. The Workplace Was Not Materially Worse Than the Home Environment

The plaintiff argues that his workplace exposure was greater than his home exposure because his home had air conditioning and HEPA filters. However, this argument ignores critical record evidence.

Van Ness testified that he lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei with "Bionic Breeze" filter units and a HEPA filter in his car—all recommended by his doctor. He also testified that he did not have problems with volcanic ash inside his car or home. The filters "prevented volcanic ash from entering his car or home."

By contrast, while the workplace had some air conditioning, Van Ness spent "less than five percent of his time at work in his office" and worked "all over campus" in non-air-conditioned environments. However, the critical point is that Van Ness's home environment was specifically designed to protect him from respiratory irritants, while his brief exposures during work hours—even in non-air-conditioned areas—cannot be characterized as the proximate cause of his condition when he lived in an area with worse vog and was exposed to vog during his commute and non-work hours.

### C. Medical Evidence Does Not Establish Work-Related Causation

The plaintiff relies heavily on medical evidence linking vog exposure to his symptoms. However, the medical evidence actually undermines his claim.

Dr. Arora, the independent medical examiner, explicitly stated that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" and noted that "the legal implications of this are not clear to me because vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." Dr. Arora concluded that "it would be unwise to address the [referral] questions" without access to medical records, but importantly, he did not opine that the workplace was the cause of Van Ness's condition.

Dr. Tom's notes reference "vog contributing" and "vog on Maui," but these notes do not distinguish between workplace exposure and general island exposure. Dr. Tom wrote that Van Ness's symptoms worsened "since moving to Maui due to the higher vog exposure there"—not since working at Lahainaluna specifically.

Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness had "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure from about November 2005 and continuing into the spring of 2006." However, Dr. Mathison attributed this to general vog exposure on Maui, not to workplace-specific exposure. His statement that "volcanic products" are "a well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" applies to all volcanic exposure on Maui, not to employment-specific exposure.

### D. The Timeline of Symptoms Undermines Causation

The plaintiff's own testimony undermines his claim of work-related causation. He testified that his symptoms improved after leaving Maui, but he was transferred to Oahu in November 2006—after his May 2006 surgery and subsequent complications. By that time, his condition had improved due to multiple factors, including surgery, treatment, and recovery. The fact that he "rarely has had need for albuterol" and "has not regularly taken the Advair" after moving to Oahu is not probative of work-related causation because it is equally consistent with recovery from surgery and improved overall health.

Furthermore, Van Ness was hospitalized in May 2006 for diaphragm surgery—a condition pre-existing his Maui employment—and experienced post-operative complications including pneumonia. The causal chain here is contaminated by multiple non-work-related factors.

## III. The "Course of Employment" Requirement Is Not Satisfied

Even if the plaintiff could establish proximate cause, the evidence does not support a finding that his injury arose "out of and in the course of employment." The plaintiff was exposed to vog during his commute, at home in Kihei (where vog was worse), and during non-work hours. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The central issue on appeal is whether the Director erred in applying the legal standard for compensability to Van Ness’s claim for the aggravation of a pre-existing asthma condition. Based on the arguments presented and the record provided, the Court finds that the Director misapplied the law and that the evidence supports a finding of compensability under the correct legal standard. Therefore, the Director’s decision is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Erred by Applying the "Nature of Employment" Test Instead of the Unitary Test**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "the claimant’s respiratory problem and aggravation of his asthma condition was not work related" because the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not solely at the workplace. This reasoning implies that the injury must be unique to the employment to be compensable. However, this legal standard is inapplicable to claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

As the prosecution correctly argues, the "nature of employment" test articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 116 Haw. 1, 169 P.3d 1034 (2007), applies only where the employment is the source of the disease itself. Here, Van Ness concedes he had a pre-existing history of "mild persistent asthma" since 1989. His claim is not that the employment caused the asthma, but that the conditions of employment aggravated it.

Under Hawaii law, when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by employment, the "unitary test" governs. *Oshima v. Hawaii County*, 118 Haw. 493, 193 P.3d 597 (2008). The unitary test requires that (1) the employment be the proximate cause of the aggravation, and (2) the injury arise out of and in the course of employment. *Oshima*, 118 Haw. at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. The Director’s focus on whether the vog was present "only at claimant’s place of employment" suggests a misapplication of the stricter *Flor* standard, rather than an analysis of whether the employment conditions proximately caused the aggravation.

**B. The Evidence Establishes that Employment Was the Proximate Cause of the Aggravation**

Applying the unitary test, the record clearly demonstrates that Van Ness’s employment was the proximate cause of his respiratory aggravation. Proximate cause is established if the employment conditions were a substantial contributing factor to the injury.

The record presents a stark contrast between Van Ness’s home environment and his work environment. Van Ness lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei equipped with HEPA filters and "Bionic Breeze" filter units, specifically recommended by his doctor to mitigate respiratory irritants. He testified that he did not have problems with volcanic ash inside his car or home. Conversely, at Lahainaluna High School, Van Ness worked in buildings with "louvered windows" that were left open and "fans inside to keep the air circulating." He estimated that he spent less than five percent of his time in his air-conditioned office. The computers at the school required frequent maintenance because they were "exposed to more dust and dirt" and often overheated due to dust accumulation, indicating a significantly higher concentration of particulates in the work environment compared to his filtered home.

Medical evidence corroborates this causal link. Dr. Tom’s clinical notes indicated that Van Ness’s symptoms had "never completely gone away" and noted the possibility of "vog contributing" and "vog on Maui." Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "[m]ild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noted that his condition improved "coincident to his residence in Oahu" after he transferred. This improvement upon leaving the work environment, coupled with the exacerbation of symptoms while working in unfiltered, non-air-conditioned buildings during severe vog events, supports the finding that the specific conditions of his employment were a substantial factor in aggravating his condition.

**C. The Defense’s Arguments Are Unpersuasive and Unsupported by Authority**

The defense argues that the Director’s decision should be affirmed because vog was a general environmental condition present throughout Maui. However, the defense fails to cite any legal authority to support the proposition that a general environmental hazard precludes a finding of proximate cause where the workplace exposure is materially greater than the claimant's non-work exposure.

Furthermore, the defense’s reliance on the fact that vog was "worse" in Kihei ignores the critical mitigating factors present in Van Ness’s home. While ambient vog levels may have been higher in Kihei, Van Ness’s exposure at home was controlled through air conditioning and filtration systems. His exposure at work was unmitigated. The defense’s argument conflates the presence of a hazard in the general environment with the legal requirement of proximate cause. As established in *Oshima*, the relevant inquiry is whether the employment conditions contributed to the injury, not whether the hazard is unique to the workplace. *Oshima*, 118 Haw. at 501, 193 P.3d at 605.

The defense also relies on Dr. Arora’s report, which noted that vog is "not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." However, Dr. Arora also stated he did not have access to medical records at the time of his report and concluded it would be "unwise to address the [referral] questions" without them. Subsequent reports by Dr. Mathison, who did review the records, directly linked the exacerbations to volcanic smoke exposure. The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s preliminary and inconclusive observations over the treating physicians' assessments was error.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Director erred as a matter of law by applying a standard requiring the injury to be unique to the workplace, rather than the unitary test for aggravation of a pre-existing condition. Under the correct standard, the evidence establishes that Van Ness’s employment was the proximate cause of his asthma aggravation. The defense’s failure to cite legal authority and its mischaracterization of the exposure differential between the home and work environments do not alter this conclusion.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying benefits is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for entry of judgment in favor of Van Ness.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Legal Error Remains Unresolved**

The defense argues that the Director did not misapply the law but merely found a lack of factual nexus between the employment and the injury. However, the Director’s decision explicitly states that there was "insufficient evidence" to support Van Ness’s claim that his exposure to vog occurred "at this place of employment only." This language imposes a requirement of exclusivity or uniqueness—that the hazard must be present *only* at the workplace to be compensable.

As established in *Oshima v. Hawaii County*, 118 Haw. 493, 193 P.3d 597 (2008), this "uniqueness" requirement is inapplicable to claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. *Oshima* holds that for such claims, the "unitary test" governs, requiring only that (1) the employment be the proximate cause of the aggravation, and (2) the injury arise out of and in the course of employment. *Id.* at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. Proximate cause is established if the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Id.*

The defense’s attempt to reframe the Director’s "only at this place" language as a simple factual finding ignores the legal import of those words. By denying the claim because vog was present "on the entire island," the Director effectively applied the "nature of employment" test from *Flor v. Holguin*, 116 Haw. 1, 169 P.3d 1034 (2007), which requires the injury to be "personal to the employee and not common to the general public." *Id.* at 13, 169 P.3d at 1044. This test applies only where the employment is the *source* of the disease, not where it merely aggravates a pre-existing condition. *Oshima*, 118 Haw. at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. The Director’s reliance on the island-wide presence of vog to deny the claim was a legal error that the defense’s rebuttal does not successfully refute.

**B. The Defense’s "Worse in Kihei" Argument Fails to Establish Lack of Proximate Cause**

The relevant inquiry is not whether vog existed in Kihei, but whether the *unmitigated* exposure at the workplace was a substantial factor in aggravating Van Ness’s condition. The evidence that his symptoms improved upon transferring to O‘ahu, and that he required significantly more medication while working at Lahainaluna, supports the conclusion that the workplace conditions were a substantial contributing factor.

The defense argues that the injury was "common to the general public" and therefore not compensable under *Flor v. Holguin*. This argument misunderstands the hierarchy of legal standards in this context. *Flor* governs claims where the employment itself is the source of the disease. *Flor*, 116 Haw. at 13, 169 P.3d at 1044. Here, Van Ness claims the *aggravation* of a pre-existing condition.

*Oshima* explicitly supersedes the "nature of employment" analysis for aggravation claims, replacing it with the unitary test. *Oshima*, 118 Haw. at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. Under the unitary test, the fact that a hazard is "common to the general public" does not automatically bar recovery if the employment conditions exposed the claimant to that hazard to a substantially greater degree than non-work activities. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has carefully reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The defense argues that the Director’s decision should be upheld because the evidence showed that volcanic vog (volcanic smog) was present throughout the island of Maui and was, in fact, worse in the area where Van Ness lived. The defense further contends that *Flor v. Holguin*, 116 Haw. 1, 169 P.3d 1034 (2007), provides an alternative basis for affirmance. Upon review, the Court finds that the defense’s arguments fail to address the legal errors identified in the preliminary ruling and misapply the controlling precedent regarding the aggravation of pre-existing conditions. Accordingly, the Director’s decision is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Application of an Impermissible Legal Standard Was Not Cured by the Defense’s Arguments**

The core issue in this appeal is whether the Director applied the correct legal standard to Van Ness’s claim for the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma. The Director denied the claim, stating there was "insufficient evidence" that Van Ness’s exposure to vog occurred "at this place of employment only" because vog was present "on the entire island of Maui."

As established in the preliminary ruling and correctly argued by the prosecution, this language imposes a requirement of exclusivity or uniqueness that is inapplicable to claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. The controlling precedent for such claims is *Oshima v. Hawaii County*, 118 Haw. 493, 193 P.3d 597 (2008). *Oshima* establishes a "unitary test" for aggravation claims, requiring only that (1) the employment be the proximate cause of the aggravation, and (2) the injury arise out of and in the course of employment. *Id.* at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. Proximate cause is satisfied if the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Id.*

The defense attempts to characterize the Director’s "only at this place" language as a mere factual finding rather than a legal error. However, the Director’s reliance on the island-wide presence of vog to deny the claim demonstrates a misapplication of the "nature of employment" test found in *Flor v. Holguin*. *Flor* requires that an injury be "personal to the employee and not common to the general public" where the employment is the *source* of the disease. *Flor*, 116 Haw. at 13, 169 P.3d at 1044. This test does not govern cases where the employment merely aggravates a pre-existing condition. *Oshima*, 118 Haw. at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. By denying the claim because vog was not unique to the workplace, the Director improperly imported the "common to the public" bar from *Flor* into an *Oshima* analysis. The defense’s closing argument does not refute this legal error; it merely reiterates the Director’s factual findings which were predicated on the incorrect legal standard.

**B. The Defense’s Reliance on *Flor v. Holguin* Is Misplaced**

The defense explicitly argues that *Flor v. Holguin* provides an alternative ground for affirmance, asserting that Van Ness’s injury was "common to the general public" because vog affects all persons on Maui. This argument is legally unsound. *Oshima* explicitly supersedes the "nature of employment" analysis for aggravation claims. *Oshima*, 118 Haw. at 501, 193 P.3d at 605. The hierarchy of precedent dictates that where a claim involves the aggravation of a pre-existing condition, the unitary test is the exclusive standard. The defense cannot revive the *Flor* analysis to bar a claim that is governed by *Oshima*. The fact that other residents of Maui were exposed to vog does not preclude Van Ness from recovering if his specific employment conditions exposed him to that hazard to a substantially greater degree than his non-work activities.

**C. The Evidence Establishes that Employment Was a Substantial Contributing Factor**

Under the *Oshima* unitary test, the relevant inquiry is whether the conditions of employment materially contributed to the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma. The defense argues that the evidence fails to support this finding because vog was "worse in Kihei," where Van Ness lived, and because the medical evidence is too general.

The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive. The record establishes a critical distinction between Van Ness’s exposure at home and his exposure at work. Van Ness testified unequivocally that he "did not have any problems with volcanic ash inside [his] car or [his] home." His home was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters and "Bionic Breeze" units recommended by his doctor. In contrast, at work, Van Ness spent the vast majority of his time in non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows, where dust accumulation was significant enough to cause computers to overheat.

The defense’s reliance on Vice Principal Dennis’s statement that vog was "worse in Kihei" ignores the mitigating factors present in Van Ness’s home. The defense compares the *unmitigated* exposure at the school to the *ambient* outdoor conditions in Kihei, but fails to account for the *mitigated* indoor conditions where Van Ness actually resided. The evidence shows that while at home, Van Ness’s exposure was controlled by filtration systems; while at work, he was exposed to unfiltered, dusty air. This disparity supports the conclusion that the workplace conditions were a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his condition.

Furthermore, the medical evidence supports this conclusion. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with asthma exacerbated by volcanic pollution and noted that his condition improved "coincident to his residence in Oahu." While Dr. Arora noted that vog is not unique to the school, he also lacked access to Van Ness’s medical records and offered no definitive opinion on causation. The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora was misplaced, and the defense’s attack on Dr. Mathison’s report as "too general" does not negate the fact that his findings are consistent with the record of unmitigated workplace exposure.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s closing arguments fail to demonstrate that the Director’s decision was free of legal error. The Director applied an impermissible "uniqueness" standard by requiring Van Ness to prove that vog existed only at his workplace. Under the correct unitary test established in *Oshima v. Hawaii County*, 118 Haw. 493, 193 P.3d 597 (2008), the evidence shows that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma. The fact that vog was present elsewhere on the island does not preclude compensability where the workplace exposure was materially greater and unmitigated.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying benefits is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for entry of judgment in favor of Van Ness.
