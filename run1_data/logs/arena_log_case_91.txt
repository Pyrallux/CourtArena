COURT ARENA LOG - Case 91
FACTS:
This case involves the admissibility of expert testimony in child sexual abuse cases. Last Kony challenges his convictions of sexual assault of a minor on the grounds that expert testimony regarding child sexual abuse is no longer relevant, the statistical data presented during his trial was misleading and highly prejudicial, and the evidence presented during his trial improperly profiled him as a sex offender. We affirm the trial court’s ruling as to the relevancy of the expert testimony in this ease regarding the unique characteristics of child sexual abuse victims admitted to assist the jury “to comprehend something not commonly known or understood”—delayed reporting. State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 557-58, 799 P.2d 48, 52-54 (1990). 

I. Background

Kony was charged in the Circuit Court of the First Circuit (circuit court) with sexual assault of a minor. The charges consisted of three counts of sexual assault in the first degree and six counts of sexual assault in the third degree.1 The complaining witness (Minor) lived in the same household with Kony, along with six family members and one other person. Kony was the boyfriend of Minor’s half-sister and father to two children in the home.2 Minor was fifteen years of age at the time of the alleged sexual assaults.

B. Testimony adduced by State

At trial, Minor testified that from May to August 2011, Kony sexually assaulted her three times in her room at the shared home. The first incident occurred on the morning of her fifteenth birthday on May 6, 2011, just after midnight. Minor testified that she was asleep in her bedroom with her door locked but the lock could be picked with a fingernail. Minor knew it was Kony because he told her “Don’t worry, be quiet, it was just him,” and she also recognized his figure and voice. Minor testified that the second incident occurred approximately two weeks later. Kony came in her bedroom, and he told her that it was him again. During the second incident, Kony told her that there would be problems if her family found out about his “coming in her room.” The last incident occurred a few days before Minor reported the events to her mother on August 12, 2011.

Minor indicated that Kony was not in her room for more than five minutes during any of the incidents. Minor testified that she did not scream during the incidents because she “was shocked it happened,” but she told Kony to “get out.” She also testified that she was worried about her “family being messed up,” she felt bad for her sister, and she was scared.

Minor testified that the first person she told about the incidents was her cousin who she told “[w]hile it was happening.” Cousin explained that she and Minor were “very close” and that Minor “didn’t know what to do and what to say to her family because [Minor] said that’s her sister’s boyfriend.” Cousin testified that Minor told her the rape happened a single time and that Kony “tried to do other things like touch her” several other times.

Minor’s mother testified that she confronted Kony, who stated that he was “sorry and he didn’t know what got into him that made him do that.” Kony did not state what he did, but he did tell the mother that he went inside Minor’s room and “he was sorry he did it.”

After the confrontation, Kony moved out of the home. Weeks later, Minor’s father was informed, and he reported Minor’s allegations to the police. Father also confronted Kony, and Father testified that during the confrontation, Kony apologized for being inside Minor’s room.

E. ICA Proceedings

Kony’s opening brief, raised a single point of error—that the circuit court erred in allowing Dr. Bivens’ testimony. Kony argued that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was not of assistance to the jury because the commonness of delayed disclosure of child victims no longer constituted specialized knowledge outside the common experience of the jury, Kony also asserted that Dr. Bivens’ testimony relied on statistics in a manner that was misleading and highly prejudicial. Kony further contended that the statistical and profile evidence cited by Dr. Bivens vouched for Minor’s credibility.

In its answering brief, the State argued that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was relevant to assist the jury in understanding behaviors and phenomena associated with sexual abuse. The State contended Kony was not prejudiced by Dr. Bivens’ testimony and maintained that Kony did not object to the testimony on the grounds that it was profile evidence or that the doctor’s use of statistics was highly prejudicial. The State asserted •that it did not misinterpret any statistics mentioned by Dr. Bivens or improperly suggest during closing argument that Kony was guilty because he matched a profile of individuals who sexually assault children.

In reply to the State, Kony argued that he did not waive his challenge to Dr. Bivens’ testimony. Kony contended that he argued in his motion in limine that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was not relevant and that its minimal probative value was outweighed by its prejudicial effect. Kony maintained that he was not required to continuously object throughout the trial after the court had definitively ruled to allow Dr. Bivens’ testimony because the court’s invitation for the defense to object was only if defense counsel believed that the State was exceeding the scope of the court’s ruling.

The ICA issued a Summary Disposition Order (SDO) affirming the circuit court’s judgment of conviction. The ICA rejected Kony’s argument that expert testimony regarding delayed reporting is no longer helpful to juries. The ICA also assumed that Kony preserved his objections to Dr. Bivens’ *8testimony for improper bolstering of the complaining witnesses’ credibility and profiling of Kony as a sex offender and held that those arguments were meritless. The ICA noted that Dr. Bivens testified that error rates are always associated with percentages and that Kony acknowledged that Dr. Bivens did not have any information about the case.

Judge Reifurth concurred in the result, but he wrote separately to clarify his understanding of the Hawai'i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rule 403 analysis that would apply— had Kony properly raised his objections that elements of Dr. Bivens’ testimony were misleading and unduly prejudicial. Judge Rei-furth explained that the potential prejudice of the evidence should be assessed against its probative value under HRE Rule 403. Judge Reifurth stated that he would have resolved the issue of prejudice, arising from expert testimony regarding the typical characteristics and behaviors of child sexual abusers, as waived because Kony failed to object at tidal to any testimony of Dr. Bivens as prejudicial.

II. Standards of Review

We apply the righVwrong standard in reviewing challenges to a court’s relevancy decision under HRE Rules 703, 401, and 402. State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). “Evidentiary decisions based on HRE Rule 403, which require a ‘judgment call’ on the part of the trial court, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion.” State v. Richie, 88 Hawai'i 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998) (footnote omitted) (quoting State v. Arceo, 84 Hawai'i 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996)). “An abuse of discretion occurs when the decisionmaker ‘exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party.’ ” Vliet, 95 Hawai'i at 107, 19 P.3d at 55 (quoting In re Water Use Permit Applications, 94 Hawai'i 97, 183, 9 P.3d 409, 495 (2000)).

III. Discussion

Kony contends that the ICA gravely erred in holding that the circuit court did not err in allowing the testimony of Dr. Bivens as an expert in clinical psychology and the dynamics of child sexual abuse and permitting testimony on the issue of delayed reporting. Kony maintains that the testimony “was not relevant, not proper expert testimony, improperly profiled [Kony] as a sex offender and relied on statistical data that was misleading and highly prejudicial.”

## INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Kony respectfully submits this opening brief in response to the State's answering brief and urges this Court to reverse the Intermediate Court of Appeals' (ICA) Summary Disposition Order and remand for a new trial. The admission of Dr. Alexander Jay Bivens' expert testimony constituted reversible error because: (1) such testimony regarding delayed reporting of child sexual abuse is no longer relevant and falls within the common knowledge of jurors; (2) the statistical evidence presented was misleading, highly prejudicial, and improperly served to profile Kony as a sex offender; and (3) the testimony improperly vouched for the credibility of the complaining witness. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting this testimony, and the ICA erred in affirming that ruling.

## I. THE EXPERT TESTIMONY ON DELAYED REPORTING WAS NOT RELEVANT

The State argues that Dr. Bivens' testimony was admissible to assist the jury in understanding "behaviors and phenomena associated with sexual abuse" that are "outside the common knowledge of jurors." This argument fails because the phenomenon of delayed reporting in child sexual abuse cases is no longer outside the common experience of jurors in 2012.

### A. The Holding in State v. Batangan Does Not Control This Case

The State relies heavily on State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 799 P.2d 48 (1990), for the proposition that expert testimony on delayed reporting is admissible. However, Batangan was decided over two decades ago, in 1990, when such testimony was relatively novel and genuinely helpful to juries unfamiliar with the dynamics of child sexual abuse. The landscape has changed dramatically since Batangan was decided.

In Batangan, this Court recognized that expert testimony regarding "delayed reporting" was admissible because it would assist the jury in understanding "something not commonly known or understood." 71 Haw. at 557-58, 799 P.2d at 52-54. However, this Court explicitly conditioned such admission on a finding that the testimony would indeed assist the jury—meaning the phenomenon in question must fall outside the common knowledge of the trier of fact.

The question before this Court is whether, in 2012, delayed reporting remains a phenomenon outside common understanding. The defense submits that it does not. Public awareness of child sexual abuse dynamics has increased substantially since 1990. Modern media, educational campaigns, and public discourse have made the concept of delayed reporting widely recognized. Jurors in 2012 are not the same as jurors in 1990. They come to the courtroom with substantially more exposure to these issues through news coverage, public service announcements, and educational materials.

The ICA erred in simply assuming without analysis that such testimony remains helpful to juries. The trial court likewise failed to conduct any inquiry into whether delayed reporting remains outside the common knowledge of modern jurors. This failure constituted an abuse of discretion.

### B. The Trial Court Failed to Make the Required Finding of Relevance

Under Hawai'i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rule 401, evidence is relevant only if it has "any tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable than it would be without the evidence." The State bore the burden of establishing that Dr. Bivens' testimony would assist the jury in understanding an issue not within common knowledge.

The trial court admitted the testimony based solely on the State's proffer that it would address "delayed reporting"—without any analysis of whether such testimony would actually assist the jury. The court stated it would "allow the State to call him on this issue of delayed reporting" without making any finding that such testimony was necessary or helpful.

This Court has held that "relevance determinations under HRE Rules 703, 401, and 402" are reviewed under the "right/wrong standard." State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). Under this standard, the trial court's failure to make any meaningful relevance finding constitutes reversible error.

## II. THE STATISTICAL EVIDENCE WAS MISLEADING AND HIGHLY PREJUDICIAL

### A. The Statistics Were Not Properly Qualified

Even assuming some relevance to the testimony, the manner in which Dr. Bivens presented statistical evidence requires reversal. Dr. Bivens testified to numerous specific percentages regarding child sexual abuse, including:

- 100% of incest offenders commit offenses in their own home
- 85% of sexual abuse victims have a pre-existing non-sexual relationship with their molester
- In Hawai'i, over 50% of cases involve a family relationship
- 95% of sexual crimes against children are committed by males
- 33% of children who tell will tell their mother; 32% will tell a close friend
- 29% said they expected not to be believed; 25% said they were embarrassed; 24% said they didn't want to upset anyone; 18% said they didn't want to harm the abuser

While Dr. Bivens offered vague qualifications that "percentages should only be used to be given a general idea," this was insufficient to cure the prejudice. The problem is that jurors do not understand statistical methodology and are susceptible to the "prosecutor's fallacy"—the mistaken belief that statistics showing general patterns apply to specific cases.

This Court has recognized that expert testimony must be carefully scrutinized when it presents statistical evidence that could mislead jurors. In State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 19 P.3d 42 (2001), this Court emphasized the importance of proper foundation and qualification of expert testimony. The trial court failed to ensure that Dr. Bivens' statistical testimony was properly framed to avoid misleading the jury.

### B. The Statistics Improperly Profiled Kony

The most troubling aspect of Dr. Bivens' testimony is that it effectively profiled Kony as a sex offender without ever mentioning him by name. Consider the cumulative effect of the following testimony:

- **Location**: "100% of incest offenders...committed their offenses in their own home" and "a hundred percent of incest victims experience sexual abuse within their own home."
- **Relationship**: "85 percent of sexual abuse victims have a pre-existing non-sexual relationship with their molester" and "a little over 50 percent do have a family relationship" in Hawai'i.
- **Gender**: "over 95 percent of sexual crimes are committed by males" and "It just seems to be a male problem."
- **Timing**: "delayed disclosure is the rule, not the exception" and "multiple incidents of abuse implies delayed reporting."

When combined with the facts of this case—that Kony lived in the same household as the minor, was the boyfriend of her half-sister, was male, and the abuse was reported months after it allegedly occurred—Dr. Bivens' testimony created an impermissible profile that the jury could use to infer guilt. The expert essentially told the jury: "This is what a typical child sex offender looks like, and the defendant fits that profile."

This Court has recognized the danger of profile evidence in criminal cases. In State v. Richie, 88 Hawai'i 19, 960 P.2d 1227 (1998), this Court cautioned that evidence of a defendant's character traits or propensity to commit crimes must be carefully limited under HRE Rule 404. While character evidence was not technically at issue, the practical effect of Dr. Bivens' testimony was to establish that Kony possessed characteristics typical of child sex offenders—an improper propensity inference.

The ICA acknowledged this issue but dismissed it by noting that "Dr. Bivens testified that error rates are always associated with percentages" and that "Kony acknowledged that Dr. Bivens did not have any information about the case." This analysis is flawed for two reasons. First, the acknowledgment that Dr. Bivens lacked case-specific information underscores the problem—he was testifying about general patterns that the jury would inevitably apply to this specific case. Second, the vague mention of "error rates" does nothing to dispel the misleading impression created by specific percentage figures. Jurors are not statistical experts; they hear "100% of incest offenders" and draw the obvious inference that Kony, as an alleged incest offender in the home, fits that pattern.

### C. The Probative Value Was Substantially Outweighed by Unfair Prejudice

Under HRE Rule 403, evidence "may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice." This Court has held that "evidentiary decisions based on HRE Rule 403, which require a 'judgment call' on the part of the trial court, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion." State v. Richie, 88 Hawai'i at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245 (quoting State v. Arceo, 84 Hawai'i 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996)).

The trial court abuse its discretion in failing to exclude Dr. Bivens' testimony under Rule 403. The probative value of the testimony was minimal at best—it merely informed the jury of general patterns that any reasonable person could infer from the facts already presented. The minor testified that Kony was her half-sister's boyfriend living in the same household; the jury did not need an expert to tell them this was a "family relationship."

Conversely, the danger of unfair prejudice was substantial. The testimony created an improper inference that Kony fit the profile of a child sex offender. It suggested that because delayed reporting is "the rule, not the exception," the minor's delay in reporting should not be viewed skeptically. It implied that because most child sex offenders are male and live in the home, Kony was more likely guilty.

The ICA's concurrence by Judge Reifurth recognized that a proper Rule 403 analysis would have been appropriate but concluded the issue was waived. However, the record demonstrates that Kony properly preserved this objection in his motion in limine and throughout trial. The defense specifically argued that the testimony's "probative value would be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, specifically Kony's propensity to commit the crime for which he was charged." This was sufficient to preserve the issue.

---

## III. THE TESTIMONY IMPROPERLY VOUCHED FOR THE COMPLAINING WITNESS'S CREDIBILITY

### A. The Expert's Testimony Addressed Credibility

Dr. Bivens' testimony regarding why child sexual abuse victims delay reporting inevitably vouched for the credibility of the complaining witness. When he testified that:

- "delayed disclosure is the rule, not the exception"
- "two thirds of adults who say that they were sexually molested as children never told anyone before the age of eighteen"
- "29 percent said they expected not to be believed"
- "25 percent said they were embarrassed"
- "24 percent said they didn't want to upset anyone or cause chaos in their family"
- "18 percent said they didn't want to harm the abuser"

He was effectively telling the jury that the minor's delay in reporting should not be held against her because it is normal behavior for victims of sexual abuse. This is the functional equivalent of an expert opining that the victim is telling the truth.

This Court has recognized that expert testimony must not improperly vouch for witness credibility. In State v. Batangan, this Court cautioned that expert testimony should not "bolster" the credibility of the complaining witness. 71 Haw. at 558, 799 P.2d at 53. Dr. Bivens' testimony did precisely that.

### B. The Circumstances of Disclosure Supported Defense Arguments

The facts of this case demonstrate why the testimony was particularly prejudicial. The minor testified that she told her cousin "[w]hile it was happening" but did not tell her mother until an argument occurred several months later. She explained she didn't tell her parents because she was not "close" to any of them.

Dr. Bivens' testimony provided a ready explanation for every aspect of the minor's conduct—explanations that essentially foreclosed the defense's ability to argue that the delay in reporting or the circumstances of disclosure suggested fabrication. By testifying that "delayed disclosure is the rule, not the exception" and that victims often don't tell anyone until adulthood, Dr. Bivens effectively told the jury that the minor's delayed disclosure was evidence of truthfulness rather than a reason for skepticism.

This is precisely the type of improper credibility vouching that expert testimony should not provide. The jury, not the expert, should assess whether the minor's delay in reporting affected her credibility.

---

## IV. THE CUMULATIVE EFFECT OF THE ERRORS REQUIRES REVERSAL

Even if this Court were to find that any single aspect of Dr. Bivens' testimony was admissible, the cumulative effect of the errors requires reversal. The testimony functioned as: (1) irrelevant general education about child sexual abuse; (2) misleading statistical profile evidence; and (3) improper credibility vouching for the complaining witness.

This Court has recognized that cumulative errors may require reversal even where individual errors might not. See State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 19 P.3d 42 (2001). Here, the cumulative effect of the improperly admitted testimony was to deprive Kony of a fair trial.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant Kony respectfully requests that this Court reverse the ICA's Summary Disposition Order, reverse the judgment of conviction, and remand for a new trial. The trial court erred in admitting Dr. Bivens' expert testimony because: (1) testimony regarding delayed reporting is no longer relevant as it falls within common knowledge; (2) the statistical evidence was misleading, highly prejudicial, and improperly profiled Kony as a sex offender; and (3) the testimony improperly vouched for the complaining witness's credibility. The ICA erred in affirming these errors.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on the appeal of Last Kony from the November 28, 2012 Judgment of Conviction and Sentence. Kony challenges the Intermediate Court of Appeals’ (ICA) Summary Disposition Order affirming his convictions for sexual assault in the first and third degrees. The specific issue on appeal is whether the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting the expert testimony of Dr. Alexander Jay Bivens regarding the dynamics of child sexual abuse. After reviewing the opening arguments of the prosecution and the defense, the court renders the following preliminary ruling.

**II. STANDARDS OF REVIEW**

We review evidentiary decisions regarding relevancy under Hawai‘i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rules 401, 402, and 703 under the right/wrong standard. State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). Evidentiary decisions based on HRE Rule 403, which require a balancing of probative value against prejudice, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion. State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998). An abuse of discretion occurs when a court "exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party." Vliet, 95 Haw. at 107, 19 P.3d at 55.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Relevancy of Expert Testimony Regarding Delayed Reporting**

Kony argues that the expert testimony regarding delayed reporting was irrelevant because such knowledge is now within the common experience of jurors, distinguishing this case from the precedent established in State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 799 P.2d 48 (1990). In Batangan, this court held that expert testimony is admissible to assist the jury in comprehending something "not commonly known or understood," such as delayed reporting in child sexual abuse cases. 71 Haw. at 557-58, 799 P.2d at 52-54.

The defense contends that societal awareness of child sexual abuse dynamics has significantly increased since 1990, rendering the "common knowledge" foundation of Batangan obsolete. While the prosecution argues that the psychological nuances of delayed reporting remain outside the layperson's expertise, the defense presents a compelling argument that the trial court failed to make a specific finding on this issue under HRE Rule 401. Under the right/wrong standard of review established in State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. 94, 19 P.3d 42 (2001), the trial court's failure to conduct a meaningful inquiry into whether the testimony remained helpful to a modern jury constitutes a potential error. However, even if we assume the testimony was generally relevant, the more significant error lies in the admission of specific statistical evidence.

**B. HRE Rule 403 Balancing: Profiling and Prejudice**

The defense argues that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was misleading and highly prejudicial, specifically that it improperly profiled Kony as a sex offender. We agree. While the trial court has discretion to admit expert testimony, that discretion is bounded by HRE Rule 403, which requires that evidence be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 960 P.2d 1227 (1998); State v. Arceo, 84 Haw. 1, 928 P.2d 843 (1996).

Dr. Bivens testified to specific statistics, including that "100 percent of incest offenders... committed their offenses in their own home" and that "over 95 percent of sexual crimes are committed by males." He further stated that 85% of victims have a pre-existing relationship with the molester. The prosecution argues these statistics were general patterns and not profiles. However, given the facts of this case—that Kony is a male who lived in the victim’s home and had a pre-existing relationship with her—these statistics effectively created a profile that the jury could use to infer guilt.

The probative value of statistics regarding the gender of offenders or the location of offenses is minimal with respect to explaining the phenomenon of delayed reporting, which was the stated purpose of the testimony. Conversely, the danger of unfair prejudice is substantial. By presenting data showing that the defendant fits the profile of a typical sex offender, the evidence invited the jury to convict Kony based on his propensity to commit the crime rather than on the specific facts of the case. This violates the principles underlying HRE Rule 404 and constitutes an abuse of discretion under Rule 403. See State v. Richie, 88 Haw. at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245.

The prosecution’s assertion that the trial court’s "question by question" control mitigated this prejudice is unpersuasive. While the court struck certain remarks, the admission of the core statistical data—specifically the "100%" and "95%" figures—was allowed. The defense properly preserved this objection through the motion in limine and subsequent objections, arguing that the testimony's probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the arguments presented, the defense has demonstrated that the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting Dr. Bivens’ testimony. Specifically, the admission of statistical evidence regarding offender characteristics under the guise of explaining delayed reporting violated HRE Rule 403. The probative value of this evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice in the form of improper profiling.

Therefore, the court preliminarily rules that the Intermediate Court of Appeals erred in affirming the circuit court’s judgment of conviction. The judgment of conviction is vacated, and the case is remanded to the circuit court for a new trial.

The defense’s assertion that societal awareness has rendered Batangan obsolete is unsupported by legal precedent or empirical evidence.  

Jurors may be generally aware that some victims delay reporting, but they lack specialized knowledge of the *psychological mechanisms* driving that delay—such as the specific reasons victims cite (e.g., fear of disbelief, shame, family disruption, or protecting the abuser), the impact of multiple incidents on reporting patterns, or the statistical likelihood of delayed disclosure in different demographic groups. As this Court recognized in *State v. Vliet*, relevance under HRE Rules 401 and 402 hinges on whether evidence "makes the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable." 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). The circuit court properly exercised its discretion in determining that Dr. Bivens’ testimony would "assist the jury in comprehending something not commonly known or understood," as required by HRE Rule 703.

The defense’s claim that Dr. Bivens’ statistical testimony "improperly profiled" Kony is baseless. This argument, however, does not justify the admission of statistics regarding where offenders commit crimes (100% in their own home), the gender of offenders (over 95% male), or the relationship between victims and offenders (85% have pre-existing relationship). These statistics do not explain the *psychological mechanisms* of delayed reporting. They explain *who commits child sexual abuse* and *where such abuse occurs*—information irrelevant to explaining why a victim delays disclosure.

As this court recognized in State v. Vliet, relevance under HRE Rules 401 and 402 requires that evidence "make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable." 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). Statistics about where incest offenders commit crimes do not make any fact regarding Minor's delayed disclosure more or less probable. They merely establish that Kony fits the profile of a typical incest offender—a fact wholly unrelated to the stated purpose of the testimony.

---

## III. THE STATISTICAL EVIDENCE CONSTITUTED IMPROPER PROFILE EVIDENCE

The prosecution's claim that Dr. Bivens' statistical testimony did not constitute improper profiling ignores the plain facts of this case. Dr. Bivens testified that:

1. **100% of incest offenders commit offenses in their own home**;
2. **Over 95% of sexual crimes against children are committed by males**;
3. **85% of victims have a pre-existing relationship with their molester**.

The prosecution argues these were merely "general patterns" presented without reference to Kony. However, the prosecution's own facts establish that Kony is a male who lived in Minor's home and had a pre-existing familial relationship with her. The prosecution cannot disclaim as "coincidence" what these statistics plainly accomplished: establishing that Kony fits the profile of a typical child sex offender.

The prosecution argues that Rule 403 "does not exclude evidence simply because it 'incidentally' aligns with a defendant's characteristics," citing State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998). However, the Richie court addressed circumstances where alignment was genuinely incidental. Here, the alignment was not incidental—it was the explicit purpose of the testimony. The prosecution's own questioning established the relevance of these statistics by drawing direct parallels to the facts of this case:

> **[Prosecutor]: Well, a lot of times? It sounds like a hundred percent of the time it's in one of those two places.**

This exchange demonstrates that the prosecution used the statistics not to educate the jury about delayed reporting, but to establish that Kony committed the crimes because he fit the profile.

The prosecution argues that "the statistics were not used to suggest Kony was 'likely' to be guilty because he matched a pattern." This argument strains credulity. The prosecution's own questioning drew explicit connections between the statistics and the facts of this case. When the prosecutor specifically elicits testimony that "100% of incest offenders commit offenses in their own home" in a case where the defendant is accused of committing incest in his own home, the logical implication for the jury is unmistakable.

The prosecution's reliance on the trial court's limiting instructions is unavailing. While the circuit court struck one statement from the record, the court allowed the core statistical evidence—specifically the "100%" and "95%" figures—to remain before the jury. As this court recognized in State v. Arceo, limiting instructions cannot cure the prejudice from inherently prejudicial evidence when the evidence "invites the jury to convict on the basis of propensity rather than proof of the specific offense." 84 Haw. 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996). The prosecution's suggestion that the jury would somehow disregard these statistics when determining Kony's guilt ignores basic human psychology.

---

## IV. THE TESTIMONY IMPROPERLY VOUCHED FOR CREDIBILITY

The prosecution argues that Dr. Bivens' testimony "did not improperly vouch for the complaining witness's credibility." This argument fails for two reasons.

First, when an expert testifies that victims delay reporting because they "fear not being believed" (29%), are "embarrassed" (25%), or "don't want to upset anyone or cause chaos in their family" (24%), the implicit message to the jury is that skepticism toward the victim's account is unwarranted because these are normal, understandable reasons for delay. This is precisely the kind of credibility vouching this court prohibited in Arceo. The expert is effectively telling the jury that the victim's failure to report immediately should not count against her credibility—a direct credibility assessment dressed in statistical clothing.

Second, the prosecution's argument that "the jury remained free to weigh Minor's credibility based on all evidence" ignores the practical effect of the testimony. When an expert with apparent scientific authority tells the jury that delayed disclosure is "the rule, not the exception" and provides percentages explaining why victims delay, the jury is primed to view the victim's delay as evidence of consistency with legitimate victims—not as potentially suspicious silence. This is vouching by implication.

The prosecution argues that "testimony that '24% of victims delay to avoid family chaos' explains why Minor might hesitate to report to her family—it does not tell the jury to believe her." This parsing ignores the functional reality of the testimony. The prosecution's stated purpose was to explain delayed reporting generally. But the specific percentages cited—fear of not being believed, embarrassment, family disruption—are precisely the explanations Minor gave for her own delay. By providing these percentages, Dr. Bivens effectively validated Minor's specific reasons for waiting months to report, thereby bolstering her credibility without ever directly stating she was truthful.

---

## V. THE CIRCUIT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION UNDER RULE 403

The prosecution argues that the circuit court "properly exercised its discretion" under Rule 403. The record demonstrates otherwise.

The prosecution points to the circuit court's statement that it would allow the testimony "question by question" and contends this satisfied Rule 403's requirements. However, the "question by question" approach is not a substitute for a meaningful Rule 403 analysis. As this court recognized in Richie, Rule 403 requires the trial court to "weigh the probative value of the evidence against the danger of unfair prejudice" before admitting evidence. 88 Haw. at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245. The circuit court failed to conduct this analysis.

The circuit court allowed Dr. Bivens to testify about statistics regarding the location of offenses, the gender of offenders, and the relationship between victims and offenders—all without any finding that these statistics were relevant to the issue of delayed reporting or that their probative value outweighed their prejudicial effect. The court simply accepted the prosecution's representation that the testimony would be limited to "hypothetical" factors and delegated its Rule 403 responsibilities to future objections.

This was an abuse of discretion. The circuit court had a duty to conduct a prospective balancing analysis before admitting this testimony, not merely to react to objections as they arose. The prosecution's characterization of the "question by question" approach as "precisely the mechanism this Court has endorsed" misreads Richie. That case endorsed careful judicial management of expert testimony; it did not endorse wholesale abdication of the court's Rule 403 responsibilities.

The prosecution argues that "the probative value of the testimony was substantial" because "it provided critical context for Minor's delayed disclosure." This argument conflates the general topic (delayed reporting) with the specific, prejudicial evidence (offender profiles). Even accepting that general testimony about delayed reporting has probative value, the specific statistics about where offenders commit crimes, their gender, and their relationship to victims do not explain delayed disclosure. They explain who commits these crimes—a fundamentally different subject with no relevance to the prosecution's stated purpose.

The prosecution's rebuttal fails to address the core holding of the preliminary ruling: that the admission of specific statistical evidence under the guise of explaining delayed reporting violated HRE Rule 403. The prosecution attempts to relitigate the relevance of general testimony on delayed reporting, but this argument ignores the preliminary ruling's specific finding that the statistics regarding offender characteristics were improperly admitted.

The defense respectfully requests that this court affirm the preliminary ruling's conclusion that the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting Dr. Bivens' testimony and remand for a new trial.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on the appeal of Last Kony from the November 28, 2012 Judgment of Conviction and Sentence. Kony challenges the Intermediate Court of Appeals’ (ICA) Summary Disposition Order affirming his convictions for sexual assault in the first and third degrees. The specific issue on appeal is whether the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting the expert testimony of Dr. Alexander Jay Bivens regarding the dynamics of child sexual abuse. Having reviewed the rebuttals of the prosecution and the defense, the court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**II. STANDARDS OF REVIEW**

We review evidentiary decisions regarding relevancy under Hawai‘i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rules 401, 402, and 703 under the right/wrong standard. State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). Evidentiary decisions based on HRE Rule 403, which require a balancing of probative value against prejudice, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion. State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998). An abuse of discretion occurs when a court "exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party." Vliet, 95 Haw. at 107, 19 P.3d at 55.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Relevancy of Expert Testimony: Distinction Between Victim Psychology and Offender Characteristics**

The prosecution argues, citing State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 799 P.2d 48 (1990), that expert testimony remains admissible to explain the "psychological mechanisms" of delayed reporting, such as fear of disbelief or family disruption, which are outside the common knowledge of jurors. The defense concedes that general testimony regarding delayed reporting may be relevant but correctly distinguishes the admissibility of such general testimony from the specific statistical data admitted at trial regarding offender characteristics.

We agree with the defense. While the prosecution is correct that the psychological reasons for delayed reporting may constitute specialized knowledge under HRE Rule 703 and Batangan, the relevancy of that testimony does not extend to statistical data regarding the gender of offenders (over 95% male), the location of offenses (100% in the home for incest offenders), or the pre-existing relationship between offender and victim (85%). As held in State v. Vliet, evidence must have a tendency to make a fact of consequence more or less probable. 95 Haw. at 107, 19 P.3d at 55. Statistics describing the demographic profile of a typical offender do not make it more probable that the victim in this case delayed reporting for psychological reasons. The defense persuasively argues that these statistics explain *who* commits sexual abuse, not *why* victims delay reporting it. Therefore, the circuit court’s admission of this specific statistical data under the broad umbrella of "delayed reporting" was erroneous under the right/wrong standard of review.

**B. HRE Rule 403 Balancing: Profiling and Prejudice**

The prosecution contends that the statistical evidence was not improper profiling because the alignment between the statistics and Kony’s characteristics was merely "incidental," citing State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 960 P.2d 1227 (1998). The defense effectively counters this by pointing to the record, specifically the prosecutor’s emphasis on the "100%" figure regarding incest offenders committing crimes in the home.

We find the defense’s argument compelling. When the prosecution elicits testimony that "100 percent of incest offenders... committed their offenses in their own home" in a case where the defendant is charged with incest offenses occurring in his own home, the alignment is not incidental; it is central to the prosecution's implied argument. As this Court recognized in State v. Arceo, 84 Haw. 1, 928 P.2d 843 (1996), evidence that invites the jury to convict on the basis of propensity is inherently prejudicial. The probative value of statistics regarding the gender of offenders or the location of offenses is non-existent with respect to explaining the phenomenon of delayed reporting. Conversely, the danger of unfair prejudice is substantial. By presenting data showing that the defendant fits the profile of a typical sex offender, the evidence invited the jury to convict Kony based on his propensity to commit the crime rather than on the specific facts of the case. This constitutes an abuse of discretion under HRE Rule 403. See Richie, 88 Haw. at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245.

**C. Vouching for Credibility**

The prosecution argues that Dr. Bivens’ testimony did not improperly vouch for the complaining witness’s credibility because it merely explained general dynamics. The defense correctly identifies that when an expert provides statistics that mirror the victim’s specific explanations for delay—such as 24% delaying to avoid family chaos or 29% fearing they will not be believed—the expert implicitly validates the victim’s veracity.

We agree with the defense. While Dr. Bivens did not explicitly state that the victim was telling the truth, his testimony provided a statistical framework that legitimized her specific reasons for delay. This "vouching by implication" undermines the jury's role as the sole judge of credibility and exceeds the permissible scope of expert testimony outlined in Arceo.

**D. Circuit Court’s Discretion**

The prosecution asserts that the circuit court’s "question by question" approach satisfied its duties under Rule 403. The defense correctly responds that this approach was an abdication of the court’s responsibility to conduct a prospective Rule 403 analysis. As stated in Richie, the trial court must weigh the probative value of the evidence against the danger of unfair prejudice before admitting it. 88 Haw. at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245. By allowing the testimony to proceed on the broad topic of "delayed reporting" without excluding the specific, prejudicial statistics regarding offender profiles, the court failed to perform the necessary balancing test. The defense’s objections at trial, preserved through the motion in limine, were sufficient to alert the court to the potential for prejudice, and the court’s failure to exclude the evidence sua sponte or upon objection constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the arguments presented in the rebuttals, the defense has demonstrated that the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting Dr. Bivens’ testimony. The admission of statistical evidence regarding offender characteristics—specifically the "100%" and "95%" figures—was irrelevant to the issue of delayed reporting and violated HRE Rule 403 because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice in the form of improper profiling. The prosecution’s arguments that the evidence was merely educational or that the prejudice was incidental are unpersuasive in light of the specific facts of this case and the prosecutor's emphasis on the statistical alignment.

Therefore, the court rules that the Intermediate Court of Appeals erred in affirming the circuit court’s judgment of conviction. The judgment of conviction is vacated, and the case is remanded to the circuit court for a new trial.

One does not need to know that 100% of incest offenders act in the home to understand that a victim might fear family disruption.

The prosecution's own expert, Dr. Bivens, acknowledged that percentages should "only be used to be given a general idea of, you know, how the phenomenon most often occurs." Yet the prosecution specifically elicited the "100%" figure regarding incest offenders committing crimes in their own home—a figure that could not be more directly on point for a case involving incest allegations occurring in the home.

## II. THE ADMISSION OF STATISTICAL EVIDENCE VIOLATED HRE RULE 403

Even assuming some minimal relevance attached to portions of Dr. Bivens' testimony, the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the specific statistical data regarding offender characteristics because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

### A. Improper Profile Evidence

The prosecution asserts that any alignment between the statistics and Kony's characteristics was "incidental." The record demonstrates otherwise. When Dr. Bivens testified that "100 percent of incest offenders... committed their offenses in their own home," and Kony was charged with incest offenses occurring in his own home, the alignment was not incidental—it was the centerpiece of the prosecution's case theory.

This Court recognized in State v. Arceo, 84 Hawai'i 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996), that evidence inviting the jury to convict on the basis of propensity is inherently prejudicial. The prosecution's emphasis on the "100%" figure was precisely an invitation to the jury to find Kony guilty because he fit the profile of a typical incest offender—living in the home where the abuse occurred.

The prosecution argues that it never explicitly told the jury to convict based on profile. This is a distinction without a difference. The natural and inevitable effect of such testimony is to suggest to the jury that the defendant fits the mold of a typical sex offender. As this Court stated in State v. Richie, 88 Hawai'i 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998), the trial court must weigh the probative value of evidence against the danger of unfair prejudice before admitting it.

### B. The Trial Court's Failure to Conduct Proper Rule 403 Analysis

The prosecution defends the trial court's "question by question" approach as responsible case management. This Court correctly characterized this approach as "an abdication of the court's responsibility to conduct a prospective Rule 403 analysis."

The trial court acknowledged in its ruling that it would allow Dr. Bivens to testify on the issue of delayed reporting. However, the court failed to distinguish between permissible testimony regarding the psychological reasons for delayed reporting and impermissible testimony regarding offender demographics. By allowing the testimony to proceed under the broad umbrella of "delayed reporting" without excluding the specific prejudicial statistics, the court failed to perform the necessary balancing test.

The defense raised this precise concern in its motion in limine, arguing that Dr. Bivens' testimony would be irrelevant and that its probative value would be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. This objection was sufficient to alert the trial court to the potential for prejudice. The court's failure to exclude the statistical evidence sua sponte or upon objection constitutes an abuse of discretion.

## III. DR. BIVENS' TESTIMONY IMPROPERLY VOUCHED FOR THE COMPLAINING WITNESS'S CREDIBILITY

The prosecution argues that Dr. Bivens' testimony did not improperly vouch for the victim's credibility because it merely explained general dynamics. This argument ignores the practical effect of the testimony.

When Dr. Bivens testified that 29% of victims delay reporting because they expect not to be believed, 24% delay to avoid family chaos, and 18% delay because they don't want to harm the abuser, he provided a statistical framework that directly mirrored the victim's specific explanations for her delay. Minor testified that she didn't tell her mother earlier because she was worried about her "family being messed up," felt bad for her sister, and was scared. These are precisely the reasons Dr. Bivens identified as common.

The prosecution is correct that Dr. Bivens never explicitly stated that the victim was telling the truth. However, his testimony provided a validation of the victim's specific reasons for delay by showing that statistically, many victims share those same concerns. This is vouching by implication—a practice this Court has recognized exceeds the permissible scope of expert testimony.

In State v. Arceo, this Court emphasized that expert testimony must assist the jury without improperly influencing their assessment of witness credibility. By providing statistical support for the victim's specific reasons for delay, Dr. Bivens' testimony improperly bolstered the victim's credibility without the jury having to make an independent assessment.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS ARE UNPERSUASIVE

The prosecution argues that the testimony was "essential" to ensure the jury could fairly evaluate the evidence. This argument assumes that without such testimony, the jury would have been unable to understand delayed reporting. This assumption is unfounded.

Jurors are capable of understanding that victims of crime—particularly crimes committed by family members in the home—may have reasons for delaying disclosure without needing a clinical psychologist to explain demographic statistics about offenders. The prosecution's argument that such testimony is "essential" proves too much: if expert testimony regarding child sexual abuse dynamics were truly essential in every such case, there would be no trial where such testimony was excluded. This is not the standard.

The prosecution also argues that the defense cannot point to any instance where the prosecution explicitly suggested the defendant was guilty because he fit a profile. This is irrelevant. The question is not whether the prosecution explicitly argued profile guilt, but whether the natural and inevitable effect of the testimony was to invite the jury to do so. The prosecution cannot escape the prejudicial effect of evidence simply by declining to explicitly argue the prejudicial inference.

## CONCLUSION

The defense has demonstrated that the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting Dr. Bivens' testimony. The admission of statistical evidence regarding offender characteristics—specifically the "100%" figure regarding incest offenders committing crimes in their own home and the "95%" figure regarding male perpetrators—was irrelevant to the issue of delayed reporting and violated HRE Rule 403 because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice in the form of improper profiling.

The prosecution's arguments that the evidence was merely educational, that any alignment was incidental, and that the testimony did not vouch for credibility are unpersuasive in light of the specific facts of this case and the prosecutor's emphasis on the statistical alignment with the defendant.

For all the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the ruling vacating the conviction and remand for a new trial.

Thank you, Your Honor.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on the appeal of Last Kony from the November 28, 2012 Judgment of Conviction and Sentence. Kony challenges the Intermediate Court of Appeals’ (ICA) Summary Disposition Order affirming his convictions for sexual assault in the first and third degrees. The specific issue on appeal is whether the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting the expert testimony of Dr. Alexander Jay Bivens regarding the dynamics of child sexual abuse. Having reviewed the closing arguments of the prosecution and the defense, the court updates and adopts its preliminary ruling as follows.

**II. STANDARDS OF REVIEW**

We review evidentiary decisions regarding relevancy under Hawai‘i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rules 401, 402, and 703 under the right/wrong standard. State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). Evidentiary decisions based on HRE Rule 403, which require a balancing of probative value against prejudice, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion. State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998). An abuse of discretion occurs when a court "exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party." Vliet, 95 Haw. at 107, 19 P.3d at 55.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Relevancy of Expert Testimony: Distinction Between Victim Psychology and Offender Characteristics**

The prosecution argues that the statistical evidence regarding offender characteristics—such as the location of abuse (100% in the home for incest offenders) and the gender of offenders (over 95% male)—was "interwoven" with the issue of delayed reporting and necessary to provide context for the jury. The prosecution contends that without this context, the jury might view the victim's delay as indicative of fabrication.

We find the prosecution’s argument unavailing. While the general psychological mechanisms of delayed reporting may be relevant under State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 557-58, 799 P.2d 48, 52-54 (1990), the specific statistical data admitted here regarding offender demographics does not assist the jury in understanding the phenomenon of delayed reporting. As the defense correctly argues, statistics describing *who* commits sexual abuse or *where* it typically occurs do not make it more probable that the victim in this case delayed reporting for psychological reasons. See State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. at 107, 19 P.3d at 55.

The prosecution’s attempt to link the "100%" statistic regarding incest offenders to the victim's fear of family disruption is tenuous. The victim’s fear stems from the specific facts of her relationship with Kony and the living arrangements, not from the statistical frequency with which incest offenders commit crimes in the home. The probative value of knowing that 95% of offenders are male or that 100% of incest offenses occur in the home is negligible with respect to explaining the victim's state of mind. Therefore, the circuit court’s admission of this specific statistical data under the broad umbrella of "delayed reporting" was erroneous under the right/wrong standard.

**B. HRE Rule 403 Balancing: Profiling and Prejudice**

The prosecution asserts that the alignment between the statistics and Kony’s characteristics was "incidental" and that the State never explicitly argued that Kony was guilty because he fit a profile. The defense counters that the admission of the "100%" figure regarding incest offenders committing crimes in their own home was highly prejudicial and central to the prosecution's case theory.

We agree with the defense. The prosecution’s focus on the "100%" statistic in a case where the defendant is charged with incest offenses occurring in his own home created an inevitable inference of guilt based on propensity. As held in State v. Arceo, 84 Haw. 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996), evidence that invites the jury to convict on the basis of propensity is inherently prejudicial. The probative value of these statistics in explaining delayed reporting is far outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The prosecution cannot escape the prejudicial effect of the evidence simply by refraining from explicitly arguing the profile during closing argument; the natural and probable effect of the testimony was to suggest that Kony was guilty because he fit the mold of a typical sex offender. See State v. Richie, 88 Haw. at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245.

**C. Vouching for Credibility**

The prosecution maintains that Dr. Bivens’ testimony did not improperly vouch for the complaining witness’s credibility because he never explicitly stated that she was telling the truth. The defense correctly identifies that the testimony provided a statistical framework that legitimized the victim’s specific explanations for delay.

We find the defense’s position persuasive. When Dr. Bivens testified that 24% of victims delay reporting to avoid family chaos or that 29% expect not to be believed—reasons that mirrored the victim’s specific testimony—he implicitly validated her veracity. This "vouching by implication" undermines the jury's role as the sole judge of credibility and exceeds the permissible scope of expert testimony. See Arceo, 84 Haw. at 11, 928 P.2d at 853. The jury was effectively told that the victim’s behavior was consistent with that of a true victim, which invaded the province of the jury.

**D. Circuit Court’s Discretion**

The prosecution argues that the circuit court’s "question by question" approach constituted responsible management of the evidence. The defense correctly characterizes this approach as an abdication of the court’s responsibility to conduct a prospective Rule 403 analysis.

Under Richie, the trial court must weigh the probative value of the evidence against the danger of unfair prejudice *before* admitting it. 88 Haw. at 37, 960 P.2d at 1245. By allowing Dr. Bivens to testify on the broad topic of "delayed reporting" without excluding the specific, prejudicial statistics regarding offender profiles, the court failed to perform the necessary balancing test. The defense’s motion in limine and subsequent objections were sufficient to preserve the issue, and the court’s failure to exclude the evidence sua sponte or upon objection constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the closing arguments and the record before the court, the defense has demonstrated that the circuit court abused its discretion in admitting Dr. Bivens’ testimony. The admission of statistical evidence regarding offender characteristics—specifically the "100%" and "95%" figures—was irrelevant to the issue of delayed reporting and violated HRE Rule 403 because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice in the form of improper profiling. The prosecution’s arguments that the evidence was merely educational or that the prejudice was incidental are unpersuasive.

Therefore, the court rules that the Intermediate Court of Appeals erred in affirming the circuit court’s judgment of conviction. The judgment of conviction is vacated, and the case is remanded to the circuit court for a new trial.
