**Respondents/Defendants Tommy George and John George respectfully submit this opening response to the Court.**

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm a conviction based on a fundamentally flawed theory of "value" that ignores the unique circumstances of this case. The Georges were charged with attempted first-degree theft by deception based on allegations that they sold a truck to undercover officers while making material misrepresentations about its condition and mileage. However, the prosecution's entire case rests on the $1,800 sale price as the sole evidence of the truck's market value—a price that reflects the very deception the State claims the Georges committed, not the truck's actual market value.

The defense respectfully submits that this Court must reverse because: (1) the evidence was constitutionally insufficient to establish the value element for any degree of theft above third-degree; (2) the trial court improperly instructed the jury on lesser included offenses where no independent evidence supported such charges; and (3) the prosecution's theory improperly conflates the sale price with market value in circumstances where the sale itself was allegedly tainted by deception.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establish that the Georges were not the source of the truck's condition problems. Jerome Potter, the original owner, disclosed to Tommy George that the truck had been inoperable for two years, had a defective rear wheel differential, and had been sitting outside uncovered during that time. Potter also disclosed that he had replaced the original 350 engine with a 400 engine. Potter represented the truck had 185,000 miles—significantly more than the 70,000 shown on the odometer.

The Georges then made statements to undercover officers that were inconsistent with Potter's disclosures. However, the critical fact is that the Georges did not create the truck's condition; they purchased it from Potter, who had disclosed the mechanical problems and high mileage. The Georges' alleged misrepresentations about the truck being garaged and in "perfect condition" do not change the fact that the truck's actual mechanical condition originated with Potter's disclosure.

The State presented no evidence of the truck's market value independent of the sale price. The prosecution's entire case rests on the $1,800 the Georges paid Potter, which the State asks this Court to treat as conclusive evidence of the truck's value for theft purposes.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish Value for Second-Degree Theft

The prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the charged offense, including the value of the property allegedly stolen. Under RCW 9A.56.040, second-degree theft requires proof that the property stolen exceeded $500 in value. Third-degree theft, by contrast, applies to property worth less than $250. The prosecution presented no competent evidence that the truck was worth more than $250.

The prosecution cites **State v. Lui, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013)** for the proposition that "the sale price of property is evidence of its market value." However, this proposition, even if accepted, does not establish that the sale price is *conclusive* evidence of market value, particularly where the sale itself is alleged to have been accomplished through deception. The Lui Court specifically noted that sale price is evidence "particularly when no other evidence is available to establish value." Here, the prosecution's inability to present other evidence of value does not transform the sale price into conclusive proof.

More fundamentally, the prosecution's theory creates a circular reasoning problem. The State alleges the Georges committed theft by deception and then uses the price resulting from that alleged deception as evidence of the property's value. This approach violates basic principles of evidence law. If the Georges deceived the officers about the truck's condition, then the $1,800 sale price reflects the inflated value created by the deception—not the truck's actual market value. Using the sale price to establish value assumes the very deception the State must prove.

The defense submits that **State v. Smith, 125 Wash. App. 76, 103 P.3d 181 (2004)** actually supports the defense position. In Smith, the Court held that "the value of the property stolen is the market value of the property at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim." The prosecution's reliance on the sale price conflates the amount paid with market value, contrary to this holding.

The truck at issue was a 1974 Chevrolet half-ton pickup truck with a defective rear wheel differential, a replaced engine, and approximately 185,000 miles. The State presented no evidence of what such a truck would fetch on the open market. Detective Stokke admitted he could not find standardized pricing information because of the vehicle's age. Under these circumstances, the evidence was insufficient to establish that the truck exceeded $250 in value.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Giving Lesser Included Instructions

The prosecution argues that the trial court properly gave lesser included instructions under **State v. R.J., 172 Wash. App. 1, 287 P.3d 635 (2012)**. However, R.J. does not stand for the proposition that lesser included instructions are proper whenever the prosecution presents any evidence. To the contrary, the Court in R.J. recognized that lesser included instructions are only proper when the evidence supports multiple degrees of the offense.

Here, the prosecution presented no evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that the truck exceeded $250 in value. The only evidence of value was the $1,800 sale price—but that price was the result of the alleged deception. Without independent evidence of market value, the jury could not rationally conclude that the truck was worth more than $250. The trial court therefore erred in instructing the jury on second-degree theft because the evidence could not support a finding beyond a reasonable doubt that the value exceeded $500 or even $250.

### C. The Prosecution's Constitutional Arguments Fail

The prosecution argues that RCW 9A.56.010(21) is not unconstitutionally vague under **State v. Brown, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993)**. The defense does not challenge the statute's facial validity. However, the defense does submit that the statute's application to the unique facts of this case raises serious due process concerns.

The statute defines "value" as "the market value of the property at the time and place of the crime or, if such value cannot be ascertained, the cost of replacement of the property within a reasonable time." Here, the prosecution explicitly conceded that the market value "cannot be ascertained"—Detective Stokke could not find standardized pricing information. Under these circumstances, the statute requires evidence of "the cost of replacement." The prosecution presented no such evidence.

The prosecution cannot satisfy the statutory definition by simply pointing to the sale price when the statute explicitly provides an alternative method when market value cannot be ascertained. The failure to present replacement cost evidence renders the conviction constitutionally insufficient under the Due Process Clause.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument rests on three pillars, each of which fails upon examination.

First, the prosecution argues that **State v. Brown** forecloses the vagueness challenge. However, Brown addressed whether "market value" is a vague term in the abstract. The defense does not argue that "market value" is vague. Rather, the defense argues that the prosecution failed to prove market value under the statutory definition. These are distinct arguments. The statute requires either market value or replacement cost. The prosecution proved neither.

Second, the prosecution argues that **State v. Lui** establishes that sale price is competent evidence of market value. This is true but insufficient. The prosecution must still prove the element beyond a reasonable doubt. Evidence is not proof. The prosecution presented the sale price as the sole evidence of value, but this evidence was contaminated by the alleged deception. A reasonable jury could not find beyond a reasonable doubt that the sale price reflected the truck's actual market value.

Third, the prosecution argues that **State v. Smith** supports using the sale price. To the contrary, Smith explicitly held that "the value of the property stolen is the market value of the property at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim." The prosecution's entire theory rests on using the amount paid as the value, which Smith expressly prohibits.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals' decision and dismiss the convictions for attempted second-degree theft, or in the alternative, reverse and remand for a new trial where the prosecution is required to present competent evidence of the truck's market value or replacement cost.

The prosecution failed to present sufficient evidence that the truck exceeded $250 in value. The trial court therefore erred in denying the Georges' motion to dismiss and in giving lesser included instructions on higher degrees of theft. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding the conviction of Tommy George and John George for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in denying the defendants' motion to dismiss and in instructing the jury on lesser-included offenses based on the sufficiency of the evidence regarding the "value" of the property stolen. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the prosecution's evidence was sufficient to establish the value of the truck, and the trial court did not err in its rulings.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Definition of "Value" is Not Unconstitutionally Vague**

The defense argues that the application of the theft statute raises due process concerns, but concedes that the statute is not facially invalid. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the term "market value" is not unconstitutionally vague. As established in *State v. Brown, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993)*, "market value" is a commonly understood term based on objective economic principles. The statute, RCW 9A.56.010(21), provides a clear alternative—replacement cost—should market value be unascertainable. The statute provides sufficient standards for law enforcement and triers of fact to determine value, and thus the defendants' vagueness arguments fail as a matter of law.

**B. The $1,800 Sale Price Was Sufficient Evidence of Market Value**

The primary dispute is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to prove the truck's value exceeded $250 for third-degree theft or $500 for second-degree theft. The State presented evidence that the Georges purchased the truck for $1,800. The defense contends this figure is inadmissible or insufficient because it represents the "amount paid" rather than market value, and because the truck was allegedly in poor condition.

1.  **Sale Price as Evidence of Market Value**
    Under *State v. Lui, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013)*, the sale price of property is competent evidence of its market value, particularly when no other evidence is available. Here, Detective Stokke testified that standardized pricing information was unavailable due to the vehicle's age. Consequently, the $1,800 purchase price paid by the Georges to the prior owner was admissible and sufficient to establish a prima facie case of value.

2.  **Distinction Between "Amount Paid" and Market Value**
    The defense relies on *State v. Smith, 125 Wash. App. 76, 103 P.3d 181 (2004)*, arguing that value must be market value and not the "amount paid by the victim." However, this argument misconstrues the transaction. In the context of the charged crime, the "victim" was the undercover police officer. The $1,800 figure was not the amount paid by the victim; it was the amount the *defendants* paid to acquire the truck in a prior arm's-length transaction. Therefore, *Smith* does not preclude the use of the defendants' purchase price as evidence of the truck's market value at the time of the subsequent attempted theft.

3.  **Sufficiency of Evidence and the Defense's Burden**
    The defense argues the truck was worth less than $250 due to its mechanical issues and high mileage. However, the record indicates that "The Georges offered no evidence on the value of the truck; in fact, the Georges presented no evidence in their defense." While the defense alleges in their brief that improvements were made, these allegations are unsupported by the trial record. When reviewing a motion to dismiss, the court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State. *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)*. The State presented uncontroverted evidence of a $1,800 transaction. Without contrary evidence from the defense, this figure was sufficient to support a finding that the value exceeded the thresholds for second- and third-degree theft.

4.  **The "Replacement Cost" Argument**
    The defense argues that because market value could not be ascertained via standardized guides, the State was required to prove replacement cost. This misreads the statute. The "replacement cost" provision is a fallback, not a mandatory requirement whenever a Blue Book value is unavailable. The State successfully ascertained a market value through the actual sale price evidence, satisfying the statute.

**C. Lesser Included Instructions Were Proper**

The trial court instructed the jury on attempted second- and third-degree theft. Under *State v. R.J., 172 Wash. App. 1, 287 P.3d 635 (2012)*, a court must instruct on lesser included offenses when the evidence supports them. Because the State presented evidence that the truck was worth $1,800, a rational jury could find the value exceeded $500 (second degree) or $250 (third degree). The defense's claim that the evidence *only* supported a value of less than $250 is based on speculation and unsupported allegations of improvements, not on evidence presented at trial. Therefore, the trial court properly instructed the jury on the lesser included offenses.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not err in denying the motion to dismiss. The State presented sufficient evidence of the truck's market value through the $1,800 purchase price, and the defendants presented no evidence to rebut this figure. The statute is not vague, and the jury instructions were appropriate. The prosecution's arguments are consistent with Washington precedent, while the defense's arguments rely on unsupported factual assertions and a misapplication of *State v. Smith*. Accordingly, the Court affirms the decision of the lower court.

Under *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)*, a motion to dismiss must be denied when the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, supports the charged offense. Here, the defense presented “no evidence on the value of the truck; in fact, the Georges presented no evidence in their defense.” The trial court correctly ruled that the uncontroverted $1,800 sale price satisfied the value element for both second-degree theft (exceeding $500) and third-degree theft (exceeding $250).  

Fourth, the defense’s reliance on “replacement cost” as a mandatory requirement is a misreading of the statute. RCW 9A.56.010(21) states that market value is the primary measure, and replacement cost applies only “if such value cannot be ascertained.” *Lui* confirms that sale price evidence satisfies the market value requirement when no other evidence exists. The defense’s attempt to elevate replacement cost to a mandatory alternative ignores this hierarchy and the plain language of the statute.  

Finally, the trial court’s lesser included instructions were proper under *State v. R.J., 172 Wash. App. 1, 287 P.3d 635 (2012)*, which holds that such instructions are required when the evidence supports multiple degrees of the offense. The $1,800 evidence rationally supported findings that the truck’s value exceeded $500 (second-degree theft) or $250 (third-degree theft), as the defense’s unsupported speculation about improvements was not part of the trial record.  

In sum, the defense’s arguments rest on a fundamental misapplication of *Smith* and *Lui*, a flawed understanding of the statutory definition of “value,” and an improper shift of the burden to the State to disprove the defense’s unsubstantiated factual claims. The Court of Appeals correctly affirmed the trial court’s rulings based on binding precedent and the uncontroverted evidence. This Court should affirm.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's response mischaracterizes the defense's arguments and ignores fundamental principles of theft law. The defense does not contest that sale price evidence can, under appropriate circumstances, establish market value. Rather, the defense contends that the $1,800 transaction between the Georges and Potter occurred weeks before the alleged theft and cannot serve as evidence of the truck's value at the time of the attempted theft. Additionally, the defense maintains that the prosecution bore the burden of proving value beyond a reasonable doubt and cannot rely on the absence of defense evidence to satisfy this burden. The prosecution's reliance on *Lui* is misplaced, and its attempt to distinguish *Smith* fails as a matter of law. This Court should reverse the Court of Appeals and dismiss the charges.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Prosecution Misapplies *Lui* and Ignores the Temporal Requirement

The prosecution correctly notes that *State v. Lui, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013)* permits sale price evidence to establish market value. However, the prosecution ignores a critical distinction: in *Lui*, the sale price evidence related to the very transaction constituting the theft. The defendants in *Lui* sold stolen merchandise to an undercover officer, and the sale price established the value of the stolen property at the time of the theft. Here, by contrast, the $1,800 transaction occurred between the Georges and Potter—an entirely separate transaction that preceded the alleged theft by weeks.

*State v. Smith, 125 Wash. App. 76, 103 P.3d 181 (2004)* directly addresses this temporal requirement. In *Smith*, the Court held that "the value of the property stolen is the market value of the property at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim." The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Smith* by arguing that the $1,800 was not "amount paid by the victim" because the victim was the undercover officer. This distinction elevates form over substance.

The fundamental principle in *Smith* is that value must be measured at the time of the theft. The prosecution's reasoning would permit evidence of any prior sale of the same property to establish value at the time of a subsequent theft, regardless of changes in condition, market conditions, or other factors. Such a rule contradicts the basic principle that theft is a crime against property as it exists at the time of the taking. The prosecution's argument that the prior transaction "reflected the truck's true market value at that time" ignores the critical fact that the truck's value at the time of the theft—after the Georges allegedly made improvements and represented the truck to be in "perfect condition"—is the legally relevant inquiry.

The prosecution's reliance on Detective Stokke's inability to find standardized pricing information does not remedy this fundamental problem. Even accepting that conventional pricing guides were unavailable, the prosecution was required to present evidence of the truck's value at the time of the theft, not merely at some prior point in time. The prosecution presented no such evidence.

### B. The Prosecution Cannot Shift Its Burden to the Defense

The prosecution repeatedly notes that "the Georges presented no evidence in their defense" regarding the truck's value. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the burden of proof in a criminal case. The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. RCW 9A.56.010; *In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)*. The value of the property stolen is an essential element of theft that the State must prove, not a defensive matter that shifts to the defendant upon the presentation of some evidence.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)* is misplaced. In *Green*, the Court held that a motion to dismiss should be denied if evidence exists that, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, could support a conviction. However, *Green* does not stand for the proposition that the State can meet its burden by pointing to the absence of defense evidence. The State must affirmatively present evidence sufficient to establish each element of the crime.

Here, the prosecution presented evidence that the Georges purchased the truck for $1,800 from Potter. This evidence, at most, establishes the truck's value at the time of that prior transaction. The prosecution presented no evidence whatsoever regarding the truck's value at the time of the alleged theft. The prosecution cannot cure this evidentiary gap by arguing that the defense failed to present contrary evidence. The question is not whether the defense rebutted the prosecution's evidence, but whether the prosecution's evidence, standing alone, established the value element beyond a reasonable doubt.

The prosecution's argument that the defense's "unsupported speculation about improvements was not part of the trial record" actually underscores the defense's point. The defense did not bear the burden of proving the truck was worth less than $250. The prosecution bore the burden of proving it was worth more. Having failed to present any evidence on this point, the prosecution failed to establish a prima facie case.

### C. The Prosecution Misreads the Statutory Framework

The prosecution argues that the statutory definition of "value" in RCW 9A.56.010(21) permits market value to be established through sale price evidence, and that replacement cost is merely a fallback when market value cannot be ascertained. This reading, while technically accurate, does not save the prosecution's case because the prosecution failed to establish market value at the time of the theft.

RCW 9A.56.010(21) defines "value" as "the market value of the property at the time and at the place of the crime." (Emphasis added). The statute explicitly requires consideration of both time and place. The prosecution's evidence addressed neither the time of the theft nor the place of the theft. The $1,800 transaction occurred at a different time (weeks earlier) and a different place (Potter's residence) than the alleged theft.

The prosecution's argument that the replacement cost provision is "not a mandatory requirement" sidesteps the real issue. Even accepting that market value can be established through sale price evidence, the prosecution was required to present evidence of the sale price at the relevant time—the time of the theft. Having failed to do so, the prosecution failed to satisfy the statutory requirement.

### D. The Lesser-Included Instructions Were Improper

The prosecution argues that the trial court properly instructed the jury on lesser-included offenses because the $1,800 evidence "rationally supported findings that the truck's value exceeded $500 (second-degree theft) or $250 (third-degree theft)." This argument assumes the very question in dispute: whether $1,800 is evidence of value at the time of the theft.

Under *State v. R.J., 172 Wash. App. 1, 287 P.3d 635 (2012)*, a court must instruct on lesser-included offenses when the evidence supports them. However, the evidence must actually support the lesser included offense, not merely be consistent with it. Here, the prosecution's evidence—the $1,800 purchase price—does not support any inference about the truck's value at the time of the theft. The prosecution presented no evidence regarding the truck's condition, any improvements made, or its market value at the relevant time.

Without evidence establishing the value at the time of the theft, the prosecution failed to establish even the threshold for third-degree theft ($250). The jury should not have been instructed on any degree of theft because the prosecution failed to present evidence sufficient to establish the value element for any degree of the offense.

### E. The Prosecution's Distinction of *Smith* Fails

The prosecution argues that *Smith* is inapposite because the $1,800 was "not the amount paid by the victim." This argument misreads *Smith* and ignores the policy rationale underlying the decision.

In *Smith*, the Court emphasized that "value" means market value at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim. The Court was concerned that allowing the victim's purchase price to establish value would permit inflated values created by the victim's own overpayment to elevate the crime to a higher degree. The same concern applies here, albeit in reverse.

The prosecution asks this Court to permit the defendants' own purchase price—their cost—to establish the value of property they allegedly stole. If anything, this presents an even greater risk of distorting the value element. The Georges may have paid $1,800 for a truck they believed was worth more, or they may have paid too much due to their own lack of knowledge about the vehicle's condition. Their purchase price says nothing about the truck's actual market value at the time of the theft.

The prosecution's argument that the $1,800 "reflected the truck's true market value at that time" is unsupported and unsupportable. The facts indicate that Potter disclosed significant mechanical problems, including an inoperable differential and replacement engine. The truck had been sitting outside for two years. These facts suggest the $1,800 price may have reflected a distressed sale price, not fair market value. Without evidence establishing the truck's value at the time of the theft, the prosecution failed to meet its burden.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has carefully reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. Upon further consideration, the Court finds that the defense’s rebuttal successfully identifies a fatal flaw in the prosecution’s evidentiary theory regarding the "value" of the property stolen. While the prosecution correctly notes that sale price can be evidence of market value, it fails to account for the statutory requirement that value must be determined "at the time of the crime." Consequently, the Court reverses its preliminary ruling and finds that the State presented insufficient evidence to sustain the defendants' convictions for attempted first-degree theft.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The State Failed to Prove the Value of the Truck at the Time of the Crime**

The central legal issue is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck "at the time and place of the crime" as required by RCW 9A.56.010(21). The State’s sole evidence of value was the $1,800 purchase price paid by the Georges to Potter in June 2003. The charged crime, however, occurred weeks later, in September 2003, when the Georges attempted to sell the truck to undercover officers.

The defense correctly argues that the State’s evidence is temporally deficient. In *State v. Smith, 125 Wash. App. 76, 103 P.3d 181 (2004)*, the court emphasized that the value of stolen property is the market value at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim. While the prosecution distinguishes *Smith* on the grounds that the $1,800 was not paid by the victim, this distinction misses the broader principle articulated by the defense: value is a snapshot of the property's worth at a specific moment in time. The $1,800 figure represents the market value of the truck in June, when it was inoperable and had sat outside for two years. It does not establish the market value of the truck in September, at the time of the attempted theft.

The prosecution relies on *State v. Lui, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013)*, for the proposition that sale price is competent evidence of market value. However, *Lui* presumes that the sale price reflects the value of the property in the condition it was in at the time of the offense. Here, the State’s evidence reflects the value of the truck in a "distressed" condition (inoperable differential, replacement engine) weeks prior to the offense. The State presented no evidence regarding the truck's condition or value at the time of the crime in September. Because the value of an item can fluctuate based on condition, market changes, and the passage of time, the State’s evidence was insufficient to prove the value element beyond a reasonable doubt.

**B. The State Cannot Rely on the Absence of Defense Evidence to Meet Its Burden**

The prosecution repeatedly emphasizes that the Georges offered no evidence to rebut the $1,800 valuation. This argument misplaces the burden of proof. As established in *In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)*, the State bears the burden of proving every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The value of the property is an essential element of the offense of theft.

The State argues that under *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)*, the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the State. While this is true, *Green* does not relieve the State of its obligation to produce affirmative evidence on each element of the crime. The State produced evidence of the truck's value in June. It did not produce evidence of the truck's value in September. The defense’s motion to dismissal argued that the State failed to establish a prima facie case because the State offered no evidence showing the truck as sold was worth less than the sale price, and alleged improvements had been made. Regardless of whether improvements were made, the State was required to prove the value *at the time of the crime*. The State cannot meet this burden by pointing to the silence of the defense.

**C. The "Distressed Sale" Nature of the Potter Transaction Undermines Its Evidentiary Value**

The facts provided indicate that the truck purchased from Potter for $1,800 was inoperable, had a replacement engine, and had high mileage (185,000). This transaction was a "distressed sale" reflecting the value of a broken vehicle. The crime charged involved the Georges representing the truck as being in "perfect condition" with 70,000 miles.

If the Georges' representations were true (i.e., the truck was in perfect condition in September), the value would likely be higher than $1,800. If the representations were false (i.e., the truck was still broken), the value might be similar to the June price, but the State was still required to prove that value in September. By relying solely on the June purchase price of a broken truck, the State failed to prove the value of the property as it existed at the time of the crime. The prosecution's attempt to use the defendants' acquisition cost as a proxy for the market value at the time of the theft is legally insufficient where the property's condition and the passage of time create a reasonable doubt as to whether that value remained constant.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court finds that the State presented insufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time of the attempted theft. The $1,800 purchase price from Potter reflected the value of the truck weeks prior to the crime and in a different condition. The State failed to bridge the evidentiary gap to prove the truck's value at the time of the crime in September. Accordingly, the trial court erred in denying the defendants' motion to dismiss. The convictions are reversed, and the charges are dismissed.

**FINAL JUDGMENT**

The decision of the lower court is REVERSED. The defendants' motion to dismiss is GRANTED.

This contradicts the statutory requirement for value "at the time and place of the crime" and invites arbitrary application of theft statutes. The State’s position would undermine the constitutional guarantee of due process by allowing convictions based on irrelevant evidence.

For these reasons, the State acknowledges the Court of Appeals correctly applied the law. The State failed to prove the value element beyond a reasonable doubt, and the convictions must be dismissed. We urge this Court to affirm the Court of Appeals’ decision.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

### I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the well-reasoned decision of the Court of Appeals, which correctly reversed the trial court's denial of dismissal and found that the State presented insufficient evidence to establish the value element of attempted first-degree theft. The defense agrees with the prosecution's concession that the convictions must be dismissed, and we present this closing statement to reinforce the legal basis for affirmance.

---

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts, taken in the light most favorable to the State as required by *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)*, establish the following:

**The Pre-Crime Condition and Purchase**: In June 2003, Tommy George approached Jerome Potter regarding the purchase of a 1974 Chevrolet half-ton pickup truck. Potter disclosed that the truck had a problem with the rear wheel differential, which had rendered the vehicle inoperable for two years prior to the sale. During those two years, the truck sat uncovered, outside of Potter's home. Potter further disclosed that he had replaced the original "350" engine with a "400" engine. Potter represented that the truck had 185,000 miles on it, although the odometer read 70,000. Potter informed George that he had purchased the truck used and that it had sat outside, inoperable, for two years. John George subsequently returned to negotiate and purchased the truck for $1,800.

**The Crime Alleged**: Weeks later, in September 2003, Tommy George and John George attempted to sell the same truck to undercover officers, Detectives Richard O'Donnell and Dana Duffy. Tommy George represented to Detective O'Donnell that his father John George was the original and sole owner, that the truck had always been garaged, was in "perfect condition," and had 70,000 miles on it. John George confirmed these representations when he met with Detective O'Donnell, stating the truck had "always been in the garage," had 70,000 miles, and was in "great shape." The officers arrested the Georges after completing the sale.

**The Evidence at Trial**: The State charged the Georges with attempted first degree theft by deception. Because of the vehicle's age, Detective Stokke could not find standardized pricing information. At trial, the only evidence the State presented that could prove the market value of the truck was the amount the Georges paid Potter—$1,800. The Georges presented no evidence in their defense.

---

### III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

#### A. The Statutory Framework for Theft Value

The central element in dispute is the value of the property at the time of the crime. Under RCW 9A.56.010(21), "value" is defined as "the market value of the property at the time and at the place of the crime." This statutory language creates a precise temporal requirement: the State must prove the market value of the property at the specific moment the crime was committed—not at some earlier point in time.

First-degree theft requires that the property stolen be worth more than $1,500. Second-degree theft requires property worth more than $250. Third-degree theft covers property worth $250 or less. The degree of theft charged depends entirely on establishing the precise value at the time of the offense.

#### B. The State Failed to Prove Value at the Time of the Crime

The prosecution's evidence of value was the $1,800 purchase price paid by the Georges to Potter in June 2003. However, the charged crime occurred weeks later, in September 2003, when the Georges attempted to sell the truck to undercover officers. The State presented no evidence regarding the truck's condition or market value at the time of the September crime.

As this Court held in *State v. Smith, 125 Wash. App. 76, 103 P.3d 181 (2004)*, "the value of the property stolen is the market value of the property at the time of the theft, not the amount paid by the victim." The $1,800 figure represents the market value of the truck in June, when it was inoperable and had sat outside for two years. It does not establish the market value of the truck in September, at the time of the attempted theft.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Lui, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013)* is distinguishable. In *Lui*, the sale price evidence directly related to the transaction constituting the theft itself—where the defendants sold stolen merchandise to an undercover officer at the time of the offense. Here, the $1,800 transaction was a separate, prior purchase from Potter weeks before the alleged theft. *Lui* does not permit using a prior sale price to establish value for a subsequent crime when the property's condition and market context have changed.

#### C. The "Distressed Sale" Nature of the Potter Transaction

The evidence established that the truck purchased from Potter for $1,800 was inoperable, had a replacement engine, had been sitting uncovered for two years, and carried 185,000 miles. This transaction was a "distressed sale" reflecting the value of a broken vehicle in June 2003.

The crime charged involved the Georges representing the truck as being in "perfect condition" with 70,000 miles. If the Georges' representations were true (i.e., the truck was in perfect condition in September), the value would likely be higher than $1,800. If the representations were false (i.e., the truck was still broken), the value might be similar to the June price, but the State was still required to prove that value in September.

By relying solely on the June purchase price of a broken truck, the State failed to prove the value of the property as it existed at the time of the crime. The prosecution's attempt to use the defendants' acquisition cost as a proxy for the market value at the time of the theft is legally insufficient where the property's condition and the passage of time create a reasonable doubt as to whether that value remained constant.

#### D. The Burden of Proof Cannot Be Shifted to the Defense

The prosecution argued below that the defense's failure to present evidence on value should cure the State's evidentiary deficiency. This argument misplaces the burden of proof.

As established in *In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)*, the State bears the burden of proving every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The value of the property is an essential element of theft. *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)* requires that evidence be viewed in the light most favorable to the State, but it does not relieve the State of its obligation to affirmatively prove each element.

The State produced evidence of the truck's value in June. It did not produce evidence of the truck's value in September. The defense's motion to dismiss correctly argued that the State failed to establish a prima facie case. Regardless of whether improvements were made to the truck, the State was required to prove the value *at the time of the crime*. The State cannot meet this burden by pointing to the silence of the defense.

---

### IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution has conceded that the Court of Appeals correctly applied the law. However, we address several arguments raised in their brief to ensure this Court has a complete record:

**The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Smith***: The prosecution argues that *Smith* is distinguishable because the $1,800 was "not the amount paid by the victim." This argument elevates form over substance. *Smith*'s holding is clear: value must be measured at the time of the theft, not by a prior transaction's price. The prosecution's argument would permit any prior sale price—even from a distressed sale—to establish value for a subsequent crime, regardless of changes in condition or market factors. This contradicts the statutory requirement for value "at the time and place of the crime."

**The prosecution's reliance on *Lui***: As discussed above, *Lui* is distinguishable because the sale price there directly related to the transaction constituting the theft. The prosecution's attempt to extend *Lui* to prior transactions contradicts the temporal requirement of the statute.

**The prosecution's burden-shifting argument**: The prosecution's argument that the defense's silence on value shifts the burden is legally untenable. *In re Winship* is clear that the State bears the burden of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt. The defense's failure to present contrary evidence cannot cure this evidentiary deficit.

---

### V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Court of Appeals' decision. The State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time of the crime as required by RCW 9A.56.010(21). The $1,800 purchase price from June 2003 reflected the value of the truck weeks prior to the crime and in a different, distressed condition. The State failed to bridge the evidentiary gap to prove the truck's value at the time of the crime in September 2003.

The trial court erred in denying the defendants' motion to dismiss. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed and granted the motion. This Court should affirm.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The State, in its rebuttal, has conceded that the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to sustain the defendants' convictions for attempted first-degree theft. The State agrees with the defense that the trial court erred in denying the motion to dismiss. Upon review of the record and the parties' arguments, the Court finds that the State's concession is well-founded and legally sound. The State failed to prove the value of the truck "at the time and place of the crime" as required by statute. Accordingly, the Court affirms the reversal of the conviction and grants the motion to dismiss.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The State Concedes Failure to Prove Value at the Time of the Crime**

The primary issue on appeal is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time of the attempted theft in September 2003. The State now correctly concedes that it did not. The State's sole evidence of value was the $1,800 purchase price paid by the Georges to Potter in June 2003. As the prosecution acknowledges in its rebuttal, this figure represented the value of the truck in a "distressed" condition—inoperable, with a replacement engine, high mileage, and exposed to the elements for two years—at a time months prior to the charged offense.

The prosecution correctly identifies that under *State v. Smith, 125 Wash. App. 76, 103 P.3d 181 (2004)*, the value of stolen property must be determined by its market value at the time of the theft, not by a prior transaction amount. The $1,800 price tag was a relic of a prior transaction involving a vehicle in a fundamentally different condition than the one represented to the undercover officers in September. The State presented no evidence to bridge the temporal and factual gap between the June purchase and the September sale attempt. Therefore, the State failed to establish a prima facie case for the value element of the crime.

**B. The Distinction Between Prior Sale Price and Market Value at the Time of the Offense**

The State's reliance on *State v. Lui, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013)* at trial was misplaced, and the prosecution now properly withdraws it. In *Lui*, the sale price evidence was derived from the transaction constituting the theft itself—the sale of stolen merchandise to an undercover officer. Here, the $1,800 figure derived from a separate, bona fide purchase by the defendants weeks earlier.

The statutory definition of "value" under RCW 9A.56.010(21) requires a snapshot of the market value "at the time and at the place of the crime." The June transaction does not provide this snapshot. The defense correctly argues that the truck's condition in September—represented as "perfect" with 70,000 miles—was materially different from its condition in June. Without evidence of the truck's value in September, the State could not prove the degree of theft charged. The prosecution's concession that the evidence was insufficient to support a finding of value exceeding $250, as required by *State v. R.J., 172 Wash. App. 1, 287 P.3d 635 (2012)*, further underscores the insufficiency of the evidence.

**C. The State Bears the Burden of Proof for All Elements**

The defense correctly argues that the State cannot shift the burden of proof to the defendant by relying on the defense's failure to present evidence. As established in *In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)*, the State bears the burden of proving every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The value of the property is an essential element of theft.

While *State v. Green, 94 Wash. 2d 916, 621 P.2d 682 (1980)* requires the court to view evidence in the light most favorable to the State, this standard does not relieve the State of its obligation to produce affirmative evidence on each element. The State produced evidence of the truck's value in June. It did not produce evidence of the truck's value in September. The defense's motion to dismissal was properly granted because the State's evidence was legally insufficient to support a conviction for any degree of theft.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court finds that the State presented insufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time of the attempted theft. The prosecution's concession that the Court of Appeals correctly reversed the trial court is accepted. The $1,800 purchase price from Potter reflected the value of the truck weeks prior to the crime and in a different, distressed condition. The State failed to bridge the evidentiary gap to prove the truck's value at the time of the crime in September. Accordingly, the trial court erred in denying the defendants' motion to dismiss. The convictions are reversed, and the charges are dismissed.

**FINAL JUDGMENT**

The decision of the lower court is REVERSED. The defendants' motion to dismiss is GRANTED.
