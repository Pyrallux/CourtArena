# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of J.M.Y. v. R.R. and A.F.Y.

---

### INTRODUCTION

May it please this Honorable Court, the defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. While the prosecution correctly states that Louisiana law provides procedures for disavowal of paternity, the defense contends that the prosecution has misapplied the relevant legal principles to the facts of this case. The plaintiff, J.M.Y., has stated viable claims that should proceed beyond the exception stage, and this Court should reverse the trial court's judgment.

---

### I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The defense accepts the following facts as true for purposes of this response:

J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married at the time A.F.Y. gave birth to a son in 1994. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. subsequently divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support for the child. On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R., alleging that R.R. was the biological father of the child born during the marriage. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments previously made. On April 9, 2008, J.M.Y. amended his petition to add A.F.Y. as a defendant and sought testing to determine biological paternity, along with reimbursement for past and future child support payments from both R.R. and A.F.Y.

The trial court granted exceptions dismissing the suit, and J.M.Y. now appeals.

---

### II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

#### A. The Plaintiff's Claims Should Proceed Beyond the Exception Stage

The defense argues that the trial court erred in granting the exceptions because the plaintiff has stated viable claims for relief. At the exception stage, the court must accept all well-pleaded facts as true and determine whether the plaintiff has stated a claim upon which relief can be granted. *See Martin v. Martin*, 535 So.2d 929, 931 (La. App. 2nd Cir. 1988). The prosecution's argument, while citing general principles of Louisiana paternity law, fails to address the specific circumstances presented here.

#### B. The Prosecution's Interpretation of In re: J.R. Is Overstated

The prosecution relies heavily on *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281 (La. 1994), for the proposition that a presumed father who has not disavowed the child has no right to seek reimbursement. However, the defense notes that the circumstances in *In re: J.R.* are distinguishable from the present case. In *In re: J.R.*, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed a situation where the presumed father was seeking reimbursement while simultaneously maintaining his parental rights and obligations. The instant case presents a different scenario where J.M.Y. seeks to establish the true biological fatherhood of R.R. and obtain reimbursement based on that establishment.

The defense submits that the prosecution's reading of *In re: J.R.* would create an impossible catch-22: a presumed father cannot recover reimbursement without disavowing the child, yet if he disavows the child, he would no longer have standing to seek reimbursement because he would no longer be the legal father. This interpretation would render the reimbursement remedy illusory in all cases, which could not have been the Supreme Court's intent.

#### C. The Smith v. Smith Case Does Not Control Here

Similarly, the prosecution's reliance on *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), is misplaced. In *Smith v. Smith*, the First Circuit addressed a husband's attempt to recover child support payments from his wife based on her alleged extramarital affair. The court there found no cause of action because the husband had not followed the disavowal procedure. However, the defense notes that *Smith v. Smith* did not address the specific situation where a presumed father seeks to establish another man's biological fatherhood and obtain reimbursement from that alleged father.

The key distinction is that the plaintiff here is not merely seeking to recover funds while retaining his legal paternal status. Rather, the plaintiff has filed suit seeking to establish R.R.'s biological paternity and has requested genetic testing to determine the truth. This procedural posture distinguishes the instant case from *Smith v. Smith*.

#### D. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 Provides an Independent Basis for the Claim

The prosecution contends that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which imposes support obligations, does not create a right to reimbursement without disavowal. The defense agrees that Article 227 imposes the obligation to support a child. However, the defense argues that when a presumed father makes support payments under legal compulsion (through a court order), and another man is subsequently proven to be the biological father, equity and justice require that the presumed father be entitled to reimbursement from the biological father.

Article 227 provides that "[t]he father and mother are bound to maintain and bring up their children," but this obligation exists because of the biological parent-child relationship. When the biological relationship exists with someone other than the legal father, the biological father bears the primary obligation under Article 227. The presumed father, having paid under legal compulsion, has a claim for reimbursement from the party who bears the primary obligation.

#### E. The Plaintiff Has a Right of Action

The prosecution argues that J.M.Y. lacks a right of action. The defense submits that J.M.Y. has a right of action because he has a legally protected interest at stake. As the legal father ordered to pay child support, J.M.Y. has a direct financial interest in determining whether another party should bear all or part of that financial burden. The right to seek reimbursement from one who bears the primary obligation is a recognized principle in Louisiana law.

In *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d 115 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 1980), the court recognized that a presumed father who has paid child support may have recourse against the biological father when paternity is established. While *Glover* may be distinguished on its facts, it demonstrates that Louisiana courts have recognized circumstances where reimbursement claims may proceed.

#### F. Policy Considerations Support Allowing the Claim to Proceed

The defense notes that denying the plaintiff any remedy would produce an unjust result. If a presumed father cannot disavow the child (due to time limitations or other circumstances) yet cannot obtain reimbursement from the biological father, he is left with an impossible burden. The biological father, meanwhile, would escape all responsibility for a child he helped create.

Louisiana courts have recognized that the purpose of the disavowal procedure is to provide certainty in parent-child relationships, not to shield biological fathers from financial responsibility. *See Succession of Robinson*, 600 So.2d 133 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1992). When the biological father's identity becomes known, justice requires that he bear responsibility for the child's support.

---

### III. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument rests on the proposition that Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 establishes an exclusive procedure that must be followed before any reimbursement claim can proceed. The defense responds that:

First, Article 184 establishes the procedure for disavowing paternity, but it does not address the situation where a presumed father seeks to establish another man's paternity while seeking reimbursement. These are distinct legal objectives.

Second, the prosecution conflates two separate issues: (1) the establishment of paternity for purposes of parental rights and obligations, and (2) reimbursement for support payments made under legal compulsion. While these issues may be related, they are not so intertwined that one must necessarily precede the other.

Third, the prosecution's interpretation would allow a biological father to avoid all responsibility for a child simply by remaining silent while the presumed father pays support. This result contradicts the fundamental principle of Article 227 that biological parents bear the obligation of support.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Honorable Court reverse the trial court's judgment granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action, and remand the case for further proceedings. The plaintiff has stated viable claims upon which relief can be granted, and the case should proceed to allow the parties to develop the facts and present their arguments on the merits.

The defense submits that the trial court erred in its application of Louisiana law and that the exceptions were improperly granted. This Court should reverse and remand.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE JUDGMENT OF THE TRIAL COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y. ("Plaintiff") from a judgment granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. ("Defendant") and dismissing Plaintiff’s suit for reimbursement of child support payments. After reviewing the facts provided, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable legal authorities, the Court affirms the judgment of the trial court.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The applicable standard of review for an exception of no cause of action is whether the petition states a cause of action upon which relief can be granted. In reviewing such an exception, the court must accept the well-pleaded factual allegations of the petition as true and determine whether the plaintiff is afforded any remedy under the law. *Martin v. Martin*, 535 So.2d 929, 931 (La. App. 2nd Cir. 1988). The exception of no right of action questions whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Id.*

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue in this case is whether a presumed father, who has not legally disavowed paternity, may maintain a direct action against an alleged biological father for reimbursement of child support payments.

The prosecution correctly identifies the controlling legal framework established by Louisiana Civil Code Article 184. This article provides the exclusive procedure for a presumed father to challenge paternity. The jurisprudence is clear that a presumed father must successfully disavow the child before he can seek reimbursement from the biological father.

In *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281, 285 (La. 1994), the Louisiana Supreme Court held that "a presumed father who has not disavowed the child has no right to seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." This ruling establishes that the right of action to recover support funds is contingent upon a prior change in legal status achieved through disavowal. Similarly, in *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590, 592 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), the court unequivocally stated that "without a prior disavowal action, the presumed father has no cause of action against the biological father for reimbursement."

The record indicates that J.M.Y. has never filed a disavowal action. Instead, he filed suit directly for reimbursement and genetic testing. By failing to initiate the statutory disavowal process, J.M.Y. remains the legal father of the child. Consequently, he lacks the legal standing to assert a claim for reimbursement against R.R. The trial court correctly recognized that the statutory procedure for disavowal is a mandatory prerequisite to any reimbursement claim.

**III. EVALUATION OF DEFENSE ARGUMENTS**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by dismissing the suit at the exception stage and that the prosecution’s reliance on *In re: J.R.* and *Smith v. Smith* is misplaced. The defense contends that requiring disavowal before seeking reimbursement creates a "catch-22" that renders the remedy illusory.

While the defense raises compelling policy arguments regarding equity and the biological father's responsibility, these arguments cannot override the clear statutory and jurisprudential mandates. The defense attempts to distinguish *In re: J.R.* by noting that J.M.Y. seeks to establish biological paternity rather than merely seeking reimbursement while maintaining rights. However, the mechanism provided by Louisiana law for a presumed father to establish the paternity of another man is the disavowal action. *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281 (La. 1994). The law prioritizes the stability of the marital presumption and requires the presumed father to clear his legal status before pursuing third parties for support.

The defense relies on *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d 115 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 1980), to suggest that reimbursement claims may proceed. However, the defense concedes that *Glover* may be distinguishable. Furthermore, *Glover* does not override the specific holding of the Louisiana Supreme Court in *In re: J.R.*, which explicitly bars the action at bar. The defense’s reliance on *Succession of Robinson*, 600 So.2d 133 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1992), regarding the purpose of the disavowal procedure, does not negate the procedural requirement that the procedure must be utilized.

The defense argues that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which imposes the obligation to support, creates an independent basis for reimbursement. However, as noted in *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), the obligation to support under Article 227 does not create a right to reimbursement without a prior disavowal. The "catch-22" described by the defense is a result of the legislature's choice to establish strict time limits and procedures for disavowal to ensure finality in family relationships. It is not within this Court's power to circumvent those statutory requirements based on equitable considerations when the plaintiff has failed to follow the prescribed legal path.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y. failed to satisfy the mandatory condition precedent of disavowing paternity before seeking reimbursement. Therefore, he has stated no cause of action upon which relief can be granted.

**JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y. is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

### REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT  
**IN THE MATTER OF J.M.Y. V. R.R. AND A.F.Y.**  

May it please this Honorable Court, the prosecution respectfully submits that the defense’s arguments are fundamentally mischaracterizations of binding Louisiana jurisprudence and statutory law. The trial court correctly dismissed the suit for lack of cause and right of action, as J.M.Y. failed to satisfy the mandatory prerequisite of disavowing paternity before seeking reimbursement. The defense’s reliance on selective case law and policy arguments cannot override clear legal precedent.  

#### I. THE DEFENSE MISAPPLIES *IN RE: J.R.* AND IGNORS ITS HOLDING  
The defense claims *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281 (La. 1994), is "distinguishable" because J.M.Y. seeks to "establish biological paternity" rather than "maintain parental rights." This is a deliberate distortion of the case. *In re: J.R.* explicitly holds: **"A presumed father who has not disavowed the child has no right to seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father."** Id. at 285 (emphasis added). The Louisiana Supreme Court did not condition this rule on the presumed father’s intent to "maintain parental rights." It squarely addressed *any* reimbursement claim by a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity—regardless of whether he seeks to establish another man’s biological paternity.  

The defense’s "catch-22" argument is legally baseless. Disavowal is not a procedural trap; it is the *only* mechanism under Louisiana law for a presumed father to terminate his legal status and create standing to pursue reimbursement. *See In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d at 285 ("The disavowal proceeding is the exclusive means for a presumed father to challenge paternity"). If J.M.Y. had filed a disavowal action, he would have had standing to sue R.R. *after* his legal paternity was extinguished. His failure to initiate that process—*before* filing suit for reimbursement—is fatal to his claim.  

#### II. *SMITH V. SMITH* DIRECTLY CONTROLS THIS CASE  
The defense argues *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), is inapplicable because it "did not address a presumed father seeking to establish another man’s biological paternity." This is incorrect. *Smith* explicitly rejected a presumed father’s reimbursement claim against a biological father *because disavowal had not been sought*. The court held:  
> "Without a prior disavowal action, the presumed father has no cause of action against the biological father for reimbursement."  
Id. at 592 (emphasis added). The defense’s attempt to limit *Smith* to "reimbursement from the wife" ignores the court’s clear language: the barrier is the *absence of disavowal*, not the defendant’s identity. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. *without first disavowing paternity*—precisely the scenario *Smith* forecloses.  

#### III. LOUISIANA CIVIL CODE ARTICLE 227 DOES NOT CREATE AN INDEPENDENT REIMBURSEMENT RIGHT  
The defense claims Article 227 imposes "primary obligation[s]" on biological fathers that "create[] a right to reimbursement." This misreads both the statute and precedent. Article 227 states: "The father and mother are bound to maintain and bring up their children." It defines the *source* of the obligation but does not establish a right to recover payments from a third party. As *Smith* confirmed: "The obligation to support under Article 227 does not create a right to reimbursement without a prior disavowal." 583 So.2d at 592.  

Louisiana law prioritizes the stability of marital presumptions. The Supreme Court in *In re: J.R.* emphasized that "the legislature has established strict procedures to ensure finality in family relationships," and courts cannot "circumvent those statutory requirements based on equitable considerations." 639 So.2d at 285. Allowing reimbursement without disavowal would undermine this policy by permitting presumed fathers to bypass the disavowal process entirely.  

#### IV. *GLOVER* AND *SUCCESSION OF ROBINSON* DO NOT SUPPORT THE DEFENSE  
The defense cites *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d 115 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 1980), for the proposition that "reimbursement claims may proceed." But *Glover* is inapposite. In *Glover*, the presumed father *had already disavowed paternity* before seeking reimbursement from the biological father. The court explicitly stated: "Having successfully disavowed paternity, [the husband] is entitled to recover from the biological father." Id. at 117 (emphasis added). The defense omits this critical detail.  

Similarly, *Succession of Robinson*, 600 So.2d 133 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1992), reinforces the disavowal requirement. That case held that the disavowal procedure exists to "protect the integrity of the marital family unit" and "prevent fraudulent claims." Id. at 135. Far from "shielding biological fathers from responsibility," the procedure ensures that reimbursement claims are only pursued *after* legal paternity is properly terminated. The defense’s attempt to frame disavowal as a "shield" is contradicted by the very case it cites.  

#### V. NO "VIALE CLAIM" EXISTS WITHOUT DISAVOWAL  
The defense’s core error is conflating two distinct legal stages:  
1. **Establishing biological paternity** (which requires disavowal first), and  
2. **Seeking reimbursement** (which requires disavowal to create standing).  
J.M.Y. cannot leapfrog the disavowal process to seek genetic testing and reimbursement simultaneously. *In re: J.R.* makes this explicit: a presumed father must "clear his legal status" through disavowal *before* "pursuing third parties for support." 639 So.2d at 285.

**FINAL RULING**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE JUDGMENT OF THE TRIAL COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y. ("Plaintiff") from a judgment granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. ("Defendant") and dismissing Plaintiff’s suit for reimbursement of child support payments. After reviewing the facts provided, the rebuttals of counsel, and the applicable legal authorities, the Court affirms the judgment of the trial court.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The applicable standard of review for an exception of no cause of action is whether the petition states a cause of action upon which relief can be granted. In reviewing such an exception, the court must accept the well-pleaded factual allegations of the petition as true and determine whether the plaintiff is afforded any remedy under the law. *Martin v. Martin*, 535 So.2d 929, 931 (La. App. 2nd Cir. 1988). The exception of no right of action questions whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Id.*

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether a presumed father, who has not legally disavowed paternity, may maintain a direct action against an alleged biological father for genetic testing and reimbursement of child support payments. The Court finds that the trial court correctly dismissed the suit based on the exclusive nature of the disavowal procedure established by Louisiana law.

**A. The Mandate of *In re: J.R.* and the Exclusivity of Disavowal**

The defense argues that J.M.Y.'s suit is distinguishable from *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281 (La. 1994), because he seeks to establish biological paternity through genetic testing rather than merely seeking reimbursement while maintaining parental rights. The defense contends that J.M.Y. is attempting to "initiate" the disavowal process by securing the biological facts necessary to pursue it.

This argument is unpersuasive in light of the Louisiana Supreme Court's holding in *In re: J.R.*. The Court explicitly stated that "the disavowal proceeding is the exclusive means for a presumed father to challenge paternity." *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d at 285. By filing a suit for reimbursement and testing against a third party instead of a disavowal action, J.M.Y. has attempted to bypass the exclusive statutory procedure. The Supreme Court further held that "a presumed father who has not disavowed the child has no right to seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." *Id.* This prohibition applies regardless of whether the plaintiff characterizes the suit as a mechanism to gather evidence for a future disavowal. The law requires the legal status of paternity to be terminated *before* a right of action against the biological father accrues.

**B. The Scope of *Smith v. Smith* and the "Catch-22" Argument**

The defense attempts to distinguish *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), by noting that the plaintiff in *Smith* sought reimbursement from his former wife, whereas J.M.Y. seeks it from the alleged biological father. The defense argues that the language in *Smith* barring a suit against the biological father assumes the identity of the biological father is already established.

The Court finds this distinction to be without merit. The court in *Smith* unequivocally stated: "Without a prior disavowal action, the presumed father has no cause of action against the biological father for reimbursement." *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d at 592. This broad language establishes the absence of a cause of action against the biological father specifically, contingent upon the completion of a disavowal action. The defense's argument that J.M.Y. cannot disavow without first establishing paternity creates a procedural dilemma, but it does not create a cause of action where the law has expressly denied one.

The defense describes this dilemma as a "catch-22," arguing that J.M.Y. cannot disavow without evidence of another man's paternity, yet cannot obtain that evidence without the suit he filed. While the Court acknowledges the procedural difficulties highlighted by the defense, the remedy lies within the disavowal proceedings themselves, where the court has the authority to order genetic testing to determine paternity. *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d at 285. The legislature's establishment of strict time limits and procedures for disavowal reflects a policy choice favoring the stability of the marital presumption over the ease of reimbursement for the presumed father. It is not the role of the judiciary to circumvent these statutory requirements based on equitable considerations when the plaintiff has failed to utilize the exclusive mechanism provided by law.

**C. The Role of *Glover v. Glover* and Article 227**

The defense relies on *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d 115 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 1980), to argue that reimbursement is proper once biological paternity is established. However, as the prosecution correctly notes, in *Glover*, the presumed father had *already* successfully disavowed paternity before seeking reimbursement. The court's holding that the husband was "entitled to recover from the biological father" was predicated on the prior termination of his legal obligations. *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d at 117. This case supports the proposition that disavowal is a prerequisite to reimbursement, rather than supporting the defense's attempt to combine the steps.

Finally, the defense argues that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which imposes the obligation to support on parents, creates a primary obligation on the biological father that cannot be ignored. While Article 227 establishes the duty of support, it does not independently create a right of action for a presumed father to recover funds from a third party without first disavowing the child. As held in *Smith v. Smith*, the obligation to support under Article 227 does not create a right to reimbursement without a prior disavowal. 583 So.2d at 592.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y. failed to satisfy the mandatory condition precedent of disavowing paternity before seeking reimbursement or genetic testing from R.R. The "catch-22" asserted by the defense does not override the clear statutory and jurisprudential mandate that disavowal is the exclusive mechanism for a presumed father to challenge paternity and seek relief from support obligations.

**JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y. is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## MAY IT PLEASE THIS HONORABLE COURT:

The Defendant, R.R., respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the plaintiff's appeal and in support of the trial court's judgment, which was affirmed by this Court in its ruling dated [date of ruling]. The defense maintains that the plaintiff, J.M.Y., has failed to state a cause of action or demonstrate a right of action for reimbursement of child support payments and genetic testing against R.R., an alleged biological father.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts of this case establish that J.M.Y. was the presumed father of a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. in 1994. Following their divorce, J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. Rather than pursuing the exclusive statutory remedy provided by Louisiana law, J.M.Y. filed a direct action against R.R. alleging that R.R. was the biological father of the child. The plaintiff seeks reimbursement for past child support payments and genetic testing to establish biological paternity.

The trial court correctly granted exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action, and this Court properly affirmed that judgment. The defense submits that the plaintiff's claims are barred as a matter of law because he failed to utilize the exclusive disavowal procedure established by the Louisiana Legislature.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Exclusive Nature of the Disavowal Procedure

Louisiana law provides a clear and exclusive mechanism for a presumed father to challenge paternity. In *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281 (La. 1994), the Louisiana Supreme Court explicitly held that "the disavowal proceeding is the exclusive means for a presumed father to challenge paternity." *Id.* at 285. This holding reflects the legislature's policy choice to provide a structured, time-limited procedure for resolving questions of paternity while protecting the stability of family relationships.

The plaintiff in this case has not initiated a disavowal proceeding. Instead, he has attempted to circumvent the statutory framework by filing a direct action against R.R. seeking both genetic testing and reimbursement. This attempt must fail because the law does not permit a presumed father to bypass the exclusive disavowal procedure.

### B. The Plaintiff Has No Cause of Action Against the Biological Father

The Louisiana Second Circuit Court of Appeal has established that the exception of no cause of action tests whether the petition states a cause of action upon which relief can be granted. *Martin v. Martin*, 535 So.2d 929, 931 (La. App. 2nd Cir. 1988). In reviewing such an exception, the court must accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and determine whether the plaintiff is afforded any remedy under the law.

In *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), the court squarely addressed whether a presumed father may maintain an action against the biological father for reimbursement. The court held: "Without a prior disavowal action, the presumed father has no cause of action against the biological father for reimbursement." *Id.* at 592. This holding is directly applicable to the case at bar.

The plaintiff argues that his suit is distinguishable because he seeks genetic testing to establish the identity of the biological father. However, this argument misapprehends the nature of the exclusive disavowal procedure. The Louisiana Supreme Court in *In re: J.R.* made clear that genetic testing may be obtained within the disavowal proceeding itself. *Id.* at 285. The plaintiff need not know the identity of the biological father before initiating disavowal; the court has authority to order testing as part of that proceeding.

### C. The Plaintiff Has No Right of Action

The exception of no right of action questions whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Martin v. Martin*, 535 So.2d at 931. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 imposes the obligation to support on parents, but this obligation runs to the child, not to other potential obligors. The plaintiff cannot maintain an action against R.R. to recover funds he paid pursuant to a court order without first terminating his legal obligation through disavowal.

The plaintiff argues that he faces a "catch-22" because he cannot disavow without evidence of another man's paternity, yet cannot obtain that evidence without the suit he filed. This argument is unavailing. The disavowal statute empowers courts to order genetic testing to determine paternity as part of the disavowal process. The plaintiff has simply failed to utilize the proper legal mechanism.

### D. Glover v. Glover Does Not Support the Plaintiff's Position

The plaintiff relies on *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d 115 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 1980), for the proposition that reimbursement is proper once biological paternity is established. However, in *Glover*, the presumed father had *already successfully disavowed* the child before seeking reimbursement. *Id.* at 117. The court specifically held that the husband was "entitled to recover from the biological father" only after his legal obligations had been terminated through disavowal. *Id.* This case supports, rather than undermines, the requirement that disavowal must precede any recovery.

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

### A. The Plaintiff Cannot Characterize His Way Around the Statutory Mandate

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish his suit by characterizing it as an action to "initiate" the disavowal process through genetic testing. This characterization does not change the nature of the suit. The Louisiana Supreme Court in *In re: J.R.* explicitly held that "a presumed father who has not disavowed the child has no right to seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." 639 So.2d at 285. This prohibition applies regardless of the plaintiff's stated purpose.

The law does not permit a presumed father to circumvent the disavowal procedure by seeking evidence through a separate lawsuit. The exclusive mechanism must be used from the outset.

### B. The "Catch-22" Argument Fails as a Matter of Law

The plaintiff argues that he cannot disavow without first establishing the identity of the biological father. However, Louisiana law provides a clear path: a presumed father may file a disavowal action and seek genetic testing within that proceeding. *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d at 285. The disavowal statute does not require the plaintiff to "know" the biological father's identity beforehand.

The plaintiff's failure to utilize the exclusive disavowal mechanism is his own doing, not a defect in the law. The procedural difficulties he cites do not create a cause of action where the law has expressly denied one.

### C. Policy Considerations Do Not Override Statutory Requirements

The plaintiff urges this Court to consider policy implications, arguing that biological fathers should not escape responsibility. However, the Louisiana Supreme Court in *In re: J.R.* recognized that the disavowal procedure's strict time limits and requirements serve important policy goals, including ensuring finality in family relationships. 639 So.2d at 285.

The judiciary cannot create "backdoor" remedies to evade the statutory framework based on equitable considerations. If the plaintiff believes the statutory framework is unjust, his remedy lies with the legislature, not this Court.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant respectfully requests that this Honorable Court:

1. **AFFIRM** the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action;

2. **AFFIRM** the dismissal of the plaintiff's suit in its entirety;

3. **ASSESS** all costs of this appeal to the plaintiff, J.M.Y.; and

4. **GRANT** such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

The plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action and lacks a right of action against the defendant. Louisiana law requires that a presumed father must first terminate his legal status through the disavowal proceeding before seeking reimbursement or testing from a third party. The plaintiff's attempt to bypass this exclusive mechanism fails as a matter of law. The trial court correctly dismissed the case, and this Court correctly affirmed that judgment.

**FINAL RULING**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE JUDGMENT OF THE TRIAL COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y. ("Plaintiff") from a judgment granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. ("Defendant") and dismissing Plaintiff’s suit for reimbursement of child support payments. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by counsel for the Defendant, which address the exclusivity of the disavowal procedure and the absence of a cause of action absent such disavowal. For the reasons that follow, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The applicable standard of review for an exception of no cause of action is whether the petition states a cause of action upon which relief can be granted. In reviewing such an exception, the court must accept the well-pleaded factual allegations of the petition as true and determine whether the plaintiff is afforded any remedy under the law. *Martin v. Martin*, 535 So.2d 929, 931 (La. App. 2nd Cir. 1988). The exception of no right of action questions whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Id.*

**II. ANALYSIS**

The sole issue on appeal is whether a presumed father, who has not legally disavowed paternity, may maintain a direct action against an alleged biological father for genetic testing and reimbursement of child support payments. Based on the arguments presented, the Court finds that the trial court correctly dismissed the suit because the disavowal proceeding is the exclusive mechanism for challenging paternity.

**A. The Exclusivity of the Disavowal Proceeding**

The Defendant argues, and the Court agrees, that Plaintiff’s attempt to seek reimbursement and genetic testing directly from R.R. is an impermissible end-run around the statutory disavowal process. The Louisiana Supreme Court has explicitly held that "the disavowal proceeding is the exclusive means for a presumed father to challenge paternity." *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d 281, 285 (La. 1994). By filing suit against a third party rather than initiating a disavowal action, Plaintiff has ignored this statutory mandate. The law requires that the legal status of paternity be terminated through the proper channels before any right of action against a biological father can accrue.

**B. Absence of Cause of Action Without Prior Disavowal**

The Defendant correctly asserts that Plaintiff fails to state a cause of action because he has not satisfied the condition precedent of disavowing the child. In *Smith v. Smith*, the court unequivocally held: "Without a prior disavowal action, the presumed father has no cause of action against the biological father for reimbursement." *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d 590, 592 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991). This holding applies regardless of whether the biological father’s identity is known or unknown. The Plaintiff’s argument that he requires genetic testing to "initiate" the disavowal process is rejected, as the law provides for such testing *within* the disavowal proceeding itself, not as a precursor to it. *In re: J.R.*, 639 So.2d at 285.

**C. The "Catch-22" Argument and the Availability of Testing**

The Plaintiff suggests a procedural "catch-22," asserting that he cannot disavow without evidence of paternity, yet cannot obtain evidence without the present suit. This argument is unpersuasive. As noted in *In re: J.R.*, the disavowal proceeding is equipped with the authority to order genetic testing to determine paternity. *Id.* The Plaintiff’s failure to utilize the exclusive statutory mechanism to obtain this evidence does not create a right of action where none exists. The legislature’s policy choice favoring the stability of the marital presumption precludes the judiciary from creating "backdoor" remedies to circumvent the established procedure.

**D. Distinguishing *Glover v. Glover***

The Plaintiff’s reliance on *Glover v. Glover* is misplaced. In *Glover*, the presumed father had *already* successfully disavowed paternity before seeking reimbursement. *Glover v. Glover*, 392 So.2d 115, 117 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 1980). The court’s holding that the husband was entitled to recover was predicated entirely on the prior termination of his legal obligations. *Id.* Because J.M.Y. has not disavowed the child, *Glover* does not support his claim for reimbursement. Furthermore, Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which establishes the obligation of support, does not independently create a private right of action for a presumed father to recover funds from a third party without first disavowing the child. *Smith v. Smith*, 583 So.2d at 592.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y. is required by law to utilize the exclusive disavowal proceeding to challenge paternity before seeking any relief from the alleged biological father. The arguments presented in support of the judgment are consistent with binding precedent and statutory law.

**JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y. is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.
