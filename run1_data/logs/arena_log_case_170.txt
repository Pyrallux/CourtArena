May it please the Court. The evidence recovered from the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed because the officer's search violated the Fourth Amendment. The officer conducted an unconstitutional pat-down of the defendant without reasonable suspicion of weapons, rendering that initial intrusion unlawful. This constitutional violation tainted any subsequent search of the vehicle, as the consent obtained during the illegal detention was involuntary. The officer's actions exceeded the bounds of lawful police conduct under established precedent, and this Court must exclude the evidence.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a police officer may conduct a pat-down search for weapons only if there is reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. Reasonable suspicion requires specific, articulable facts that would lead a reasonable officer to believe the individual poses an immediate threat. Here, the officer had no such facts. The officer testified that he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4) and explicitly acknowledged that "aside from [his] not believing the story that he is lost, there's no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). The officer observed no furtive movements, no bulges indicating weapons, no visible weapons, and no signs of intoxication or drug use. The defendant was described as "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" with "no fear for [the officer's] safety" (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 34:8-35:13). These facts demonstrate a complete absence of reasonable suspicion to justify a pat-down. A pat-down based solely on a subjective belief that a story is "suspicious"—without any objective basis linking the defendant to weapons—is precisely the type of generalized suspicion *Terry* forbids.

Moreover, the officer illegally detained the defendant beyond the lawful purpose of the traffic stop. Once the officer issued the citation for driving while suspended and explained the license forfeiture procedures, the stop should have ended. As this Court held in *United States v. Sharpe*, 470 U.S. 675 (1985), "a traffic stop must last no longer than necessary to effectuate the purpose of the stop." Here, the officer had no reasonable suspicion to prolong the detention for further questioning. The officer's own testimony confirms that the only "suspicious" element was the defendant's story of being lost—a factor that, as the court noted, does not objectively support suspicion of criminal activity beyond the driving violation (Suppression Tr. 54:14). The officer conducted the pat-down during this illegal detention, transforming a routine traffic enforcement action into an unlawful seizure.

Any consent to search the vehicle obtained during this illegal detention was involuntary. In *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), the Supreme Court unequivocally ruled that "consent to search obtained during an illegal detention is not voluntary." Similarly, in *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), the Court held that "a person's consent to search must be voluntary and not the product of coercion." The officer's pat-down and continued detention without justification rendered the consent to search the vehicle involuntary as a matter of law. The officer had no probable cause to search the vehicle without consent, as he observed no contraband in plain view, smelled no marijuana, and detected no other indicia of criminal activity (Suppression Tr. 31:12-15, 38:14-17, 43:4-16). The search was therefore unconstitutional on multiple grounds.

For these reasons, the evidence discovered in the vehicle search must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. The officer's actions violated the Fourth Amendment, and this Court should exclude the evidence to uphold the constitutional rights of all citizens.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE MATTER OF THE SEARCH OF DEFENDANT'S VEHICLE

---

May it please the Court.

The Defense respectfully submits that the plaintiff's motion to suppress should be denied. The search of the defendant's vehicle was conducted in full compliance with the Fourth Amendment. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the officer's actions constituted an unconstitutional seizure or search. To the contrary, the evidence shows that: (1) the initial encounter was a valid investigatory stop supported by reasonable articulable suspicion; (2) the subsequent detention was lawfully extended based on the officer's legitimate concerns; and (3) the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. Each of these elements independently supports the admissibility of the evidence, and the Court should deny the suppression motion.

---

## I. THE INITIAL ENCOUNTER WAS A VALID INVESTIGATORY STOP SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution erroneously characterizes the initial encounter as an unlawful seizure. However, the facts establish that the officer had ample reasonable suspicion to initiate an investigatory stop of the defendant's vehicle.

Under **Terry v. Ohio**, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and its progeny, a police officer may stop a vehicle if he has reasonable articulable suspicion that criminal activity is occurring. The officer in this case observed the defendant driving in an unusual manner in the early morning hours—the vehicle was "cocked to the right" in the center lane, well past the marked stop line, and executed an erratic maneuver by turning right, then immediately pulling into the straight lane, proceeding through the intersection, and stopping on the shoulder. These observations, viewed through the lens of a trained officer with fifteen years of experience, provided specific and articulable facts supporting reasonable suspicion. See **United States v. Sokolow**, 490 U.S. 1, 9 (1989) (holding that reasonable suspicion may be based on "the whole picture").

The prosecution emphasizes that "the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." This fact is not determinative. When an officer activates emergency lights and approaches a vehicle, the encounter is evaluated under the standards of a investigatory stop. However, the defendant's voluntary pulling over does not negate the reasonable suspicion that justified the officer's actions. The officer's observations of erratic driving at 3:42 a.m. in an area near but across the border from his jurisdiction provided sufficient grounds for the investigatory approach.

---

## II. THE OFFICER POSSESSED PROBABLE CAUSE TO DETAIN THE DEFENDANT FOR THE TRAFFIC VIOLATION

Once the officer approached the defendant and obtained his driver's license, the subsequent license check revealed that the defendant's license was suspended. This information provided the officer with probable cause to believe that the defendant had committed the traffic offense of driving while license suspended, a criminal violation in Pennsylvania. At that moment, the officer lawfully detained the defendant for the purpose of issuing a citation. The detention was therefore supported by probable cause, not mere suspicion. See **Pennsylvania v. Labron**, 518 U.S. 938 (1996) (per curiam) (holding that probable cause for a traffic violation justifies a stop).

---

## III. THE EXTENSION OF THE ENCOUNTER WAS JUSTIFIED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION OF DRUG TRAFFICKING

The prosecution argues that the officer unlawfully prolonged the detention once the citation was issued. This argument fails because the officer possessed reasonable suspicion to believe that the defendant was engaged in drug trafficking.

The officer's suspicion was based on several factors. The defendant was driving at 3:42 a.m. from Philadelphia, a known source city for narcotics, toward Lansdale. The defendant claimed to be "lost" despite having been to Lansdale before and knowing that Route 309 connected Philadelphia to Lansdale. The officer testified that this inconsistency was "somewhat suspicious" (Suppression Tr. 16:12-16). The officer also noted that the defendant was in the area near Upper Gwynedd Township, which the officer's training and experience indicated was relevant to drug activity.

These factors, taken together, provided reasonable suspicion. Courts have consistently held that driving from source cities at unusual hours, combined with inconsistent or implausible explanations, can support a finding of reasonable suspicion for drug-related activity. See **United States v. Hensley**, 469 U.S. 221, 229 (1985) (holding that "reasonable suspicion may be based upon a police officer's experience and knowledge of the area").

The prosecution relies heavily on the trial court's observation that "subjectively, the officer was suspicious" and argues that this subjective suspicion was not objectively reasonable. However, the record demonstrates that the officer's suspicions were based on specific, articulable facts observed during the encounter, not mere hunches. The combination of the late hour, the origin from Philadelphia, the defendant's contradictory statements about being lost, and the officer's experience all contribute to an objective basis for reasonable suspicion.

---

## IV. THE DEFENDANT'S CONSENT TO SEARCH WAS VOLUNTARY

The prosecution's central argument is that the consent to search was tainted by an unlawful detention and is therefore involuntary under **Florida v. Royer**, 460 U.S. 491 (1983). This argument fails because the defendant was not in custody at the time he gave consent, and his consent was voluntarily given.

The facts establish that at the time of the search request, the defendant was informed that he was "free to go" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). He had already received his paperwork and was waiting for his girlfriend to arrive. The officer approached the defendant and asked if he "could ask him a question." The defendant agreed. The officer then made a statement about problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia and asked if the defendant "had anything that would concern me." The defendant responded by consenting to a search.

Under **Schneckloth v. Bustamonte**, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973), consent to search is voluntary when it is "freely and voluntarily given." The burden is on the prosecution to prove voluntariness by a preponderance of the evidence. Here, the prosecution has met this burden.

First, the defendant was not in custody. He was told he was free to go, was not handcuffed, and was awaiting a ride. The officer's weapon was not drawn. The encounter was conversational and cooperative. Under **United States v. Mendenhall**, 446 U.S. 544, 555 (1980), a person is seized only when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. A reasonable person in the defendant's position—having just been told he was free to go and waiting for a ride—would have felt free to decline the search request.

Second, the defendant gave his consent voluntarily. He "offered" to be searched, placing his hands on the side of the vehicle without prompting. The officer described the defendant as "agreeable." There is no evidence of coercion, duress, or intimidation. The defendant was fully informed that he could decline, and his willingness to cooperate was genuine.

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement about "people bringing things out of Philadelphia" constituted impermissible coercion. This argument is without merit. Officers are permitted to ask questions and make observations during consensual encounters. The statement was not a threat, nor did it rise to the level of coercion that would vitiate consent. See **Florida v. Bostick**, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991) (holding that police may approach individuals and ask questions without creating a seizure).

---

## V. EVEN IF THE DETENTION WAS UNLAWFUL, THE EVIDENCE IS ADMISSIBLE UNDER THE INDEPENDENT SOURCE DOCTRINE

Alternatively, even assuming arguendo that some portion of the detention was unlawful, the evidence discovered during the search is nonetheless admissible under the independent source doctrine. The officer would have discovered the evidence through lawful means—namely, the voluntary consent—regardless of any prior unlawful conduct. Under **Murray v. United States**, 487 U.S. 533, 537 (1988), evidence is admissible if it was discovered through an independent source untainted by constitutional violation.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the plaintiff's motion to suppress. The officer conducted a lawful investigatory stop based on reasonable suspicion, possessed probable cause to detain the defendant for the traffic violation, extended the encounter based on legitimate concerns of drug trafficking, and obtained voluntary consent to search the vehicle. The evidence discovered during the search is admissible, and the defendant is entitled to have his day in court.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the arguments presented and the factual findings provided, the motion to suppress is DENIED.**

**I. PROCEDURAL POSTURE**

This matter comes before the Court on a motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of the defendant's vehicle. The prosecution argues that the search violated the Fourth Amendment because it was predicated on an unconstitutional pat-down and an illegal detention unsupported by reasonable suspicion. The defense contends that the encounter was consensual, supported by reasonable suspicion and probable cause, and that the defendant voluntarily consented to the search. Based on the record provided, the Court finds that the seizure ended prior to the request for consent, rendering the subsequent search lawful.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Stop and Detention Were Lawful**

The Fourth Amendment allows an officer to conduct an investigatory stop if the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that a traffic violation has occurred. *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 663 (1979). The factual findings indicate that the officer observed the defendant's vehicle "cocked to the right" in the center lane, well past the marked stop line, at 3:42 A.M. These specific and articulable facts provided the officer with reasonable suspicion to initiate the encounter. Furthermore, upon checking the defendant's driver's license, the officer discovered it was suspended. This provided probable cause to detain the defendant for the traffic violation of driving while under suspension. *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938, 940 (1996) (per curiam).

**B. The Seizure Concluded Prior to the Request for Consent**

The prosecution argues that the officer unlawfully prolonged the detention beyond the scope of the traffic violation to investigate drug activity. It is well settled that a traffic stop "must last no longer than necessary to effectuate the purpose of the stop." *United States v. Sharpe*, 470 U.S. 675, 685 (1985). However, the record clearly establishes that the officer concluded the lawful purpose of the stop before requesting consent to search the vehicle.

The officer testified that after explaining the license forfeiture documents, he told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer then "started walking back to [his] car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). At this juncture, the detention related to the suspended license had terminated. A person is seized only when, "by means of physical force or a show of authority, [the officer] has in some way restrained the liberty of a citizen." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 19 n.16 (1968). Here, the officer's affirmative statement that the defendant was free to leave, coupled with his physical withdrawal, negated any continued show of authority sufficient to constitute a seizure.

**C. The Defendant Re-Engaged the Officer in a Consensual Encounter**

The critical fact distinguishing this case from those involving illegal detentions is that the defendant re-initiated the interaction. The factual findings state: "Defendant engaged me again in conversation. He asked me again what he is supposed to do with the license[-]seizure form." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). Only after this re-engagement did the officer return to the defendant and ask if he could ask a question.

Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), a person has not been seized unless "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." Given that the officer had just told the defendant he was free to go and had turned to leave, a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have understood that he was free to terminate the conversation or simply wait for his ride. The defendant's choice to ask further questions transformed the encounter into a consensual one, not requiring reasonable suspicion or probable cause. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (holding that police may approach citizens and ask questions without implicating the Fourth Amendment so long as a reasonable person would feel free to decline).

**D. The Consent to Search Was Voluntary**

The prosecution contends that any consent was involuntary because it was obtained during an illegal detention. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 501 (1983). Because the Court finds the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, this argument fails. Furthermore, the consent itself was voluntary. The defendant "offered" to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle and was described as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). The officer did not draw his weapon, use physical force, or threaten the defendant. Voluntariness is assessed by looking at the totality of the circumstances, and here, the defendant's cooperative demeanor and the lack of coercive police conduct support the conclusion that the consent was valid. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973).

**E. The Pat-Down Does Not Require Suppression of the Vehicle Evidence**

The prosecution correctly notes that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down for weapons under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), as he testified to having "no safety concerns." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). However, the evidence sought to be suppressed was found in the vehicle, not on the defendant's person. Even assuming the pat-down was unconstitutional, the evidence from the vehicle search is not the fruit of that illegality. The consent to search the vehicle was obtained after the "free to go" statement and the defendant's re-engagement with the officer. These intervening events broke the causal chain between any potential Fourth Amendment violation during the pat-down and the discovery of the evidence in the car. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975) (holding that mere temporal proximity does not automatically establish a causal connection for exclusion; courts must examine intervening circumstances).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The officer lawfully stopped the defendant based on observed traffic violations and lawfully detained him upon discovering the suspended license. The officer terminated this detention by informing the defendant he was free to go. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement of the officer constituted a consensual encounter during which the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. Therefore, the search did not violate the Fourth Amendment, and the motion to suppress is denied.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

**UPDATED RULING**

**Pursuant to the rebuttals presented and the factual findings provided, the motion to suppress is DENIED.**

**I. PROCEDURAL POSTURE**

This matter is before the Court on a motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of the defendant's vehicle. The defendant argues that the search violated the Fourth Amendment because it was the fruit of an unconstitutional detention and an unlawful pat-down. The prosecution contends that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for the pat-down and that the detention was unlawfully prolonged. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties and the factual findings, the Court finds that the detention lawfully concluded prior to the request for consent, and the defendant's subsequent actions constituted a consensual encounter.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Seizure Concluded Prior to the Re-engagement**

The prosecution argues that the seizure continued throughout the encounter because the officer's emergency lights remained activated and a backup officer was present, thereby negating the effect of the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go." The Court is not persuaded.

Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), a person is seized only when "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." Here, the officer explicitly informed the defendant that he was "free to go" and physically demonstrated this by "started walking back to [his] car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This affirmative communication and physical withdrawal are objective manifestations that the detention had ended.

The prosecution relies on *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 436 (1991), to argue that the presence of multiple officers and flashing lights created a coercive environment. However, *Bostick* involved a defendant confined on a bus who was not free to walk away. Here, the defendant was on a public road shoulder with his own vehicle and a ride en route. Furthermore, in *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the presence of multiple officers and the display of a badge, without more, does not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure. The officer's testimony established that the lights were for safety, and the backup officer was standard protocol. Given the officer's clear statement that the defendant was free to leave and his physical departure, a reasonable person would have understood the detention to be over.

**B. The Defendant Re-Initiated the Encounter**

The critical fact that breaks the causal chain of any potential illegality is that the defendant re-engaged the officer. The factual findings state: "Defendant engaged me again in conversation. He asked me again what he is supposed to do with the license[-]seizure form." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). Only after this re-engagement did the officer return to the defendant to answer the question and subsequently ask for consent to search.

This voluntary re-engagement distinguishes this case from those involving prolonged detention. The defendant was not compelled to speak; he chose to ask another question after being told he was free to go. This interaction transformed the encounter into a consensual one, not requiring reasonable suspicion or probable cause. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437.

**C. The Pat-Down Does Not Taint the Vehicle Search**

The prosecution correctly argues that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), as the officer testified to having "no safety concerns." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). However, the exclusionary rule does not require suppression of the vehicle evidence in this instance.

The vehicle search was not the direct result of the pat-down. The officer searched the car based on the defendant's consent, which was offered after the defendant re-engaged the officer. Under *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975), evidence obtained after an illegal seizure is not admissible unless "intervening circumstances" break the causal chain. Here, the intervening circumstances are the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go," the officer's withdrawal, and the defendant's voluntary re-engagement of the officer. These acts broke the connection between the initial traffic stop (and any subsequent pat-down) and the consent to search the vehicle.

**D. The Consent Was Voluntary**

The prosecution argues that the consent was involuntary because the defendant was not free to decline. However, because the Court finds the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, the defendant was free to decline. The defendant "offered" to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle and was described as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). The officer did not draw his weapon or use physical force.

Voluntariness is assessed by looking at the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973). The defendant's cooperative demeanor, the lack of coercion, and the fact that he initiated the conversation that led to the search request all support the conclusion that the consent was voluntary. The prosecution's argument that the defendant's cooperation is irrelevant ignores the reality that a defendant can validly consent to a search during a consensual encounter.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The officer lawfully stopped the defendant based on observed traffic violations and lawfully detained him upon discovering the suspended license. The officer terminated this detention by informing the defendant he was free to go and walking away. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement of the officer constituted a consensual encounter during which the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. Even if the pat-down was unconstitutional, it does not taint the vehicle search due to the intervening circumstances of the terminated detention and the defendant's voluntary re-initiation of contact. Therefore, the search did not violate the Fourth Amendment, and the motion to suppress is denied.

The defendant argues that the search violated the Fourth Amendment because it was the fruit of an unconstitutional detention and an unlawful pat-down. The prosecution contends that the detention was unlawfully prolonged and that the pat-down lacked reasonable suspicion. After reviewing the closing arguments and the factual findings, the Court finds that the detention lawfully concluded prior to the request for consent, and the defendant's subsequent actions constituted a consensual encounter untainted by any prior illegality.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Seizure Lawfully Concluded**

The prosecution argues that the detention never lawfully concluded because the officer's emergency lights remained activated and a backup officer was present, creating a coercive environment that negated the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go." The Court is not persuaded.

Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), a person is seized only when "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." Here, the officer explicitly informed the defendant that he was "free to go" and physically demonstrated this by "started walking back to [his] car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This affirmative communication and physical withdrawal are objective manifestations that the detention had ended.

The prosecution relies on *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 436 (1991), to argue that the presence of multiple officers and flashing lights created a coercive environment. However, *Bostick* involved a defendant confined on a bus who was not free to walk away. Here, the defendant was on a public road shoulder with his own vehicle and a ride en route. Furthermore, in *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the presence of multiple officers and the display of a badge, without more, does not convert a consensual encounter into a seizure. The officer's testimony established that the lights were for safety, and the backup officer was standard protocol. Given the officer's clear statement that the defendant was free to leave and his physical departure, a reasonable person would have understood the detention to be over.

**B. The Defendant Voluntarily Re-Initiated the Encounter**

The critical fact that breaks the causal chain of any potential illegality is that the defendant re-engaged the officer. The factual findings state: "Defendant engaged me again in conversation. He asked me again what he is supposed to do with the license[-]seizure form." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). Only after this re-engagement did the officer return to the defendant to answer the question and subsequently ask for consent to search.

This voluntary re-engagement distinguishes this case from those involving prolonged detention. The defendant was not compelled to speak; he chose to ask another question after being told he was free to go. This interaction transformed the encounter into a consensual one, not requiring reasonable suspicion or probable cause. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437.

**C. The Pat-Down Does Not Taint the Vehicle Search**

The prosecution correctly argues that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), as the officer testified to having "no safety concerns." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). However, the exclusionary rule does not require suppression of the vehicle evidence in this instance.

The vehicle search was not the direct result of the pat-down. The officer searched the car based on the defendant's consent, which was offered after the defendant re-engaged the officer. Under *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975), evidence obtained after an illegal seizure is not admissible unless "intervening circumstances" break the causal chain. Here, the intervening circumstances are the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go," the officer's withdrawal, and the defendant's voluntary re-engagement of the officer. These acts broke the connection between the initial traffic stop (and any subsequent pat-down) and the consent to search the vehicle.

**D. The Consent Was Voluntary**

The prosecution argues that the consent was involuntary because the defendant was not free to decline. However, because the Court finds the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, the defendant was free to decline. The defendant "offered" to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle and was described as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). The officer did not draw his weapon or use physical force.

Voluntariness is assessed by looking at the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973). The defendant's cooperative demeanor, the lack of coercion, and the fact that he initiated the conversation that led to the search request all support the conclusion that the consent was voluntary. The prosecution's argument that the defendant's cooperation is irrelevant ignores the reality that a defendant can validly consent to a search during a consensual encounter.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The officer lawfully stopped the defendant based on observed traffic violations and lawfully detained him upon discovering the suspended license. The officer terminated this detention by informing the defendant he was free to go and walking away. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement of the officer constituted a consensual encounter during which the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. Even if the pat-down was unconstitutional, it does not taint the vehicle search due to the intervening circumstances of the terminated detention and the defendant's voluntary re-initiation of contact. Therefore, the search did not violate the Fourth Amendment, and the motion to suppress is denied.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
