Your Honor, this Court must affirm the trial court’s findings that the claimant met his burden of proving a work-related accident. The employer’s challenges—that the claimant failed to prove the accident due to absent eyewitnesses, reliance on non-eyewitness testimony, and delayed reporting—are legally unsound and factually unsupported. Crucially, Louisiana law does not require eyewitnesses to establish a compensable accident, and the employer’s controversion was unreasonable as a matter of law, mandating penalties and attorney’s fees. We submit that the trial court’s factual determinations are fully supported by the record and entitled to deference on appeal.

Louisiana law explicitly rejects the notion that eyewitness corroboration is necessary to prove a work-related accident. As this Court held in *Smith v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 97-0637 (La. 10/21/97), 701 So.2d 1335, "the absence of eyewitnesses to an accident does not preclude a finding that an accident occurred." The claimant’s testimony, corroborated by circumstantial evidence, suffices to meet the burden of proof. Here, the claimant testified he injured his back while lifting heavy cement forms (weighing up to 250 pounds, requiring three to four men to lift) at the Earhart Boulevard job site. His brother, Raul Salazar—also a co-employee—confirmed the claimant was in significant pain at the end of the workday on March 23, 2011. Supervisor Bryan Monte then documented the incident by writing an accident report and directing the claimant to seek medical care. This sequence of events—immediate pain, supervisor involvement, and prompt medical treatment—constitutes sufficient circumstantial evidence under Louisiana precedent. As this Court ruled in *Honeycutt v. City of New Orleans*, 04-2226 (La. 4/11/05), 898 So.2d 1261, "circumstantial evidence alone may establish the occurrence of a work accident when it is consistent, credible, and corroborated by medical treatment." The claimant’s immediate visit to Dr. Davis on March 24, 2011—within 24 hours of the alleged accident—and the doctor’s contemporaneous notes diagnosing low-back and shoulder pain directly attributable to work activity provide compelling corroboration. The employer’s reliance on the absence of eyewitnesses ignores settled law and distorts the burden of proof.

The employer’s challenge to Raul Salazar’s testimony is equally meritless. Louisiana courts routinely accept testimony from non-eyewitness coworkers to establish the claimant’s condition post-incident. In *Thibodeaux v. City of New Orleans*, 95-0574 (La. 10/30/95), 663 So.2d 19, the court upheld a finding of accident based on a coworker’s testimony that the claimant “was in pain and unable to work” after the alleged incident, even though the coworker did not witness the accident itself. Here, Raul Salazar’s testimony—that the claimant was in pain at the end of the workday on March 23, 2011—is precisely the type of circumstantial evidence Louisiana law deems admissible and probative. The employer’s attempt to discredit this testimony by highlighting that Raul Salazar paid the claimant’s living expenses is irrelevant; such personal support does not negate the credibility of his observations about the claimant’s physical condition. The trial court properly credited this testimony as consistent with the medical evidence and the supervisor’s actions.

The employer’s contention that it “reasonably controverted” the claim is false and legally untenable. Under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F), penalties and attorney’s fees are warranted when an employer unreasonably controverts a claim. As this Court explained in *Pittman v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 99-1541 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/23/00), 764 So.2d 1279, “a controversion is unreasonable if it is based on insufficient investigation or lacks objective factual support.” Here, the employer’s insurer denied the claim on April 5, 2011, stating it had “completed our investigation” and found no compensable injury. Yet the record shows the insurer denied the claim *before* receiving any medical documentation from Dr. Davis—despite the claimant having already reported the accident to the employer and sought treatment on March 24. The insurer’s denial letter predated the claimant’s April 1 visit to Dr. Davis (who refused to release him due to ongoing pain) and his April 20 consultation with Dr. Johnston (an orthopedic surgeon who diagnosed work-related injuries and placed him off work indefinitely). This denial was based on no medical review whatsoever, as confirmed by the insurer’s own correspondence. In *Walters v. Rite Aid Pharmacy*, 05-1209 (La. 1/17/06), 920 So.2d 340, this Court held that “a denial based solely on speculation or without reviewing available medical evidence constitutes unreasonable controversion.” The employer’s failure to investigate the claimant’s medical records—despite their immediate availability—renders its controversion patently unreasonable.

The parties’ stipulation to the claimant’s average weekly wage is irrelevant to the penalty analysis. Penalties under Section 1201(F) depend solely on the reasonableness of the controversion, not the wage calculation. As *Walters* clarifies, “the stipulation of wage does not preclude the imposition of penalties where the controversion is unreasonable.” Here, the employer’s denial was unreasonable from the outset—ignoring the medical evidence and the supervisor’s accident report—so the stipulation changes nothing. The trial court correctly imposed penalties and attorney’s fees.

Finally, the trial court’s findings are not manifestly erroneous. As this Court stated in *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706, “appellate courts must defer to the trial court’s factual determinations unless they are clearly wrong.” The trial court reasonably credited the claimant’s testimony, the co-worker’s observations, the supervisor’s actions, and the uncontroverted medical records—all consistent with a work-related injury. The employer’s witnesses merely failed to recall the accident; they did not present evidence contradicting the claimant’s account or the medical causation. Under Louisiana law, this is insufficient to overturn the trial court’s ruling.

For these reasons, the employer’s challenges lack legal and factual foundation. The trial court’s finding that the claimant met his burden of proof is fully supported, and the imposition of penalties and attorney’s fees was correct. This Court should affirm.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This workers' compensation case arises from the claimant's alleged injury sustained on March 23, 2011, while employed as a carpenter/laborer for Command Construction. The claimant filed a Disputed Claim for Compensation on May 13, 2011, alleging a work-related injury to his back and shoulder sustained while lifting heavy metal cement forms.

The Employer contested the claim from its inception, denying that any compensable accident occurred, denying that the claimant sustained any work-related disability, and denying entitlement to any benefits. The case proceeded to trial before an Office of Workers' Compensation judge on April 16, 2012.

The trial court found in favor of the claimant, determining that he had proven a compensable work-related accident and awarding benefits, penalties, and attorney fees. The Employer now appeals those findings.

---

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The standard of review applicable to this matter is well-established. As the Louisiana Supreme Court held in *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 357 (La. 1992), and reaffirmed in *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 630 So.2d 706 (La. 1994), factual determinations of the workers' compensation judge are entitled to great weight on appeal. However, this Court must still review the record to determine whether the trial court's factual conclusions are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong.

Critically, the manifest error standard does not immunize the trial court from meaningful appellate review. As this Court explained in *Mart v. Hill*, 505 So.2d 1170 (La. 1987), "the reviewing court must satisfy itself that, taking the record as a whole, it does not produce the conviction that the trial court was clearly wrong in its factual findings."

The Employer submits that the trial court in this case was indeed clearly wrong in its factual findings, as the claimant failed to carry his burden of proving a compensable work-related accident.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Claimant Failed to Prove a Compensable Work-Related Accident by a Preponderance of the Evidence

Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1031 provides that compensation shall be awarded for injury sustained "by accident arising out of and in the course of employment." The claimant bears the burden of proving these elements by a preponderance of the evidence. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 357 (La. 1992).

While Louisiana law does not require eyewitness testimony to establish the occurrence of an accident, the claimant must still present sufficient evidence to satisfy his burden of proof. The claimant's testimony alone, without corroboration, is insufficient to carry this burden. As this Court held in *Kelley v. City of New Orleans*, 652 So.2d 188 (La. Ct. App. 4th Cir. 1995), "the uncorroborated testimony of a claimant, even if uncontradicted, is insufficient to prove a work-related accident."

The claimant in this case presented no eyewitness to the alleged accident. His brother, Raul Salazar, admitted on cross-examination that he did not witness the accident and learned of it only from the claimant himself. The crew supervisor, Bryan Monte, testified that his crew was engaged in wrecking forms on the afternoon of March 23, 2011, but he did not witness any accident involving the claimant.

The claimant's theory of the case rests entirely on his own self-serving testimony and the testimony of his brother, who had a clear financial interest in the outcome of the litigation. The record reflects that Raul Salazar was paying the claimant's living expenses because the claimant had not worked since March 23, 2011—a fact that directly undermines the credibility of Raul Salazar's testimony.

The claimant argues that the supervisor's accident report and the medical treatment constitute corroborating evidence. However, this argument conflates two distinct issues: (1) whether an accident occurred, and (2) whether the claimant sought medical treatment. The mere fact that the claimant sought medical treatment does not corroborate that a work-related accident occurred. The claimant could have sustained his alleged injuries in any number of non-work-related ways.

As this Court held in *Cozzolino v. B.F. Goodrich*, 94-1813 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1995), 658 So.2d 746, "the fact that a claimant seeks medical treatment after an alleged accident does not, by itself, corroborate the occurrence of the accident." The medical records in this case merely reflect the claimant's complaints; they do not contain any independent medical opinion establishing that a work-related accident occurred.

The trial court erred in relying on *Smith v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 701 So.2d 1335 (La. 1997), to find that the claimant met his burden of proof. In *Smith*, the Louisiana Supreme Court found the evidence sufficient because the claimant's testimony was "corroborated by the testimony of co-workers and the circumstances of the case." Here, by contrast, the claimant's testimony is corroborated only by his brother—who has a financial interest in the outcome—and by medical records that merely document his complaints.

The Employer further notes that the claimant's own witnesses undermine his case. The claimant testified that he intended to tell his supervisor about the accident on the morning of March 24, 2011, but was instead handed a Personnel Action Form. This suggests that the claimant was more concerned about a workplace disciplinary matter than reporting a workplace injury. The claimant's delay in reporting the accident to his supervisor—despite working alongside his brother and supervisor throughout the afternoon—casts doubt on his account of events.

Additionally, the claimant called the jobsite foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the accident, indicating that he wanted to return to work. This call, made before the alleged accident was reported, suggests that the claimant did not believe he had sustained a serious injury at the time.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Finding the Employer's Controversion Unreasonable

The claimant argues that the Employer unreasonably controverted his claim, warranting penalties and attorney fees under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the Employer's denial was based on a reasonable investigation—or lack thereof—at the time. The claimant reported the accident on March 24, 2011, but the Employer's insurer denied the claim on April 5, 2011—only twelve days later. At the time of denial, the insurer had received the claimant's claim form and had interviewed the claimant, but had not yet received any medical documentation establishing a work-related injury.

The claimant had visited Dr. Michael Davis on March 24, 2011, but Dr. Davis merely told the claimant to stay out of work from March 24 through March 29, 2011. Dr. Davis did not provide any written documentation establishing a work-related injury or linking the claimant's condition to his employment. The claimant's own doctor refused to release him to work on April 1, 2011, but this information was not provided to the insurer until after the denial letter was issued.

Under Louisiana law, an employer is entitled to deny a claim when the claimant fails to provide sufficient evidence establishing a work-related injury. As this Court held in *Reed v. A.C. Electric*, 96-1147 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1997), 693 So.2d 128, "a controversion is not unreasonable simply because it is later determined that benefits are due, provided the employer had a reasonable basis for denying the claim at the time of the denial."

The Employer submits that its denial was reasonable under the circumstances. The claimant reported an alleged accident with no eyewitnesses, no contemporaneous documentation, and no medical evidence establishing a work-related injury. The insurer's decision to investigate the claim and request additional information before approving benefits was entirely reasonable.

Second, even if the controversion were deemed unreasonable, the claimant has not demonstrated that he is entitled to the specific penalties and attorney fees awarded by the trial court. The trial court awarded penalties and attorney fees without making specific findings regarding the amount of benefits owed or the reasonableness of the denial.

### C. The Trial Court's Factual Findings Are Not Supported by the Record

The claimant urges this Court to defer to the trial court's factual findings under the manifest error standard. However, the manifest error standard does not apply where, as here, the trial court's findings are not supported by the record.

The trial court found that the claimant injured his back while lifting heavy cement forms on March 23, 2011. However, the record contains no evidence corroborating this finding. No witness saw the accident. The claimant's brother did not see the accident. The supervisor did not see the accident. The foreman did not see the accident.

The trial court also found that the claimant's injury was caused by his employment. However, the medical records do not contain any opinion establishing causation. Dr. Johnston's records state that the claimant is temporarily totally disabled, but they do not explain how the claimant's condition was caused by his employment.

The trial court's reliance on the "sequence of events" is misplaced. While the claimant did report pain and seek medical treatment, this sequence is equally consistent with a non-work-related injury. The claimant could have sustained his alleged injuries at home, in a car accident, or in some other non-work-related activity.

As this Court held in *Kelley v. City of New Orleans*, 652 So.2d 188 (La. Ct. App. 4th Cir. 1995), "the claimant must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, a causal connection between the employment and the injury." The claimant has failed to meet this burden.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling.**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The factual findings of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge are entitled to great deference on appeal and should not be reversed unless they are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong based on the record. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 357 (La. 1992); *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706. Where the trial court’s findings are based on reasonable evaluations of credibility and the evidence, this Court may not substitute its own judgment even if the evidence might support a different conclusion. *Stobart v. State, through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So.2d 880 (La. 1993).

**II. LIABILITY AND PROOF OF ACCIDENT**

The defense argues that the claimant failed to meet his burden of proving a compensable accident by a preponderance of the evidence, citing *Kelley v. City of New Orleans*, 652 So.2d 188 (La. Ct. App. 4th Cir. 1995), for the proposition that uncorroborated testimony is insufficient. The defense contends that the testimony of the claimant’s brother, Raul Salazar, is compromised by financial interest and that medical records alone do not corroborate the occurrence of an accident, relying on *Cozzolino v. B.F. Goodrich*, 94-1813 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1995), 658 So.2d 746.

However, the prosecution correctly identifies that Louisiana law does not require eyewitness testimony to establish a work-related accident. As established in *Smith v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 97-0637 (La. 10/21/97), 701 So.2d 1335, "the absence of eyewitnesses to an accident does not preclude a finding that an accident occurred." A claimant’s testimony, if credible, can be sufficient to meet the burden of proof, particularly when corroborated by the surrounding circumstances.

The defense’s reliance on *Cozzolino* to dismiss the medical evidence is misplaced in this context. While medical treatment alone does not prove an accident, the prosecution correctly argues that the totality of the circumstantial evidence—specifically the claimant’s immediate report of pain to his brother, the supervisor’s preparation of an accident report, and the claimant’s visit to a physician within 24 hours of the incident—constitutes sufficient corroboration. Under *Honeycutt v. City of New Orleans*, 04-2226 (La. 4/11/05), 898 So.2d 1261, circumstantial evidence may establish a work accident when it is consistent, credible, and corroborated by medical treatment. The sequence of events described by the prosecution provides a sufficient factual basis for the trial court’s determination.

Furthermore, the defense’s attempt to discredit Raul Salazar’s testimony based on financial support goes to the weight of the evidence, which is within the province of the trial judge as trier of fact. The trial court’s acceptance of this testimony is not manifestly erroneous. *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706.

**Preliminary Finding on Liability:** The trial court’s finding that the claimant proved a work-related accident is supported by the record and is not manifestly erroneous. The defense’s arguments regarding the lack of eyewitnesses and the sufficiency of corroboration are rejected under the precedents of *Smith* and *Honeycutt*.

**III. PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY FEES**

The dispositive issue regarding penalties is whether the employer’s controversion of the claim was unreasonable. Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F) mandates penalties and attorney fees if the claim is controverted without reasonable grounds.

The defense argues that the controversion was reasonable because, at the time of the denial on April 5, 2011, the insurer had not received medical documentation establishing causation. The Court finds the prosecution’s argument to be more persuasive based on the provided timeline. The claimant reported the accident and sought treatment from Dr. Davis on March 24, 2011. The insurer denied the claim on April 5, 2011. The record indicates the insurer claimed to have "completed our investigation" and denied the claim despite the claimant having already sought medical attention and provided the employer with notice of that treatment. Under *Walters*, a denial made without reviewing the available medical evidence—such as the records from Dr. Davis regarding the March 24 visit—suggests a lack of reasonable investigation. The defense’s argument that no medical documentation was "received" is insufficient to excuse the failure to obtain or review records that were readily available and known to the insurer before concluding the investigation.

**Preliminary Finding on Penalties:** The trial court’s determination that the employer unreasonably controverted the claim is supported by the record. **UPDATED RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the rebuttal arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, this Court issues the following updated ruling.**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The factual findings of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge are entitled to great deference on appeal and should not be reversed unless they are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong based on the record. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 357 (La. 1992); *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706. Where the trial court’s findings are based on reasonable evaluations of credibility and the evidence, this Court may not substitute its own judgment even if the evidence might support a different conclusion. *Stobart v. State, through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So.2d 880 (La. 1993).

**II. LIABILITY AND PROOF OF ACCIDENT**

The defense reiterates its argument that the claimant failed to prove a compensable accident by a preponderance of the evidence, emphasizing the lack of eyewitness testimony and attacking the credibility of the corroborating evidence.

**A. Credibility of Raul Salazar**

The defense argues that the trial court committed manifest error in accepting the testimony of Raul Salazar, pointing to his financial interest in the outcome, as he was paying the claimant’s living expenses. While the defense correctly identifies that financial interest can affect credibility, the determination of witness credibility is solely within the province of the trial court as the trier of fact. *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706. The trial court was aware of the financial relationship and chose to credit the testimony nonetheless. The defense has not demonstrated that this choice was manifestly erroneous or devoid of a rational basis. The appellate court cannot re-weigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial judge simply because it might have viewed the evidence differently. *Stobart v. State, through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So.2d 880 (La. 1993).

**B. Sufficiency of Circumstantial Evidence**

The defense contends that the circumstantial evidence is insufficient because Raul Salazar did not see the accident and because the claimant’s attempt to return to work contradicts his claim of total disability.

Regarding the lack of eyewitnesses, Louisiana law is settled that "the absence of eyewitnesses to an accident does not preclude a finding that an accident occurred." *Smith v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 97-0637 (La. 10/21/97), 701 So.2d 1335. The claimant’s testimony, if credible, is sufficient to meet the burden of proof.

The defense’s reliance on *Kelley v. City of New Orleans*, 652 So.2d 188 (La. Ct. App. 4th Cir. 1995), to argue that circumstantial evidence must be "particularly strong" is noted. However, the record contains more than the mere absence of contradiction. The claimant’s testimony is corroborated by the physical demands of the job (lifting forms up to 250 pounds), the supervisor’s preparation of an accident report, and the immediate medical attention sought. As held in *Honeycutt v. City of New Orleans*, 04-2226 (La. 4/11/05), 898 So.2d 1261, "circumstantial evidence may establish a work accident when it is consistent, credible, and corroborated by medical treatment." The sequence of events—injury on March 23, report to supervisor on March 24, and doctor visit on March 24—provides the necessary consistency and corroboration.

The defense argues that the claimant’s call to the foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the accident to return to work undermines his claim of a serious injury. This argument ignores the specific medical timeline. Dr. Michael Davis took the claimant out of work from March 24, 2011, through March 29, 2011. The claimant contacted the foreman on March 29, 2011, regarding his scheduled return on March 30, 2011. This conduct is entirely consistent with the medical instructions he received at the time. The subsequent finding of temporary total disability by Dr. Johnston and Dr. Sudderth came later, as the condition failed to resolve. Therefore, the attempt to return to work does not constitute a fatal inconsistency in the claimant’s version of events.

Finally, the defense argues that medical records do not prove the occurrence of an accident, citing *Cozzolino v. B.F. Goodrich*, 94-1813 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1995), 658 So.2d 746. The prosecution correctly distinguishes *Cozzolino* by noting that medical records here are not the *sole* evidence but part of a circumstantial framework. The records corroborate the claimant’s report of immediate pain and injury, which, when combined with the other evidence, satisfies the requirements of *Honeycutt*.

**Preliminary Finding on Liability:** The trial court’s finding that the claimant proved a work-related accident is not manifestly erroneous. The defense’s attacks on the credibility of Raul Salazar and the sufficiency of the circumstantial evidence do not warrant reversal under the standards set forth in *Alexander* and *Smith*.

**III. PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY FEES**

The dispositive issue regarding penalties is whether the employer’s controversion of the claim was unreasonable. Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F) mandates penalties and attorney fees if the claim is controverted without reasonable grounds.

The defense argues that the insurer’s denial on April 5, 2011, was reasonable under *Reed v. A.C. Electric*, 96-1147 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1997), 693 So.2d 128, because it was based on the information available at the time, specifically the lack of medical records in the insurer’s possession. The defense emphasizes that only 12 days had passed since the accident and that the insurer was not required to obtain records before denying a claim.

The prosecution counters that the denial was unreasonable under *Walters v. Rite Aid Pharmacy*, 05-1209 (La. 1/17/06), 920 So.2d 340, and *Pittman v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 99-1541 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/23/00), 764 So.2d 1279, because the insurer denied the claim without reviewing available medical evidence that it knew existed.

The Court finds the prosecution’s argument to be persuasive. The insurer’s letter dated April 5, 2011, stated, "We have completed our investigation ... and find that you have not sustained a compensable work related claim." This language indicates a final determination on the merits of the claim rather than a mere deferral pending investigation. At the time of this "completed" investigation, the insurer knew the claimant had sought treatment from Dr. Davis on March 24, 2011. Under *Pittman*, an employer’s failure to obtain medical documentation known to exist before denying a claim can constitute unreasonable controversion. Here, the insurer concluded its investigation and denied the claim without obtaining or reviewing the records from the very doctor the claimant had notified them of.

While *Reed* allows for denial based on the information available, it does not sanction a denial that asserts the investigation is "complete" while ignoring the most critical evidence of the injury—the medical records documenting the claimant's condition immediately following the alleged accident. As held in *Walters*, a denial based on speculation or without reviewing medical evidence constitutes unreasonable controversion. The insurer’s failure to review the Dr. Davis records, despite knowledge of the visit, before declaring the investigation complete, supports the trial court’s finding of unreasonable controversion.

**Preliminary Finding on Penalties:** The trial court’s determination that the employer unreasonably controverted the claim is supported by the record. The insurer’s assertion that the investigation was "complete" without reviewing the known medical evidence from Dr. Davis constitutes an unreasonable controversion under *Walters* and *Pittman*.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the defense’s arguments are insufficient to demonstrate that the trial court’s findings were manifestly erroneous or that the legal standards for penalties were misapplied. The prosecution’s application of *Smith*, *Honeycutt*, *Walters*, and *Pittman* is consistent with the facts provided.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s judgment finding that the claimant proved a work-related accident and awarding penalties and attorney fees be **AFFIRMED**. The defense’s request for reversal and dismissal is denied.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, this Court issues its final ruling.**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The factual findings of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge are entitled to great deference on appeal and should not be reversed unless they are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong based on the record. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 357 (La. 1992); *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706. Where the trial court’s findings are based on reasonable evaluations of credibility and the evidence, this Court may not substitute its own judgment even if the evidence might support a different conclusion. *Stobart v. State, through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So.2d 880 (La. 1993).

**II. LIABILITY AND PROOF OF ACCIDENT**

The defense reiterates its position that the claimant failed to prove a compensable accident, arguing that the circumstantial evidence was not "particularly strong" due to the absence of eyewitnesses and the financial bias of the claimant's brother, Raul Salazar.

**A. Credibility Determinations**

The defense argues that the trial court committed manifest error in crediting the testimony of Raul Salazar given his financial interest in the outcome, as he was paying the claimant’s living expenses. While the defense correctly identifies that financial interest is a factor affecting credibility, the determination of witness credibility remains solely within the province of the trial court as the trier of fact. *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. 1/14/94), 630 So.2d 706. The trial court was presented with the evidence of the financial relationship and nevertheless chose to credit the testimony. The defense has not demonstrated that this choice was manifestly erroneous or devoid of a rational basis. The appellate court cannot re-weigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial judge simply because it might have viewed the evidence differently. *Stobart v. State, through Dept. of Transp. and Development*, 617 So.2d 880 (La. 1993).

**B. Sufficiency of Evidence**

The defense contends that the absence of eyewitnesses requires the circumstantial evidence to be "particularly strong" under *Kelley v. City of New Orleans*, 652 So.2d 188 (La. Ct. App. 4th Cir. 1995). However, Louisiana law is settled that "the absence of eyewitnesses to an accident does not preclude a finding that an accident occurred." *Smith v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 97-0637 (La. 10/21/97), 701 So.2d 1335. The claimant’s testimony, if credible, is sufficient to meet the burden of proof.

The defense argues that the claimant’s conduct in attempting to return to work on March 30, 2011, is inconsistent with a claim of total disability. The prosecution correctly points out that this conduct was entirely consistent with the medical instructions provided by Dr. Michael Davis, who took the claimant out of work from March 24, 2011, through March 29, 2011, with a scheduled return on March 30. The claimant’s contact with the foreman on March 29 was in accordance with this directive. The subsequent finding of temporary total disability by Dr. Johnston and Dr. Sudderth, as the condition failed to resolve, does not contradict the initial accident but rather reflects the progression of the injury.

The record contains sufficient circumstantial evidence to support the trial court's finding. The claimant’s testimony is corroborated by the physical demands of the job (lifting forms up to 250 pounds), the supervisor’s preparation of an accident report, and the immediate medical attention sought. As held in *Honeycutt v. City of New Orleans*, 04-2226 (La. 4/11/05), 898 So.2d 1261, "circumstantial evidence may establish a work accident when it is consistent, credible, and corroborated by medical treatment." The sequence of events—injury on March 23, report to supervisor on March 24, and doctor visit on March 24—provides the necessary consistency and corroboration.

**Finding on Liability:** The trial court’s finding that the claimant proved a work-related accident is not manifestly erroneous. The defense’s arguments regarding the weight of the evidence and the credibility of Raul Salazar do not warrant reversal under the standards set forth in *Alexander* and *Smith*.

**III. PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY FEES**

The dispositive issue regarding penalties is whether the employer’s controversion of the claim was unreasonable. Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F) mandates penalties and attorney fees if the claim is controverted without reasonable grounds.

The defense argues that the insurer’s denial on April 5, 2011, was reasonable under *Reed v. A.C. Electric*, 96-1147 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1997), 693 So.2d 128, because it was based on the information available at the time, specifically the lack of medical records in the insurer’s possession. The defense emphasizes that only 12 days had passed since the accident and that the insurer was not required to obtain records before denying a claim.

The Court finds the prosecution’s argument to be persuasive. The insurer’s letter dated April 5, 2011, stated, "We have completed our investigation ... and find that you have not sustained a compensable work related claim." This language indicates a final determination on the merits of the claim rather than a mere deferral pending investigation. At the time of this "completed" investigation, the insurer knew the claimant had sought treatment from Dr. Davis on March 24, 2011. Under *Pittman v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 99-1541 (La. App. 1st Cir. 6/23/00), 764 So.2d 1279, an employer’s failure to obtain medical documentation known to exist before denying a claim can constitute unreasonable controversion.

While *Reed* allows for denial based on the information available, it does not sanction a denial that asserts the investigation is "complete" while ignoring the most critical evidence of the injury—the medical records documenting the claimant's condition immediately following the alleged accident. The defense argues that the insurer only knew of the "visit" and not the "findings." However, if the insurer did not know the findings, it could not logically conclude that the investigation was "complete" and that the claim was not compensable. As held in *Walters v. Rite Aid Pharmacy*, 05-1209 (La. 1/17/06), 920 So.2d 340, a denial based on speculation or without reviewing medical evidence constitutes unreasonable controversion. The insurer’s failure to review the Dr. Davis records, despite knowledge of the visit, before declaring the investigation complete, supports the trial court’s finding of unreasonable controversion.

**Finding on Penalties:** The trial court’s determination that the employer unreasonably controverted the claim is supported by the record. The insurer’s assertion that the investigation was "complete" without reviewing the known medical evidence from Dr. Davis constitutes an unreasonable controversion under *Walters* and *Pittman*.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the defense’s arguments are insufficient to demonstrate that the trial court’s findings were manifestly erroneous or that the legal standards for penalties were misapplied. The prosecution’s application of *Smith*, *Honeycutt*, *Walters*, and *Pittman* is consistent with the facts provided.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s judgment finding that the claimant proved a work-related accident and awarding penalties and attorney fees be **AFFIRMED**. The defense’s request for reversal and dismissal is denied.
