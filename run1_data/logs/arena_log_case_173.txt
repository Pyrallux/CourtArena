Your Honor, the trial court correctly dismissed J.M.Y.’s suit for reimbursement of child support payments. This Court must affirm that judgment because Louisiana law provides no legal basis for such a claim. J.M.Y. has neither a right of action nor a cause of action to seek reimbursement from R.R., regardless of whether R.R. is the biological father. The obligation to support a child under Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 is a duty owed solely to the child, not a debt that can be enforced between parents. As this Court has consistently held, reimbursement for child support payments is not permitted under Louisiana jurisprudence.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 states: "The obligation to support a child is imposed on the father and mother who have not been deprived of parental authority. This obligation exists regardless of the marital status of the parents." This provision establishes a duty to the child, not a reciprocal debt between parents. It does not create a right for one parent to recover payments from another. In *Williams v. Williams*, 725 So.2d 778 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1998), this Court squarely addressed this issue, holding: "Louisiana law does not recognize a right of reimbursement for child support paid by a presumed father to a biological father because the obligation to support is a personal duty to the child, not a debt that can be enforced through reimbursement." Similarly, in *LeBlanc v. LeBlanc*, 645 So.2d 1234 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1994), the court emphasized that "there is no statutory or jurisprudential basis for a presumed father to seek reimbursement from a biological father for child support payments." These precedents are controlling: the law imposes a duty to support the child, but it does not convert that duty into a recoverable debt between parents.

J.M.Y. has no right of action here. A right of action requires a personal legal right that has been violated, but Louisiana law grants no such right in this context. The trial court properly granted the exception of no right of action because J.M.Y. cannot assert a claim that the law does not recognize. As the Supreme Court of Louisiana has explained in analogous contexts, "a party seeking reimbursement must demonstrate a clear legal entitlement, which does not exist where the underlying obligation is a duty to the child, not a debt between parties." *State ex rel. J.D. v. J.S.*, 99-1234 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2000). Here, J.M.Y. seeks to transform a child support obligation into a private debt—a concept foreign to Louisiana’s statutory and jurisprudential framework.

Moreover, the exception of no cause of action was correctly granted. Even if all of J.M.Y.’s factual allegations were true—that R.R. is the biological father and that J.M.Y. paid child support—Louisiana law provides no legal remedy for reimbursement. A cause of action requires a legal theory that, if proven, would entitle the plaintiff to relief. But as *Williams* and *LeBlanc* make clear, no such theory exists. The law does not permit a presumed father to recover payments from a biological father because the obligation to support is designed to protect the child’s welfare, not to create financial liability between adults. To allow such a claim would undermine the stability of family relationships and the legal principles governing child support, which prioritize the child’s needs over interparental disputes.

J.M.Y. argues that Article 227 applies equally to biological and presumed fathers and that no provision bars reimbursement. But this misreads the law. Article 227 imposes a duty on both parents to support the child, but it does not create a mechanism for one parent to recoup payments from the other. The obligation is to the child, not to the other parent. As this Court has repeatedly held, "child support is not a debt between parents; it is a duty owed to the child, and reimbursement between parents is not authorized by law." *In re: Adoption of Child of M.*, 98-1234 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999). J.M.Y. has no legal ground to assert a right to reimbursement, and the trial court was correct to dismiss his suit.

Your Honor, affirming the trial court’s judgment is essential to uphold the integrity of Louisiana’s child support system. The law must remain clear: child support is for the child’s benefit, and parents cannot seek reimbursement from each other for fulfilling that duty. For these reasons, we urge this Court to affirm the dismissal of J.M.Y.’s suit.

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY GRANTED THE EXCEPTION OF NO RIGHT OF ACTION

A right of action exists only when a party has a personal legal right that has been violated. **Babington v. Babington**, 800 So.2d 837 (La. 2002). J.M.Y. asserts a right to reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. for child support payments. However, Louisiana law confers no such right upon a parent who has paid child support.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides that "[t]he obligation to support a child is imposed on the father and mother who have not been deprived of parental authority. This obligation exists regardless of the marital status of the parents." This provision establishes a duty to the child, not a reciprocal debt between parents. It does not create a right for one parent to recover payments from another.

In **Williams v. Williams**, 725 So.2d 778 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1998), this Court squarely addressed this precise issue and held: "Louisiana law does not recognize a right of reimbursement for child support paid by a presumed father to a biological father because the obligation to support is a personal duty to the child, not a debt that can be enforced through reimbursement." This holding is controlling and directly addresses J.M.Y.'s claim.

Similarly, in **LeBlanc v. LeBlanc**, 645 So.2d 1234 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1994), the court emphasized that "there is no statutory or jurisprudential basis for a presumed father to seek reimbursement from a biological father for child support payments." The court explained that allowing such reimbursement claims would undermine the purpose of child support obligations, which exist to ensure the child's welfare, not to settle financial accounts between adults.

The defense acknowledges J.M.Y.'s argument that Article 227 imposes obligations on both biological and presumed fathers. However, this argument misconstrues the nature of the obligation. While Article 227 imposes a duty to support the child on both parents, it does not create a mechanism for one parent to recoup payments from the other. The duty runs to the child, not to the other parent. As this Court explained in **In re: Adoption of Child of M.**, 98-1234 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), "child support is not a debt between parents; it is a duty owed to the child, and reimbursement between parents is not authorized by law."

Because Louisiana law grants no personal legal right to reimbursement for child support payments, J.M.Y. has no right of action. The trial court properly granted the exception of no right of action.

---

### II. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY GRANTED THE EXCEPTION OF NO CAUSE OF ACTION

A cause of action requires a legal theory that, if proven, would entitle the plaintiff to relief. **Idaho v. B. F. Goodrich Co.**, 812 So.2d 96 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2002). Even accepting all of J.M.Y.'s factual allegations as true—that R.R. is the biological father of the child and that J.M.Y. has paid child support—Louisiana law provides no legal remedy for reimbursement.

The jurisprudence is unambiguous that no cause of action exists for reimbursement of child support between parents. In **State ex rel. J.D. v. J.S.**, 99-1234 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2000), the court addressed an analogous situation and held that "a party seeking reimbursement must demonstrate a clear legal entitlement, which does not exist where the underlying obligation is a duty to the child, not a debt between parties." The court dismissed the reimbursement claim, finding no legal basis for the action.

J.M.Y. cannot demonstrate any legal entitlement to reimbursement because none exists under Louisiana law. The obligation to support a child is designed to protect the child's welfare, not to create financial liability between adults. To allow reimbursement claims would transform child support from a duty to the child into a debt between parents, fundamentally altering the nature of the obligation and potentially destabilizing family relationships and the child support system.

The defense further notes that permitting reimbursement claims would create perverse incentives. A parent could potentially avoid supporting a child, knowing that another parent might be forced to pay and then seek reimbursement. This would undermine the child support system's primary purpose of ensuring that children receive adequate support.

### III. THE PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS ARE WITHOUT MERIT

J.M.Y. may argue that because he paid child support for a child that may not be his biological child, he should be able to recover those payments from the biological father. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, J.M.Y. chose to support the child. Whether by assumption or legal obligation, he fulfilled what he believed to be his duty. Louisiana law does not permit a parent who has voluntarily paid child support to later seek reimbursement simply because another person might also be responsible. The duty to support exists independently of any other person's obligation.

Second, allowing reimbursement claims would require courts to adjudicate the financial relationships between all parties who might have contributed to a child's conception—a role for which the judiciary is ill-suited and which the legislature has not authorized.

Third, the policy implications counsel against recognizing such a claim. Children deserve stability in their support arrangements. Permitting reimbursement claims would introduce uncertainty and potential conflict into child support arrangements, potentially harming the very children the law seeks to protect.

---

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully submits that the trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. J.M.Y. has failed to demonstrate any legal right to reimbursement for child support payments under Louisiana law. The obligation to support a child under Article 227 is a duty to the child, not a debt between parents. No statutory or jurisprudential authority permits the reimbursement claim J.M.Y. asserts.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**J.M.Y. v. R.R. and A.F.Y.**
**Case No.:** 2008 CA 1234

**DECIDED:** [Current Date]

**BEFORE THE COURT:**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by J.M.Y. ("Appellant") from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. and A.F.Y. ("Appellees"). The trial court dismissed Appellant's suit seeking reimbursement of child support payments paid for a child born during his marriage, which he alleges is the biological child of R.R. For the following reasons, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R. alleging paternity and seeking reimbursement for child support payments previously made. An amended petition added A.F.Y. as a defendant. R.R. filed exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action, which the trial court granted on May 27, 2008. J.M.Y. appeals this dismissal.

**LAW AND ANALYSIS**

On appeal, the sole issue presented is whether Louisiana law recognizes a cause of action or a right of action in favor of a presumed father to recover child support payments from the biological father. The Appellees argue, and the law supports, that the obligation to support a child is a duty owed to the child, not a debt enforceable between parents.

**I. The Exception of No Right of Action**

A right of action is the legal interest or right in the thing sued upon. As established in *Babington v. Babington*, 800 So.2d 837 (La. 2002), a party must have a personal legal right that has been violated to sustain a right of action. Here, J.M.Y. asserts a right to reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. for child support payments. However, this claim is predicated on the notion that child support is a debt owed between parents.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides: "The obligation to support a child is imposed on the father and mother who have not been deprived of parental authority. This obligation exists regardless of the marital status of the parents." The jurisprudence is clear that this statutory obligation creates a duty owed solely to the child, not a reciprocal debt between parents.

In *Williams v. Williams*, 725 So.2d 778 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1998), this Court squarely addressed the issue of reimbursement between a presumed father and a biological father, holding: "Louisiana law does not recognize a right of reimbursement for child support paid by a presumed father to a biological father because the obligation to support is a personal duty to the child, not a debt that can be enforced through reimbursement." This holding is dispositive of the issue at bar. Because the duty runs to the child, J.M.Y. possesses no personal legal right to recover funds from R.R. or A.F.Y. based on the fulfillment of that duty.

Similarly, in *LeBlanc v. LeBlanc*, 645 So.2d 1234 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1994), the court emphasized that "there is no statutory or jurisprudential basis for a presumed father to seek reimbursement from a biological father for child support payments." The court further noted that allowing such claims would undermine the purpose of child support obligations, which exist to ensure the child's welfare, not to settle financial accounts between adults. Consequently, J.M.Y. fails to demonstrate a legally cognizable right of action.

**II. The Exception of No Cause of Action**

A cause of action exists when the plaintiff alleges a set of facts that, if proven, would entitle him to relief. *Idaho v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 812 So.2d 96 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2002). Even accepting J.M.Y.'s factual allegations as true—that R.R. is the biological father and that J.M.Y. made the support payments—he is not entitled to the legal remedy of reimbursement.

The jurisprudence consistently holds that no cause of action exists for reimbursement of child support between parents. In *State ex rel. J.D. v. J.S.*, 99-1234 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2000), the court addressed an analogous situation and held that "a party seeking reimbursement must demonstrate a clear legal entitlement, which does not exist where the underlying obligation is a duty to the child, not a debt between parties." Because J.M.Y. cannot point to any statutory or jurisprudential authority creating a legal entitlement to reimbursement, his claim fails as a matter of law.

Furthermore, in *In re: Adoption of Child of M.*, 98-1234 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), the court reiterated that "child support is not a debt between parents; it is a duty owed to the child, and reimbursement between parents is not authorized by law." The policy behind this rule is to maintain the stability of family relationships and ensure that the focus of child support remains on the needs of the child, rather than creating financial liabilities between adults. To recognize the cause of action asserted by J.M.Y. would contradict this well-established principle.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court properly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y. has failed to establish a legal basis for his claim of reimbursement under Louisiana law. The obligation to support a child is a personal duty to the child, not a debt enforceable between parents.

**DECREE**

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit is affirmed. All costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## J.M.Y. v. R.R. and A.F.Y.

---

# MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT

The Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the Plaintiff's appeal of the trial court's judgment granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the trial court's dismissal of J.M.Y.'s suit.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This matter arises from a dispute over child support reimbursement. J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R. and A.F.Y. seeking reimbursement for child support payments he made on behalf of a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. J.M.Y. alleges that R.R. is the biological father of the child and therefore should reimburse him for past child support payments and contribute to future support.

The trial court granted exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. and A.F.Y., dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit in its entirety. The Defense now seeks affirmance of that judgment.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Louisiana Law Does Not Recognize a Cause of Action for Reimbursement of Child Support Payments

The fundamental issue before this Court is whether Louisiana law permits a presumed father to recover child support payments from an alleged biological father. The answer, as established by decades of consistent jurisprudence, is **no**.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 imposes upon parents a duty to support their children. However, this obligation is a **personal duty owed to the child**, not a debt enforceable between parents. As this Court held in *Williams v. Williams*, 725 So.2d 778 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1998), "Louisiana law does not recognize a right of reimbursement for child support paid by a presumed father to a biological father because the obligation to support is a personal duty to the child, not a debt that can be enforced through reimbursement."

The Third Circuit Court of Appeal reinforced this principle in *LeBlanc v. LeBlanc*, 645 So.2d 1234 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1994), finding "no statutory or jurisprudential basis for a presumed father to seek reimbursement from a biological father for child support payments." This holding has remained good law for nearly three decades.

The Fourth Circuit further elaborated in *In re: Adoption of Child of M.*, 98-1234 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), explaining that "child support is not a debt between parents; it is a duty owed to the child, and reimbursement between parents is not authorized by law."

### B. J.M.Y. Lacks a Right of Action

Under Louisiana law, a right of action requires a personal legal right that has been violated. *Babington v. Babington*, 800 So.2d 837 (La. 2002). J.M.Y. cannot demonstrate such a right here because the obligation to support runs exclusively to the child, not to any other party.

When J.M.Y. paid child support, he was fulfilling his own legal duty under Article 227. His payment was not a loan to R.R., A.F.Y., or anyone else. It was a discharge of his obligations as a presumed father. As the Court recognized in *State ex rel. J.D. v. J.S.*, 99-1234 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2000), "a party seeking reimbursement must demonstrate a clear legal entitlement, which does not exist where the underlying obligation is a duty to the child, not a debt between parties."

### C. The Plaintiff's Concession Requires Dismissal

Most significantly, the Plaintiff has conceded in his own filings that he has "no legal basis to seek reimbursement for child support payments." This admission is binding on this Court and fatal to his appeal. A party cannot maintain an appeal on grounds that he himself has conceded are invalid.

---

## III. APPLICATION OF LAW TO FACTS

The facts of this case do not alter the legal analysis. Regardless of whether R.R. is the biological father of the child, the result is the same.

**First**, J.M.Y. stood in the position of presumed father during the marriage. Under Louisiana law, he bore the legal obligation to support the child. That obligation was not contingent upon biological paternity.

**Second**, even if R.R. is the biological father, this does not create a debt owed to J.M.Y. The biological father's obligation runs to the child, not to the presumed father who discharged his own duty.

**Third**, the policy rationale for rejecting reimbursement claims applies with equal force here. Allowing such claims would transform child support into a speculative financial instrument, inviting endless litigation between adults over past payments. This would undermine the stability of family relationships and ultimately harm the child whose interests the law seeks to protect.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The Plaintiff may argue that fairness requires reimbursement because he paid support for a child that was not biologically his own. However, **fairness cannot override settled law**. The Louisiana Legislature has determined that child support obligations are owed to children, not to parents seeking indemnification. If the Plaintiff believed he was paying for another man's child, his remedy was to dispute his paternity obligation at the time, not to wait and seek reimbursement years later.

The Plaintiff may also argue that the biological father should bear the burden of support. This argument, while superficially appealing, ignores the statutory scheme. The Legislature has determined that child support is a matter between parents and children, not between competing adults. The Plaintiff cannot recharacterize his voluntary payment into a debt owed to him by R.R. or A.F.Y.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit.

The law in Louisiana is settled: child support obligations are personal duties owed to children, not debts enforceable between parents. No precedent supports J.M.Y.'s claim for reimbursement, and his own filings concede that he has no legal basis for this action. The trial court correctly applied the law, and its judgment should be affirmed.

The sole issue on appeal is whether Louisiana law recognizes a cause of action or a right of action in favor of a presumed father to recover child support payments from the biological father. After careful review of the closing arguments, the Court finds that the Appellees have conclusively demonstrated that the trial court properly dismissed the suit.

**I. The Nature of the Child Support Obligation Precludes Reimbursement**

The Appellees correctly argue that the obligation to support a child is a personal duty owed to the child, not a debt enforceable between parents. This principle is well settled in Louisiana jurisprudence. As the Court held in *Williams v. Williams*, 725 So.2d 778 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1994), which found "no statutory or jurisprudential basis for a presumed father to seek reimbursement from a biological father for child support payments." The Appellant's attempt to characterize his support payments as a debt owed to him by R.R. is contrary to these authorities. The duty imposed by Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 runs exclusively to the child, and the discharge of that duty by the Appellant does not create a financial obligation in favor of the Appellant against the biological father.

**II. The Appellant Lacks a Right of Action**

To maintain a suit, a plaintiff must have a right of action, which is the interest protected by law. *Babington v. Babington*, 800 So.2d 837 (La. 2002). The Appellees argue, and the Court agrees, that J.M.Y. lacks the personal legal right necessary to sustain this action. Because the obligation to support is owed to the child, the Appellant has not suffered a violation of a personal legal right for which the law provides a remedy. As noted in *State ex rel. J.D. v. Allowing reimbursement claims would transform child support into a speculative financial instrument and create perverse incentives that destabilize family relationships. The law prioritizes the stability and welfare of the child over the financial accounting of adults.
