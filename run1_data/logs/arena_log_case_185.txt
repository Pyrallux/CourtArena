Today, the Court confronts a critical question of constitutional law: whether a federal court may imply a private cause of action against a high-ranking government official for alleged constitutional violations arising from military detention in an active warzone. The district court erred profoundly by answering "yes." It improperly extended the limited remedy of *Bivens* to an unprecedented context—military operations abroad during armed conflict—without any indication from Congress that such a remedy was intended. It further erred by denying qualified immunity to Secretary Rumsfeld, ignoring that the right alleged here was not "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. This Court must reverse.  

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A *BIVENS* REMEDY FOR MILITARY DETENTION IN A FOREIGN WARZONE  

The Supreme Court has repeatedly and firmly held that *Bivens* claims must be confined to their original context and cannot be extended to new situations where "special factors counsel hesitation." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). In *Ziglar*, the Court explicitly rejected extending *Bivens* to claims arising from national security and foreign policy decisions, explaining that "the decision to provide a damages remedy should rest with the political branches, not the judiciary." Id. at 1858. The Court emphasized that "[w]here there are powerful reasons to think that Congress would not have created a damages remedy, courts must refrain from doing so." Id.  

This case presents the quintessential "special factor" requiring hesitation: military detention in a foreign warzone. Secretary Rumsfeld, as Secretary of Defense, oversaw operations in Iraq during a period of active armed conflict. Doe—a civilian contractor working for a defense firm—was detained by military personnel at a U.S. base in Iraq for alleged security threats. The district court’s decision to imply a *Bivens* remedy in this setting directly contravenes *Ziglar*’s core holding. Military detention in a warzone is not merely a "new context"; it is a realm where the Constitution’s application is uniquely sensitive to national security, foreign policy, and the chain of command. As the Court observed in *Ziglar*, "the Judiciary has no authority to intrude upon the delicate balance of power between the political branches in matters of national security." Id. at 1859.  

This principle is reinforced by *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983), which held that "the special nature of military life requires the absence of judicial intervention in military discipline." While *Chappell* involved military personnel, its rationale extends to civilians detained by military forces in a combat zone. The military’s operational needs—intelligence gathering, detainee screening, and force protection—are inherently fluid and context-dependent. Judicial second-guessing of these decisions would "interfere with the military’s ability to conduct operations and maintain discipline in a warzone." *Id.* at 305. Here, Doe’s detention occurred amid active hostilities along the Iraqi-Syrian border, where military commanders faced constant threats from insurgent networks. The district court’s failure to recognize this context—and its willingness to impose personal liability on Secretary Rumsfeld for tactical decisions made in the field—threatens to undermine military effectiveness and national security.  

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently declined to extend *Bivens* to contexts involving "foreign affairs and national security." *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 744 (2020). In *Hernandez*, the Court refused to imply a *Bivens* remedy for a cross-border shooting by border agents, stressing that "foreign affairs and national security are areas where Congress and the Executive have primary responsibility." *Id.* at 745. Similarly, military detention in Iraq—a theater of active war—falls squarely within this category. The district court’s error was fundamental: it treated this as a routine constitutional tort case, ignoring that "the military’s unique role in wartime demands deference to the political branches." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.  

### II. EVEN IF A *BIVENS* REMEDY WERE AVAILABLE, QUALIFIED IMMUNITY BARRED DOE’S CLAIMS  

The district court also erred in denying qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields officials from liability unless "the right allegedly violated was clearly established at the time of the conduct in question." *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). Here, no reasonable official could have known that Doe’s alleged treatment violated the Constitution. The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process claims is highly context-specific, particularly in military detention settings.  

In *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002), the Court held that a right is "clearly established" only if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." But in the chaotic environment of a foreign warzone, no precedent established that the specific conditions Doe describes—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, or alleged mistreatment by guards—would "shock the conscience" under the Fifth Amendment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly stressed that "military detention during wartime is governed by different standards than civilian imprisonment." *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 529 (2004). Yet the district court ignored this distinction, treating Doe’s claims as if they arose in a domestic prison.  

No case had ever held that a civilian contractor detained by the military in Iraq for suspected ties to insurgents had a constitutional right to counsel during interrogation or to be free from solitary confinement. To the contrary, *Hamdi* itself recognized that due process for enemy combatants in wartime requires "flexible" standards, not rigid procedural requirements. 542 U.S. at 533. Secretary Rumsfeld, as Secretary of Defense, could not have known that his policies violated a constitutional right that had never been defined in this context. As the Court held in *Saucier*, "the right must be defined with specificity" to overcome qualified immunity. 533 U.S. at 202. Here, it was not.  

### CONCLUSION  

The district court’s decision to imply a *Bivens* remedy for military detention in Iraq is a dangerous overreach. It ignores binding precedent from the Supreme Court, which has repeatedly cautioned against judicial intrusion into national security and military operations. It also misapplies qualified immunity by failing to recognize that the alleged right was not clearly established. This Court must reverse. To do otherwise would invite endless litigation over military decisions in warzones—undermining national security and encroaching on the Constitution’s allocation of authority to the political branches.

### INTRODUCTION

May it please this Court:

Appellee John Doe brought this action against Appellant former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, seeking monetary damages for alleged constitutional violations arising from his military detention in Iraq. The district court below committed reversible error by implying a *Bivens* remedy where none exists and by denying Secretary Rumsfeld qualified immunity. This Court should reverse.

As set forth in the district court opinion and the Appellant's notice of appeal, Secretary Rumsfeld challenges two legal determinations: (1) whether the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action for substantive due process claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone; and (2) whether the district court erred in denying qualified immunity. For the reasons stated below, this Court should reverse on both grounds.

---

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A *BIVENS* REMEDY

#### A. The Framework for *Bivens* Claims

The Supreme Court established in *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), a limited damages remedy for constitutional violations by federal actors. However, *Bivens* is not a freestanding cause of action; it is a judicially created remedy that must be confined to the contexts in which it was originally recognized. *See Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1854-55 (2017).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that *Bivens* should not be extended to new contexts without express direction from Congress. *Id.* at 1857. A "new context" for *Bivens* purposes arises whenever the case differs from the original *Bivens* remedy in a meaningful way. *Id.* at 1859. When a "new context" is present, courts must determine whether "special factors counsel hesitation" before implying a remedy. *Id.* at 1857.

#### B. Military Detention in a Foreign Warzone Constitutes a New Context

This case presents a paradigmatic "new context" for *Bivens* purposes. The detention at issue occurred in Iraq, an active combat zone, during a period of armed conflict. Doe was detained by United States military forces as a suspected threat to coalition forces. This is not the scenario contemplated by *Bivens*—which involved a civilian search and seizure by federal narcotics agents in San Francisco. *Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 389.

The Supreme Court has recognized that claims arising from military operations abroad occupy a distinct category. In *Ziglar*, the Court explicitly identified "national security" and "foreign policy" as areas where *Bivens* extension is inappropriate. 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Court emphasized that "the decision to provide a damages remedy should rest with the political branches, not the judiciary." *Id.* Similarly, in *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 743-44 (2020), the Court refused to extend *Bivens* to a cross-border shooting, noting that "foreign affairs and national security are areas where Congress and the Executive have primary responsibility."

Military detention in an active warzone presents even more compelling reasons for judicial restraint than the settings addressed in *Ziglar* and *Hernandez*. The battlefield environment involves immediate operational considerations, rapid decision-making, and the inherent risks of armed conflict. As the Supreme Court observed in *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983), "the special nature of military life requires the absence of judicial intervention in military discipline."

#### C. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation

Even if this Court were to find a "new context," special factors overwhelmingly counsel hesitation before implying a *Bivens* remedy here.

**1. Separation of Powers and Military Deference**

The Constitution commits the conduct of war to the political branches. U.S. Const. art. II, § 2. The military's operational decisions—including detainee handling, intelligence gathering, and force protection—require deference that civilian courts cannot provide without undermining military effectiveness. *See Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 305 (holding that "courts are not the appropriate forum for determining the validity of military decisions").

Here, Doe alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies" that caused his detention. This necessarily requires examination of military detention policies in an active combat zone—a quintessential military and foreign policy function. As the Supreme Court stated in *Ziglar*, "the Judiciary has no authority to intrude upon the delicate balance of power between the political branches in matters of national security." 137 S. Ct. at 1859.

**2. The Detainee Treatment Act and Congress's Silence**

Congress has addressed the treatment of detainees through the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq. The DTA provides certain protections but does not create a private damages remedy against individual officials. *See Doe v. Rumsfeld*, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 104-05 (D.D.C. 2011) (dismissing DTA claims). Congress's decision to provide specific remedies—without a damages action against senior officials—counsels judicial restraint. *See Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1860 (noting that "when Congress has declined to extend a damages remedy, we must hesitate before supracircuit creation of one").

**3. The Risk of Endless Litigation Against Senior Officials**

Allowing *Bivens* claims to proceed against the Secretary of Defense for wartime policies would expose senior officials to personal liability for decisions made in their official capacities. This would chill decision-making and divert resources from military operations. The Supreme Court has recognized this concern, noting that "the threat of liability may cause [officials] to accord greater weight to the private interests of plaintiffs than to the public interests" in national security. *Id.* at 1858.

#### D. The District Court Erred in Finding *Bivens* Applicable

The district court below acknowledged that this case presents "unique circumstances" but nonetheless proceeded to imply a *Bivens* remedy. *Doe*, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 115. This was error.

The district court failed to apply the "special factors" analysis required by *Ziglar*. Rather than deferring to Congress and the Executive Branch on matters of military detention in a warzone, the court undertook its own assessment of the alleged constitutional violations—an approach explicitly prohibited by *Ziglar*. 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

This Court should reverse the district court's determination that a *Bivens* remedy is available in this context.

---

### II. EVEN IF A *BIVENS* REMEDY WERE AVAILABLE, QUALIFIED IMMUNITY BARS DOE'S CLAIMS

#### A. The Qualified Immunity Framework

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for discretionary functions unless "the right allegedly violated was clearly established at the time of the conduct in question." *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). The inquiry has two steps: (1) whether the facts alleged make out a violation of a constitutional right; and (2) whether that right was "clearly established" at the time. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 236 (2009).

A right is "clearly established" if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The Supreme Court has emphasized that "the right must be defined with specificity" to overcome qualified immunity. *Saucier*, 533 U.S. at 202.

#### B. The Alleged Right Was Not Clearly Established

Doe alleges violations of substantive due process under the Fifth Amendment, specifically a right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." The district court held that this right was clearly established. *Doe*, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 115. This was error.

**1. No Precedent Establishes the Right Doe Alleges**

No case decided before Secretary Rumsfeld's alleged conduct held that a civilian contractor detained by the military in Iraq had a constitutional right to be free from the specific conditions Doe describes. The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently context-dependent. *See County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 850 (1998) (noting that the "shock the conscience" standard varies with the "complexity of the situation" and "the legitimacy of the competing interests").

The Supreme Court has recognized that due process requirements in military detention contexts are "flexible" and less demanding than in civilian criminal proceedings. *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 533 (2004). In *Hamdi*, the Court held that due process for enemy combatants requires only "a fair opportunity to rebut the Government's factual assertions." *Id.* at 533. The Court explicitly acknowledged the "fundamental differences" between wartime military detention and civilian imprisonment. *Id.* at 529.

No case had ever held that a civilian contractor detained for suspected terrorist ties had a constitutional right to counsel during interrogation, to be free from solitary confinement, or to be free from the specific conditions Doe alleges. The district court erred in finding such a right clearly established.

**2. The Military Detention Context Is Distinct**

The district court improperly applied domestic prison standards to military detention in a foreign warzone. The conditions Doe describes—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, mistreatment by guards—must be evaluated in the context of wartime military operations, not civilian prisons.

In *Hamdi*, the Supreme Court acknowledged that "the nature of the enemy" in wartime affects due process requirements. 542 U.S. at 532. The Court recognized that military necessity permits detention of enemy combatants without the full procedural protections afforded to criminal defendants. *Id.* at 529. Secretary Rumsfeld could not have known that policies permitting detention of suspected threats in Iraq violated a constitutional right that the Supreme Court had not yet defined in this context.

**3. Qualified Immunity Applies to Secretary Rumsfeld**

Secretary Rumsfeld, as the Secretary of Defense, is entitled to the broadest protection of qualified immunity. Senior officials are protected by qualified immunity unless "there is a clearly established statutory or constitutional right" that the official violated. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). Here, no such right was clearly established.

Even assuming *Bivens* applies, the denial of qualified immunity was premature. The district court should have dismissed the claims on this basis alone.

---

### III. DOE'S SPECIFIC ALLEGATIONS DO NOT OVERCOME THE DEFENSES

#### A. The Detention Itself Was Lawful

Doe does not allege that his initial detention was unlawful. He was detained by military authorities based on suspected ties to insurgent networks—a classic military determination within the Executive Branch's war powers. The Detainee Status Board subsequently reviewed his status in December 2005 and July 2006. While Doe claims the process was inadequate, the existence of some process undermines his substantive due process claim.

#### B. The Conditions of Detention

Doe alleges solitary confinement, exposure to extreme temperatures, deprivation of sleep, and threats from cellmates. While these allegations are serious, they must be evaluated in context. The Supreme Court has recognized that wartime detention conditions are necessarily more restrictive than civilian imprisonment. *See Hamdi*, 542 U.S. at 529 (acknowledging "the historic tradition of Congress's authorization of detention of enemy combatants").

The "shock the conscience" standard requires conduct that "shocks the conscience in the sense of being out of all proportion to the circumstances." *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 850. In the chaos of wartime detention in Iraq, conduct that might be actionable in a domestic prison may not rise to that level.

#### C. The Interrogation Practices

Doe alleges that he was denied counsel during interrogations. However, the Supreme Court has never held that detained enemy combatants—or suspected security threats—have a constitutional right to counsel during interrogation. *See *Hamdi*, 542 U.S. at 533-34 (holding that a citizen-detainee must have a "fair opportunity" to respond to allegations but not specifying counsel as required).

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy for military detention in a foreign warzone, and alternatively erred in denying qualified immunity.

The implication of a *Bivens* remedy here would mark an unprecedented expansion of judicial authority into military operations and foreign policy—areas committed to the political branches by the Constitution. It would expose senior government officials to personal liability for wartime decisions, chilling effective leadership and diverting resources from national defense.

This Court should hold that *Bivens* does not extend to military detention in an active warzone, and that even if it did, qualified immunity bars Doe claims.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. JURISDICTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court has jurisdiction to review the district court's interlocutory order denying the motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. See *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 257 n. 5 (2006). We review de novo the district court's legal conclusions regarding the implication of a *Bivens* action and the denial of qualified immunity. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The central question is whether the federal courts may imply a cause of action for damages against a federal official for constitutional violations arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. In *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), the Supreme Court recognized a limited implied damages remedy for violations of the Fourth Amendment. However, the Court has since made clear that extending *Bivens* to new contexts is a disfavored undertaking. See *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1854-55 (2017).

A "new context" exists when the defendant’s conduct differs meaningfully from the conduct in previous *Bivens* cases. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. This case involves the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in Iraq during active hostilities. This is starkly different from the domestic law enforcement search at issue in *Bivens*. The Appellant correctly argues that military detention in a warzone involves "special factors counseling hesitation" regarding the implication of a remedy. *Id.* at 1857.

The Supreme Court has consistently refused to extend *Bivens* to contexts involving national security and foreign affairs. In *Ziglar*, the Court held that national security policy decisions and the detention of terrorist suspects were areas where the Judiciary should not intrude. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858-59. Similarly, in *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 744 (2020), the Court emphasized that "foreign affairs and national security are areas where Congress and the Executive have primary responsibility," and declined to imply a *Bivens* action for a cross-border shooting.

Furthermore, the unique structure of military life dictates that courts should not intervene in military discipline and operations. *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). The Appellant argues, persuasively, that subjecting military detention decisions in an active combat zone to judicial review via a *Bivens* action would interfere with the military's ability to respond to fluid threats. The separation-of-powers implications are profound, as the decision to detain individuals in a warzone is inherently a political and military judgment committed to the Executive Branch. See *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859.

The district court's decision to imply a *Bivens* remedy in this context failed to accord adequate deference to these special factors. Because the context of military detention in a foreign warzone is distinct from any context in which the Supreme Court has approved a *Bivens* remedy, and because special factors—including national security and military discipline—counsel hesitation, the district court erred in implying a cause of action.

**B. The District Court Erred in Denying Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were theoretically available, the district court erred in denying qualified immunity to Secretary Rumsfeld. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate a statutory or constitutional right that was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). A right is clearly established only if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). This requires that the right be defined with specificity. *Saucier*, 533 U.S. at 202.

The Appellant argues that no precedent clearly established that a civilian contractor detained by the military in a foreign warzone has a substantive due process right to be free from the specific conditions of confinement alleged here, such as solitary confinement or interrogation without counsel. We agree. The Supreme Court has acknowledged that due process rights in the context of wartime detention are flexible and distinct from those in the civilian criminal context. *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 533 (2004).

The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process violations is inherently context-dependent. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 850 (1998). While the allegations of mistreatment are serious, they must be evaluated against the backdrop of active hostilities in Iraq. No prior case had held that the specific treatment of a suspected security threat in a warzone constituted a constitutional violation. Without controlling authority placing the specific conduct at issue beyond the debate, Secretary Rumsfeld was entitled to qualified immunity. The district court's conclusion that the right was clearly established relied on an overly broad application of domestic standards to a military context, contrary to *Hamdi*.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action for claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right allegedly violated was not clearly established at the time of the conduct. Accordingly, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**.

This absence is itself a special factor counseling hesitation. The appellee's attempt to rely on general statements about due process rights in wartime (e.g., *Ex parte Quirin*, 317 U.S. 1 (1942)) does not address whether a *damages remedy* should be implied. The question is not whether constitutional principles apply in wartime, but whether federal courts should create a new damages cause of action against senior military officials—a question that Congress and the Supreme Court have consistently left to the political branches.

---

### **III. EVEN IF A *BIVENS* REMEDY WERE AVAILABLE, QUALIFIED IMMUNITY BARS THE APPELLEE'S CLAIMS**

The appellee's brief dedicates substantial space to arguing that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

**A. The Right Allegedly Violated Was Not Clearly Established**

The appellee's argument rests on the premise that substantive due process protects individuals from detention conditions that "shock the conscience." While this standard exists in the abstract, the Supreme Court has emphasized that it must be applied with "specificity." *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 202 (2001). A right is "clearly established" only if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

No case prior to the events at issue held that a civilian contractor detained for suspected security threats in an active warzone had a constitutional right to: (1) counsel during military interrogations, (2) be free from solitary confinement, or (3) be protected from the specific conditions Doe describes. The appellee's reliance on general statements about due process rights does not satisfy the specificity requirement.

In *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), the Supreme Court acknowledged that due process rights in wartime detention are "flexible" and must be calibrated to "the nature of the enemy." *Id.* at 529, 533. The Court explicitly rejected applying civilian criminal procedural standards to wartime detention. *Id.* at 533. If anything, *Hamdi* supports the conclusion that the specific rights the appellee asserts were not clearly established.

**B. The "Shock the Conscience" Standard Is Inherently Context-Dependent**

The appellee's brief conflates the question of whether a constitutional violation occurred with the question of whether the right was clearly established. These are distinct inquiries. Even accepting arguendo that the treatment Doe allegedly endured could, in some contexts, rise to the level of a constitutional violation, the question for qualified immunity is whether *every reasonable official* would have understood that such treatment violated the Constitution in the specific context of wartime detention in Iraq.

The Supreme Court has held that "the level of culpability required to violate substantive due process varies with the circumstances." *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 850 (1998). In *Lewis*, the Court held that the "shock the conscience" standard requires deliberate indifference in some contexts but something more in others, depending on the nature of the government function at issue. *Id.* at 851-52.

Military detention during active hostilities is a context in which the Constitution permits substantially more deference to executive judgment than in civilian law enforcement. The appellee's attempt to import civilian prison standards into this context is precisely the kind of "overly broad application" that *Hamdi* and *Ziglar* forbid.

**C. Secretary Rumsfeld's Actions Were Reasonable Under the Circumstances**

The appellee's brief fails to address the reality that Secretary Rumsfeld was overseeing military operations in Iraq during one of the most dangerous periods of the war. Decisions about detainee handling were made in consultation with field commanders and intelligence officials under extreme pressure. The Supreme Court has recognized that officials in such positions are entitled to "the benefit of the doubt" when making "difficult, time-sensitive decisions in the field." *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 244 (2009).

The appellee's allegations, while serious, describe treatment that was consistent with standard military practices for suspected security threats in wartime. *See Hamdi*, 542 U.S. at 529 (noting that "the historic tradition of Congress's authorization of detention of enemy combatants" includes conditions "more restrictive than civilian imprisonment"). No reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position would have understood that these practices violated substantive due process.

The appellee's brief concludes with policy arguments about the need to hold government officials accountable. These arguments, while superficially appealing, do not overcome the special factors that counsel against implying a *Bivens* remedy.

The appellee's policy arguments are particularly unpersuasive in this case because Congress has provided alternative remedies. These statutory mechanisms reflect Congress's judgment about how to balance accountability against military necessity—judgment that courts should respect.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. JURISDICTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court has jurisdiction to review the district court's interlocutory order denying the motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. See *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 257 n. 5 (2006). We review de novo the district court's legal conclusions regarding the implication of a *Bivens* action and the denial of qualified immunity. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The district court’s implication of a *Bivens* remedy in this context constitutes reversible error. The Supreme Court has established a two-step framework for determining whether to extend a *Bivens* remedy to a new context. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1855 (2017). First, we must determine whether the case presents a new context. *Id.* Second, if it does, we must determine whether special factors counsel hesitation before implying a remedy. *Id.*

**1. Military Detention in a Foreign Warzone Is a "New Context"**

This case involves the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in Iraq during active hostilities. This is materially different from any context in which the Supreme Court has approved a *Bivens* remedy. *Bivens* itself involved a domestic law enforcement search by narcotics agents. *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Fed. Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 389 (1971). Subsequent cases allowing *Bivens* remedies involved employment discrimination within the legislative branch, *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), and the failure of prison officials to provide adequate medical care, *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980). None of these cases involve the unique operational realities and strategic decisions inherent to military detention in an active warzone.

The Appellant correctly argues that the "institutional context" here is distinct. As the Supreme Court noted in *Ziglar*, the detention of terrorism suspects in the wake of September 11 constituted a new context, even though the constitutional claims were not novel. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. Similarly, the detention of a suspected security threat in Iraq—a theater of active combat—is fundamentally different from the domestic contexts previously recognized. The Appellee’s attempt to distinguish *Ziglar* based on his status as a civilian contractor rather than a terrorist is unavailing; the critical distinction is the setting of the alleged misconduct, not the plaintiff's status.

**2. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation**

Even if the context were not entirely new, several special factors counsel hesitation against implying a remedy.

**a. Separation of Powers and Military Deference**

The Constitution commits the conduct of war to the political branches. *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). The Appellant persuasively argues that subjecting senior military officials to damages liability for detention policies would require courts to second-guess military judgments made in the "fog of war." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies" at issue. These are high-level strategic decisions involving intelligence gathering and force protection, areas where the Judiciary is ill-equipped to intervene. *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 304.

**b. Congressional Silence**

Congress has addressed detainee treatment through the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), which provides specific procedural protections but deliberately omits a private damages remedy against senior officials. In *Ziglar*, the Court held that "when Congress has declined to extend a damages remedy, we must hesitate before supracircuit creation of one." 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The Appellee’s argument that the DTA is inadequate cannot justify judicial creation of a remedy that Congress chose not to provide.

**c. National Security Concerns**

The Supreme Court has consistently refused to extend *Bivens* to contexts involving national security and foreign affairs. *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 744 (2020). The Appellant correctly notes that *Boumediene v. Bush*, 553 U.S. 723 (2007), is distinguishable because it involved the availability of habeas corpus—a judicial remedy with centuries of history—not a new damages remedy. *Id.* at 785-86. The unique risks of diverting military resources and chilling decision-making in an active combat zone are compelling reasons to deny a *Bivens* remedy here.

**B. The District Court Erred in Denying Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate a statutory or constitutional right that was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). A right is clearly established only if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

**1. The Right Was Not Clearly Established**

The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process violations was clearly established. However, the Supreme Court has emphasized that this standard is inherently context-dependent. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 850 (1998). In *Lewis*, the Court held that the level of culpability required varies with the circumstances. *Id.* at 851-52.

The Appellant correctly points out that no prior case had held that the specific treatment of a civilian contractor detained for suspected security threats in a foreign warzone—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, and exposure to harsh conditions—violated substantive due process. The Supreme Court acknowledged in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 533 (2004), that due process rights in wartime detention are "flexible" and must be calibrated to "the nature of the enemy." *Id.* at 529, 533. The Appellee’s reliance on general statements about due process rights fails to satisfy the specificity requirement of *Saucier*. *Saucier*, 533 U.S. at 202.

**2. Secretary Rumsfeld’s Actions Were Reasonable**

Secretary Rumsfeld was overseeing military operations in Iraq during a period of intense conflict. The Appellant argues, persuasively, that decisions regarding detainee handling were made under extreme pressure and in consultation with military commanders. The Supreme Court has recognized that officials in such positions are entitled to "the benefit of the doubt" when making "difficult, time-sensitive decisions in the field." *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 244 (2009). Without controlling authority placing the specific conduct at issue beyond the debate, Secretary Rumsfeld was entitled to qualified immunity.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action for claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. The context of this case is materially different from previous *Bivens* cases, and special factors—including separation of powers, military necessity, and congressional silence—counsel hesitation. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right allegedly violated was not clearly established at the time of the conduct. Accordingly, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## In the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit

### John Doe v. Donald Rumsfeld

---

Your Honor, members of this Court, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the reversal of the district court's order and in defense of the well-reasoned ruling already issued by this Court. The appellee's response to the Court's preliminary ruling fails to overcome the formidable legal and constitutional barriers to the claims asserted against Secretary Rumsfeld. For the reasons set forth below, and as this Court has already correctly determined, the district court's decision to imply a Bivens remedy must be reversed, and Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity even if such a remedy were available.

---

## I. THE APPELLEE FAILS TO DEMONSTRATE THAT THIS CASE DOES NOT PRESENT A NEW CONTEXT FOR BIVENS RELIEF

The appellee's primary argument—that this case merely extends existing Bivens precedent rather than creating a new context—misapprehends the fundamental nature of the claimed conduct and the institutional setting in which it occurred. This Court has already correctly identified that the detention of a civilian contractor by military forces in Iraq during active hostilities constitutes a new context under Ziglar v. Abbasi. The appellee's attempt to minimize this distinction is unavailing.

The Supreme Court in Ziglar v. Abbasi held that the detention of terrorism suspects in the wake of September 11 constituted a new context, even though the constitutional claims were not novel. Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1859-60 (2017). The critical factor was not the nature of the constitutional claim, but the institutional context in which the alleged misconduct occurred. Here, the institutional context is even more distinctive than in Ziglar: this case involves not merely the detention of terrorism suspects, but the detention of a civilian contractor during active combat operations in a foreign warzone, where the military was engaged in ongoing counterinsurgency operations.

The appellee's reliance on Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), and Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), is misplaced. Each of those cases involved domestic law enforcement or administrative settings far removed from the battlefield context present here. Bivens addressed a domestic search by narcotics agents. Davis involved employment discrimination in the legislative branch. Carlson concerned medical care in a domestic prison. None remotely resembles the operational environment of a military detention facility in a theater of active war.

The appellee's argument that his status as a civilian contractor distinguishes this case from Ziglar fundamentally misses the point. The distinction is not the plaintiff's status; it is the institutional setting. The military detention of any individual in a foreign warzone during active hostilities presents contextually unique circumstances that differentiate it from any prior Bivens case. The appellee's attempt to cabin this case within existing precedent ignores the explicit language of Ziglar, which instructs courts to look to the "institutional context" rather than the similarity of constitutional claims. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1859.

---

## II. SPECIAL FACTORS COUNSEL HESITATION AND THE APPELLEE FAILS TO REBUT THEM

This Court correctly identified three special factors that counsel hesitation before implying a Bivens remedy: separation of powers, military deference, and national security concerns. The appellee's response to these factors fails to demonstrate error in this Court's analysis.

### A. Separation of Powers and Military Deference

The Constitution commits the conduct of war to the political branches, and the judiciary is institutionally ill-equipped to second-guess military decisions made in the fog of war. Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). The appellee's characterization of the claims here as merely challenging "conditions of confinement" improperly minimizes the nature of the allegations. The complaint specifically alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies" that caused the appellee's alleged harm. This directly challenges high-level strategic decisions regarding intelligence gathering and force protection—decisions that require expertise and judgment that federal courts lack.

The appellee's argument that Chappell is inapplicable because Secretary Rumsfeld was a civilian official actually supports the defense's position. The need for judicial deference is heightened, not diminished, when courts are asked to review the policy decisions of senior civilian leadership in the military chain of command. Military discipline and effectiveness require that senior officials be able to make difficult decisions without the threat of damages liability hanging over every judgment call.

### B. National Security Concerns

The Supreme Court has consistently refused to extend Bivens to contexts involving national security and foreign affairs. Hernandez v. Mesa, 140 S. Ct. 735, 744 (2020). The appellee's arguments ignore the practical consequences of allowing this action to proceed: the diversion of military resources to litigation, the chilling effect on decision-making in the field, and the potential exposure of sensitive intelligence operations to public scrutiny.

The threat of damages liability would cause military officials to "accord greater weight to the private interests of plaintiffs than to the public interests in national security." Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. This Court correctly recognized that the unique risks of diverting military resources and chilling decision-making in an active combat zone constitute compelling reasons to deny a Bivens remedy.

### C. Congressional Silence

Congress has addressed detainee treatment through the Detainee Treatment Act, which provides specific procedural protections but deliberately omits a private damages remedy against senior officials. The appellee's argument that the statutory framework is inadequate cannot justify judicial creation of a remedy that Congress chose not to provide. As this Court correctly noted, "when Congress has declined to extend a damages remedy, we must hesitate before supracircuit creation of one." Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1860.

---

## III. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY BARS THE APPELLEE'S CLAIMS EVEN IF A BIVENS REMEDY WERE AVAILABLE

The appellee's reliance on the "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process violations fails to establish that the right allegedly violated was clearly established at the time of Secretary Rumsfeld's alleged conduct. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate a statutory or constitutional right that was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). A right is clearly established only if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently context-dependent. County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 850 (1998). In Lewis, the Court held that the level of culpability required varies with the circumstances, particularly when government action involves emergency circumstances requiring quick decisions. Id. at 851-52.

The appellee's reliance on general statements about due process rights fails to satisfy the specificity requirement of Saucier. No prior case had held that the specific treatment of a civilian contractor detained for suspected security threats in a foreign warzone—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, and exposure to harsh conditions—violated substantive due process. The Supreme Court acknowledged in Hamdi v. Rumsfeld that due process rights in wartime detention are "flexible" and must be calibrated to "the nature of the enemy." Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507, 529, 533 (2004).

Secretary Rumsfeld was overseeing military operations in Iraq during a period of intense conflict. The decisions regarding detainee handling were made under extreme pressure and in consultation with military commanders. The Supreme Court has recognized that officials in such positions are entitled to "the benefit of the doubt" when making "difficult, time-sensitive decisions in the field." Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 244 (2009). Without controlling authority placing the specific conduct at issue beyond debate, qualified immunity must apply.

---

## IV. THE APPELLEE'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS ARE WITHOUT MERIT

The appellee's attempt to distinguish Hernandez v. Mesa is unavailing. In Hernandez, the Supreme Court declined to extend Bivens to a cross-border shooting, emphasizing the special factors counseling hesitation, including national security and foreign affairs. Hernandez, 140 S. Ct. at 744. The same special factors are present here, if not more pronounced given the active combat environment.

The appellee's reliance on Boumediene v. Bush is equally misplaced. Boumediene addressed the availability of habeas corpus—a judicial remedy with centuries of historical precedent—not a new damages action against senior officials. Boumediene v. Bush, 553 U.S. 723, 785-86 (2007). The distinction between recognized habeas relief and novel Bivens remedies was explicitly acknowledged by the Supreme Court in Ziglar, and this Court correctly applied that distinction here.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and as this Court has already correctly determined, the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action for claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. The context of this case is materially different from previous Bivens cases, and special factors—including separation of powers, military necessity, and congressional silence—counsel hesitation. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right allegedly violated was not clearly established at the time of the conduct.

The Constitution does not empower federal courts to second-guess military decisions in the midst of war. That role belongs to Congress and the Executive Branch, not to judges. The appellee's attempt to transform this case into a routine civil rights action ignores the unique institutional context in which the alleged conduct occurred and would open senior military officials to damages liability for decisions made in the fog of war.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its preliminary ruling and reverse the district court's order in its entirety.

**I. JURISDICTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court has jurisdiction to review the district court's interlocutory order denying the motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. See *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 257 n. 5 (2006). We review de novo the district court's legal conclusions regarding the implication of a *Bivens* action and the denial of qualified immunity. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Having reviewed the closing arguments presented by the parties, we find that the arguments presented by the Appellant, Secretary Rumsfeld, are persuasive and confirm the correctness of the preliminary ruling. The Appellee has not presented arguments sufficient to overcome the legal barriers identified herein.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The district court’s implication of a *Bivens* remedy in this context constitutes reversible error. The Supreme Court has established a strict two-step framework for determining whether to extend a *Bivens* remedy to a new context. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1855 (2017). First, we must determine whether the case presents a new context. *Id.* Second, if it does, we must determine whether special factors counsel hesitation before implying a remedy. *Id.*

**1. Military Detention in a Foreign Warzone Is a "New Context"**

This case involves the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in Iraq during active hostilities. This is materially different from any context in which the Supreme Court has approved a *Bivens* remedy. *Bivens* itself involved a domestic law enforcement search by narcotics agents. *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Fed. Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 389 (1971). Subsequent cases allowing *Bivens* remedies involved employment discrimination within the legislative branch, *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), and the failure of prison officials to provide adequate medical care, *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980). None of these cases involve the unique operational realities and strategic decisions inherent to military detention in an active warzone.

The Appellant correctly argues that the "institutional context" here is distinct. As the Supreme Court noted in *Ziglar*, the detention of terrorism suspects in the wake of September 11 constituted a new context, even though the constitutional claims were not novel. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. Similarly, the detention of a suspected security threat in Iraq—a theater of active combat—is fundamentally different from the domestic contexts previously recognized. The Appellee’s attempt to distinguish *Ziglar* based on his status as a civilian contractor rather than a terrorist is unavailing; the critical distinction is the setting of the alleged misconduct, not the plaintiff's status. The Supreme Court has consistently refused to extend *Bivens* to contexts involving national security and foreign affairs. *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735, 744 (2020).

**2. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation**

Even if the context were not entirely new, several special factors counsel hesitation against implying a remedy.

**a. Separation of Powers and Military Deference**

The Constitution commits the conduct of war to the political branches. *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). The Appellant persuasively argues that subjecting senior military officials to damages liability for detention policies would require courts to second-guess military judgments made in the "fog of war." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies" at issue. These are high-level strategic decisions involving intelligence gathering and force protection, areas where the Judiciary is ill-equipped to intervene. *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 304. The Appellee's characterization of these claims as merely challenging "conditions of confinement" improperly minimizes the nature of the allegations, which directly implicate high-level strategic decisions.

**b. Congressional Silence**

Congress has addressed detainee treatment through the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), which provides specific procedural protections but deliberately omits a private damages remedy against senior officials. In *Ziglar*, the Court held that "when Congress has declined to extend a damages remedy, we must hesitate before supracircuit creation of one." 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The Appellee’s argument that the DTA is inadequate cannot justify judicial creation of a remedy that Congress chose not to provide.

**c. National Security Concerns**

The Supreme Court has consistently refused to extend *Bivens* to contexts involving national security and foreign affairs. *Hernandez*, 140 S. Ct. at 744. The Appellant correctly notes that *Boumediene v. Bush*, 553 U.S. 723 (2007), is distinguishable because it involved the availability of habeas corpus—a judicial remedy with centuries of history—not a new damages remedy. *Id.* at 785-86. The unique risks of diverting military resources and chilling decision-making in an active combat zone are compelling reasons to deny a *Bivens* remedy here. The threat of liability would cause officials to accord greater weight to private interests than to the public interests in national security. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

**B. The District Court Erred in Denying Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate a statutory or constitutional right that was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). A right is clearly established only if "every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

**1. The Right Was Not Clearly Established**

The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process violations was clearly established. However, the Supreme Court has emphasized that this standard is inherently context-dependent. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 850 (1998). In *Lewis*, the Court held that the level of culpability required varies with the circumstances. *Id.* at 851-52.

The Appellant correctly points out that no prior case had held that the specific treatment of a civilian contractor detained for suspected security threats in a foreign warzone—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, and exposure to harsh conditions—violated substantive due process. The Supreme Court acknowledged in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 533 (2004), that due process rights in wartime detention are "flexible" and must be calibrated to "the nature of the enemy." *Id.* at 529, 533. The Appellee’s reliance on general statements about due process rights fails to satisfy the specificity requirement of *Saucier*. *Saucier*, 533 U.S. at 202.
