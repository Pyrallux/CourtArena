FACTS:
Last year, we held that compliance with OCGA § 44-14-161, Georgia’s confirmation statute, “is a condition precedent to the lender’s ability to pursue a guarantor for a deficiency after a foreclosure has been conducted, but a guarantor retains the contractual ability to waive the condition precedent requirement.” PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass’n v. Smith, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016). We granted certiorari in this case to consider additional questions regarding creditors’ ability to pursue deficiency actions against guarantors. We conclude that Jim L. York and John A. Drillot (“the Guarantors”) waived any defense based on the failure of creditor RES-GA LJY, LLC (“RES-GA”) to confirm the relevant foreclosure sales, and thus we affirm the Court of Appeals decision that upheld deficiency judgments against them.

1. Background

The relevant facts are undisputed. The Community Bank loaned money to several entities (“the Borrowers”) over the course of several years. The Borrowers executed five promissory notes, granting the bank a security interest in real estate located in three different counties. To further secure the loans, the Guarantors signed commercial guaranties (“the Guaranties”) in which they guaranteed full payment of the notes.1 The Guaranties each included an *870identical2 waiver provision (“the Waiver”), which provides in relevant part:

Guarantor also waives any and all rights or defenses based on suretyship or impairment of collateral including, but not limited to, any rights or defenses arising by reason of (A) the provisions of O.C.G.A. Section 10-7-24 concerning Guarantor’s right to require Lender to take action against Borrower or any “one action” or “anti-deficiency” law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency, against Guarantor, before or after Lender’s commencement or completion of any foreclosure action, either judicially or by exercise of a power of sale; ... or (F) any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity other than actual payment and performance of the Indebtedness.
The promissory notes and Guaranties were later assigned to RES-GA.

In 2011, RES-GA foreclosed on and bought the properties that were serving as collateral. It then filed confirmation actions in the three counties in which the secured properties were located. In each instance, the trial court entered an order refusing to confirm the sale, finding that RES-GA had failed to prove that it obtained the fair market value of the property in question, and refusing to allow a resale. RES-GA appealed two of those orders, and the Court of Appeals affirmed in each case. See RES-GA LJY, LLC v. South Crestview Drive, LLC, 326 Ga. App. XXIII (Case No. A13A1810) (March 10, 2014) (unpublished); RES-GALJY, LLC v. Y.D.I., Inc., 322 Ga. App. 607 (745 SE2d 820) (2013).

In July 2013, the Court of Appeals issued its decision in HWA Properties, Inc. v. Community & Southern Bank, 322 Ga. App. 877 (746 SE2d 609) (2013), finding that a creditor’s failure to obtain a valid confirmation of a foreclosure sale3 did not impair its authority to obtain a deficiency judgment against the loan’s personal guarantor. Id. at 885-888 (2) (b). RES-GA then filed this deficiency action against the Guarantors. The trial court denied the Guarantors’ *871motion for summary judgment and granted summary judgment to RES-GA, concluding that the Guarantors had waived any defense based on RES-GA’s failure to confirm the sales. At RES-GA’s suggestion, the trial court calculated RES-GA’s damages as the difference between the amount due on the notes and the higher values of each property as contended by the Guarantors’ appraisers in testimony submitted during the confirmation proceedings. In all, the trial court enteredjudgment against Yorkfor $12,381,103.09, plus $44,432.22 in attorney fees, and enteredjudgment against Drillot in the amount of $1,505,772.10.

The Guarantors appealed to the Court of Appeals, which affirmed. York v. RES-GA LJY, LLC, 336 Ga. App. 253 (784 SE2d 96) (2016). Pointing to our decision in PNC Bank, the panel noted that the confirmation requirement of OCGA § 44-14-161 (a) can be waived by a guarantor. Id. at 255. The panel rejected the Guarantors’ argument that, because a defense arising out of OCGA § 44-14-161 (a) is not “based on suretyship,” the Waiver did not encompass such a defense. Id. at 255-256. The panel concluded that the Guaranties are themselves contracts of suretyship and that the Waiver explicitly applies to defenses arising from laws that prevent a lender from pursuing a deficiency claim against a guarantor. Id. We granted the Guarantors’ petition for certiorari and directed the parties to address (1) whether the Court of Appeals erred in its determination that the Guarantors waived their rights under OCGA § 44-14-161 (a); and (2) whether RES-GA retained the power to pursue a deficiency judgment against the Guarantors after RES-GA sought and was denied judicial confirmation under the statute.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF GEORGIA

---

**Introduction and Summary of Argument**

The plaintiff (RES-GA LJY, LLC) asks this Court to enforce a deficiency judgment against the Guarantors based on the theory that they waived defenses arising from Georgia's confirmation statute, OCGA § 44-14-161. This Court should reverse the Court of Appeals and find that the Guarantors did not effectively waive their rights under the confirmation statute. The plaintiff's interpretation of the waiver provision is overbroad, contradicts the plain meaning of the contract, and ignores fundamental principles of Georgia law regarding the waiver of statutory protections.

---

## I. The Waiver Provision Does Not Encompass Defenses Arising Under OCGA § 44-14-161

The plaintiff contends that the waiver explicitly covers "anti-deficiency laws" and therefore encompasses OCGA § 44-14-161. This argument fails because the confirmation statute is fundamentally different from the anti-deficiency laws referenced in the waiver.

### A. The Confirmation Statute Is Not an "Anti-Deficiency Law" in the Traditional Sense

The waiver provision references "anti-deficiency law" in the context of laws concerning "one action" rules and procedures that prevent lenders from pursuing multiple remedies. See O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24 (concerning a guarantor's right to require lender to proceed against the borrower). These are procedural protections that ensure a lender pursues collateral before pursuing the guarantor.

OCGA § 44-14-161, by contrast, is a substantive protection that addresses the fairness of the foreclosure process itself. It requires judicial confirmation that the property brought fair market value before a deficiency may be pursued. This is not a procedural "one action" rule—it is an independent substantive requirement that goes to the very authority of the creditor to seek a deficiency. As this Court recognized in *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016), compliance with the confirmation statute is a "condition precedent" to the lender's ability to pursue a deficiency.

The phrase "anti-deficiency law" in the waiver must be read in context. It refers to laws like the one-action statutes that govern the sequencing of remedies, not to substantive confirmation requirements that determine whether a deficiency may be pursued at all.

### B. The Ejusdem Generis Canon Applies to Construe the Waiver Narrowly

The plaintiff dismisses the Guarantors' invocation of ejusdem generis as a "fundamental misreading." However, this Court has consistently applied ejusdem generis to interpret contractual provisions, particularly where general terms follow specific examples. See *Murray v. Jenkins*, 259 Ga. 654, 656 (385 SE2d 417) (1989) (applying ejusdem generis to construe ambiguous contractual language).

The waiver lists specific examples: (A) O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24, "one action" laws, and "anti-deficiency" laws. These all share a common characteristic—they are procedural protections governing the sequence of remedies available to lenders. The general phrase "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action" must be read in light of these specific examples. It encompasses procedural obstacles to suit, not substantive conditions precedent like confirmation requirements.

To adopt the plaintiff's interpretation would render the specific examples meaningless surplusage—because under the plaintiff's reading, "any other law" would cover everything regardless of whether it fits the category of the specific examples. This violates fundamental principles of contract interpretation. See *Lowe's Home Centers, Inc. v. Walker*, 277 Ga. 145, 148 (587 SE2d 602) (2003) (contract terms must be construed to give effect to all provisions).

### C. Subsection (F) Does Not Sweep in All Defenses

The plaintiff argues that subsection (F)'s reference to "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity other than actual payment and performance" sweeps in the confirmation statute defense. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the phrase "other than actual payment and performance" establishes that the waiver covers only defenses that would excuse non-payment—defects in the enforceability of the obligation itself. The confirmation statute does not address whether the debt is valid or whether the guarantor received consideration. It addresses whether the creditor complied with a procedural prerequisite to seeking a deficiency. This is not a defense to "payment and performance" in the sense contemplated by subsection (F).

Second, even if subsection (F) were read broadly, it must be reconciled with the rest of the waiver. Under Georgia law, a waiver must be clear and unequivocal. See *Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. W. G. Homes, Inc.*, 119 Ga. App. 298, 304 (167 SE2d 253) (1969). Ambiguous provisions are construed against the drafter—in this case, the lender.

---

## II. The Confirmation Statute Creates a Substantive Right That Cannot Be Waived

Even if the waiver language could be stretched to encompass OCGA § 44-14-161, public policy prevents enforcement of such a waiver.

### A. The Confirmation Requirement Serves Important Public Policy

Georgia's confirmation statute exists to protect borrowers and guarantors from unfair deficiency judgments. It ensures that creditors cannot recover deficiencies when they have failed to obtain fair market value at foreclosure—either through inadequate bidding at the sale or through failure to pursue the highest reasonable price. See *Short v. First Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n*, 245 Ga. 434, 436 (265 SE2d 583) (1980).

This is not a mere procedural technicality. It is a substantive protection that reflects the General Assembly's judgment that creditors should not be permitted to profit from depressed foreclosure prices while simultaneously pursuing deficiency judgments against borrowers and guarantors. The statute embodies important public policy concerns that transcend the private contract between the parties.

### B. Statutory Protections May Not Be Waived

Georgia law provides that certain statutory protections cannot be waived by private contract. While the plaintiff correctly notes that *PNC Bank* held that the confirmation requirement "can be waived," that holding was based on the specific language at issue in *PNC Bank*—not on a blanket rule that all waivers are enforceable. The waiver in *PNC Bank* explicitly and unambiguously waived "any and all rights" under the confirmation statute. The waiver here is materially different.

Moreover, even *PNC Bank* recognized limits. This Court emphasized that the waiver in that case was "explicit" and "unambiguous." Here, by contrast, the waiver is ambiguous—it does not specifically mention OCGA § 44-14-161 or the confirmation requirement. The plaintiff asks this Court to infer such a waiver from general language about "anti-deficiency laws," which as explained above, refers to a different category of laws.

---

## III. The Trial Court Erred in Calculating Damages

Even if this Court were to find that the waiver encompasses the confirmation defense (which it should not), the trial court's damage calculation was fundamentally flawed.

The trial court calculated damages using "the higher values of each property as contended by the Guarantors' appraisers in testimony submitted during the confirmation proceedings." This approach was erroneous because:

1. The confirmation proceedings resulted in orders *refusing to confirm* the sales. The trial court in those proceedings found that RES-GA failed to prove fair market value. The appraisers' values were contested, and the confirmation courts did not adopt them.

2. Using the Guarantors' appraised values—values that were rejected in the confirmation proceedings—to calculate the deficiency rewards RES-GA for its failure to confirm the sales. This turns the confirmation statute on its head.

3. The proper measure of damages, if a deficiency were permissible, would be the fair market value as established in the confirmation proceedings or at a new hearing—not the figures advanced by one party's appraisers in proceedings that led to denial of confirmation.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Guarantors respectfully request that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals and hold that:

1. The waiver provision in the Guaranties does not encompass defenses arising from OCGA § 44-14-161;
2. Even if the waiver could be so construed, public policy prevents enforcement of a waiver of the confirmation requirement; and
3. The trial court's damage calculation was erroneous.

The judgment below should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to enter judgment for the Guarantors.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Guarantors’ appeal from the grant of summary judgment in favor of RES-GA LJY, LLC ("RES-GA"). The central issue is whether the Guarantors waived their right to assert the failure to confirm the foreclosure sales as a defense to the deficiency action pursuant to OCGA § 44-14-161. Based on the plain language of the guaranty agreements and controlling precedent, the Court finds that the waiver provision is broad enough to encompass the confirmation defense, and therefore, the trial court’s judgment must be affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Waiver Provision Encompasses Defenses Arising Under OCGA § 44-14-161**

The construction of a contract is a question of law for the court. *Lowe's Home Centers, Inc. v. Walker*, 277 Ga. 145, 148 (587 SE2d 602) (2003). The primary rule of contract interpretation is to ascertain the intention of the parties. If the language of the contract is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce the contract as written.

The Waiver provision in the Guaranties states:
> Guarantor also waives any and all rights or defenses based on suretyship or impairment of collateral including, but not limited to, any rights or defenses arising by reason of (A) the provisions of O.C.G.A. Section 10-7-24 concerning Guarantor’s right to require Lender to take action against Borrower or any “one action” or “anti-deficiency” law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency, against Guarantor... or (F) any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity other than actual payment and performance of the Indebtedness.

The Prosecution correctly argues that this language is unambiguous. Subsection (A) explicitly waives rights arising under "any 'anti-deficiency' law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency, against Guarantor." OCGA § 44-14-161 is a statute that prevents a lender from bringing a deficiency action unless the foreclosure sale is confirmed. *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016). Therefore, it falls squarely within the category of laws described in the Waiver.

The Defense contends that the canon of *ejusdem generis* requires the general phrase "any other law" to be limited to the specific category of "protections that ensure a lender pursues collateral before pursuing the guarantor," such as O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24. See *Murray v. Jenkins*, 259 Ga. 654, 656 (385 SE2d 417) (1989). However, this argument fails for two reasons.

First, the specific list in the Waiver includes "anti-deficiency law" as a standalone category, not merely as a descriptor of § 10-7-24. The confirmation statute is, by definition, an "anti-deficiency" law because it restricts the lender's ability to obtain a deficiency judgment. Second, the introductory phrase "including, but not limited to" signals that the list of examples is illustrative rather than exhaustive. To apply *ejus dem generis* to restrict the scope of the waiver here would be to ignore the parties' clear intent to waive "any and all rights or defenses."

Furthermore, Subsection (F) provides a catch-all waiver of "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity other than actual payment and performance of the Indebtedness." The Defense attempts to limit this to defects in the enforceability of the obligation, but the plain text "any defenses" admits of no such limitation. The failure to confirm a sale is a defense "at law" to the recovery of a deficiency, and thus it is waived by Subsection (F).

**B. The Waiver Is Valid Under Public Policy and Precedent**

The Defense argues that the confirmation statute creates a substantive right that cannot be waived as a matter of public policy, citing *Short v. First Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n*, 245 Ga. 434 (265 SE2d 583) (1980). This argument is directly contrary to the controlling precedent of *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818 (785 SE2d 505) (2016). In *PNC Bank*, this Court explicitly held that compliance with OCGA § 44-14-161 is a condition precedent that "a guarantor retains the contractual ability to waive." Id. at 824. The Defense's attempt to distinguish *PNC Bank* based on the specificity of the waiver language in that case is unpersuasive. The waiver in this case is equally explicit regarding "anti-deficiency" laws and "any other law," satisfying the requirement that a waiver be clear and unequivocal. See *Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. W. G. Homes, Inc.*, 119 Ga. App. 298, 304 (167 SE2d 253) (1969).

**C. The Trial Court’s Calculation of Damages**

The Defense argues that the trial court erred in calculating damages by using the higher values contended by the Guarantors’ appraisers in the confirmation proceedings. The facts indicate that the trial court entered judgment against York for $12,381,103.09 and against Drillot for $1,505,772.10. The Defense contends that using the Guarantors' own appraised values—values which were not adopted by the confirmation court—rewards RES-GA for failing to confirm the sale.

However, the Defense's argument on this point is logically inconsistent with their position on the waiver. If the Waiver is valid, as this Court finds it to be, then the strict procedural requirements of the confirmation statute are no longer the sole mechanism for determining the property's value. The trial court utilized the evidence of record, specifically the appraisals submitted by the Guarantors themselves, to calculate the deficiency. By using the "higher values" contended by the Guarantors, the trial court actually minimized the deficiency judgment, acting in the Guarantors' favor. The Defense has not demonstrated how this calculation constitutes reversible error, particularly given that the Waiver allows the lender to pursue the deficiency without the formal confirmation process.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Guarantors executed a valid and enforceable waiver that explicitly covers "anti-deficiency laws" and "any other law" preventing the lender from pursuing a deficiency claim. OCGA § 44-14-161 falls within this category. Consistent with *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818 (2016), the Guarantors waived the defense of non-confirmation. The trial court correctly granted summary judgment to RES-GA, and the Court of Appeals properly affirmed.

**JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is **AFFIRMED**.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

**I. INTRODUCTION**  
The Defense’s arguments mischaracterize the plain language of the guaranty waiver, ignore controlling precedent, and improperly seek to rewrite the contract’s unambiguous terms. The waiver’s language is clear, comprehensive, and consistent with established principles of contract interpretation.  

**II. THE WAIVER PROVISION UNAMBIGUOUSLY COVERS THE CONFIRMATION DEFENSE**  
The Defense erroneously claims the confirmation statute is not an "anti-deficiency law" within the waiver’s scope. This fails as a matter of law. The provision states "including, but not limited to," which explicitly signals that the listed examples—such as O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24 and "one action" laws—are illustrative, not exhaustive. This phrase defeats any application of *ejusdem generis*, which requires a restrictive reading of general terms following specific examples. If the waiver applied only to "procedural" protections like § 10-7-24, the phrase "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action" would be meaningless. The waiver’s purpose is to broadly waive *all* defenses to deficiency claims, and the "but not limited to" language confirms this intent.

The Defense’s attempt to narrow Subsection (F)—which waives "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity other than actual payment and performance"—is baseless. The Defense’s claim that this subsection only applies to "defects in enforceability" ignores the text’s plain meaning. It expressly references "anti-deficiency laws" and "any other law" preventing deficiency actions—language that unambiguously includes O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161.  

Public policy does not override clear contractual terms when, as here, the parties knowingly and voluntarily agreed to waive the confirmation defense. The General Assembly has recognized that guarantors may contractually waive confirmation requirements, and this Court has repeatedly enforced such waivers when the language is unambiguous. # DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Prosecution's rebuttal misapplies controlling precedent, ignores critical distinctions in statutory interpretation, and attempts to transform a broad but limited waiver into an all-encompassing release of all possible defenses. This Court should reject the Prosecution's overreaching arguments and hold that the Guarantors did not waive their rights under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161. The waiver provision, properly construed, does not encompass the confirmation defense, and even if it did, public policy and the specific language of the statute would invalidate such a waiver.

## II. THE WAIVER DOES NOT UNAMBIGUOUSLY COVER O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161

The Prosecution incorrectly asserts that O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161 is "by definition" an anti-deficiency law. This characterization conflates distinct statutory concepts and ignores the fundamental differences between procedural sequencing rules and substantive confirmation requirements.

### A. The Confirmation Statute Is Not a Traditional Anti-Deficiency Law

O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161 operates fundamentally differently from the "one-action" and "anti-deficiency" laws referenced in the waiver. Traditional anti-deficiency laws, such as O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24, establish procedural sequencing—requiring a lender to exhaust collateral before pursuing the guarantor. See O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24 (requiring lender to proceed against collateral before obtaining judgment against guarantor). These laws address the timing and order of remedies.

In contrast, O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161 is a substantive protection that conditions the very right to pursue a deficiency on judicial confirmation that the foreclosure sale brought fair market value. The statute does not merely delay the deficiency action; it prohibits it entirely unless confirmation is obtained. This Court recognized in PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016), that compliance with the confirmation statute is a "condition precedent" to the lender's ability to pursue a deficiency.

The distinction is critical: the waiver's reference to "anti-deficiency laws" and "one-action" laws addresses procedural rules about sequencing of remedies, not substantive conditions precedent that bar recovery entirely. The Prosecution's attempt to conflate these distinct concepts ignores the plain meaning of the statutory scheme.

### B. Ejusdem Generis Applies to Limit the Waiver

The Prosecution's argument that "including, but not limited to" defeats application of ejusdem generis is incorrect as a matter of law. The canon of ejusdem generis provides that general terms following specific examples should be interpreted to include only items of the same genus as those specifically enumerated. Murray v. Jenkins, 259 Ga. 654, 656 (385 SE2d 417) (1989).

Here, the specific examples in the waiver are O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24 and "one action" laws—both procedural rules requiring lenders to pursue collateral before guarantors. The general phrase "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency" must be read in context to include only laws of the same character: procedural sequencing requirements, not substantive conditions precedent like confirmation statutes.

The Prosecution's reading would render "anti-deficiency law" surplusage, as it would be encompassed within "any other law." More importantly, if the parties intended to waive all possible statutory defenses, they could have used clearer language. The waiver's reference to "anti-deficiency law" in the context of procedural rules demonstrates limited intent.

## III. SUBSECTION (F) DOES NOT WAIVE STATUTORY CONFIRMATION DEFENSES

The Prosecution erroneously argues that Subsection (F)'s reference to "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity" encompasses statutory defenses under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161. This interpretation stretches the plain language beyond recognition.

### A. "Defenses at Law" Refers to Common Law Defenses

The phrase "defenses at law" in Subsection (F) refers to common law defenses available to guarantors, such as payment, release, fraud, or lack of consideration. These are traditional legal defenses that have existed for centuries. A statutory defense created by the Georgia Legislature in 1935 is not a "defense at law" in this traditional sense.

### B. The Confirmation Defense Is Statutory, Not Common Law

O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161 was enacted by the General Assembly as part of the state's mortgage foreclosure scheme. It creates a substantive right that did not exist at common law. The defense of non-confirmation is not a traditional guarantor defense—it is a statutory protection specific to Georgia's foreclosure regime.

Subsection (F) waives "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity"—meaning defenses recognized by the common law or by equitable principles. It does not waive statutory defenses created by the legislature. The Prosecution's attempt to transform this language into a blanket waiver of all statutory protections ignores the distinction between common law and statutory rights.

## IV. THE WAIVER FAILS THE "CLEAR AND UNEQUIVOCAL" STANDARD

Even assuming the waiver language could be construed to cover O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161, it fails the "clear and unequivocal" standard required for waivers of substantive rights. Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. W. G. Homes, Inc., 119 Ga. App. 298, 304 (167 SE2d 253) (1969).

### A. The Waiver Language Is Ambiguous

The Prosecution claims the waiver is unambiguous, but this Court has recognized that guaranty waivers must be construed strictly against the drafter. The language referencing "anti-deficiency law" is reasonably susceptible to multiple interpretations—particularly when, as here, the statute in question operates differently from traditional anti-deficiency laws.

### B. PNC Bank Does Not Control This Case

While this Court held in PNC Bank, 298 Ga. 818 (2016), that waivers can encompass confirmation defenses, that case involved materially different language. In PNC Bank, the waiver specifically addressed the confirmation statute by name and included explicit language about waiver of "any and all rights" under specific code sections. The waiver here, by contrast, uses general language about "anti-deficiency laws" that does not specifically reference O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161.

This distinction is critical. When a guarantor waives a substantive statutory right, the waiver must be clear and specific. General language about "anti-deficiency laws" does not unambiguously encompass Georgia's unique confirmation statute, which operates differently from laws in other jurisdictions that bear the "anti-deficiency" label.

## V. PUBLIC POLICY LIMITS THE WAIVER

The Prosecution incorrectly argues that public policy does not limit waivers of confirmation rights. To the contrary, this Court recognized in Short v. First Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n, 245 Ga. 434 (265 SE2d 583) (1980), that certain statutory protections cannot be waived because they serve important public policy functions.

### A. The Confirmation Statute Serves Substantive Public Policy

O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161 was enacted to protect borrowers and guarantors from deficiencies that exceed the fair market value of foreclosed property. The statute reflects a legislative determination that lenders should not be permitted to recover more than the property's fair market value. This protection is especially important when, as here, the lender purchases the property at its own foreclosure sale.

### B. The Waiver Should Be Limited to Preserve Statutory Purpose

Even if the waiver language could be construed to cover O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161, public policy requires that such waivers be limited to preserve the statute's core purpose. A waiver that completely eliminates the confirmation requirement allows a lender to purchase property at a depressed price and then recover the full deficiency—precisely the evil the statute was designed to prevent.

The Prosecution's argument that "parties can contract around public policy" ignores the fundamental principle that courts will not enforce contracts that violate public policy. Short, 245 Ga. at 436. The confirmation statute reflects the General Assembly's determination that this protection is essential to the fairness of Georgia's foreclosure system.

## VI. THE DAMAGES CALCULATION IS ERRONEOUS

The Prosecution's defense of the damages calculation ignores the fundamental inconsistency in the trial court's approach.

### A. RES-GA Failed to Confirm the Sales

The undisputed facts show that RES-GA foreclosed on the properties and then sought judicial confirmation in three counties. In each case, the trial court refused to confirm the sale, finding that RES-GA failed to prove fair market value. RES-GA appealed two of these orders, and the Court of Appeals affirmed. See RES-GA LJY, LLC v. South Crestview Drive, LLC, 326 Ga. App. XXIII (Case No. A13A1810) (March 10, 2014) (unpublished); RES-GA LJY, LLC v. Y.D.I., Inc., 322 Ga. App. 607 (745 SE2d 820) (2013).

### B. The Trial Court Used Confirmation Proceedings Evidence Despite Failed Confirmation

The trial court calculated damages using values from the confirmation proceedings—specifically, the higher values contended by the Guarantors' appraisers. This approach is fundamentally inconsistent.

If confirmation were required, the proper measure would be the fair market value as determined through the confirmation process. Since RES-GA failed to obtain confirmation, the deficiency should either be barred entirely or calculated using the foreclosure sale price.

Instead, the trial court cherry-picked favorable evidence from the failed confirmation proceedings to calculate damages. This approach rewards RES-GA for failing to confirm while simultaneously using confirmation-type evidence to establish damages. The result is an end-run around the confirmation requirement that frustrates the statute's purpose.

### C. The Proper Measure Is the Foreclosure Sale Price

Under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161, when a lender fails to obtain confirmation, the proper measure of any deficiency is the amount bid at the foreclosure sale, not speculative appraised values. The trial court erred in using the Guarantors' appraised values—the very values that were rejected in the confirmation proceedings—to calculate the deficiency.

## VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that:

1. The waiver provision does not unambiguously encompass defenses arising under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161;
2. Even if the waiver could be construed to cover the confirmation defense, it fails the "clear and unequivocal" standard required for waivers of substantive statutory rights;
3. Public policy limits waivers of confirmation rights; and
4. The damages calculation was erroneous because it used values from the failed confirmation proceedings.

The Guarantors did not knowingly and voluntarily waive their rights under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-161. The judgment should be reversed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both the Prosecution (RES-GA LJY, LLC) and the Defense (Jim L. York and John A. Drillot). The central issue remains whether the Guarantors waived their right to assert the failure to confirm the foreclosure sales as a defense to the deficiency action pursuant to OCGA § 44-14-161. After careful review of the parties’ arguments and the applicable law, the Court finds that the Prosecution has successfully demonstrated that the waiver provision is broad enough to encompass the confirmation defense. The Defense’s attempts to limit the waiver through statutory interpretation canons and public policy arguments are unpersuasive in light of the contract’s plain language and controlling precedent. Therefore, the preliminary ruling is affirmed, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals is upheld.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Waiver Provision Unambiguously Encompasses OCGA § 44-14-161**

The construction of a contract is a question of law for the court. *Lowe's Home Centers, Inc. v. Walker*, 277 Ga. 145, 148 (587 SE2d 602) (2003). The primary rule of contract interpretation is to ascertain the intention of the parties. Where the language of a contract is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce the contract as written.

The Waiver provision at issue states that the Guarantor waives rights arising by reason of "any 'one action' or 'anti-deficiency' law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency, against Guarantor."

The Defense argues that OCGA § 44-14-161 is a substantive condition precedent rather than a procedural "anti-deficiency" law, and thus falls outside the scope of the waiver. This argument ignores the plain meaning of the phrase "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action." OCGA § 44-14-161 explicitly prevents a lender from bringing a deficiency action unless the foreclosure sale is confirmed. *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016). Therefore, it falls squarely within the category of laws described in the Waiver. The Defense’s distinction between "procedural sequencing" and "substantive conditions" is a semantic one not supported by the text of the contract, which broadly sweeps in any law that prevents the lender from bringing a deficiency action.

**B. The Canon of *Ejusdem Generis* Does Not Limit the Waiver**

The Defense relies on the canon of *ejusdem generis*, arguing that the general phrase "any other law" must be limited to the same class as the specific examples preceding it (i.e., procedural "one-action" laws). See *Murray v. Jenkins*, 259 Ga. 654, 656 (385 SE2d 417) (1989). However, this argument is defeated by the Waiver’s introductory phrase: "including, but not limited to."

The use of the phrase "but not limited to" is a clear textual indicator that the list of examples provided in the Waiver is illustrative rather than exhaustive. When a contract explicitly states that a list is not limited, the application of *ejusdem generis* to restrict the scope of the agreement is inappropriate. The parties intended to waive a broad range of defenses, not merely those identical to O.C.G.A. § 10-7-24. The Defense’s interpretation would render the phrase "any other law" meaningless, violating the fundamental principle that every word in a contract should be given effect.

**C. Subsection (F) Waives Statutory Defenses**

The Defense contends that Subsection (F), which waives "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity other than actual payment and performance," applies only to common law defenses and not to statutory defenses like the confirmation requirement. This argument is without merit.

The phrase "defenses at law" is a broad term that encompasses all legal defenses, whether derived from common law or statute. A statutory defense is, by definition, a defense "at law." The Defense’s attempt to limit this language to centuries-old common law concepts ignores the modern reality that statutory rights are fundamental to the legal landscape. The Waiver’s use of the unqualified phrase "any defenses" admits of no limitation to common law defenses.

**D. The Waiver Satisfies the "Clear and Unequivocal" Standard**

The Defense argues that the waiver fails the "clear and unequivocal" standard required to waive substantive rights, citing *Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. W. G. Homes, Inc.*, 119 Ga. App. 298, 304 (167 SE2d 253) (1969). The Court disagrees.

In *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818 (785 SE2d 505) (2016), this Court held that compliance with OCGA § 44-14-161 is a condition precedent that "a guarantor retains the contractual ability to waive." Id. at 824. The Defense attempts to distinguish *PNC Bank* by claiming the waiver there was more specific. However, the waiver in this case is equally, if not more, expansive. By explicitly referencing "anti-deficiency laws" and "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action," the parties clearly signaled their intent to waive the confirmation defense. The language is sufficiently clear and unequivocal to put a guarantor on notice of the rights being surrendered.

**E. Public Policy Does Not Bar the Waiver**

The Defense argues that public policy, as articulated in *Short v. First Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n*, 245 Ga. 434 (265 SE2d 583) (1980), prohibits the waiver of confirmation rights. This argument is foreclosed by the controlling precedent of *PNC Bank*. The General Assembly has recognized that guarantors may contractually waive confirmation requirements, and this Court has enforced such waivers when the language is unambiguous. The public policy concerns raised by the Defense regarding the protection of borrowers from low foreclosure sales are matters for legislative consideration, not judicial rewriting of clear contracts. Where, as here, sophisticated commercial parties voluntarily agree to a broad waiver, the Court will not invalidate it based on a generalized public policy objection.

**F. The Trial Court’s Calculation of Damages Was Proper**

The Defense challenges the trial court’s calculation of damages, arguing that it was improper to use the Guarantors’ own appraised values from the failed confirmation proceedings. The facts show that the trial court entered judgment using the higher values contended by the Guarantors’ appraisers.

The Defense’s argument on this point is logically inconsistent. If the Waiver is valid, the lender is entitled to a deficiency judgment calculated based on the debt and the value of the collateral. The trial court utilized the evidence of record—the appraisals submitted by the Guarantors themselves—to determine that value. By using the "higher values" contended by the Guarantors, the trial court actually minimized the deficiency judgment, acting in the Guarantors' favor. The Defense has not demonstrated how this calculation constitutes reversible error. The alternative calculation proposed by the Defense—using the foreclosure sale price—would have resulted in a *higher* deficiency judgment, which contradicts their stated interests.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Guarantors executed a valid and enforceable waiver that explicitly covers "anti-deficiency laws" and "any other law" preventing the lender from pursuing a deficiency claim. OCGA § 44-14-161 falls within this category. The Defense’s attempts to limit the waiver through *ejusdem generis* or distinctions between common law and statutory defenses are rejected. Consistent with *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818 (2016), the Guarantors waived the defense of non-confirmation. The trial court correctly granted summary judgment to RES-GA, and the Court of Appeals properly affirmed.

**JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is **AFFIRMED**.

## I. THE WAIVER PROVISION DOES NOT UNAMBIGUOUSLY ENCOMPASS OCGA § 44-14-161

The Court's ruling concludes that the phrase "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency" unambiguously includes OCGA § 44-14-161. The Defense respectfully disagrees with this characterization.

**OCGA § 44-14-161** operates as a substantive condition precedent that must be satisfied before a lender may pursue any deficiency action. As this Court recognized in **PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith**, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016), the confirmation requirement is not merely procedural—it is a statutory mandate that goes to the very authority of the lender to maintain a deficiency claim. The statute provides that no deficiency judgment shall be allowed if the foreclosure sale has not been confirmed. This is fundamentally different from procedural "one-action" or "anti-deficiency" laws that merely regulate the sequencing of legal remedies.

The Waiver provision specifically references "O.C.G.A. Section 10-7-24 concerning Guarantor's right to require Lender to take action against Borrower" and "any 'one action' or 'anti-deficiency' law." These are procedural laws that determine when and how a lender may bring an action. **OCGA § 44-14-161**, by contrast, creates a substantive right that must be satisfied before the lender acquires any standing to pursue a deficiency. The distinction is not "semantic" but rather goes to the heart of the nature of the right being waived.

The Court in **PNC Bank** specifically noted that the confirmation requirement "is a condition precedent to the lender's ability to pursue a guarantor for a deficiency after a foreclosure has been conducted." Id. at 824. A condition precedent is not equivalent to an anti-deficiency law. The former requires affirmative compliance as a prerequisite to maintaining an action; the latter limits the remedies available even after compliance with procedural requirements. The Waiver's reference to "anti-deficiency laws" should be interpreted in light of this established distinction.

The Court's ruling rejects the application of **ejusdem generis** based on the phrase "but not limited to" in the Waiver. The Defense maintains that this analysis erred in its application of interpretive principles.

The canon of **ejusdem generis** provides that general words following specific words are construed to include only items of the same class as those specified. See **Murray v. Jenkins**, 259 Ga. 654, 656 (385 SE2d 417) (1989). The specific examples in the Waiver—"one action" or "anti-deficiency" law—are procedural in nature. To accept the Plaintiff's interpretation would be to read the Waiver as if it said "any law whatsoever" rather than the more specific "any 'one action' or 'anti-deficiency' law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action." **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the closing statement filed by the Defense (Jim L. York and John A. Drillot). **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Waiver Provision Encompasses Substantive Conditions Precedent**

The Defense argues that OCGA § 44-14-161 is a "substantive condition precedent" rather than a procedural "anti-deficiency" law, and therefore falls outside the scope of the Waiver. While the Defense correctly notes that *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016), characterized the confirmation requirement as a condition precedent, this distinction does not remove the statute from the Waiver’s reach.

The Waiver explicitly covers "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency." The Defense’s semantic distinction between "procedural sequencing" and "substantive conditions" ignores the functional reality of the statute. OCGA § 44-14-161 operates to *prevent* a lender from bringing a deficiency action unless specific steps are taken. Whether the prevention arises from a procedural bar or a failure to satisfy a condition precedent, the effect is the same: the lender is prevented from bringing the action. The plain language of the Waiver captures this effect. The parties agreed to waive any law that acts as a bar to the deficiency claim, and the confirmation statute is precisely such a bar.

**B. The Canon of *Ejusdem Generis* Does Not Override Express Contractual Language**

The Defense reiterates its argument that the canon of *ejusdem generis* limits the general phrase "any other law" to the same class as the specific examples of "one action" or "anti-deficiency" laws. See *Murray v. Jenkins*, 259 Ga. 654, 656 (385 SE2d 417) (1989). The Defense contends that because the specific examples are procedural, the general term cannot encompass substantive laws like the confirmation statute.

This argument fails because the Waiver explicitly states that the list of examples is "including, but not limited to." As established in the preliminary ruling, this phrase is a clear textual indicator that the parties intended the list to be illustrative, not exhaustive. When a contract includes such expansive language, the application of *ejusdem generis* to restrict the agreement is inappropriate. The Defense’s interpretation would require the Court to ignore the parties' explicit instruction that the list is not limited, thereby violating the fundamental principle of contract interpretation that every word should be given effect. See *Lowe's Home Centers, Inc. v. Walker*, 277 Ga. 145, 148 (587 SE2d 602) (2003).

**C. "Defenses at Law" Includes Statutory Defenses**

The Defense argues that Subsection (F), which waives "any defenses given to guarantors at law or in equity," applies only to common law defenses and not to statutory rights. This interpretation is untenable. The phrase "defenses at law" is a broad legal term of art that encompasses all defenses arising from the law, including statutory rights. Statutes are a primary source of "the law." To interpret "defenses at law" as excluding statutory defenses would be to ignore the modern legal landscape where statutory rights pervade commercial lending. The Waiver’s use of the unqualified phrase "any defenses" confirms the parties’ intent to waive a broad spectrum of defenses, including those rooted in statute.

**D. The Waiver Meets the "Clear and Unequivocal" Standard**

The Defense contends that the Waiver fails the "clear and unequivocal" standard required to waive substantive rights, citing *Fidelity & Deposit Co. of Maryland v. W. G. Homes, Inc.*, 119 Ga. App. 298, 304 (167 SE2d 253) (1969). The Court disagrees.

The Waiver explicitly references "anti-deficiency laws" and "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action." This language is sufficiently specific to put a sophisticated commercial guarantor on notice that they are waiving rights that could otherwise bar the lender from seeking a deficiency. The Defense’s argument that the Waiver is too general because it does not specifically cite OCGA § 44-14-161 by number is unpersuasive. The Waiver describes the *effect* of the statute—preventing the lender from bringing an action—which is sufficient to identify the rights being surrendered. As held in *PNC Bank*, the confirmation requirement is waivable, and the language here meets the threshold for clarity.

**E. Public Policy Does Not Bar Enforcement**

The Defense argues that public policy prohibits the waiver of confirmation rights, relying on *Short v. First Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n*, 245 Ga. 434 (265 SE2d 583) (1980). However, this argument is foreclosed by the controlling precedent of *PNC Bank*. In *PNC Bank*, this Court explicitly recognized that guarantors may contractually waive the confirmation requirement. The General Assembly has not prohibited such waivers in commercial guaranties.
