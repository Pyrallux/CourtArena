COURT ARENA LOG - Case 139
FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1
Applying these standards, and based on the paltry evidence before us, defendants have not presented a compelling case of prejudice. To begin with, they cite only newspaper articles and editorials. There is no *108evidence whatsoever of prejudiced radio or television coverage or public sentiment generally. But see Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723; Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1487. Furthermore, the newspaper articles, while prone to hyperbole, are not inflammatory. Granted, some of the headlines describe the defendants of a “prostitution ring” or “sex ring.” But, the articles make an effort to point out that the defendants have not been found guilty; rather, they are alleged to have committed the crimes. In fact, most of the articles contain an attempt to reproduce testimony and arguments from court, heard in a public forum open to all. That is, they contain information that an average citizen would be privy to were he to go to court himself. Finally, the defendants reproduced only six articles, a far cry from the type of overwhelming saturation which can lead to a presumption of prejudice. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

Your Honor, the prosecution respectfully submits that the trial court correctly denied defendants' motion for change of venue because defendants utterly failed to meet the extraordinary burden required to establish either actual or presumed prejudice. This is not merely a case of insufficient evidence—it is a case where defendants' arguments contradict established legal standards and the factual record before the court.  

The law is clear: presumed prejudice—where the pretrial publicity is so pervasive and inflammatory that impartial jurors cannot be secured—is a "rare" remedy reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); see also *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). Defendants cannot satisfy this standard. They presented only six newspaper articles—none of which were inflammatory or sensationalized. As the trial court properly noted, these articles repeatedly emphasized that defendants were *alleged* perpetrators, not convicted criminals, and merely reproduced testimony from public court proceedings that any citizen could observe firsthand. This falls far short of the "overwhelming saturation" required to trigger presumed prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537; *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).  

Compare this to *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where the Supreme Court reversed a conviction after a defendant’s televised, in-custody confession—recorded without counsel—was broadcast repeatedly to tens of thousands of community members. That case involved "pervasive" exposure to a single, highly prejudicial piece of evidence that "tainted the community" to such an extent that "any subsequent court proceeding... could be but a hollow formality." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727. Here, defendants presented no evidence of radio, television, or social media coverage—only six newspaper articles. No juror interviews were conducted, and no evidence of public sentiment was submitted. As the trial court observed, "the defendants cited only newspaper articles and editorials. There is no evidence whatsoever of prejudiced radio or television coverage or public sentiment generally." *Rideau* and its progeny demand far more to establish presumed prejudice.  

The cultural bias argument is equally without merit. The trial court correctly rejected the notion that "bigotry from a 'culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here'" created a prejudicial atmosphere. The record contains no evidence of widespread societal prejudice against foreign-born defendants—only isolated newspaper editorials, which the court properly recognized do not equate to community-wide bias. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725–28 (1961) (requiring juror responses in voir dire to discern actual prejudice; absent such evidence, claims of bias remain speculative).  

Moreover, defendants’ request for a change of venue is fundamentally impractical in American Samoa’s small, insular community. As the trial court rightly held, "we have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll." Transferring the case would yield no meaningful change because "the jury would be selected from the same pool," and "the compact social structure of this island community" means media exposure would infect the entire territory. *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (noting change of venue is unsuitable in territories with limited jury pools). A.S.C.A. § 46.0601—cited by defendants—merely permits "physical change[s] of venue" for logistical convenience, not a true change of jurisdiction like Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 21. It was enacted long before jury trials were constitutionally mandated in American Samoa (*King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977)), and has never been used for criminal venue transfers.  

The trial court’s solution—allowing a detailed voir dire questionnaire in English and Samoan to identify any prejudiced jurors—was precisely the appropriate remedy. As *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273, confirms, "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages," a change of venue is not the only option; courts must "assure a fair trial" through tailored procedures. Here, the trial court properly recognized that "only then [during voir dire] can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment."  

In sum, defendants failed to present even a scintilla of evidence meeting the high bar for presumed prejudice. Their arguments ignore controlling precedent, the factual record, and the practical realities of American Samoa’s judicial system. The trial court’s denial of the motion was legally sound and should be affirmed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE HIGH COURT OF AMERICAN SAMOA

---

**Defendant-Appellants Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang respectfully submit this opening response in opposition to the prosecution's argument and in support of their motion for change of venue.**

---

The prosecution's argument fundamentally mischaracterizes both the legal standards governing pretrial publicity and the factual record before this Court. While the prosecution correctly recites the demanding standard for presumed prejudice, it incorrectly concludes that the defendants have failed to meet that standard. Furthermore, the prosecution dismisses the cultural bias argument without adequate consideration of the unique demographic and social context of American Samoa. Finally, the prosecution's reliance on the impracticality of a change of venue conflates logistical challenges with constitutional requirements. This Court should reverse the trial court's denial and grant the requested change of venue.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT APPLIED AN INCORRECT LEGAL STANDARD BY REQUIRING "OVERWHELMING SATURATION"

The prosecution cites *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), for the proposition that defendants must demonstrate "overwhelming saturation" of pretrial publicity. However, the trial court's application of this standard failed to consider the *quality* of the publicity alongside its quantity. In *Coleman*, the Eleventh Circuit acknowledged that presumed prejudice analysis requires examination of "the nature and extent of the pretrial publicity," not merely a numerical count of articles. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court erred by focusing exclusively on the number of articles while ignoring the inflammatory nature of the coverage.

The prosecution argues that the articles "merely reproduced testimony from public court proceedings." This characterization is misleading. The trial court itself acknowledged that "some of the headlines describe the defendants of a 'prostitution ring' or 'sex ring.'" Such labeling is inherently prejudicial, as it communicates to potential jurors that the defendants are engaged in organized criminal activity—regardless of whether the articles technically note that the defendants are "alleged" perpetrators. The use of the term "ring" carries connotations of organized, systematic criminal enterprise that simple denials of guilt cannot overcome in the minds of average jurors.

Moreover, in *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), the Supreme Court emphasized that presumed prejudice may be established when "the community's attitude [is] one of hostiltiy" toward the defendant. *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725. The prosecution's argument that no evidence of "public sentiment" was submitted ignores the reality that in a small island community like American Samoa, public sentiment is expressed through the very newspaper outlets that defendants cited. The publication of biased editorials and letters to the editor constitutes evidence of community sentiment.

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON RIDEAU IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), demonstrates the type of extreme circumstances required for presumed prejudice. However, the prosecution's reading of *Rideau* is overly restrictive. In *Rideau*, the Supreme Court held that "due process of law" requires a trial "in a fair and impartial tribunal." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727. The Court reversed not merely because of the specific content of the broadcast, but because the community had been exposed to "a spectacle" that tainted the subsequent proceedings. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727.

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Rideau* on the grounds that it involved "a defendant's televised, in-custody confession" broadcast "repeatedly to tens of thousands." However, the principle underlying *Rideau*—that community-wide exposure to prejudicial content creates a presumption of unfairness—is not limited to televised confessions. As the Court stated in *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966), "the trial court should have sure that the jury's verdict was based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources." *Sheppard*, 384 U.S. at 351. The cumulative effect of multiple newspaper articles describing defendants as operators of a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring" constitutes precisely the type of "outside source" contamination that *Sheppard* cautioned against.

Furthermore, the prosecution overlooks that the *degree* of prejudice required may be satisfied by less extensive coverage in smaller communities. In *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), the First Circuit recognized that in territories with limited jury pools, the impact of pretrial publicity may be more concentrated. *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. American Samoa's community is smaller and more insular than the communities in many cases where presumed prejudice was found. The prosecution's argument that more extensive coverage is required ignores the unique characteristics of this territory.

## III. THE CULTURAL BIAS ARGUMENT WAS PROPERLY RAISED AND REQUIRES SERIOUS CONSIDERATION

The prosecution dismisses the defense's argument regarding "bigotry from a 'culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here'" as "without merit." This dismissal is erroneous. The trial court acknowledged that defendants Wei Ki Fang and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals, while defendant Siituu Sanerivi was born in Western Samoa. The defense submitted evidence of newspaper editorials and letters to the editor expressing prejudicial views toward foreign-born individuals.

The prosecution argues that "isolated newspaper editorials" do not "equate to community-wide bias." However, in a community of American Samoa's size, newspaper editorials and letters to the editor are not "isolated"—they represent the primary forum for public discourse. The trial court itself noted that "the island's media outlets are virtual monopolies." Under these circumstances, publication of prejudicial content cannot be dismissed as the work of a "very limited section of society." To the contrary, in a small community with limited media sources, such publications may represent the dominant narrative.

The prosecution cites *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), for the proposition that "claims of bias remain speculative" without juror responses in voir dire. However, *Irvin* addressed the question of *actual* prejudice, which requires juror responses. The question before this Court is presumed prejudice, which may be established without juror interviews precisely because the atmosphere created "belies any claim of impartiality." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727; see also *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1543. The prosecution's attempt to impose an *Irvin*-style showing onto a presumed prejudice analysis conflates two distinct legal standards.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S "IMPRACTICALITY" ARGUMENT CONFLATES LOGISTICS WITH CONSTITUTIONAL REQUIREMENTS

The prosecution echoes the trial court's reasoning that "transferring the case would yield no meaningful change" because "we have but one jury roll" and "the jury would be selected from the same pool." This reasoning is constitutionally deficient. The Constitution requires a fair trial, not a convenient one. *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 722 (quoting *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)).

The prosecution relies on *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), for the proposition that change of venue is "unsuitable" in territories with limited jury pools. However, *Moreno* held only that change of venue was not the *sole* remedy available—not that it was unavailable entirely. The court explicitly stated that "we need only assure a fair trial" through "alternative remedies." *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. The trial court interpreted this dictum as authorization to deny change of venue entirely, but the question is whether the defendants have demonstrated constitutional prejudice, not whether alternative remedies exist.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If transfer within American Samoa is futile because the entire territory is tainted, then the appropriate remedy might be transfer to a different jurisdiction entirely—not denial of any transfer. The prosecution cites A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 as authority for the proposition that only local transfers are available, but the statute itself authorizes the Chief Justice to "order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa." A.S.C.A. § 46.0601. The statute does not expressly *prohibit* transfer outside American Samoa; it simply does not address the question. The prosecution's argument that the statute was "enacted long before criminal jury trials were constitutionally mandated" cuts against, not in favor of, its position. If the statute was enacted before jury trials were required, it was not intended to address the full range of constitutional protections that attach to criminal jury trials.

Finally, the prosecution's emphasis on the "small, insular community" actually supports the defense's position. The trial court stated that "because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest corners of the Territory." This observation demonstrates precisely why a change of venue is appropriate: if the entire territory is contaminated, the only way to secure an impartial jury is to draw from outside the territory.

## V. THE PROPOSED REMEDY OF VOIR DIRE IS INADEQUATE

The prosecution supports the trial court's proposed remedy of a voir dire questionnaire, arguing that this approach "was precisely the appropriate remedy." However, the proposed voir dire process cannot cure the constitutional defect.

First, the trial court's proposed questionnaire is severely limited. The court stated that the questionnaire "shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets." These restrictions effectively prevent exploration of the precise nature and extent of potential jurors' exposure to pretrial publicity and any bias arising therefrom.

Second, in *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966), the Supreme Court held that "the trial court should have taken strong measures to ensure that the jury [was] uninfluenced by the publicity." *Sheppard*, 384 U.S. at 355. The Court emphasized that "the judge should have removed from the jury room and from the jurors themselves all reading material and all 'off the record' comments in the presence of the jury." *Sheppard*, 384 U.S. at 358. A voir dire questionnaire cannot address the underlying problem of community-wide prejudice; it can only identify some of its victims after the fact.

Third, the prosecution's argument that voir dire allows the court to "meaningfully gauge the public's sentiment" puts the cart before the horse. The purpose of due process is to ensure a fair trial *before* prejudice infects the jury, not to detect prejudice after it has taken hold. As the Supreme Court stated in *Rideau*, "what was done in this case was done in the community where the trial was to be had." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726. The community had already been exposed to the prejudicial material, and no subsequent voir dire could undo that exposure.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants respectfully request that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of their motion for change of venue and grant the requested relief. The cumulative effect of the pretrial publicity—including headlines describing the defendants as operators of a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring," combined with evidence of cultural bias against foreign-born individuals in the community—creates a presumption of prejudice that the prosecution has failed to rebut. The practical difficulties of transferring venue within American Samoa do not override the constitutional requirement of a fair trial before an impartial tribunal.

The prosecution's argument that the defendants failed to meet their "extraordinary burden" ignores the fact that the burden exists precisely because the remedy is extraordinary—not because the underlying constitutional injury is any less real. The defendants have demonstrated that the pretrial publicity, combined with the unique characteristics of American Samoa's small and insular community, creates a substantial risk that the defendants cannot receive a fair trial. The trial court's denial of the motion was erroneous, and this Court should reverse.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the defendants’ appeal from the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, argue that the trial court erred in denying their motion for a change of venue based on alleged pre-trial publicity and cultural bias. After reviewing the trial court’s order, the prosecution’s opening argument, and the defense’s opening response, the Court affirms the denial of the motion.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is a fundamental requirement of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either actual prejudice or presumed prejudice. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th Cir. 1999). Presumed prejudice is a "rare" finding reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); see also *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendants Failed to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court applied an incorrect standard by focusing on the quantity of publicity rather than its quality. Specifically, they contend that the use of terms like "prostitution ring" and "sex ring" in headlines created an inherently prejudicial atmosphere that satisfies the *Coleman* standard.

The Court is not persuaded. While the defense correctly notes that presumed prejudice analysis considers the "nature and extent" of the publicity, *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490, the trial court’s finding that the coverage was not "inflammatory" is supported by the record. The articles cited by the defendants explicitly stated that the defendants were alleged perpetrators and reproduced testimony from open court proceedings. This distinguishes the case from *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where the Supreme Court found presumed prejudice where a defendant’s televised confession was broadcast repeatedly to the community. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726-27. Here, there was no televised confession, nor was there evidence of radio or television coverage. The submission of only six newspaper articles falls far short of the "overwhelming saturation" required to presume prejudice in the absence of such extreme facts. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

**B. The Evidence of Cultural Bias Is Insufficient**

The defendants argue that the trial court ignored the cultural bias against foreign-born individuals, asserting that newspaper editorials in a small community represent the dominant narrative rather than isolated views.

The trial court properly characterized the prejudiced opinions expressed in the editorials as belonging to a "very limited section of society." The defense’s assertion that these editorials equate to community-wide bias is speculative. As noted in *Irvin*, actual prejudice is typically "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28. Without juror interviews or broader evidence of public sentiment, the defense cannot transform a handful of letters to the editor into a presumption of a biased community. The trial court’s determination that the publicity did not create a "hostile" community attitude, *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725, was not an abuse of discretion.

**C. The Impracticality of Change of Venue in American Samoa**

The defense contends that the trial court and prosecution conflated logistical challenges with constitutional requirements, arguing that if the entire territory is tainted, the only remedy is transfer outside of American Samoa.

This argument fails because the defendants have not established that the entire territory is tainted. The threshold for presumed prejudice has not been met. Consequently, the Court need not address the constitutional feasibility of transferring the case outside the territory. Furthermore, the trial court’s observation regarding the "one jury roll" in American Samoa is factually accurate and legally sound. As recognized in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), a change of venue is often unsuitable in territories with limited jury pools. The statute cited by the defense, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, authorizes physical changes of location for convenience, not a change of jurisdiction to protect against prejudice. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977).

**D. Voir Dire Is an Adequate Remedy**

The defense argues that the proposed voir dire questionnaire is inadequate because it restricts inquiries into substantive arguments and hypothetical facts. However, the purpose of voir dire is to determine the existence of bias, not to educate the jury on the law or test their reactions to hypothetical case scenarios. The trial court’s offer to allow a questionnaire in both English and Samoan, coupled with the possibility of individual voir dire, provides a sufficient mechanism to identify any actual prejudice that may exist. This aligns with the principle that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages... we need only assure a fair trial." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defendants have not met the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice. The pre-trial publicity, while featuring some hyperbolic language, was not so pervasive or inflammatory as to deny the defendants a fair trial. The trial court’s denial of the motion for change of venue is affirmed. The case shall proceed with the implemented voir dire procedures to ensure the selection of an impartial jury.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the defense's position and ignores critical distinctions in the applicable legal standards. While the defense acknowledges the heavy burden required to establish presumed prejudice, the prosecution erroneously conflates the quantity of publicity with its qualitative impact on the community's perception of the defendants. The defense maintains that the trial court applied an overly restrictive standard that fails to account for the unique circumstances of this case, including the small community dynamics of American Samoa, the inflammatory nature of the media coverage, and the cultural bias evidenced in the published materials. This rebuttal will address each of the prosecution's arguments and demonstrate that the defense has presented sufficient evidence to warrant a change of venue or, at minimum, a more thorough examination of the prejudice issue.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Prosecution Misapplies the Standard for Presumed Prejudice by Overemphasizing Quantity at the Expense of Quality

The prosecution argues that the defense cannot establish presumed prejudice because only six newspaper articles were submitted, falling short of the "oversaturation" requirement under Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985). This argument misreads the applicable standard and ignores the qualitative nature of the publicity at issue.

The Coleman court explicitly stated that presumed prejudice analysis considers "the nature and extent of the publicity," not merely the volume of coverage. Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court acknowledged that some headlines described the defendants as operating a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring," language that carries inherent inflammatory character regardless of quantity. The prosecution's reliance on the presence of disclaimers that defendants are "alleged" perpetrators fails to recognize that such disclaimers cannot neutralize the prejudicial impact of sensationalized headlines. As the Supreme Court noted in Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), the question is whether the publicity "so infected the trial forum" that the proceedings became a "hollow formality." Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727.

The prosecution's attempt to distinguish this case from Rideau based on the absence of a televised confession ignores the fundamental principle that presumed prejudice can arise from various forms of inflammatory publicity, not only televised confessions. In United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), the First Circuit recognized that presumed prejudice analysis must be flexible enough to address the "atmosphere" created by publicity, not constrained to specific media formats. Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. The prosecution's rigid focus on quantity contradicts this flexible approach.

Furthermore, the prosecution overlooks the cumulative effect of the publicity when combined with the cultural bias expressed in the editorial pages. Even assuming six articles alone might be insufficient, the trial court acknowledged that these articles appeared alongside editorials expressing "narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions" against foreign-born individuals. The combination of sensationalized crime reporting with explicit expressions of cultural prejudice creates an atmosphere distinct from either form of publicity alone. The prosecution's atomistic analysis improperly isolates the quantity of articles from their qualitative impact.

### B. The Defense Has Established Sufficient Evidence of Cultural Bias to Support a Finding of Presumed Prejudice

The prosecution argues that the cultural bias evidence is "speculative" because no juror interviews have been conducted. This argument improperly shifts the burden of proof and ignores the distinction between actual and presumed prejudice.

Presumed prejudice exists precisely to avoid the necessity of proving actual juror bias through interviews when the community atmosphere is so tainted that impartiality cannot be assumed. As the Supreme Court established in Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), presumed prejudice may be established without showing that individual jurors were actually prejudiced, because "the atmosphere created by the news media" can be "so pervasive" that it "negatives [the] claim of impartiality." Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728. The defense has presented evidence of published editorial opinions expressing hostility toward foreign-born individuals in a community where, as the trial court acknowledged, "both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals."

The prosecution's reliance on the trial court's characterization of these opinions as representing "a very limited section of society" ignores the practical realities of media consumption in American Samoa. The trial court itself noted that the island's media outlets are "virtual monopolies." In such an environment, even limited publication reaches a disproportionate segment of the community, and the absence of countervailing perspectives amplifies the impact of prejudiced viewpoints. The prosecution's argument that the defense must prove "community-wide bias" through juror interviews conflates actual prejudice with presumed prejudice and effectively reads the latter doctrine out of existence.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument that Irvin requires actual juror interviews to establish actual prejudice is correct but inapplicable here, where the defense seeks to establish presumed prejudice. The Irvin Court specifically noted that "in many cases, the publicity surrounding the case... would seem to be quite less than that in which the community was saturated" in cases where presumed prejudice was found. Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725. The defense need not wait for juror interviews when the published materials themselves demonstrate the existence of prejudicial sentiment in the community.

### C. The Prosecution Incorrectly Concludes That Change of Venue Is Infeasible in American Samoa

The prosecution argues that United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), establishes that change of venue is unsuitable in American Samoa. This argument inverts the Moreno holding and ignores the critical distinction between feasibility and constitutional requirement.

In Moreno, the First Circuit addressed the feasibility of venue transfer in Puerto Rico but explicitly held that "the Constitution does not require a change of venue; it requires only a fair trial." Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. The court noted that alternative remedies, including voir dire and change of venue within the territory, might suffice to ensure fairness. The prosecution conflates the question of what remedies are practically available with the question of what the Constitution requires.

The prosecution correctly notes that American Samoa has "but one jury roll" and that transferring the case to another part of the islands would not change the jury pool. However, this observation does not resolve the constitutional question. If the entire territory is indeed tainted by pre-trial publicity, as the defense contends, then the inability to transfer to a different venue outside American Samoa does not excuse the constitutional violation—it highlights the need for alternative remedies that the trial court has not adequately addressed.

The prosecution also misreads the trial court's statutory analysis. The court acknowledged that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 might authorize physical relocation of court sessions "for convenience," but correctly noted that this statute was not designed to address prejudice-based venue changes. However, the court's acknowledgment that the statute does not contemplate the type of transfer the defense seeks does not mean no constitutional remedy exists. The Constitution requires "a fair trial," not a specific remedy. Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. If change of venue is infeasible, the court must explore alternative remedies, which may include expanded voir dire, change of venue to a different location within the territory, or potentially a bench trial.

The prosecution's argument that the defense must first establish presumed prejudice before the court need address the venue transfer issue puts the cart before the horse. The question of whether presumed prejudice exists cannot be fully separated from the question of what remedies would be available if it were found. If no effective remedy exists for presumed prejudice, the appropriate response may be to require a higher showing of prejudice rather than to deny the motion outright.

### D. The Proposed Voir Dire Procedures Are Constitutionally Inadequate to Address the Presumed Prejudice Claim

The prosecution argues that voir dire is a constitutionally adequate remedy under Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333 (1966). This argument ignores the specific circumstances of this case and the limitations of the proposed voir dire procedures.

In Sheppard, the Supreme Court recognized that voir dire can serve as a remedy for pre-trial publicity, but only where the trial court takes "affirmative steps" to ensure an impartial jury. Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 355. The Court emphasized that the trial court must "take strong measures" to insulate the jury from prejudicial publicity. The trial court's proposed voir dire questionnaire, while perhaps adequate under different circumstances, contains significant limitations that the prosecution glosses over.

First, the trial court specifically limited the questionnaire to "yes or no" responses. This restriction prevents prospective jurors from explaining the nature and extent of their exposure to pre-trial publicity, making it impossible to assess whether that exposure created actual bias. As the Supreme Court noted in Irvin, actual prejudice can only be discerned by reviewing "the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28. The trial court's yes-or-no restriction undermines this assessment.

Second, the trial court prohibited questions "touching on any anticipated instructions," "the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations," and "substantive arguments of the case." These restrictions prevent the defense from exploring whether prospective jurors have formed preconceptions about the nature of the case that would prevent them from fairly considering the evidence. While the prosecution is correct that voir dire should not educate the jury on the law, it should probe for existing bias.

Third, the trial court offered "individual voir dire if necessary," but the criteria for determining when individual voir dire is "necessary" remain unclear. The prosecution assumes individual voir dire will be available if needed, but the trial court's order provides no guarantee. The defense is entitled to more certainty regarding the protections available.

Finally, the prosecution's reliance on Sheppard is misplaced because the circumstances here are distinguishable. In Sheppard, the Supreme Court found that the trial court had failed to take adequate measures to protect the jury from pre-trial publicity. Here, the defense argues that the publicity itself, combined with cultural bias, has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice that voir dire alone cannot cure. The prosecution cannot rely on Sheppard to justify minimal voir dire procedures when those procedures may be insufficient to address the claimed prejudice.

The prosecution has failed to rebut the defense's showing that the combination of sensationalized media coverage and explicit cultural bias in published editorials creates a substantial risk that the defendants cannot receive a fair trial in American Samoa. While the defense acknowledges the heavy burden required to establish presumed prejudice, that burden has been met when the qualitative nature of the publicity, the small community dynamics, and the cultural context are properly considered.

The prosecution's arguments regarding the infeasibility of venue transfer and the adequacy of voir dire conflate distinct constitutional questions and improperly restrict the remedies available to address proven prejudice. The Constitution requires a fair trial, and the defense is entitled to meaningful assurance that this requirement will be met.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the defendants’ appeal from the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, seek reversal of the trial court’s order, arguing that the court erred in its analysis of presumed prejudice, failed to adequately account for cultural bias in a small community, and proposed constitutionally inadequate voir dire procedures. After careful consideration of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The right to an impartial jury is a fundamental component of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either actual prejudice or presumed prejudice. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th Cir. 1999). Presumed prejudice is a "rare" finding reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); see also *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The burden rests entirely on the defendant to show that the publicity is so prejudicial that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured. *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 800 (1975).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendants Failed to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the trial court and prosecution improperly focused on the quantity of publicity rather than its qualitative impact. Specifically, the defense contends that the use of terms like "prostitution ring" and "sex ring" in headlines, combined with editorial expressions of cultural bias, creates an atmosphere of presumed prejudice regardless of the number of articles.

The Court is not persuaded. While the defense correctly notes that *Coleman* requires an analysis of both the "nature and extent" of the publicity, *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490, the trial court’s finding that the coverage was not "inflammatory" is supported by the record. The articles cited by the defendants explicitly stated that the defendants were "alleged" perpetrators and largely reproduced testimony from open court proceedings. This factual reporting distinguishes the case from *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where the Supreme Court found presumed prejudice based on a televised, in-custody confession broadcast repeatedly to the community. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726-27. As the prosecution notes, there is no evidence here of a televised confession or similar "spectacle."

The defense’s reliance on the "qualitative" impact of the headlines is insufficient to meet the "heavy burden" of presumed prejudice. The Supreme Court has held that due process "is not violated by a 'trial atmosphere' which is 'perfectly normal'... even though the case is one that has attracted widespread public attention." *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 722-23 (quoting *Stroble v. California*, 343 U.S. 181, 197 (1952)). The submission of only six newspaper articles, without evidence of radio or television saturation, falls far short of the "overwhelming saturation" required to presume prejudice in the absence of extreme facts like those in *Rideau*. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

**B. The Evidence of Cultural Bias Is Insufficient to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the "virtual monopoly" of media in American Samoa amplifies the impact of prejudiced editorials, creating a presumption of community-wide bias against foreign-born defendants. The defense contends that the trial court erred by characterizing these views as those of a "very limited section of society."

The Court finds that the defense’s argument conflates the potential for influence with the legal standard for presumed prejudice. While the defense correctly observes that presumed prejudice exists to avoid the necessity of proving actual juror bias when the community atmosphere is tainted, *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728, the record here does not support such a finding. The existence of prejudiced editorials, while protected speech, does not equate to a community atmosphere that "belies any claim of impartiality." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court properly noted that the airing of such views exposes their absurdity and does not warrant the assumption that the entire jury pool shares them.

The defense’s argument that the media monopoly necessitates a finding of presumed prejudice is speculative. As noted in *Irvin*, actual prejudice is typically "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28. Without juror interviews or broader evidence of public sentiment beyond the editorials themselves, the defense cannot transform a handful of letters into a presumption of a biased community. The trial court’s determination that the publicity did not create a "hostile" community attitude was not an abuse of discretion.

**C. The Impracticality of Change of Venue in American Samoa**

The defense argues that the prosecution and trial court have conflated logistical challenges with constitutional requirements, asserting that if the entire territory is tainted, the inability to transfer the case outside of American Samoa constitutes a denial of due process.

This argument fails because the defendants have not established that the entire territory is tainted. The threshold for presumed prejudice has not been met. Consequently, the Court need not address the constitutional feasibility of transferring the case outside the territory. Furthermore, the trial court’s observation regarding the "one jury roll" in American Samoa is factually accurate and legally sound. As recognized in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), a change of venue is often unsuitable in territories with limited jury pools. The statute cited by the defense, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, authorizes physical changes of location for convenience, not a change of jurisdiction to protect against prejudice. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). The Constitution requires a fair trial, not a specific remedy that is geographically impossible within the territory.

**D. Voir Dire Is an Adequate Remedy**

The defense argues that the proposed voir dire questionnaire is constitutionally inadequate because it restricts inquiries to "yes or no" responses and prohibits questions about substantive arguments or hypothetical facts. The defense contends these restrictions prevent the meaningful exploration of bias required by *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966).

The Court finds the trial court’s proposed procedures sufficient to identify any actual prejudice. The purpose of voir dire is to determine the existence of bias, not to educate the jury on the law or test their reactions to hypothetical case scenarios. The trial court’s offer to allow a questionnaire in both English and Samoan, coupled with the explicit provision for "individual voir dire if necessary," provides a sufficient mechanism to identify any actual prejudice that may exist.

The defense’s concern that "yes or no" responses are inadequate is mitigated by the trial court’s assurance that "based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps... including individual voir dire if necessary." This process allows the court to screen jurors initially and then conduct a more probing inquiry of those who indicate exposure to the publicity. This aligns with the principle that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages... we need only assure a fair trial." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273. The restrictions on "anticipated instructions" and "substantive arguments" are proper to ensure that voir dire does not become a forum for educating the jury or previewing the evidence.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defendants have not met the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice. The pre-trial publicity, while featuring some hyperbolic language, was not so pervasive or inflammatory as to deny the defendants a fair trial. The combination of newspaper articles and editorials does not rise to the level of the "spectacle" presented in *Rideau*. The trial court’s denial of the motion for change of venue is affirmed. The case shall proceed with the implemented voir dire procedures to ensure the selection of an impartial jury.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the defendants’ appeal from the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, seek reversal of the trial court’s order, arguing that the court erred in finding no presumed prejudice, failed to account for the amplifying effect of a media monopoly in a small territory, and proposed constitutionally inadequate voir dire procedures. After reviewing the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The right to an impartial jury is a fundamental requirement of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either actual prejudice or presumed prejudice. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th Cir. 1999). Presumed prejudice is a "rare" finding reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); see also *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The burden rests entirely on the defendant to show that the publicity is so prejudicial that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured. *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 800 (1975).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendants Failed to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the trial court and prosecution improperly focused on the quantity of publicity rather than its qualitative impact. Specifically, the defense contends that the use of terms like "prostitution ring" and "sex ring" in headlines, combined with editorial expressions of cultural bias, creates an atmosphere of presumed prejudice regardless of the number of articles.

The Court is not persuaded. While the defense correctly notes that *Coleman* requires an analysis of both the "nature and extent" of the publicity, *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490, the trial court’s finding that the coverage was not "inflammatory" is supported by the record. The articles cited by the defendants explicitly stated that the defendants were "alleged" perpetrators and largely reproduced testimony from open court proceedings. This factual reporting distinguishes the case from *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where the Supreme Court found presumed prejudice based on a televised, in-custody confession broadcast repeatedly to the community. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726-27. As the prosecution notes, there is no evidence here of a televised confession or similar "spectacle."

The defense’s reliance on the "qualitative" impact of the headlines is insufficient to meet the "heavy burden" of presumed prejudice. The Supreme Court has held that due process "is not violated by a 'trial atmosphere' which is 'perfectly normal'... even though the case is one that has attracted widespread public attention." *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 722-23 (quoting *Stroble v. California*, 343 U.S. 181, 197 (1952)). The submission of only six newspaper articles, without evidence of radio or television saturation, falls far short of the "overwhelming saturation" required to presume prejudice in the absence of extreme facts like those in *Rideau*. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

**B. The Evidence of Cultural Bias Is Insufficient to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the "virtual monopoly" of media in American Samoa amplifies the impact of prejudiced editorials, creating a presumption of community-wide bias against foreign-born defendants. The defense contends that the trial court erred by characterizing these views as those of a "very limited section of society."

The Court finds that the defense’s argument conflates the potential for influence with the legal standard for presumed prejudice. While the defense correctly observes that presumed prejudice exists to avoid the necessity of proving actual juror bias when the community atmosphere is tainted, *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728, the record here does not support such a finding. The existence of prejudiced editorials, while protected speech, does not equate to a community atmosphere that "belies any claim of impartiality." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court properly noted that the airing of such views exposes their absurdity and does not warrant the assumption that the entire jury pool shares them.

The defense’s argument that the media monopoly necessitates a finding of presumed prejudice is speculative. As noted in *Irvin*, actual prejudice is typically "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28. Without juror interviews or broader evidence of public sentiment beyond the editorials themselves, the defense cannot transform a handful of letters into a presumption of a biased community. The trial court’s determination that the publicity did not create a "hostile" community attitude was not an abuse of discretion.

**C. The Impracticality of Change of Venue in American Samoa**

The defense argues that the prosecution and trial court have conflated logistical challenges with constitutional requirements, asserting that if the entire territory is tainted, the inability to transfer the case outside of American Samoa constitutes a denial of due process.

This argument fails because the defendants have not established that the entire territory is tainted. The threshold for presumed prejudice has not been met. Consequently, the Court need not address the constitutional feasibility of transferring the case outside the territory. Furthermore, the trial court’s observation regarding the "one jury roll" in American Samoa is factually accurate and legally sound. As recognized in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), a change of venue is often unsuitable in territories with limited jury pools. The statute cited by the defense, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, authorizes physical changes of location for convenience, not a change of jurisdiction to protect against prejudice. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). The Constitution requires a fair trial, not a specific remedy that is geographically impossible within the territory.

**D. Voir Dire Is an Adequate Remedy**

The defense argues that the proposed voir dire questionnaire is constitutionally inadequate because it restricts inquiries to "yes or no" responses and prohibits questions about substantive arguments or hypothetical facts. The defense contends these restrictions prevent the meaningful exploration of bias required by *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966).

The Court finds the trial court’s proposed procedures sufficient to identify any actual prejudice. The purpose of voir dire is to determine the existence of bias, not to educate the jury on the law or test their reactions to hypothetical case scenarios. The trial court’s offer to allow a questionnaire in both English and Samoan, coupled with the explicit provision for "individual voir dire if necessary," provides a sufficient mechanism to identify any actual prejudice that may exist.

The defense’s concern that "yes or no" responses are inadequate is mitigated by the trial court’s assurance that "based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps... including individual voir dire if necessary." This process allows the court to screen jurors initially and then conduct a more probing inquiry of those who indicate exposure to the publicity. This aligns with the principle that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages... we need only assure a fair trial." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273. The restrictions on "anticipated instructions" and "substantive arguments" are proper to ensure that voir dire does not become a forum for educating the jury or previewing the evidence.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defendants have not met the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice. The pre-trial publicity, while featuring some hyperbolic language, was not so pervasive or inflammatory as to deny the defendants a fair trial. The combination of newspaper articles and editorials does not rise to the level of the "spectacle" presented in *Rideau*. The trial court’s denial of the motion for change of venue is affirmed. The case shall proceed with the implemented voir dire procedures to ensure the selection of an impartial jury.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
