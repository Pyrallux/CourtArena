**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding three claims raised by the Appellant: (1) whether the trial court erred in admitting certain testimony by Officer Shugrue; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third claim. Having reviewed the record, the arguments of the Prosecution and the Defense, and the applicable law, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Admission of Officer Shugrue’s Testimony**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in allowing Officer Shugrue to comment on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions regarding sleep. The Court acknowledges that Officer Shugrue’s voluntary comment on the Defendant’s silence was improper. However, the admissibility of this testimony does not mandate reversal if the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

Under federal constitutional standards, a trial error is subject to harmless error review, and reversal is required only if the error had a "substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury's verdict." *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). Similarly, Florida law requires reversal only if the error can be shown to have "contributed to the conviction." *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129, 1135 (Fla. 1986).

Applying these standards to the facts at hand, the evidence of the Defendant's guilt was overwhelming. The record indicates that the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests and provided a breathalyzer reading of .12 percent. Under § 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding .10 establishes *prima facie* evidence that the Defendant was under the influence of alcohol. Given the strength of this scientific evidence and the Defendant's failure to complete field sobriety exercises, the Court finds that the improper comment by Officer Shugrue did not influence the jury's verdict. Therefore, the error is deemed harmless.

**B. Discovery Violation Regarding Breathalyzer Inspection Reports**

The Appellant contends that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to produce the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Defense agrees with the Prosecution that no violation occurred.

The administrative rules cited in the record, specifically § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules, do not impose an affirmative duty on the State to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing as part of its *prima facie* case. Instead, these rules serve as a predicate for the admissibility of the reading *should* the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment.

The facts provided indicate that the Defendant did not challenge the condition of the breathalyzer equipment at trial. Consequently, the State was not under a duty to produce the inspection reports. Because the procedural mechanism requiring disclosure was not triggered by the Defendant, the State cannot be held to have committed a discovery violation.

**C. Imposition of Court Costs**

The State has conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. The Court accepts this concession. Accordingly, the cause must be remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing costs and to conduct appropriate proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant in the future.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the conviction on the merits, finding that any error regarding Officer Shugrue's testimony was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt and that no discovery violation occurred. The Court further orders that the case be remanded to the Trial Court for the sole purpose of vacating the order imposing court costs.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the Prosecution's response to the Court's preliminary ruling. While the Prosecution correctly acknowledges that Officer Shugrue's testimony was "improper and calculated to prejudice the Defendant," the Defense contends that the Prosecution fails to meet the heavy burden required to establish harmless error beyond a reasonable doubt. Additionally, the Defense maintains its position regarding the discovery violation and addresses the court costs issue.

## II. REBUTTAL TO HARMLESS ERROR ARGUMENT

### A. The Prosecution's Burden Under Harmless Error Doctrine

The Prosecution correctly cites *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), for the proposition that reversal is required only if the error had a "substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury's verdict." However, the Prosecution overlooks a critical principle: the burden rests entirely upon the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. at 24.

In *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129, 1135 (Fla. 1986), the Florida Supreme Court emphasized that the harmless error doctrine requires the appellate court to determine whether the error "contributed to the conviction." The Court further noted that this determination cannot be made merely by weighing the evidence but requires a careful examination of the improper testimony's potential impact on the jury.

### B. The Improper Testimony Was Not Harmless

The Prosecution argues that the evidence of guilt was "overwhelming" because the Defendant failed roadside sobriety tests and had a breathalyzer reading of .12 percent. However, this characterization ignores the specific nature of Officer Shugrue's improper comment.

The record indicates that Officer Shugrue voluntarily commented on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions regarding how much sleep the Defendant had on the night preceding the arrest. This comment directly implicated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. As this Court acknowledged in its ruling, such conduct from an officer with seventeen years of experience is particularly troubling because "it can lead to the loss of the case."

The Prosecution's reliance on the breathalyzer reading as sufficient evidence ignores a fundamental principle: the admissibility of breathalyzer evidence requires proper predicate. The reading of .12 percent only establishes *prima facie* evidence of intoxication under § 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), if the test was properly administered and the equipment was functioning correctly. The Defense was entitled to challenge these foundational requirements without the State having already benefited from improper testimony that bolstered the prosecution's case.

### C. The Prosecution's Characterization of Evidence Is Overstated

The Prosecution contends that the evidence was "overwhelming" because the Defendant "failed to perform roadside sobriety tests." However, this assertion requires scrutiny. The very fact that the Defendant failed to perform these tests was established through Officer Shugrue's testimony—the same witness whose improper comment regarding the Defendant's silence tainted the proceedings. The Prosecution cannot rely on testimony from the same witness to establish the "overwhelming" nature of the evidence while simultaneously arguing that the same witness's improper comment was harmless.

Furthermore, field sobriety tests are subjective assessments that juries frequently question. The weight given to such tests depends heavily on the credibility of the testing officer. When that officer has made improper comments about the Defendant's exercise of constitutional rights, the jury's assessment of the officer's credibility—and consequently the weight given to the roadside test results—becomes fundamentally compromised.

### D. The Prosecution Fails to Establish Harmlessness

The Prosecution has not demonstrated that Officer Shugrue's comment "could not have influenced the jury's verdict." To the contrary, in a DUI case where the State's evidence depends heavily on the officer's observations and the breathalyzer reading, an improper comment suggesting the Defendant had something to hide by refusing to answer questions could easily have tipped the scales toward conviction.

The Prosecution's argument rests on the assumption that the jury would have convicted regardless of the improper testimony. This assumption is speculative and cannot satisfy the State's burden of proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. *See State v. Godbee*, 419 So. 2d 411, 414 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982) (holding that the State must prove that error did not contribute to conviction).

## III. REBUTTAL TO DISCOVERY VIOLATION ARGUMENT

### A. The Defense Did Not Waive Its Right to Discovery

The Prosecution, in agreement with the trial court, argues that no discovery violation occurred because the Defendant "did not challenge the condition of the breathalyzer equipment at trial." The Defense contends this reasoning is fundamentally flawed.

The Defendant was charged with DUI under § 316.1931, Florida Statutes. To establish a *prima facie* case, the State was required to prove that the Defendant was driving while under the influence of alcohol to the extent that his normal faculties were impaired, or that the Defendant had a blood alcohol level of .10 or more. The breathalyzer reading of .12 percent was central to the State's case.

Under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, the State is required to disclose evidence favorable to the defendant, including evidence that could be used for impeachment or that undermines the reliability of critical evidence. *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963). The breathalyzer inspection reports fall squarely within this category because they could reveal mechanical defects or calibration errors that would undermine the reliability of the .12 reading.

The Defense should not be required to specifically challenge the condition of equipment before receiving discovery to which it is entitled. Such a requirement would effectively require the Defense to prove a negative—that the equipment was not properly maintained—without access to the very documents that would establish this fact.

### B. The Administrative Rules Do Not Limit Discovery

The trial court cited § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules for the proposition that the State need not prove the machinery was fit for proper testing unless the Defendant challenges the equipment. However, the Defense notes that administrative rules governing evidentiary predicates do not limit the defendant's constitutional right to discovery.

The Florida Rules of Criminal Procedure mandate broad discovery in criminal cases. *See Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220*. These rules reflect the fundamental principle that the pursuit of truth and fairness requires that both parties have access to relevant evidence. The Prosecution cannot rely on administrative convenience to deny the Defense access to material evidence.

### C. The Defense Was Entitled to the Inspection Reports

The monthly inspection reports for the breathalyzer machine used in this case constitute material evidence. These reports could have revealed:

1. Prior calibration errors or failures
2. Maintenance issues that could affect accuracy
3. Inconsistencies between the machine's performance and regulatory standards

Such evidence could have been used to challenge the reliability of the .12 reading, to cross-examine the testing officer, and to present alternative theories to the jury. The Defense's inability to access this evidence deprived the Defendant of a fair trial.

DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986). While the Defense argues that the officer’s taint undermines the "unassailable" nature of the evidence referenced in *State v. Godbee*, 419 So. 2d 411 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982), the record reflects that the Defendant did not challenge the condition or admissibility of the breathalyzer equipment at trial. Because the reading is statutory *prima facie* evidence of intoxication, and the Defense failed to challenge the reliability of the machine itself, the conviction rests on independent, scientific proof rather than solely on Officer Shugrue’s testimony. The improper comment regarding the Defendant’s sleep, while unfortunate, does not undermine the mechanical accuracy of the breathalyzer reading. Therefore, the State has met its burden to show that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**B. Discovery Violation Regarding Breathalyzer Inspection Reports**

The Appellant contends that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to produce the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports, arguing that the "upon challenge" framework of the administrative rules creates a "catch-22" that violates due process under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963).

The Court acknowledges the Defense's constitutional concerns. However, the specific procedural framework governing this issue is established by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules. As stated in the case background, these rules do not require the State to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing to establish a *prima facie* case. Instead, the rules serve as a predicate for the admissibility of the reading *should* the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment.

The Defense argues that it could not challenge the equipment without first seeing the reports. However, the rules place the duty on the State to produce the documentation only upon a challenge by the Defendant. The facts indicate that the Defendant did not challenge the condition of the breathalyzer equipment at trial. To find a *Brady* violation, the evidence must be material to guilt or punishment. Without a challenge to the equipment's condition, and absent any specific allegation that the reports would have revealed exculpatory information, the State’s failure to produce the reports does not constitute a violation of due process. The State complied with the procedural requirements of the administrative rules as applied to the facts of this case.

**C. Imposition of Court Costs**

The State has conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. The Court accepts this concession. Accordingly, the cause must be remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing costs and to conduct appropriate proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant in the future.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the conviction on the merits. The Court finds that any error regarding Officer Shugrue's testimony was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt in light of the undisputed breathalyzer reading of .12, which constitutes *prima facie* evidence of intoxication under Florida law. Furthermore, the Court finds no discovery violation occurred, as the State’s duty to produce inspection reports was not triggered by the Defendant under the applicable administrative rules. The case is remanded solely for the purpose of vacating the order imposing court costs.
