FACTS:
In this case we consider, among other issues, a question previously left open (see Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] [hereafter Richmond}) regarding the validity of a taxation scheme enacted for the apparent purpose of avoiding the supermajority voter approval requirement imposed by a 1978 initiative measure (Prop. 13) with respect to any “special taxes” sought to be imposed by “cities, counties and special districts” (see Cal. Const., art. XIII A, § 4 [hereafter section 4]). At issue here is the validity of a sales tax (retail transaction and use tax) imposed on sales occurring in San Diego County (hereafter the County) for the purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities (hereafter justice facilities) for the County. We conclude the tax is invalid because it was not approved by at least two-thirds of the County’s voters, as required by section 4.

In 1987, in express recognition of the County’s need for improved courtrooms and jails, the Legislature passed an act (Gov. Code, §§ 26250-26285) creating the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (hereafter the Agency) and setting forth the Agency’s obligations. Under the act, the Agency was charged with adopting a tax ordinance imposing a supplemental sales tax of one-half of 1 percent throughout the County for the purpose of financing the construction of justice facilities. (Id., §§ 26267, 26271-26275.) The act provided for a county wide election held for the purpose of approving the tax ordinance by simple majority vote. (Id., §§ 26271, 26273.) The act also provided that the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax. (Id., § 26283.)

*6At an election held in June 1988, the County’s voters approved the tax ordinance by a bare (50.8 percent) majority vote. Plaintiffs, being County taxpayers, filed the present suit to challenge the validity of the tax. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 863.) As pertinent here, the complaint asserted the tax violated the supermajority vote requirements of both section 4 and Government Code sections 53720-53730 (added by Prop. 62, discussed below). Prior to trial, the tax went into operation; tax revenues have been collected and accumulated pending final decision.

The trial court found in plaintiffs/taxpayers’ favor, concluding the tax constituted a deliberate and unavailing attempt to circumvent section 4 and its requirement of two-thirds voter approval of special taxes imposed by special districts such as the Agency.

The Court of Appeal disagreed and reversed the trial court’s judgment declaring the tax invalid. The appellate court acknowledged that the act creating the Agency “gives the Agency no significant governmental discretion . . . with respect to how the tax revenues will be spent. ” Nonetheless, deeming itself bound by Richmond, the Court of Appeal reasoned that section 4 is inapplicable to districts such as the Agency which have no power to levy a property tax. The appellate court further concluded that application to the Agency of a similar statutory supermajority voter approval provision in Government Code section 53722 would be improper as an attempted local tax referendum.

In Richmond, this Court considered whether the Los Angeles County Transportation Commission was subject to Section 4's two-thirds voter approval requirement. The Richmond majority held that "section 4 applies only to 'special districts' as defined in section 1 of article XIII A—that is, to districts that have 'the power to levy a property tax.'" Richmond, 31 Cal.3d at 208. The majority reasoned that "the drafters of article XIII A clearly contemplated that the term 'special district' would be understood in the context of the property tax system" and that Section 4 was "intended to supplement, not replace, the property tax limitations already placed on local governments by article XIII A." The Agency in this case concededly possesses no power to levy a property tax. The plaintiff argues that Richmond was wrongly decided and urges this Court to adopt the dissenting view. However, the defense submits that the Richmond majority's interpretation represents the correct construction of Section 4. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The central question presented in this matter is whether the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (hereafter the Agency) is subject to the supermajority voter approval requirements of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4 (hereafter section 4) for the imposition of a special tax. The Agency argues that because it lacks the power to levy property taxes, it falls outside the definition of a "special district" under section 4, as interpreted by *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. The Plaintiffs, as County taxpayers, contend that the Agency is a political subdivision imposing a special tax and that to exclude it from section 4 would sanction a direct evasion of the Constitution's supermajority mandate.

Based on the record provided, the arguments of counsel, and the controlling constitutional framework, the Court finds that the tax imposed by the Agency is invalid. The Agency is a political subdivision subject to the constraints of section 4, and the tax, having been approved by only a simple majority, fails to meet the constitutional threshold for enactment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Agency is a "Political Subdivision" Subject to Section 4**

The Defense argues vigorously that the Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of section 4 because it possesses no power to levy property taxes. This argument relies heavily on the majority opinion in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941], which limited the application of section 4 to entities with property tax authority. While the Court of Appeal felt bound by this reasoning, this Court finds that such a rigid textualist interpretation undermines the fundamental purpose of Proposition 13.

Section 4 provides that "special taxes" shall be imposed only after approval by two-thirds of the voters of the "political subdivision" imposing the tax. Cal. Const., art. XIII A, § 4. The Agency is undeniably a political subdivision. It was created by an act of the Legislature (Gov. Code, §§ 26250-26285) to exercise governmental authority—specifically, the power to tax. The fact that the Legislature, in its enabling act, chose to deny the Agency property tax power (Gov. Code, § 26283) does not strip the Agency of its status as a political subdivision. To hold otherwise would allow the Legislature to bypass the constitutional will of the people simply by labeling a taxing entity as a "special district" without property tax powers.

**B. The Tax is a "Special Tax" Requiring Supermajority Approval**

The tax at issue is a supplemental sales tax imposed for the "purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities." Gov. Code, § 26267. This is the quintessential definition of a "special tax"—a tax dedicated to a specific purpose rather than one raised for general governmental use.

The Prosecution correctly argues that the Defense's interpretation creates a loophole that eviscerates section 4. If a county or city can avoid the two-thirds requirement simply by creating a separate agency—like the Agency here—to impose a sales tax for a specific project, the supermajority requirement of Proposition 13 would be rendered a nullity. As noted in the dissenting opinion in *Richmond*, the majority's interpretation "will allow cities and counties to avoid the two-thirds requirement for special taxes simply by imposing a sales tax instead of a property tax." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 220 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] (Kaus, J., dissenting).

The Agency was created with "no significant governmental discretion" regarding the spending of the revenues; it exists solely to levy this specific tax. This functional reality confirms that the Agency is an instrumentality of the County used to finance capital projects. Therefore, the Agency must be held to the same standard of voter approval as the County itself.

**C. Rejection of the "Property Tax Power" Limitation**

The Defense contends that applying section 4 to the Agency would extend the constitutional provision beyond its intended scope, which they argue is limited to the property tax system. However, section 4 by its own terms applies to "special taxes" imposed by "cities, counties and special districts." It does not limit its application to property taxes. The voters who enacted Proposition 13 sought to limit the ability of local governments to raise revenue for specific purposes without broad consensus. The text of the initiative, along with the historical context of tax reform, supports the conclusion that the supermajority requirement applies to all special taxes, regardless of the entity imposing them or the mechanism of collection.

The Court of Appeal's reliance on *Richmond* to uphold the tax is misplaced. While *Richmond* addressed the definition of a special district in the context of that specific case, it did not—and could not—sanction a constitutional evasion. The Agency's lack of property tax power is a statutory contrivance that cannot override the constitutional mandate for supermajority approval on special taxes.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Agency is a political subdivision imposing a special tax. The tax was approved by 50.8% of the voters, which is insufficient to meet the two-thirds requirement mandated by California Constitution article XIII A, section 4. To validate this tax would be to allow the precise loophole Proposition 13 was designed to close.

Accordingly, the preliminary ruling is that the sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is invalid. The judgment of the trial court is reinstated, and the judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

May it please the Court, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in opposition to the plaintiff's challenge to the validity of the sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency. The Defense maintains that the tax is valid and should be upheld.

The plaintiff asks this Court to ignore nearly four decades of binding precedent and to judicially rewrite the California Constitution. Specifically, the plaintiff urges this Court to hold that the Agency—a creature of statute that possesses no property tax power and was created for the sole purpose of financing justice facilities—is nevertheless a "special district" subject to the two-thirds voter approval requirement of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4. This interpretation contradicts the plain meaning of the constitutional text, ignores the binding precedent established by the California Supreme Court in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941], and would lead to absurd results that the Legislature could never have intended.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The Defense submits that the following facts are material to the legal analysis:

The California Legislature created the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency in 1987 through Government Code sections 26250-26285. The Legislature expressly found that the County needed improved courtrooms and jails, and it created the Agency as a special-purpose financing entity to address this need.

Critically, the Legislature granted the Agency **no property tax power**. Government Code section 26283 provides that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax." The Agency was designed solely to impose a supplemental sales tax of one-half of 1 percent throughout the County for the purpose of financing the construction of justice facilities.

At an election held in June 1988, the County's voters approved the tax ordinance by a vote of 50.8 percent—a clear majority. This approval came after the Legislature specifically provided for a simple majority vote in the enabling legislation. The tax subsequently went into operation, and revenues have been collected and accumulated pending final resolution of this dispute.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Binding Precedent: *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond*

The central legal question in this case was definitively answered by the California Supreme Court in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. In that case, the Court held that the term "special districts" as used in California Constitution article XIII A, section 4 refers to "special districts" as that term was understood under prior law—namely, entities that possess the power to levy property taxes.

The *Richmond* majority expressly stated that "special districts" within the meaning of section 4 are "those entities which, under prior law, were authorized to levy property taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. This interpretation was based on the historical understanding of the term and the constitutional text.

The Defense acknowledges that the *Richmond* majority noted that a question remained open regarding "whether a taxation scheme enacted for the apparent purpose of avoiding the supermajority requirement is valid." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. However, the majority's statement that this question was "left open" was precisely that—an acknowledgment that the specific question before this Court had not been definitively resolved. The Defense submits that the proper resolution of that question is found in the *Richmond* majority's framework: if an entity lacks property tax power, it is not a "special district" under section 4.

### B. The Plain Meaning of the Constitutional Text

The Defense submits that the plaintiff's interpretation contradicts the plain meaning of the constitutional text. California Constitution article XIII A, section 4 provides in pertinent part:

> "Cities, counties and special districts shall not impose, extend or increase any special tax unless approved by two-thirds of the voters voting on an election to impose such special tax."

The Constitution does not define "special districts" in section 4. When a term is used in the Constitution without definition, the Court must give it the meaning it bore at the time of the Constitution's adoption. As the *Richmond* majority explained, at the time Proposition 13 was adopted, "special districts" were understood to be entities authorized to levy property taxes. *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941].

The plaintiff argues that the Agency is a "political subdivision" and therefore falls within section 4. However, the constitutional text specifically refers to "cities, counties and **special districts**"—not all political subdivisions. The Legislature's creation of the Agency as an entity **without property tax power** reflects a deliberate legislative judgment that this entity was not intended to be a "special district" in the constitutional sense.

### C. The Agency's Lack of Property Tax Power Is Determinative

The Defense submits that the Agency's lack of property tax power is dispositive. The Legislature expressly provided that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than" the sales tax at issue. Government Code § 26283. This is not an accident or an evasion—it is a deliberate policy choice that reflects the Legislature's understanding of the constitutional requirements.

The plaintiff argues that allowing the Agency to impose a sales tax without a two-thirds vote would "eviscerate" Proposition 13. However, this argument ignores the fundamental purpose of Proposition 13, which was specifically directed at **property taxes**. As the *Richmond* majority observed, Proposition 13 was "an initiative measure authored and sponsored by taxpayers" that was "intended to provide substantial relief from property taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 206 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941].

The supermajority requirement of section 4 was designed to apply to entities that could previously have imposed property taxes as special taxes. The Agency, which has never possessed property tax authority, falls outside this category.

### D. The Dissent in *Richmond* Does Not Control

The plaintiff places great reliance on the dissenting opinion in *Richmond*. However, dissenting opinions do not establish binding precedent. As the majority explicitly stated, the interpretation of "special districts" to include only those with property tax power was the holding of the Court. The dissent's warning about potential evasion does not change this result.

Moreover, the plaintiff's argument that the dissent's predicted "loophole" has now materialized assumes that the Legislature acted in bad faith. The Defense submits that the Legislature's decision to deny the Agency property tax power was a legitimate policy choice, not an attempt to evade the Constitution. The Agency was created to address a specific public need—financing justice facilities—and the Legislature determined that a sales tax was the appropriate mechanism.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

### A. The Plaintiff Misreads *Richmond*

The plaintiff claims that *Richmond* "left open" the question whether a taxation scheme enacted to avoid the supermajority requirement is valid. However, the *Richmond* majority did not leave this question open in the manner the plaintiff suggests. Rather, the majority established the framework that governs this case: entities without property tax power are not "special districts" under section 4.

The plaintiff's argument that the Agency was created for the "express purpose" of avoiding the supermajority requirement mischaracterizes the legislative history. The Agency was created to finance justice facilities—a legitimate public purpose. The Legislature's decision to deny the Agency property tax power reflected a policy judgment about the appropriate scope of the Agency's authority, not an attempt to evade constitutional requirements.

### B. The Plaintiff Cannot Rewrite the Constitution

The plaintiff asks this Court to add words to the Constitution that do not exist. The Constitution says "special districts"—not "all entities that exercise the taxing power." The plaintiff cannot bootstrap the Agency into the category of "special districts" simply because it possesses some taxing authority.

The *Richmond* majority's interpretation was based on the historical understanding of the term "special districts" at the time Proposition 13 was adopted. This Court should follow that interpretation rather than invent a new definition that contradicts the constitutional text.

### C. The Plaintiff's "Loophole" Argument Is Overstated

The plaintiff warns that invalidating the Agency's tax would open a "loophole" that would allow local governments to avoid Proposition 13's requirements. However, this argument proves too much. The Legislature could always create entities with different tax powers, and the question of which entities fall within section 4 must be resolved by reference to the constitutional text and historical understanding—not by fear of speculative abuse.

Moreover, the plaintiff's argument would lead to absurd results. Under the plaintiff's theory, any entity created by the Legislature to impose a tax—even an entity with no property tax authority—would be subject to section 4. This interpretation would extend the supermajority requirement far beyond what the Constitution contemplates.

---

## V. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

The Defense respectfully submits that the sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is valid. The Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4, because it lacks property tax power. The tax was properly approved by a majority of the County's voters, as provided by the Legislature in the enabling act.

The plaintiff asks this Court to ignore binding precedent and to judicially rewrite the Constitution. This Court should decline to do so. The California Supreme Court in *Richmond* definitively interpreted section 4 to apply only to entities with property tax authority. This interpretation is correct, and it compels the conclusion that the Agency's tax is valid.

**WHEREFORE**, the Defense respectfully prays that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeal, declaring the sales tax imposed by the Agency to be valid and enforceable.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has carefully considered the closing arguments presented by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The central issue for determination is whether the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency) is subject to the supermajority voter approval requirements of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4 (hereafter section 4). The Defense argues that binding precedent, specifically *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941], categorically exempts the Agency from section 4 because it lacks the power to levy property taxes. The Prosecution contends that to accept the Defense's interpretation is to endorse a constitutional loophole that the *Richmond* majority explicitly left open for resolution.

Upon review of the statutory framework, the constitutional provisions, and the applicable precedent, the Court finds that the tax imposed by the Agency is invalid. While the Agency lacks property tax power, it is a political subdivision created for the express purpose of imposing a special tax. To allow the Legislature to circumvent the supermajority requirement of section 4 by creating a special-purpose entity with no property tax authority would frustrate the will of the electorate as expressed in Proposition 13.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Left Open" Question of *Richmond* Controls This Dispute**

The Defense relies heavily on the majority holding in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941], which interpreted "special districts" in section 4 to mean only those entities with the power to levy property taxes. The Defense asserts that this definition is binding and dispositive.

However, the Defense’s reliance on *Richmond* is incomplete. The *Richmond* majority expressly acknowledged that the case before them did not involve, and therefore did not decide, "a question previously left open... regarding the validity of a taxation scheme enacted for the apparent purpose of avoiding the supermajority voter approval requirement." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. The present case presents precisely that question. The Agency was created by the Legislature (Gov. Code, §§ 26250-26285) with a specific limitation: "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax." Gov. Code, § 26283. This statutory restriction strips the Agency of the property tax power that the *Richmond* court deemed determinative of "special district" status.

The Court cannot apply the *Richmond* definition mechanically to validate a scheme that appears designed to evade the Constitution. Where the application of a precedent would sanction a circumvention of the Constitution's core mandates, the Court must address the substantive constitutional question left unresolved in that precedent.

**B. The Agency is a Statutory Instrument of Constitutional Evasion**

The Prosecution correctly characterizes the Agency as a "shell" entity. The facts show that the Agency has "no significant governmental discretion" and possesses "no tax power other than" the sales tax at issue. Gov. Code, § 26283. The Agency’s sole function is to levy a tax for the specific purpose of financing justice facilities.

The Defense argues that the Legislature’s denial of property tax power to the Agency was a "deliberate policy choice." While the Legislature has broad authority to create political subdivisions, it does not have the authority to contravene the Constitution by statutory fiat. The Constitution reserves to the voters the power to approve "special taxes" imposed by "cities, counties and special districts." Cal. Const., art. XIII A, § 4. If the Legislature could simply create a new entity, label it a "financing agency," deny it property tax power, and thereby exempt it from section 4, the supermajority requirement would be eviscerated.

The dissenting opinion in *Richmond* warned of this exact outcome, noting that the majority's interpretation "will allow cities and counties to avoid the two-thirds requirement for special taxes simply by imposing a sales tax instead of a property tax." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 220 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] (Kaus, J., dissenting). The Agency is the embodiment of that warning. The Court will not endorse a interpretation of the Constitution that permits its own evasion through the creation of dependent special-purpose entities.

**C. The Tax is a "Special Tax" Requiring Supermajority Approval**

The tax imposed by the Agency is a "special tax" because the revenues are earmarked for the "purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities." Gov. Code, § 26267. It is not a general tax deposited into the general fund.

The Defense argues that because the Agency is not a "special district" under the *Richmond* definition, the tax is not subject to section 4. However, the Agency is undeniably a political subdivision of the state, exercising the sovereign power of taxation. The Constitution's limitation on this power applies to the function being performed—the imposition of a special tax—not merely to the label of the entity performing it. To hold otherwise would allow the Legislature to bypass the constitutional rights of the voters by simply renaming the taxing authority.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Defense asks the Court to prioritize a judicial gloss—the property tax power requirement—over the plain purpose of the Constitution. The Court declines to do so. The Agency is a political subdivision created to impose a special tax. The fact that the Legislature denied it property tax power does not exempt it from the supermajority requirements of section 4; rather, it highlights the legislative intent to circumvent those requirements.

The sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency was approved by only 50.8% of the voters. This fails to meet the constitutional threshold of two-thirds. Therefore, the tax is invalid.

The judgment of the trial court is reinstated, and the judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed.
