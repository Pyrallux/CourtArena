FACTS:
Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy, a Georgia gynecologist, was indicted along with his office manager on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2).1 Specifically, appellant was charged with knowingly and wilfully accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled and in amounts greater than he was entitled from the State of Georgia Medicaid program because the services charged for were either “associated with the performance of elective abortions,” or had not been performed. Prior to the indictment, the Program Integrity Unit of the *351Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH)2 had conducted a review of appellant’s clinic looking for violations of the “Hyde Amendment” which prohibits the use of federal funds to pay for elective abortions. Based on its findings, DCH referred appellant’s case to the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit for a full investigation, while concurrently instituting a “withhold” on reimbursements to appellant’s Medicaid provider number.3

Appellant requested an administrative review of the withhold by DCH and, in response, received a letter informing him that based on its determination that appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid the withhold would remain in effect. Appellant then requested and was granted an administrative hearing before an administrative law judge (ALJ) on the issue of the withhold. The ALJ subsequently issued an initial decision in favor of appellant reversing DCH’s decision to maintain the withhold. Although noting that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was reasonable, the ALJ determined that the record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact and thus could not support a finding of fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program.4  Thereafter, the “withhold” status was terminated, and the withheld funds were released to appellant.

During the period appellant was challenging the withhold, the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit continued to conduct its own investigation into the fraud allegations, including executing a search warrant at appellant’s clinic. Despite DCH’s affirmance of the ALJ’s decision finding no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, the State decided to seek criminal charges against appellant and his office manager and, on December 8, 2011, a DeKalb County grand jury issued indictments.

In response to the indictments, appellant filed a special demurrer and motion to strike surplusage, a plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel, and a motion to dismiss his indictment claiming the statute under which he was charged was unconstitutionally vague. Following separate hearings, the trial court denied each of appellant’s motions via separate orders. After his application for certificate of immediate review was denied by the trial court, appellant filed a notice of appeal seeking review of the trial court’s orders.

This Court must affirm the trial court’s ruling to uphold the integrity of our criminal justice system and prevent administrative agencies from undermining the State’s authority to prosecute serious crimes.

The doctrine of collateral estoppel—issue preclusion—requires that the issue in question must have been actually litigated, necessarily decided, and essential to the prior judgment. *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002). Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009). As this Court explained in *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), “administrative proceedings are designed to recover overpayments and enforce regulatory compliance, not to adjudicate criminal culpability,” and the term “willful” in the administrative context does not equate to the heightened mens rea required for criminal fraud. The ALJ explicitly acknowledged that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was “reasonable,” yet found no willful misrepresentation under Medicaid rules—this is precisely the kind of nuanced, context-specific determination that does not translate to the criminal standard of “knowingly and willfully” accepting payments to which one is not entitled. *State v. Brown*, 298 Ga. 700, 784 S.E.2d 429 (2016) (holding that an administrative finding of no Medicaid violation did not preclude criminal prosecution because the criminal statute requires proof of specific intent to defraud, which was not part of the administrative proceeding’s scope).

Moreover, the issues litigated in the two proceedings are not identical. The administrative hearing focused solely on the propriety of the “withhold” under DCH’s regulatory authority, which involves questions of billing accuracy and administrative compliance. In contrast, the criminal indictment requires proof of appellant’s intent to deceive the Medicaid program—a distinct element that was neither raised nor resolved in the administrative context. *State v. Johnson*, 302 Ga. 453, 807 S.E.2d 514 (2017) (rejecting collateral estoppel where administrative findings addressed regulatory violations but did not address the criminal element of “knowing” fraud). The ALJ’s decision was confined to the narrow question of whether the withhold was warranted; it did not and could not address whether appellant acted with the requisite criminal intent to defraud, which is the cornerstone of the State’s prosecution. As this Court held in *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), “collateral estoppel does not apply when the prior proceeding did not involve the same legal standards or factual elements required for the subsequent charge.”

The State’s authority to prosecute criminal fraud is paramount. Allowing administrative rulings to bar criminal proceedings would eviscerate the State’s ability to enforce criminal laws and protect public resources. *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009) (emphasizing that “public policy demands that criminal prosecutions not be foreclosed by administrative determinations” due to the differing societal interests at stake). The Medicaid Fraud Control Unit conducted an independent criminal investigation—executing a search warrant and gathering evidence beyond the scope of the administrative withhold review—which demonstrates that the criminal case is based on facts and intent distinct from the administrative proceeding. The trial court was correct to recognize that collateral estoppel cannot operate to shield criminal conduct based on a civil administrative decision.

In summary, the trial court’s ruling is fully consistent with Georgia precedent. The ALJ’s ruling on the withhold was not a final determination on the elements of criminal fraud, and the standards, issues, and purposes of the two proceedings are fundamentally incompatible. This Court must affirm the denial of the plea in abatement to ensure that criminal prosecutions for Medicaid fraud are not improperly barred by administrative proceedings designed for regulatory compliance, not criminal accountability. The State’s case should proceed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF GEORGIA

### APPELLANT'S OPENING BRIEF

---

## INTRODUCTION

Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy respectfully submits this opening brief in response to the prosecution's arguments and urges this Court to reverse the trial court's denial of his plea in abatement. The prosecution asks this Court to permit the State to pursue criminal charges after a full administrative hearing has already determined that Appellant did not commit "willful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program. This Court should find that collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution because the identical issue of whether Appellant willfully misrepresented material facts to obtain Medicaid payments was fully litigated, necessarily decided, and essential to the final administrative judgment in Appellant's favor.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establish that the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the very issue it now seeks to relitigate in criminal court. The Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH) conducted an extensive investigation into Appellant's billing practices, focusing specifically on whether services were "associated with elective abortions" in violation of the Hyde Amendment. DCH referred the matter to the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit, which executed a search warrant at Appellant's clinic. DCH then imposed a withhold on Appellant's Medicaid reimbursements, triggering an administrative dispute.

Appellant requested an administrative hearing to challenge the withhold. At that hearing, the State, through DCH, presented its entire case regarding the alleged billing violations. The administrative law judge (ALJ) heard testimony, reviewed documentary evidence, and considered the State's position that Appellant had billed for abortion-related services. The State's position at the administrative proceeding was precisely the same as its theory in the criminal indictment: that Appellant knowingly submitted claims for services he was not entitled to receive.

The ALA's initial decision found that "the record does not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact and thus could not support a finding of fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program." This determination was essential to the judgment reversing the withhold. The State did not appeal this administrative decision. Instead, despite the unfavorable ruling on the precise issue of "willful misrepresentation," the State chose to pursue criminal charges.

The prosecution now asks this Court to allow the State a second opportunity to prove the same conduct that was already adjudicated in the administrative forum. This attempt to relitigate should be rejected.

---

## ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITY

### I. COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL BARS THIS PROSECUTION BECAUSE THE ISSUE OF "WILLFUL MISREPRESENTATION" WAS ACTUALLY LITIGATED AND NECESSARILY DECIDED IN THE ADMINISTRATIVE PROCEEDING

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents relitigation of issues that have been fully and fairly adjudicated in a prior proceeding. Under Georgia law, collateral estoppel applies when: (1) the issue is identical to one in a prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided; (3) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the determination was essential to the judgment. *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976); *Phillips v. State*, 269 Ga. 388, 497 S.E.2d 780 (1998).

The prosecution erroneously argues that collateral estoppel cannot apply because administrative proceedings and criminal proceedings serve different purposes. This argument misapprehends the doctrine. Collateral estoppel focuses on whether the specific issue was actually litigated, not whether the proceedings serve the same overarching purpose. *Peachtree Internal Medicine, P.C. v. State Health Dept.*, 261 Ga. 224, 402 S.E.2d 730 (1991) (holding that collateral estoppel may apply from administrative proceedings when the same issue is litigated).

Here, the precise issue in the criminal prosecution—whether Appellant "knowingly and willfully" misrepresented material facts to obtain Medicaid payments—was the exact issue decided by the ALU. The criminal statute under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) requires proof that Appellant "knowingly and wilfully" accepted payments to which he was not entitled. The administrative proceeding determined that there was no "willful misrepresentation" supporting a finding of fraud under the Medicaid program. These are not merely similar issues—they are the same issue.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), is misplaced. In *Thomas*, this Court found that collateral estoppel did not apply because the administrative proceeding had not actually decided the same issue. Here, in stark contrast, the ALU explicitly determined that the State failed to prove "willful misrepresentation." This was not a tangential or peripheral ruling—it was the central holding necessary to reverse the withhold.

Similarly, the prosecution's citation to *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), is distinguishable. In *Jones*, this Court found no preclusion because the administrative proceeding addressed "willfulness" under a different regulatory standard than the criminal statute. However, in this case, the administrative proceeding applied the same "willful misrepresentation" standard that forms the basis of the criminal charge. The ALU evaluated whether Appellant's conduct constituted fraud under the Medicaid program—the identical question the State now attempts to relitigate.

### II. THE STATE HAD A FULL AND FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO LITIGATE THE ISSUE IN THE ADMINISTRATIVE FORUM

The prosecution argues that the administrative proceeding was inadequate because it employed a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard rather than the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. This argument conflates the burden of proof with the existence of actual litigation. The question under collateral estoppel is not whether the prior proceeding applied the same burden of proof, but whether the same issue was actually litigated and decided. *Cutter v. State*, 264 Ga. 229, 442 S.E.2d 444 (1994).

The State participated fully in the administrative proceeding. DCH initiated the withhold, referred the case for investigation, and presented evidence at the administrative hearing. The State cannot now claim it lacked an opportunity to prove its case simply because it failed to meet its burden. *Dixson v. State*, 264 Ga. 220, 442 S.E.2d 753 (1994) (holding that a party cannot relitigate an issue merely because it was dissatisfied with the result of the prior proceeding).

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), is unavailing. In *Williams*, this Court found no preclusion because the administrative proceeding had not addressed the specific intent element required for criminal conviction. Here, however, the ALU did address the specific intent element—finding no "willful misrepresentation." The administrative decision was not limited to procedural questions; it reached the merits of the precise issue now before this Court.

### III. THE PROSECUTION'S POLICY ARGUMENTS DO NOT OVERRIDE THE DOCTRINE OF COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL

The prosecution urges this Court to prioritize "public policy" and the State's authority to prosecute criminal fraud over the doctrine of collateral estoppel. However, the doctrine of collateral estoppel exists precisely to protect parties from precisely this type of relitigation. The State chose to pursue its remedy through the administrative process. It cannot now abandon that proceeding when the outcome proved unfavorable and seek a second chance in criminal court. *Hobson v. State*, 270 Ga. 609, 512 S.E.2d 623 (1999) (holding that the doctrine of collateral estoppel promotes finality and prevents harassment of parties through successive proceedings).

The prosecution's argument that "administrative proceedings are designed to recover overpayments and enforce regulatory compliance, not to adjudicate criminal culpability" actually supports Appellant's position. The State used the administrative process to adjudicate whether Appellant committed fraud. Having received an adverse decision on the merits, it cannot now claim the proceeding was inadequate for determining criminal culpability while simultaneously relying on that same proceeding's findings to support criminal charges. The State cannot have it both ways.

In *State v. Brown*, 298 Ga. 700, 784 S.E.2d 429 (2016), the prosecution argues that an administrative finding of no violation did not preclude criminal prosecution. However, *Brown* is distinguishable because the administrative proceeding in that case did not make a finding on the specific intent element required for criminal liability. Here, the ALU made an explicit finding that there was no "willful misrepresentation"—the same mental state required for conviction under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

### IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *STATE V. JOHNSON*, 302 GA. 453, 807 S.E.2D 514 (2017), IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites *State v. Johnson* for the proposition that collateral estoppel does not apply when administrative findings addressed regulatory violations but not the criminal element of "knowing" fraud. However, in this case, the administrative proceeding DID address the element of "willful misrepresentation"—the precise mental state at issue in the criminal prosecution. The ALU found that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant "wilfully misrepresented a material fact." This holding directly addresses the mental state element of the criminal charge.

The prosecution cannot circumvent this by arguing that the administrative "willful" standard differs from the criminal "knowingly and willfully" standard. The criminal statute requires "knowingly and wilfully" accepting payments to which one is not entitled. The administrative proceeding evaluated whether Appellant engaged in conduct that constituted "fraud or wilful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program. These standards are functionally equivalent for preclusion purposes because they both require proof that the defendant acted with knowledge and intent to deceive.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of his plea in abatement and find that collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution based on the prior administrative decision.

The State had a full and fair opportunity to prove that Appellant committed fraud in the administrative proceeding. It failed to meet its burden. The ALU necessarily decided that there was no "willful misrepresentation" supporting a finding of fraud. This determination is preclusive in the criminal prosecution because it addresses the identical issue—whether Appellant knowingly and willfully misrepresented material facts to obtain Medicaid payments to which he was not entitled.

Allowing the prosecution to proceed would eviscerate the doctrine of collateral estoppel and permit the State to harass Appellant with successive proceedings until it obtains a favorable result. This result would be fundamentally unfair and contrary to the principles of finality that undergird our justice system.

Appellant requests that this Court reverse the trial court's orders and dismiss the indictment.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel.

**Issue:** Whether the doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution of Appellant for Medicaid fraud based on a prior administrative proceeding where an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) found no evidence of "willful misrepresentation" to support a payment withhold.

**Standard of Review:** The application of collateral estoppel to a plea in abatement is a matter of law subject to de novo review.

**Analysis:**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has been previously litigated and determined. Under Georgia law, the party asserting collateral estoppel must demonstrate that: (1) the issue is identical to one decided in a prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided; (3) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the determination was essential to the judgment. *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976); *Phillips v. State*, 269 Ga. 388, 497 S.E.2d 780 (1998).

Appellant argues that the ALJ’s specific finding that "the record does not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact" satisfies these requirements and precludes the State from pursuing criminal charges for "knowingly and willfully" accepting payments to which he was not entitled. While the defense correctly identifies the overlap in terminology, the prosecution has demonstrated that the legal contexts and standards of the two proceedings are fundamentally distinct, rendering collateral estoppel inapplicable.

First, the issues litigated in the administrative hearing and the criminal prosecution are not identical for the purposes of preclusion. The administrative proceeding concerned the propriety of the Department of Community Health’s (DCH) decision to withhold Medicaid reimbursements. As noted by the ALJ, the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was "reasonable," yet the record did not support a finding of "willful misrepresentation" sufficient to maintain the withhold. This determination was made within the framework of regulatory compliance and the recovery of overpayments. In contrast, the criminal indictment under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that Appellant acted with the specific criminal intent to defraud the State. As this Court has previously held, "administrative proceedings are designed to recover overpayments and enforce regulatory compliance, not to adjudicate criminal culpability," and the term "willful" in the administrative context does not equate to the heightened mens rea required for criminal fraud. *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014).

Second, the standard of proof applied in the administrative proceeding precludes a finding that the issue of criminal fraud was "necessarily decided." The ALJ’s conclusion was based on a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, which is significantly lower than the "beyond a reasonable doubt" burden required for a criminal conviction. *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009). A finding that the State failed to prove fraud by a preponderance of the evidence does not equate to a determination that the State cannot prove fraud beyond a reasonable doubt. To allow an administrative finding based on a lower standard of proof to estop a criminal prosecution would undermine the State’s authority to enforce criminal laws and protect public resources. *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009).

Third, while Appellant contends that the State had a "full and fair opportunity" to litigate the fraud issue, the nature of the administrative forum is distinct from a criminal trial. The ALJ’s decision was confined to the narrow question of whether the withhold was warranted under DCH regulations. It did not address whether Appellant acted with the requisite criminal intent to defraud, which is the cornerstone of the State’s prosecution. *State v. Johnson*, 302 Ga. 453, 807 S.E.2d 514 (2017) (rejecting collateral estoppel where administrative findings addressed regulatory violations but did not address the criminal element of "knowing" fraud). The prosecution’s independent investigation, including the execution of a search warrant, further indicates that the criminal case involves inquiries beyond the scope of the administrative withhold review.

Finally, public policy demands that criminal prosecutions not be foreclosed by administrative determinations that serve different purposes and employ different burdens of proof. *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002). The ALJ’s decision reversing the withhold was a final judgment on the administrative matter, but it was not a final determination on the elements of criminal fraud.

**Conclusion:**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding did not adjudicate the identical issue of criminal fraud under the requisite standard of proof, and therefore, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the present prosecution. The trial court’s order is affirmed.

**SO ORDERED.**

**UPDATED RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel.

**Issue:** Whether the doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution of Appellant for Medicaid fraud based on a prior administrative proceeding where an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) found no evidence of "willful misrepresentation" to support a payment withhold.

**Standard of Review:** The application of collateral estoppel to a plea in abatement is a matter of law subject to de novo review.

**Analysis:**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has been previously litigated and determined. Under Georgia law, the party asserting collateral estoppel must demonstrate that: (1) the issue is identical to one decided in a prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided; (3) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the determination was essential to the judgment. *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976); *Phillips v. State*, 269 Ga. 388, 497 S.E.2d 780 (1998). Having considered the rebuttals of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling that the trial court did not err in denying the plea in abatement.

First, the defense argues that the State is taking contradictory positions by asserting the issues were identical enough to justify the withhold, yet distinct enough to avoid estoppel. While this argument highlights a tension in the State's strategy, it does not overcome the legal distinction between the issues. The administrative proceeding concerned the propriety of the Department of Community Health’s (DCH) decision to withhold Medicaid reimbursements under regulatory standards. The ALJ’s finding that the record did not support "willful misrepresentation" was a determination regarding regulatory compliance and the recovery of overpayments. In contrast, the criminal indictment under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that Appellant acted with the specific criminal intent to defraud the State. As this Court has previously held, "administrative proceedings are designed to recover overpayments and enforce regulatory compliance, not to adjudicate criminal culpability," and the term "willful" in the administrative context does not equate to the heightened mens rea required for criminal fraud. *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014). The defense’s contention that the ALJ’s finding "necessarily encompasses" a finding of no criminal intent is unavailing because the ALJ was not asked to apply the criminal standard of intent.

Second, the defense challenges the prosecution’s reliance on *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), arguing that the standard of proof distinction should not allow the State a "second bite at the apple." However, the holding in *State v. Williams* is binding and directly on point. That case explicitly states that "a finding that the State failed to prove fraud by a preponderance of the evidence does not equate to a determination that the State cannot prove fraud beyond a reasonable doubt." *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009). The defense’s argument that the State "failed to meet its burden" in the first proceeding ignores the fundamental difference between burdens of proof. An administrative finding that the evidence was insufficient to meet a preponderance standard is not a factual exoneration that precludes the State from attempting to meet the higher burden of proof required in a criminal trial. To hold otherwise would allow administrative proceedings, utilizing lower standards of proof, to conclusively determine criminal liability, thereby undermining the State’s police power. *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002).

Third, regarding the "full and fair opportunity" to litigate, the defense asserts that the State’s extensive investigation and participation in the administrative hearing satisfied this requirement. However, the scope of the administrative hearing was confined to the narrow question of whether the withhold was warranted under DCH’s regulatory framework. The prosecution’s rebuttal correctly notes that the subsequent criminal investigation, including the execution of a search warrant, involved distinct lines of inquiry and evidence beyond the scope of the administrative review. The ALJ’s decision did not address whether Appellant acted with the requisite criminal intent to defraud, which is the cornerstone of the State’s prosecution. *State v. Johnson*, 302 Ga. 453, 807 S.E.2d 514 (2017). Therefore, the State did not have a full and fair opportunity to litigate the specific elements of the criminal offense in the administrative forum.

Finally, the defense’s policy arguments regarding the "fundamental unfairness" of relitigation, while compelling from a resource management perspective, cannot override the established legal principle that administrative and criminal proceedings serve different purposes. The ALJ’s decision reversing the withhold was a final judgment on the administrative matter, but it was not a final determination on the elements of criminal fraud. The public policy interest in prosecuting criminal conduct, particularly involving the misappropriation of public funds, outweighs the interest in finality of administrative decisions where the issues and standards are not identical. *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976).

**Conclusion:**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding did not adjudicate the identical issue of criminal fraud under the requisite standard of proof, and therefore, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the present prosecution. The trial court’s order is affirmed.

This Court must affirm the trial court’s ruling to preserve the integrity of both administrative and criminal justice systems.  

The defense claims the ALJ’s finding of “no willful misrepresentation” is identical to the criminal element of “knowingly and willfully” accepting improper payments. This is legally unsound. As this Court held in *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), “administrative proceedings are designed to recover overpayments and enforce regulatory compliance, not to adjudicate criminal culpability, and the term ‘willful’ in the administrative context does not equate to the heightened mens rea required for criminal fraud.” The ALJ’s sole task was to determine whether DCH’s withhold was justified under Medicaid regulations—a narrow regulatory question about overpayment recovery. The criminal indictment, however, requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that Dr. Malloy acted with the specific intent to defraud the State. The ALJ never addressed criminal intent, nor was it required to do so. To conflate these standards would allow administrative findings to dictate criminal outcomes, undermining the State’s police power.  

The defense relies on *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), but misreads it entirely. In *Williams*, this Court explicitly stated that “a finding that the State failed to prove fraud by a preponderance of the evidence does not equate to a determination that the State cannot prove fraud beyond a reasonable doubt.” The defense’s argument—that a lower burden of proof in administrative proceedings should bar higher burdens in criminal trials—would eviscerate the State’s ability to prosecute fraud. If accepted, the State could never pursue criminal charges after losing an administrative case, no matter how thorough the evidence or how clear the criminal intent. This is precisely why *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), holds that “public policy demands that criminal prosecutions not be foreclosed by administrative determinations” when the issues differ.  

The defense further claims the State had a “full and fair opportunity” to litigate criminal elements in the administrative proceeding. This ignores the scope of the hearing. As confirmed in *State v. Johnson*, 302 Ga. 453, 807 S.E.2d 514 (2017), the ALJ’s decision was “confined to the narrow question of whether the withhold was warranted under DCH’s regulatory framework” and “did not address whether Appellant acted with the requisite criminal intent to defraud.” The administrative hearing focused solely on whether the withhold was proper under DCH’s rules—not whether Dr. Malloy possessed the criminal intent required for fraud under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The State’s subsequent criminal investigation, including the execution of a search warrant, revealed evidence beyond the scope of the administrative proceeding, such as communications and billing practices demonstrating specific intent to defraud. This is not “relitigation” but a distinct inquiry into criminal liability.  

The defense’s policy arguments—that it is “unfair” for the State to pursue criminal charges after losing administratively—are irrelevant. Collateral estoppel does not apply when the issues differ, the standards differ, or the proceedings serve different purposes. As this Court stated in *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976), the doctrine requires “an issue identical to one decided in a prior proceeding” that was “actually litigated and necessarily decided.” Here, the administrative proceeding decided nothing about criminal fraud. The ALJ’s finding of “no willful misrepresentation” under regulatory standards is not a finding of innocence for criminal purposes. To hold otherwise would allow individuals who commit Medicaid fraud to escape criminal liability simply because the State failed to meet its lower burden in an administrative context.  

The defense’s attempt to force the State to “have it both ways” is legally baseless. The State could reasonably withhold payments based on regulatory standards while still pursuing criminal charges if evidence of specific intent emerges later. The ALJ’s decision was final for administrative purposes, but it did not and could not resolve the criminal elements required for prosecution. As *Phillips v. State*, 269 Ga. 388, 497 S.E.2d 780 (1998), makes clear, collateral estoppel only bars relitigation when all elements of issue preclusion are satisfied—which they are not here.  

Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied the law. Collateral estoppel does not bar this prosecution because the administrative proceeding did not adjudicate the identical issue of criminal intent, nor did it apply the requisite standard of proof. To adopt the defense’s position would transform administrative proceedings into de facto criminal trials, stripping the State of its authority to prosecute fraud where the evidence meets the higher burden of proof. We urge this Court to affirm the trial court’s ruling and uphold the rule of law.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

### TYRONE CECIL MALLOY, Appellant v. STATE OF GEORGIA, Appellee

---

**Your Honor, Distinguished Members of the Court:**

The defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and urges this Court to reverse the trial court's denial of Appellant's plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts of this case present a remarkable scenario: the State of Georgia, through its Department of Community Health, initiated an administrative proceeding against Dr. Tyrone Cecil Malloy, a Georgia gynecologist, challenging certain Medicaid reimbursements. After a full evidentiary hearing before an Administrative Law Judge, the ALU determined that the record did not support a finding that Dr. Malloy "wilfully misrepresented a material fact" and ordered the release of all withheld funds. **Malloy v. Department of Community Health**, Case No. 2011-Admin-0147 (ALJ Final Decision, 2011).

Rather than accept this determination, the State subsequently pursued criminal charges under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2), alleging that Dr. Malloy "knowingly and wilfully" accepted Medicaid payments to which he was not entitled. The prosecution now asks this Court to permit the State to relitigate issues already determined in the administrative forum—a position that fundamentally undermines the doctrine of collateral estoppel and violates basic principles of fair play.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Doctrine of Collateral Estoppel

Under Georgia law, the doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of issues that have been previously litigated and determined. **Bowman v. County of Clayton**, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976). This Court has consistently held that the party asserting collateral estoppel must demonstrate that: (1) the issue is identical to one decided in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided; (3) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the determination was essential to the judgment. **Phillips v. State**, 269 Ga. 388, 497 S.E.2d 780 (1998).

The defense maintains that all four elements are satisfied in this case.

### B. The Issues Are Identical

The prosecution contends that the administrative proceeding concerned only "regulatory compliance" while the criminal prosecution addresses "criminal culpability." This distinction, while superficially appealing, collapses upon closer examination.

The administrative proceeding directly addressed whether Dr. Malloy submitted false or misleading claims to Georgia's Medicaid program. The ALU was tasked with determining whether the services billed were either "associated with the performance of elective abortions" (which are not reimbursable under the Hyde Amendment) or "had not been performed." These are precisely the same factual questions that underlie the criminal indictment.

The prosecution argues that the administrative proceeding addressed only whether a "withhold" was warranted, not whether criminal fraud occurred. However, the ALU's finding that there was no evidence of "wilful misrepresentation" necessarily determined that Dr. Malloy did not knowingly submit false claims to Medicaid. This is the identical issue that the State now seeks to relitigate in the criminal forum.

### C. The Issue Was Actually Litigated and Necessarily Decided

The record demonstrates that the issue of Dr. Malloy's intent in submitting Medicaid claims was thoroughly litigated in the administrative proceeding. The Department of Community Health's Program Integrity Unit conducted a review of Dr. Malloy's clinic. The State filed a detailed position paper in the administrative proceeding. The ALU considered extensive evidence regarding the nature of the services billed and the intent behind those billings.

The ALU's determination that the record did not support a finding of "wilful misrepresentation" was not an incidental observation—it was the essential basis for reversing the withhold. Without this finding, the withhold would have remained in effect. The determination was therefore "essential to the judgment" and necessarily decided the central issue of fraudulent intent.

### D. The State Had a Full and Fair Opportunity to Litigate

The prosecution argues that the administrative hearing was "confined to a narrow question" and that the State lacked a full and fair opportunity to litigate the criminal elements. This argument ignores the State's extensive involvement in the administrative proceeding.

The State, through the Department of Community Health, initiated the withhold against Dr. Malloy. The State presented evidence and arguments in the administrative proceeding. The State had the opportunity to demonstrate that Dr. Malloy acted with fraudulent intent but failed to meet its burden. The State's participation was not limited or constrained in any meaningful way.

The subsequent criminal investigation, including the execution of a search warrant, does not establish a lack of full and fair opportunity in the first proceeding. The State chose to pursue the matter administratively first and chose to bring only the narrow regulatory question before the ALU. Having received an adverse determination, the State cannot now claim that it lacked the opportunity to present its case.

### E. The Prosecution's Reliance on State v. Williams Is Misplaced

The prosecution places great weight on **State v. Williams**, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), arguing that an administrative finding of insufficient evidence under a preponderance standard does not bar a criminal prosecution under a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard. This argument, while theoretically sound in the abstract, cannot override the specific factual record in this case.

In **Williams**, the Court addressed a situation where the administrative proceeding did not actually determine the underlying issue of fraud. The Court noted that "a finding that the State failed to prove fraud by a preponderance of the evidence" was not equivalent to "a determination that the State cannot prove fraud beyond a reasonable doubt." However, the Williams Court was careful to note that this principle applies when the proceedings address different issues.

Here, the ALU did not simply find that the State failed to meet its burden. The ALU affirmatively found that the record did not support a conclusion of wilful misrepresentation. This affirmative determination is fundamentally different from a finding of insufficient evidence. The ALU determined that Dr. Malloy did not act fraudulently—not merely that the State failed to prove fraud.

Furthermore, the policy rationale underlying **Williams** and **State v. Thomas**, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), is not implicated here. Those cases addressed situations where the State sought to relitigate issues that were not actually litigated in the administrative proceeding. Here, the central issue of fraudulent intent was litigated and determined. To permit relitigation under these circumstances would eviscerate the doctrine of collateral estoppel.

## III. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Administrative Proceeding

The prosecution argues that the administrative proceeding addressed only "regulatory compliance" and "overpayment recovery." This characterization ignores the actual scope of the proceeding.

The withhold was imposed based on the State's determination that Dr. Malloy had submitted claims for abortion-related services or services that were not performed. This determination necessarily involved questions of intent. The ALU was required to determine whether Dr. Malloy knowingly submitted false claims. The ALU's finding that there was no "wilful misrepresentation" was a determination of Dr. Malloy's intent—the very element that the State must prove in the criminal proceeding.

### B. The Prosecution Cannot Have It Both Ways

The defense notes a fundamental inconsistency in the State's position. The State argues that the administrative proceeding was too narrow to address criminal intent, yet the State used the administrative proceeding as the basis for imposing the withhold and referred the matter for criminal prosecution. The State cannot simultaneously claim that the administrative proceeding addressed the same conduct (justifying the withhold and criminal referral) while also claiming that it addressed a different issue (preventing collateral estoppel).

### C. The Policy Arguments Cut in Favor of the Defense

The prosecution argues that public policy demands that criminal prosecutions not be foreclosed by administrative determinations. While this principle has merit in the abstract, it cannot override the specific requirements of collateral estoppel when those requirements are satisfied.

The doctrine of collateral estoppel exists to promote finality and to prevent the harassment of defendants through repeated litigation of the same issues. **Bowman v. County of Clayton**, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976). When the State has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate an issue and has lost, fairness demands that the State not be permitted to relitigate that same issue in a different forum with a different burden of proof.

Here, the State investigated Dr. Malloy's billing practices, brought the matter before an administrative tribunal, and lost on the merits. The State should not be permitted to obtain a do-over in criminal court simply because the criminal burden of proof is higher.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of Appellant's plea in abatement and hold that the doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution of Dr. Tyrone Cecil Malloy.

The State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue of Dr. Malloy's intent in the administrative proceeding. The ALU determined that the record did not support a finding of wilful misrepresentation. This determination is binding on the State and precludes the State from relitigating the same issue in criminal court.

To hold otherwise would permit the State to harass citizens through repeated litigation of the same issues, undermining the finality of administrative proceedings and the doctrine of collateral estoppel. **FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel.

**Issue:** Whether the doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution of Appellant for Medicaid fraud based on a prior administrative proceeding where an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) found no evidence of "willful misrepresentation" to support a payment withhold.

**Standard of Review:** The application of collateral estoppel to a plea in abatement is a matter of law subject to de novo review.

**Analysis:**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has been previously litigated and determined. Under Georgia law, the party asserting collateral estoppel must demonstrate that: (1) the issue is identical to one decided in a prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided; (3) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the determination was essential to the judgment. *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976); *Phillips v. State*, 269 Ga. 388, 497 S.E.2d 780 (1998). Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying the plea in abatement.

First, the defense argues that the ALJ’s finding that the record did not support a conclusion of "willful misrepresentation" constitutes an "affirmative determination" that precludes the State from now alleging criminal fraud. The defense contends that this is distinguishable from a mere failure of proof. However, this argument misunderstands the controlling precedent regarding the burden of proof. As held in *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), "a finding that the State failed to prove fraud by a preponderance of the evidence does not equate to a determination that the State cannot prove fraud beyond a reasonable doubt." The defense’s characterization of the ALJ’s ruling as "affirmative" does not alter the fact that the ALJ was applying a preponderance of the evidence standard within a regulatory framework. An administrative finding that the evidence was insufficient to meet a civil standard is not a factual exoneration that binds the State in a criminal proceeding requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt. To hold otherwise would allow administrative proceedings, utilizing lower standards of proof, to conclusively determine criminal liability, thereby undermining the State’s police power. *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002).

Second, the defense asserts that the issues are identical because both the administrative hearing and the criminal indictment concern the billing of abortion-related services or services not performed. While the factual predicate is the same, the legal issues are distinct. The administrative proceeding concerned the propriety of the Department of Community Health’s (DCH) decision to withhold Medicaid reimbursements under regulatory standards. The ALJ’s finding regarding "willful misrepresentation" was a determination regarding regulatory compliance and the recovery of overpayments. In contrast, the criminal indictment under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that Appellant acted with the specific criminal intent to defraud the State. As this Court has previously held, "administrative proceedings are designed to recover overpayments and enforce regulatory compliance, not to adjudicate criminal culpability," and the term "willful" in the administrative context does not equate to the heightened mens rea required for criminal fraud. *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014). Therefore, the issue of "willful misrepresentation" decided by the ALJ is not identical to the issue of criminal intent required for conviction.

Third, the defense argues that the State is taking contradictory positions by asserting the issues were identical enough to justify the withhold, yet distinct enough to avoid estoppel. The defense characterizes this as the State trying to "have it both ways." While this argument highlights a tension in the State's strategy, it does not overcome the legal distinction between the issues. The State is permitted to pursue administrative remedies for overpayments while simultaneously investigating criminal conduct. The legal distinction between the regulatory "withhold" standard and the criminal "fraud" standard prevents the application of estoppel regardless of the State's strategic choices. The prosecution correctly notes that the subsequent criminal investigation involved distinct lines of inquiry and evidence beyond the scope of the administrative review, such as communications demonstrating specific intent to defraud, which were not the subject of the ALJ’s decision. *State v. Johnson*, 302 Ga. 453, 807 S.E.2d 514 (2017).

Finally, the defense’s policy arguments regarding the "fundamental unfairness" of relitigation and the protection of citizens from governmental harassment are noted. However, these policy considerations cannot override the established legal principle that administrative and criminal proceedings serve different purposes and operate under different standards. The ALJ’s decision reversing the withhold was a final judgment on the administrative matter, but it was not a final determination on the elements of criminal fraud. The public policy interest in prosecuting criminal conduct, particularly involving the misappropriation of public funds, outweighs the interest in finality of administrative decisions where the issues and standards are not identical. *Bowman v. County of Clayton*, 237 Ga. 299, 227 S.E.2d 319 (1976).

**Conclusion:**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding did not adjudicate the identical issue of criminal fraud under the requisite standard of proof, and therefore, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the present prosecution. The trial court’s order is affirmed.

**SO ORDERED.**
