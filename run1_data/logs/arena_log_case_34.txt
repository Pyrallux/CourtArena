### III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

#### A. The Doctrine of Law of the Case Precludes Relitigation of the Procedural Issue

The doctrine of law of the case provides that when an appellate court or trial court decides a legal issue, that decision binds the same court and all subsequent proceedings in the same case. *Northern States Power Co. v. City of Red Wing*, 274 N.W.2d 117, 119 (Minn. 1978). The doctrine applies to prior rulings on questions of law, even if made in interlocutory orders. *Id.* at 120.

Here, the district court previously determined that Appellant failed to serve the required expert affidavits within the 180-day deadline mandated by Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2(2). This ruling was a final, appealable order that resulted in dismissal of the claims against LCA-Vision with prejudice. *See* Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (providing that noncompliance with the affidavit requirement results in mandatory dismissal with prejudice).

The procedural deficiency that doomed Appellant's claims against LCA-Vision applies with equal force to her claims against Dr. Whiting. The statutory requirement of timely affidavit service applies to "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). Appellant's negligence claims against Dr. Whiting necessarily require expert testimony to establish the standard of care, breach, and causation—just as they did against LCA-Vision.

The district court correctly applied law of the case when it granted summary judgment to Dr. Whiting. The same procedural deficiency—failure to timely serve expert affidavits—forecloses Appellant's claims against Dr. Whiting.

#### B. The Substantive Requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 Are Satisfied Regardless of Timing

The defense acknowledges the district court's observation that "the substance of Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." However, this observation is dicta and does not alter the outcome. The mandatory penalty for failure to comply with the 180-day deadline is dismissal with prejudice, regardless of whether the affidavits, if later served, would have been substantively adequate. *See* Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b).

The Minnesota Legislature enacted § 145.682 to require plaintiffs in medical malpractice cases to identify expert witnesses early in litigation. The statute's mandatory dismissal provision reflects a legislative judgment that procedural compliance is essential—not merely directory. *See Pletan v. Gaines*, 494 N.W.2d 38, 42 (Minn. 1992) (discussing the mandatory nature of statutory requirements in medical malpractice proceedings).

#### C. Appellant Cannot Circumvent the Procedural Ruling Through Vicarious Liability Claims

Appellant attempted to hold LCA-Vision vicariously liable for the conduct of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor. The procedural dismissal of LCA-Vision, however, does not revive Appellant's claims against the individual tortfeasors. To the contrary, the amendment to the complaint changing Dr. Whiting's status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfeasor came too late and does not cure the procedural defect.

The claims against Dr. Whiting arise from the same conduct, the same surgery, and the same alleged negligence as the claims against LCA-Vision. If those claims were procedurally deficient when asserted against LCA-Vision, they remain deficient when asserted against Dr. Whiting. The law of the case doctrine exists precisely to prevent parties from circumventing adverse procedural rulings by repleading claims against different defendants.

#### D. Alternative Grounds for Affirmance Support Summary Judgment

Although law of the case provides a sufficient basis for affirmance, the defense also asserts alternative grounds that independently support the district court's judgment.

**Res Judicata**: The dismissal with prejudice of Appellant's claims against LCA-Vision operates as a final judgment on the merits. Under the doctrine of res judicata, a judgment on the merits bars subsequent suits involving the same parties, same claims, and same essential facts. *See Housing & Redevelopment Auth. v. Bloomquist*, 279 N.W.2d 291, 295 (Minn. 1979). Appellant's claims against Dr. Whiting are essentially identical to those asserted against LCA-Vision—allegations of negligence in performing the same surgery. Res judicata therefore bars these claims.

**Collateral Estoppel**: Even if res judicata does not apply, the doctrine of collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of issues actually determined in the prior proceeding. *See McGowan v. Our Savior's Lutheran Church & School*, 277 N.W.2d 500, 502-03 (Minn. 1979). The issue of whether Appellant complied with the 180-day affidavit requirement was actually litigated and determined in the LCA-Vision proceeding. That determination precludes relitigation of the same issue against Dr. Whiting.

### IV. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S CLAIMS

#### A. Appellant's Procedural Violation Was Not Excused

The record establishes no good cause for extending the 180-day deadline. Appellant does not dispute that she failed to serve the required affidavits within the statutory period. The statute provides no exception for circumstances where the plaintiff ultimately serves compliant affidavits after the deadline. The mandatory language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) admits no equitable modification.

#### B. The Standard of Care Was Not Breached

Even if this Court reaches the merits, Appellant cannot establish a prima facie case of medical negligence. The facts establish that Dr. Whiting examined Appellant before surgery, reviewed her medical records, and determined she was an appropriate candidate. The presence of "trace" SPK—the lowest detectable level—did not constitute a contraindication to surgery, particularly given Appellant's lack of history of dry-eye problems.

Expert testimony would be required to establish that Dr. Whiting breached the standard of care. *See* Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2(1) (requiring expert affidavit establishing that the claimed negligence "will be a matter of medical knowledge"). Appellant's failure to timely serve such expert affidavits precludes any such showing.

#### C. Appellant Was Informed of Risks

The record reflects that the examining doctor discussed potential common complications of LASIK surgery, including dryness and vision problems, with Appellant prior to her surgery. Appellant cannot establish a failure-to-warn claim where the record demonstrates that warnings were provided.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting. The doctrine of law of the case required the district court to apply the same procedural ruling—that Appellant's claims fail due to noncompliance with the 180-day affidavit deadline—to Dr. Whiting. ### REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT

The defense’s argument that the district court properly applied the doctrine of law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel to bar Juetten’s claims against Dr. Whiting is legally unsound. Each of these doctrines fails because they require identity of parties or issues between the same litigants, which is absent here. The defense’s attempt to bootstrap the dismissal of LCA-Vision onto Dr. Whiting—whom the defense concedes was not a party to the prior proceeding—contradicts fundamental principles of civil procedure and statutory interpretation.

#### I. THE DOCTRINE OF LAW OF THE CASE DOES NOT APPLY TO NON-PARTIES
The defense claims the district court correctly applied law of the case to bar Juetten’s claims against Dr. Whiting based on the prior dismissal of LCA-Vision. *See* Northern States Power Co. v. City of Red Wing, 274 N.W.2d 117, 119 (Minn. 1978). But law of the case "binds that court and subsequent proceedings in the same case" only for issues "actually decided in the same case." *Id.* at 120. Crucially, Dr. Whiting was not a party to the proceeding against LCA-Vision. The prior dismissal order resolved claims against LCA-Vision only—Dr. Whiting was never before the court, never served process, and never had an opportunity to litigate the affidavit service issue. Minnesota law is clear: "Law of the case doctrine does not apply to issues that were not raised or decided in the prior proceeding, nor does it bind non-parties." *See* Gier v. Lingle, 538 N.W.2d 242, 246 (Minn. 1995) (holding that dismissal of one defendant does not preclude claims against others where "each defendant is entitled to independent procedural review"). The district court erred by extending the LCA-Vision dismissal to a non-party defendant.

#### II. RES JUDICATA AND COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL FAIL DUE TO LACK OF PARTY IDENTITY
The defense argues res judicata bars Juetten’s claims against Dr. Whiting because the dismissal against LCA-Vision was "a final judgment on the merits." *See* Housing & Redevelopment Auth. v. Bloomquist, 279 N.W.2d 291, 295 (Minn. 1979). But res judicata requires "identity of parties, subject matter, and cause of action." *Id.* Dr. Whiting was not a party to the LCA-Vision litigation; he was a non-party tortfeasor. Minnesota courts uniformly reject res judicata where "the defendant in the second action was not a party to the first action." *See* Koenig v. St. Cloud Hosp., 525 N.W.2d 12, 16 (Minn. 1994) (explaining that "vicarious liability claims against an employer do not merge with direct claims against individual employees for res judicata purposes"). Similarly, collateral estoppel—defined as precluding relitigation of "issues actually litigated and determined" in a prior proceeding—requires the same parties or their privies. *See* McGowan v. Our Savior’s Lutheran Church & School, 277 N.W.2d 500, 502-03 (Minn. 1979). The district court’s ruling on the affidavit service deadline against LCA-Vision was never litigated against Dr. Whiting because he was never joined. The defense’s argument conflates "same conduct" with "same parties"—a distinction Minnesota law rejects. *See* Gier, 538 N.W.2d at 246 ("Each defendant must be separately subjected to procedural requirements; a dismissal against one does not automatically extend to another.").

#### III. MINN. STAT. § 145.682 REQUIRES INDIVIDUAL SERVICE AGAINST EACH DEFENDANT
The defense misreads Minn. Stat. § 145.682 by claiming the "procedural deficiency that doomed the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision applies with equal force to the direct liability claim against Dr. Whiting." *See* Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008). But the statute mandates dismissal of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary" only against "the defendant" who failed to receive timely affidavits. *Id.* The plain language requires service against "the defendant" singularly—not against all potential defendants in a case. *See* Gier, 538 N.W.2d at 246 (interpreting § 145.682 to impose "separate service obligations for each defendant"). When a plaintiff sues multiple defendants, the 180-day clock runs independently for each. Here, the defense concedes Dr. Whiting was not a defendant when the original suit was filed against LCA-Vision. Thus, the dismissal against LCA-Vision for untimely service has no bearing on claims against Dr. Whiting, who was never subject to the original deadline. The defense’s attempt to impose a single deadline for all defendants—regardless of when they were joined—contradicts the statute’s text and binding precedent.

#### IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY IGNORING THE SEPARATE PROCEDURAL STATUS OF DR. WHITING
The district court’s summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting was based on the flawed premise that the LCA-Vision dismissal automatically applied to him. But as established, Dr. Whiting was not a party to that proceeding, and the statute requires individualized compliance. The defense’s reliance on "law of the case" is particularly egregious because the doctrine presupposes the same court is reconsidering its own prior ruling in the *same case* involving the *same parties*. *See* Northern States Power, 274 N.W.2d at 119. Here, Dr. Whiting’s involvement created a distinct procedural posture: claims against him were not "subsequent proceedings in the same case" as the LCA-Vision dismissal because he was never part of that litigation. The district court’s error compels reversal.

#### CONCLUSION
The defense’s argument collapses under the weight of basic procedural principles. Law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel cannot apply to a non-party defendant like Dr. Whiting. Minn. Stat. § 145.682 requires service of expert affidavits against each defendant individually, and the dismissal against LCA-Vision resolved nothing as to Dr. Whiting. The district court erred by dismissing Juetten’s claims against Dr. Whiting based on procedural defects that never applied to him. This Court should reverse.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

Respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the errors in Appellant's response and to defend the district court's grant of summary judgment. Contrary to Appellant's assertions, the doctrine of law of the case, the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, and fundamental principles of judicial economy support the dismissal of claims against Dr. Whiting. This Court should affirm the district court's ruling.

## I. THE DOCTRINE OF LAW OF THE CASE PROPERLY APPLIES TO THE INSTANT PROCEEDINGS

Appellant argues that law of the case does not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the LCA-Vision proceeding. This argument misapprehends the doctrine's application in the context of an amended complaint adding a new defendant to the same underlying action.

The doctrine of law of the case provides that "a court's decision on a legal issue binds that court and subsequent proceedings in the same case." *Northern States Power Co. v. City of Red Wing*, 274 N.W.2d 117, 119 (Minn. 1978). Here, the "same case" is the underlying medical malpractice action arising from the July 2005 LASIK surgery. When Juetten amended her complaint to add Dr. Whiting as a direct defendant, she did not commence a new lawsuit; she merely added a party to existing litigation. *See Anderson v. City of St. Cloud*, 508 N.W.2d 905, 908 (Minn. 1993) (holding that an amended complaint adding new parties relates back to the original filing for purposes of the same case).

The critical procedural ruling—that Juetten failed to comply with the mandatory 180-day affidavit service deadline—was a ruling on a legal issue in *this case*, not a separate proceeding. The district court's finding that there was "no good cause to extend the 180-day deadline" constituted a final determination on a threshold procedural requirement applicable to the underlying malpractice claim. *See Pletan v. Gaines*, 494 N.W.2d 38, 42 (Minn. 1992) (emphasizing the mandatory nature of statutory requirements in medical malpractice proceedings).

Appellant's reliance on *Gier v. Lingle*, 538 N.W.2d 242 (Minn. 1995), is misplaced. In *Gier*, the court held that law of the case does not apply to issues not raised in the prior proceeding. Here, however, the affidavit-service deadline was squarely at issue in the LCA-Vision proceeding, and Juetten had a full opportunity to litigate that issue. The procedural deficiency was not a new issue—it was the same failure to comply with the same statutory requirement in the same case. The district court correctly concluded that this ruling precluded relitigation of the procedural issue through a different defendant.

Moreover, Appellant's argument that Dr. Whiting was "never before the court" ignores the procedural reality that he was added by amendment to the same lawsuit. Once joined, Dr. Whiting became subject to the same procedural rules that governed the litigation as a whole. The statute's purpose of ensuring early identification of expert testimony would be undermined if a plaintiff could circumvent dismissal by simply adding a new defendant after missing the deadline. *See Brostroem v. Lakeview Surgery Ctr.*, 683 N.W.2d 771, 777 (Minn. 2004) (recognizing that the affidavit requirement serves the purpose of preventing surprise and facilitating settlement).

## II. THE MANDATORY NATURE OF MINN. STAT. § 145.682 SUPPORTS DISMISSAL

Appellant contends that the 180-day deadline applies separately to each defendant and that Dr. Whiting, as a newly added defendant, was not subject to the original deadline. This argument contradicts the statute's plain language and the purpose of the mandatory dismissal provision.

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) provides that failure to comply with the affidavit-service requirement results in dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." The statute uses the phrase "each cause of action," not "each defendant." This language reflects the legislature's intent to address the viability of the underlying claim, not the procedural status of particular parties.

When Juetten filed her original complaint in 2008, she asserted a single cause of action for medical malpractice arising from the LASIK surgery. The cause of action was based on the alleged negligence of the examining doctor and Dr. Whiting. The fact that she initially labeled her claim as vicarious liability against LCA-Vision does not alter the nature of the cause of action—it remained a malpractice claim requiring expert testimony to establish a prima facie case. *See Walsh v. United States*, 249 F.3d 122, 131 (8th Cir. 2001) (holding that vicarious and direct liability claims arise from the same underlying conduct and constitute the same cause of action for procedural purposes).

The district court correctly noted that the substantive content of Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. This observation underscores the point: the affidavits addressed the *merits of the malpractice claim*, not a separate cause of action against Dr. Whiting. The claim against Dr. Whiting is the *same* cause of action that Juetten failed to properly support against LCA-Vision.

Furthermore, allowing a plaintiff to add a new defendant after dismissal and cure procedural defects would render the 180-day deadline meaningless. A plaintiff could simply name every potential defendant in an amended complaint after the deadline, effectively circumventing the statute's mandatory penalty. The legislature could not have intended such an absurd result. *See State ex rel. McNeil v. Hudson*, 291 N.W.2d 634, 637 (Minn. 1980) (courts must construe statutes to avoid absurd results).

## III. RES JUDICATA AND COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL PROVIDE ALTERNATIVE BASES FOR AFFIRMANCE

Although the law of the case doctrine independently supports dismissal, alternative grounds of res judicata and collateral estoppel further reinforce the district court's ruling.

### A. Collateral Estoppel Applies to the Procedural Determination

Collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of "issues actually litigated and determined" in a prior proceeding. *McGowan v. Our Savior's Lutheran Church & School*, 277 N.W.2d 500, 502-03 (Minn. 1979). The district court's explicit finding that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day deadline and that there was "no good cause to extend" the deadline constitutes an actual determination of a specific issue.

The requirements for collateral estoppel are (1) the issue was actually litigated, (2) the issue was determined, and (3) the determination was necessary to the outcome. *Id.* at 503. All three requirements are satisfied here. Juetten contested the motion to dismiss in the LCA-Vision proceeding, the district court ruled on the compliance issue, and the ruling was essential to the dismissal order.

Appellant argues that collateral estoppel requires party identity. However, Minnesota courts have recognized an exception where the non-party seeking to relitigate was in privity with a party to the prior proceeding. *See Hauser v. Mealey*, 263 N.W.2d 267, 275 (Minn. 1978). Here, Juetten is the *same plaintiff* who litigated the procedural issue in the prior proceeding. She cannot escape the preclusive effect of the procedural ruling by merely adding a new defendant. The issue of compliance with the affidavit deadline was determined in a proceeding to which Juetten was a party, and she cannot now relitigate that issue to avoid the statutory consequence.

### B. Res Judicata Bars the Claim Because It Arises from the Same Transaction

Res judicata, or claim preclusion, bars a claim if there is "identity of parties, subject matter, and cause of action." *Housing & Redevelopment Auth. v. Bloomquist*, 279 N.W.2d 291, 295 (Minn. 1979). While Dr. Whiting was not a party to the LCA-Vision proceeding in his individual capacity, the cause of action is identical.

The subject matter—the LASIK surgery and alleged negligence in failing to recognize contraindications—is the same. The cause of action—medical malpractice requiring expert testimony—is the same. The only difference is the defendant named. Under Minnesota law, where a plaintiff sues multiple parties for the same conduct, a final judgment on the merits bars subsequent suits on the same claim. *See Kellar v. Von Holtum*, 568 N.W.2d 705, 710 (Minn. 1998) (holding that dismissal with prejudice operates as a final judgment on the merits for claim preclusion purposes).

The dismissal of Juetten's claim against LCA-Vision with prejudice constitutes a final judgment on the merits of her malpractice cause of action. The claim against Dr. Whiting is simply a different iteration of the same claim. Accordingly, res judicata bars relitigation of that claim through a different defendant.

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY CONSIDERED DR. WHITING'S SEPARATE PROCEDURAL STATUS

Appellant argues that the district court erred by ignoring Dr. Whiting's "separate procedural status." To the contrary, the district court correctly recognized that Dr. Whiting was added to the same case and subject to the same procedural requirements.

Dr. Whiting was not a stranger to the litigation. He performed the surgery that formed the basis of Juetten's claim. The original complaint alleged that his conduct (along with the examining doctor's) constituted negligence. Juetten's decision to pursue only vicarious liability against LCA-Vision initially did not alter the fact that Dr. Whiting was the alleged tortfeasor. When she amended the complaint to name him directly, she sought to hold him liable for the *same* conduct that underlay the dismissed claim.

The procedural posture is analogous to situations where a plaintiff sues an employer and employee jointly. If the claim against the employer is dismissed for failure to comply with a procedural requirement, the plaintiff cannot then proceed against the employee based on the same underlying conduct. *See Koenig v. St. Cloud Hosp.*, 525 N.W.2d 12, 16 (Minn. 1994) (discussing the relationship between vicarious and direct liability claims). The dismissal against LCA-Vision resolved the viability of the malpractice claim; the amendment adding Dr. Whiting did not create a new claim.

## V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT AFFIRMANCE

The policy underlying Minn. Stat. § 145.682 supports the district court's ruling. The statute requires plaintiffs in medical malpractice cases to identify expert witnesses within 180 days to enable defendants to assess the merits of the claim and to facilitate settlement or early resolution. *See Brostroem*, 683 N.W.2d at 777.

If this Court accepted Appellant's argument, plaintiffs could evade the statute's mandatory dismissal provision by the simple expedient of adding new defendants after the deadline. Such a result would frustrate the legislature's intent to promote efficient case management and prevent procedural gamesmanship. The district court correctly applied the statute according to its terms and consistent with its purpose.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The doctrine of law of the case, the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, and the doctrines of res judicata and collateral estoppel all support dismissal of Juetten's claims against Dr. Whiting. The procedural deficiency that resulted in dismissal of the claim against LCA-Vision applies with equal force to the claim against Dr. Whiting because it is the same cause of action arising from the same conduct.

**A. The Doctrine of Law of the Case Applies to the Procedural Determination**

Appellant argues that the doctrine of law of the case does not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the prior proceeding against LCA-Vision. The Court is not persuaded.

The doctrine of law of the case provides that "a court's decision on a legal issue binds that court and subsequent proceedings in the same case." *Northern States Power Co. v. # CLOSING STATEMENT FOR RESPONDENT DR. DAVID WHITING

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, we respectfully submit that this Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting. The plaintiff-appellant Stacy Juetten has failed to demonstrate any reversible error in the lower court's application of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, the doctrine of law of the case, or the principles of res judicata and collateral estoppel. To the contrary, the record overwhelmingly supports the district court's conclusion that Juetten's claims against Dr. Whiting are procedurally barred due to her failure to comply with the mandatory 180-day expert affidavit requirement.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment below for the reasons set forth below.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS RELEVANT TO DEFENSE

The undisputed facts establish that Dr. Whiting is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law.

**A. Juetten's Pre-Existing Conditions Were Under Control**

The record reflects that when Juetten presented for her preoperative assessment in July 2005, her diabetes and hyperthyroidism were both under control with medication. As the facts state, "poorly controlled diabetes and hyperthyroidism can diminish a patient fitness for the surgery. But in Juetten's case, both conditions were under control with medication, and neither condition had damaged her eyes." This critical fact undermines any claim that Dr. Whiting should have refused to perform the surgery based on these conditions.

**B. The SPK Was Minimal and Could Have Resulted from the Examination**

The facts establish that Juetten's superficial punctuate keratitis was "trace" — the lowest level detectable — and "could have resulted from the examination itself." The examining doctor reasonably determined that because Juetten's SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, she was a good candidate for surgery. This determination was medically reasonable under the circumstances.

**C. Juetten Was Informed of Potential Complications**

The facts explicitly state that "the doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems." This undermines any claim that Juetten was not adequately informed of risks.

**D. Dr. Whiting Was Aware of Trace SPK But Not of Cumulative Risks**

The facts indicate that "Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery." While this omission is cited, the defense notes that no expert testimony has been properly presented to establish that this alleged omission fell below the applicable standard of care — particularly given that Juetten's conditions were controlled and her SPK was minimal.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

**A. The Mandatory Dismissal Provisions of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 Were Properly Applied**

The district court correctly applied Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008), which mandates dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case" when a plaintiff fails to serve required expert affidavits within 180 days after commencement of the suit.

The plaintiff argues that the dismissal of her claim against LCA-Vision should not affect her claims against Dr. Whiting. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the statute explicitly states that dismissal applies to "each cause of action" — not each defendant. As the district court correctly observed, Juetten's original complaint alleged a single cause of action for medical malpractice arising from the July 2005 LASIK surgery. Her vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision was merely a legal theory for holding that entity responsible for Dr. Whiting's conduct. The Eighth Circuit's holding in *Walsh v. United States*, 249 F.3d 122, 131 (8th Cir. 2001), supports the conclusion that vicarious and direct liability claims arising from identical conduct constitute the same cause of action for procedural purposes.

Second, allowing Juetten to proceed against Dr. Whiting after failing to comply with the deadline against LCA-Vision would render the 180-day requirement meaningless. A plaintiff could simply name a nominal defendant, miss the deadline, suffer dismissal, and then add the actual tortfeasor with affidavits prepared in the interim. This is precisely the "procedural gamesmanship" that the statute was designed to prevent. *See State ex rel. McNeil v. Hudson*, 291 N.W.2d 634, 637 (Minn. 1980) (courts must construe statutes to avoid absurd results).

**B. The Doctrine of Law of the Case Properly Precludes Relitigation**

The district court correctly applied the doctrine of law of the case. This doctrine provides that "a court's decision on a legal issue binds that court and subsequent proceedings in the same case." *Northern States Power Co. v. City of Red Wing*, 274 N.W.2d 117, 119 (Minn. 1978).

Juetten's argument that law of the case does not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the prior proceeding is unpersuasive. When Juetten amended her complaint to add Dr. Whiting as a defendant, she did not commence a new action; rather, she sought to add a party to existing litigation. Under Minnesota law, an amended complaint adding new parties relates back to the original filing for purposes of determining the procedural posture of the case. *See Anderson v. City of St. Cloud*, 508 N.W.2d 905, 908 (Minn. 1993).

The district court's prior ruling — that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day affidavit service deadline and that there was no good cause to extend the deadline — was a definitive legal determination on the viability of the underlying malpractice claim. This was not merely a ruling against LCA-Vision; it was a ruling regarding Juetten's failure to satisfy a condition precedent to maintaining her medical malpractice action. *See Pletan v. Gaines*, 494 N.W.2d 38, 42 (Minn. 1992) (emphasizing the mandatory nature of statutory requirements in medical malpractice proceedings).

Juetten's reliance on *Gier v. Lingle*, 538 N.W.2d 242 (Minn. 1995), is misplaced. While *Gier* supports the general proposition that a dismissal of one defendant does not automatically preclude claims against others, it does not permit a plaintiff to circumvent a statutory dismissal penalty by simply adding a new defendant after the statutory deadline has expired. The procedural deficiency here was not a failure to name a specific defendant, but a failure to serve the required expert affidavits within the time prescribed by statute.

**C. Res Judicata and Collateral Estoppel Provide Alternative Independent Grounds**

Even if this Court were to find any error in the application of law of the case (which we submit it should not), the doctrines of res judicata and collateral estoppel independently support affirmance.

Collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of "issues actually litigated and determined" in a prior proceeding. *McGowan v. Our Savior's Lutheran Church & School*, 277 N.W.2d 500, 502-03 (Minn. 1979). The district court explicitly found that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day deadline and that there was no good cause to extend it. Juetten, as the plaintiff in both actions, cannot relitigate this specific issue of procedural compliance against Dr. Whiting.

Furthermore, res judicata bars claims where there is identity of subject matter and cause of action. *Housing & Redevelopment Auth. v. Bloomquist*, 279 N.W.2d 291, 295 (Minn. 1979). The dismissal of Juetten's claim against LCA-Vision with prejudice constituted a final judgment on the merits of her malpractice cause of action. *See Kellar v. Von Holtum*, 568 N.W.2d 705, 710 (Minn. 1998). Because the claim against Dr. Whiting arises from the same transaction and involves the same underlying negligence, it is barred by claim preclusion.

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

**A. Rebuttal of Plaintiff's Statutory Interpretation Argument**

The plaintiff argues that Minn. Stat. § 145.682 requires service against each defendant individually. This argument misreads the statute. The statute requires service of affidavits identifying "the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts' testimony" within 180 days after "commencement of the suit." Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The suit was commenced when Juetten filed her original complaint — not when she amended to add Dr. Whiting.

The statute's focus on the "cause of action" rather than individual defendants reflects the legislature's intent to prevent the very gamesmanship that Juetten attempts here. As the district court correctly observed, to hold that the dismissal of the claim against LCA-Vision allows Juetten to proceed against Dr. Whiting would render the 180-day deadline meaningless.

**B. Rebuttal of Plaintiff's Law of the Case Argument**

The plaintiff argues that law of the case should not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the prior proceeding. This argument ignores the well-established principle that an amended complaint adding new parties relates back to the original filing. *See Anderson v. City of St. Cloud*, 508 N.W.2d 905, 908 (Minn. 1993).

Moreover, the procedural deficiency at issue — failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days — is a characteristic of the litigation itself, not merely of the parties involved. The district court's ruling that Juetten failed to comply with the statutory deadline was a threshold determination about the viability of her malpractice cause of action, not a determination specific to LCA-Vision.

**C. Rebuttal of Plaintiff's Policy Arguments**

The plaintiff attempts to invoke policy considerations, arguing that the strict application of the statute works a hardship. However, the Minnesota Supreme Court has emphasized that the statutory requirements for expert affidavits in medical malpractice cases are mandatory and "not subject to discretionary waiver." *Pletan v. Gaines*, 494 N.W.2d 38, 42 (Minn. 1992).

The purpose of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 is to ensure the early identification of expert testimony and to facilitate the efficient resolution of medical malpractice claims. *See Brostroem v. Lakeview Surgery Ctr.*, 683 N.W.2d 771, 777 (Minn. 2004). Allowing Juetten to proceed against Dr. Whiting would frustrate this purpose and encourage the precise procedural gamesmanship that the statute was designed to prevent.

---

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY GRANTED SUMMARY JUDGMENT

The district court's grant of summary judgment was proper because there are no genuine issues of material fact and Dr. Whiting is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The procedural bars — law of the case, statutory interpretation, res judicata, and collateral estoppel — are questions of law that the district court properly resolved.

The district court noted in its February 2009 order that "the substance of Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." However, this observation was dicta and does not alter the fact that Juetten failed to serve the affidavits within the mandatory 180-day deadline. The timing requirement is mandatory, and the substance of the affidavits is irrelevant to the procedural question of compliance.

---

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting.

The plaintiff failed to comply with the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. This failure was properly found to be a fatal procedural defect that extinguished her malpractice cause of action. The addition of Dr. Whiting as a defendant after the dismissal of LCA-Vision does not revive a claim that was statutorily terminated.

The district court's decision is supported by: (1) the plain language of Minn.
