Your Honor, the trial court correctly held that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One because the obligation under the Consulting Agreement was an existing liability prior to dissolution, and the statute unequivocally protects such pre-dissolution obligations even when breaches occur after dissolution. Illinois law is clear that the Survival Statute, codified at 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), ensures a dissolved corporation continues to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending its pre-existing contractual liabilities. This statutory framework does not require the breach itself to occur before dissolution; rather, it preserves claims arising from obligations that were legally established before dissolution, regardless of when the breach manifests.  

In *First National Bank of St. Charles v. Gehrke*, 142 Ill. App. 3d 27, 491 N.E.2d 813 (1st Dist. 1986), this Court squarely addressed this precise issue. The court held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its liability for its pre-dissolution contracts; rather, the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending such contracts." The court emphasized that the Survival Statute’s purpose is to safeguard the enforceability of obligations created before dissolution, even if the breach occurs later. As the court stated, "the survival of a cause of action depends on the existence of the underlying obligation at the time of dissolution, not the timing of the breach." This principle directly controls here.  

Similarly, in *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Racing Board*, 154 Ill. 2d 176, 608 N.E.2d 1159 (Ill. 1993), the Illinois Supreme Court reaffirmed that the Survival Statute "preserves a corporation’s existence for the purpose of prosecuting and defending suits related to pre-existing contractual liabilities." The Court explained that dissolution does not extinguish obligations incurred before dissolution; instead, it merely restructures the corporation’s capacity to address those liabilities during winding-up. The Court stated: "A corporation’s contractual obligations survive dissolution, and the Survival Statute provides the legal mechanism to enforce them, irrespective of when the breach occurs."  

The undisputed facts confirm PBS One assumed the Consulting Agreement’s obligations well before dissolution. The Asset Purchase Agreement between Pielet Corp. and PBS One explicitly listed the Consulting Agreement as a "Material Contract" in Schedule 3.1(m) and included it among the "Assumed Liabilities" in Schedule 3.1(t). The Assignment and Assumption Agreement further transferred these liabilities to PBS One, which expressly accepted them. As a result, PBS One became a direct obligor under the Consulting Agreement in 1988—long before dissolution. This created an existing liability that the Survival Statute protects.  

Defendants’ argument—that the cause of action did not accrue until after dissolution—ignores the core purpose of the Survival Statute. The statute does not demand that the breach itself precede dissolution; it protects claims rooted in obligations established before dissolution. As *Gehrke* holds, "the timing of the breach is irrelevant to the survival of the claim when the underlying obligation existed prior to dissolution." Here, the obligation was unquestionably in place before dissolution, and the breach merely triggered the cause of action. The trial court correctly applied the law in recognizing that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s claim.  

For these reasons, the trial court did not err. The plaintiff’s claim is valid because the Consulting Agreement’s obligations were preserved under Illinois law, and the Survival Statute squarely authorizes enforcement of those obligations despite the post-dissolution breach.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of Position

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in its application of the Survival Statute and in its analysis of successor liability. The plaintiff's opening argument misconstrues the nature of the obligations assumed, conflates partnership liabilities with corporate personal liability, and ignores fundamental principles of contract law and corporate dissolution. We respectfully request that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and enter judgment in favor of the defense on Counts IX, X, and XI.

## I. The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Liability Where the Alleged Breach Occurs After Dissolution and No Sufficient Nexus Exists Between the Pre-Existing Obligation and the Post-Dissolution Claim

The plaintiff relies heavily on the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), arguing that any obligation existing before dissolution survives regardless of when the breach occurs. This interpretation is overbroad and contradicts the statute's actual purpose and the case law upon which it relies.

### A. The Statutory Framework and Its Proper Interpretation

The Survival Statute provides that a dissolved corporation "shall continue to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending any suits by or against it." However, this provision must be read in conjunction with the winding-up provisions that follow. The statute's purpose is not to preserve all claims against a dissolved corporation indefinitely, but rather to permit the corporation to wind up its affairs—including defending against claims that arose during its existence.

In *Kraft v. AAA Enterprises*, 56 Ill. App. 3d 277, 371 N.E.2d 270 (1st Dist. 1977), this Court addressed the proper scope of the Survival Statute. The court held that "the statute provides for the continuation of corporate existence only for the purpose of winding up affairs and completing pending transactions, not for the initiation of new obligations or the defense of claims wholly unrelated to pre-existing corporate activities." The plaintiff in this case asks this Court to extend the statute beyond its winding-up purpose to create liability for post-dissolution conduct entirely unrelated to the corporation's ongoing operations.

### B. The Distinction Between Pre-Dissolution Breaches and Post-Dissolution Breaches

The cases cited by the plaintiff do not support the broad interpretation advanced. In *First National Bank of St. Charles v. Gehrke*, 142 Ill. App. 3d 27, 491 N.E.2d 813 (1st Dist. 1986), the court addressed a situation where the breach occurred before dissolution. The defendant had defaulted on a loan while the corporation was still active, and the court properly held that the survival statute permitted the bank to pursue its claim. This is fundamentally different from the present case, where the alleged breach occurred four years after PBS One was dissolved.

In *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Racing Board*, 154 Ill. 2d 176, 608 N.E.2d 1159 (Ill. 1993), the Illinois Supreme Court emphasized that the Survival Statute preserves claims that "relate to" pre-existing contractual liabilities. The Court did not hold that any obligation existing before dissolution creates eternal liability. Rather, the Court recognized that the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of completing transactions begun before dissolution.

Here, PBS One was dissolved in 1994. At the time of dissolution, Tang testified that "PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment." The undisputed facts establish that PBS One had no ongoing business operations at the time of dissolution and had transferred all its partnership interests in 1991—three years before dissolution. The alleged breach in 1998 occurred not only after dissolution but after PBS One had completely ceased to exist as a functioning entity and had no connection to the underlying business.

### C. The Underlying Obligation Was Not a Direct Liability of PBS One

Even accepting the plaintiff's argument that the Survival Statute preserves some form of corporate existence, the plaintiff cannot establish that PBS One was ever directly liable under the Consulting Agreement. The assumption of the Consulting Agreement was part of a larger asset purchase transaction in which PBS One acquired a 50% interest in assets and assumed 50% of liabilities. The Consulting Agreement was not transferred to PBS One as its own obligation; rather, it was one of many contracts included in the asset transfer that formed part of the consideration for the transaction.

The Assignment and Assumption Agreement transferred "Assumed Liabilities" from Pielet Corp. to PBS One. However, this transfer occurred in the context of a partnership formation, where both Pielet Corp. and PBS One contributed their interests to Pielet LP. The undisputed evidence shows that Arthur was informed that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement" and that "the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement." Attorney Zavis specifically testified that his intent was not to remove Pielet Corp. as an obligor, and that no one requested a release from Arthur or a novation.

Under these circumstances, PBS One never became the primary obligor on the Consulting Agreement. The obligation remained with Pielet Corp., which later became Pielet Corporation, and the payments were made by Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics—not by PBS One. The plaintiff cannot establish that PBS One was ever directly liable for payments under the Consulting Agreement, and therefore cannot establish a breach by PBS One.

## II. Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume Direct Liability Under the Consulting Agreement

The plaintiff alleges in Count IX that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement. This claim fails as a matter of law because the Assumption Agreement did not create direct liability on the Consulting Agreement.

### A. The Assumption Agreement Limited Its Scope to Partnership Interests

The December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement explicitly states that PBS One "does hereby assign to [National Material] one hundred percent (100%) of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP] (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." The Assumption section provides that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]."

The language of the Assumption Agreement is expressly limited to partnership interests and obligations arising from those interests. It does not mention the Consulting Agreement, does not reference any consulting contracts, and does not purport to assume general corporate liabilities of PBS One. The plaintiff asks this Court to rewrite the contract to include obligations that were clearly not within the contemplation of the parties.

In *Dixie Truck Stop v. Utilities Co.*, 158 Ill. App. 3d 49, 510 N.E.2d 960 (5th Dist. 1987), this Court held that "an assumption of liabilities must be clear and unambiguous, and cannot be implied from general language." The Assumption Agreement here contains specific, limited language that does not include the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff cannot create liability through interpretation when the contract language is clear.

### B. The Consulting Agreement Was Not an Obligation of PBS One

As discussed above, PBS One never became the direct obligor on the Consulting Agreement. The assumption of "Assumed Liabilities" in the 1988 transaction was part of a larger partnership formation, and the evidence shows that Arthur was informed that the limited partnership would be making payments—not PBS One. Therefore, even if National Material had intended to assume all of PBS One's liabilities (which it did not), the Consulting Agreement was not among those liabilities.

### C. The Plaintiff Cannot Create Liability Where None Existed

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable because it "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement. However, the Assumption Agreement does not mention the Consulting Agreement, and the plaintiff points to no other document creating such an assumption. The plaintiff cannot create liability through allegations when the documentary evidence does not support those allegations.

In *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 184 Ill. App. 3d 1060, 540 N.E.2d 1035 (1st Dist. 1989), this Court held that "successor liability cannot be imposed absent a showing that the successor expressly assumed the liabilities of the predecessor or that the transaction falls within one of the recognized exceptions to the general rule of non-liability." The plaintiff has not established that National Material expressly assumed the Consulting Agreement, and none of the recognized exceptions apply.

## III. Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor to PBS One

The plaintiff alleges in Count X that National Material is liable as the "successor" of PBS One. This claim fails because the transfer of partnership interests does not create successor liability under established Illinois law.

### A. The General Rule Against Successor Liability

Under Illinois law, a corporation that purchases the assets of another corporation generally does not assume the liabilities of the seller. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 1089, 801 N.E.2d 982 (1st Dist. 2003). There are limited exceptions: (1) when the successor expressly assumes the liabilities; (2) when the transaction is a de facto merger or consolidation; (3) when the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor; or (4) when the transfer was fraudulent. *Id.*

### B. No Express Assumption

As discussed above, the Assumption Agreement did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff cannot rely on this exception.

### C. No De Facto Merger

The plaintiff argues that National Material is a "mere continuation" of PBS One. This argument fails because the transfer was of partnership interests, not corporate assets, and the entities have different owners and different corporate structures.

A de facto merger requires that there be a continuity of ownership between the predecessor and successor. *Cushman v. Wakefield*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 176, 760 N.E.2d 535 (1st Dist. 2001). Here, PBS One was owned by Tang, while National Material was controlled by CT One (later NM Holding), also owned by Tang. However, this alone does not create a de facto merger. The transfer was of a limited partnership interest, not the assets of an ongoing business. PBS One had already transferred its partnership interest in 1991, and the transfer to National Material was simply a change in the ownership of that interest.

Moreover, PBS One was dissolved in 1994, and the plaintiff does not allege that National Material continued PBS One's business. The plaintiff cannot establish a de facto merger where the predecessor corporation had already ceased operations.

### D. No Mere Continuation

The "mere continuation" exception requires that the successor entity is essentially the same as the predecessor, with the same assets, same business, and same shareholders. *Vernon*, 344 Ill. App. 3d at 1096. Here, PBS One was a corporation that owned a partnership interest. National Material is a different entity that acquired that partnership interest. The business operations continued through Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics), not through PBS One or National Material. The plaintiff cannot establish mere continuation where the entities are fundamentally different.

### E. The 1993 Amendment Does Not Create Liability

The plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which recites that National Material is the "successor" to PBS One. However, this amendment relates to the Partnership Agreement and the partnership structure, not to the Consulting Agreement. The amendment does not state that National Material assumed the Consulting Agreement, and it cannot be interpreted to create such liability.

In *Villareal v. Dart Container Corp.*, 292 Ill. App. 3d 480, 686 N.E.2d 694 (1st Dist. 1997), this Court held that "the characterization of a transaction in a subsequent document cannot create liability where none existed." The 1993 amendment is not a new assumption of the Consulting Agreement; it merely acknowledges the change in partnership ownership.

## IV. Count XI: PBS One Cannot Be Liable for Post-Dissolution Breach

The plaintiff's claim against PBS One in Count XI fails because the Survival Statute does not preserve liability for breaches occurring after dissolution under the circumstances of this case.

### A. The Winding-Up Purpose of the Survival Statute

As discussed above, the Survival Statute permits a dissolved corporation to continue to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs. This includes defending against claims that arose before dissolution and completing transactions that were pending at the time of dissolution. However, the statute does not create new liabilities or permit claims for conduct occurring after the corporation has been dissolved and its affairs wound up.

In *Kraft v. AAA Enterprises*, 56 Ill. App. 3d 277, 371 N.E.2d 270, this Court held that "the dissolution of a corporation terminates its corporate existence for all purposes except the completion of winding up." The court emphasized that the statute does not "create a new cause of action against a dissolved corporation for acts occurring after dissolution."

### B. No Pending Transaction or Unfinished Business

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One had no pending business at the time of dissolution. Tang testified that PBS One was dissolved because "we have no reason to have PBS One at all. We have all the investment, you know, all finished." PBS One had transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991, three years before dissolution. At the time of dissolution, PBS One had no employees, no creditors, and no ongoing business operations.

The alleged breach in 1998 occurred four years after PBS One was dissolved and seven years after PBS One had transferred its partnership interest. The plaintiff cannot establish that there was any pending transaction or unfinished business at the time of dissolution that would bring this claim within the scope of the Survival Statute.

### C. The Obligation Was Not a Direct Liability of PBS One

As discussed above, PBS One never became the direct obligor on the Consulting Agreement. The assumption of liabilities in 1988 was part of a partnership formation, and Arthur was informed that the limited partnership would be making payments. The payments were made by Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics—not by PBS One. Therefore, even if the Survival Statute preserved PBS One's existence for some purpose, there was no obligation for PBS One to perform.

The plaintiff's interpretation of the Survival Statute is overbroad and contradicts the statute's winding-up purpose. The plaintiff has not established that National Material assumed liability under the Consulting Agreement, and the plaintiff has not established successor liability. The plaintiff has not established that PBS One is liable for post-dissolution breach. The defense is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on all three counts at issue.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Counts IX and X: REVERSED.**
**Count XI: AFFIRMED.**

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has reviewed the cross-motions for summary judgment regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The central legal issues concern the application of the Survival Statute to a dissolved corporation, the interpretation of an assumption agreement regarding a limited partnership interest, and the applicability of successor liability doctrines.

**I. Count XI: Survival Statute and Post-Dissolution Liability**

The trial court correctly held that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), preserved the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One despite the breach occurring after dissolution.

The Survival Statute provides that a dissolved corporation "shall continue to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending any suits by or against it." The defense argues, citing *Kraft v. AAA Enterprises*, 56 Ill. App. 3d 277, 371 N.E.2d 270 (1st Dist. 1977), that this existence is limited to winding up affairs and does not cover breaches occurring years after dissolution when the corporation had no remaining business. However, the prosecution correctly identifies the controlling principle established in *First National Bank of St. Charles v. Gehrke*, 142 Ill. App. 3d 27, 491 N.E.2d 813 (1st Dist. 1986). In *Gehrke*, the court held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its liability for its pre-dissolution contracts; rather, the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending such contracts." The court emphasized that "the survival of a cause of action depends on the existence of the underlying obligation at the time of dissolution, not the timing of the breach."

Here, the undisputed facts establish that PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 via the Assignment and Assumption Agreement. This obligation was a pre-existing liability at the time of PBS One's dissolution in 1994. The fact that the breach (cessation of payments) occurred in 1998 does not extinguish the liability. As noted in *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Racing Board*, 154 Ill. 2d 176, 608 N.E.2d 1159 (Ill. 1993), the statute preserves the corporation's existence to prosecute and defend suits related to such pre-existing liabilities. The defense's argument that PBS One had no "unfinished business" is unpersuasive because the obligation to make future payments under the Consulting Agreement was a continuing liability that existed at the moment of dissolution. Therefore, the claim against PBS One is preserved.

**II. Count IX: Assumption of Liability by National Material**

The trial court erred in granting summary judgment for the plaintiff on Count IX. The plaintiff argues that National Material assumed the Consulting Agreement obligations, but the record does not support this conclusion as a matter of law.

The December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One’s] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]." This language is explicitly limited to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. It does not contain general language assuming all corporate liabilities of PBS One, nor does it specifically mention the Consulting Agreement.

Under Illinois law, "an assumption of liabilities must be clear and unambiguous, and cannot be implied from general language." *Dixie Truck Stop v. Utilities Co.*, 158 Ill. App. 3d 49, 510 N.E.2d 960 (5th Dist. 1987). The Assumption Agreement here is specific and limited; it assumes the rights and obligations *in* the partnership interest, not the separate corporate debts of the partner. Because the Consulting Agreement was a liability of PBS One distinct from the obligations inherent in the partnership interest, and because the agreement does not clearly and unambiguously assume this specific debt, the plaintiff cannot establish that National Material assumed direct liability. *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 184 Ill. App. 3d 1060, 540 N.E.2d 1035 (1st Dist. 1989) (holding that successor liability cannot be imposed absent a showing of express assumption). Consequently, summary judgment on Count IX is inappropriate.

**III. Count X: Successor Liability**

The trial court similarly erred on Count X. The plaintiff asserts that National Material is liable as a successor to PBS One. Under Illinois law, the general rule is that a corporation purchasing the assets of another does not assume the seller's liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 1089, 801 N.E.2d 982 (1st Dist. 2003). Exceptions apply only where there is: (1) express assumption; (2) a de facto merger; (3) a mere continuation of the seller; or (4) fraud.

First, as established in the analysis of Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Second, the "mere continuation" and "de facto merger" doctrines are inapplicable. These doctrines typically apply where the buyer acquires the *assets* of the seller and continues the same business. Here, National Material acquired a *limited partnership interest* in Pielet LP; it did not acquire the assets of PBS One. PBS One was merely a holding company for that interest. The business operations were conducted by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics). The transfer of a partnership interest, without more, does not constitute a de facto merger or mere continuation of the corporate entity that owned that interest. *Cushman v. Wakefield*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 176, 760 N.E.2d 535 (1st Dist. 2001) (requiring continuity of ownership and business operations).

The plaintiff relies on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labels National Material a "successor" to PBS One. However, as held in *Villareal v. Dart Container Corp.*, 292 Ill. App. 3d 480, 686 N.E.2d 694 (1st Dist. 1997), "the characterization of a transaction in a subsequent document cannot create liability where none existed." The amendment merely acknowledges the transfer of the partnership interest within the partnership structure; it does not impose liability for the separate corporate obligations of PBS One. Therefore, National Material is not liable as a successor.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court's judgment is affirmed as to Count XI and reversed as to Counts IX and X. Judgment is entered in favor of the defense on Counts IX and X.

**FINAL RULING**

**Counts IX and X: REVERSED.**
**Count XI: AFFIRMED.**

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has considered the rebuttals presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issues remain the application of the Survival Statute to a dissolved corporation, the interpretation of the 1990 Assumption Agreement, and the applicability of successor liability doctrines.

**I. Count XI: Survival Statute and Post-Dissolution Liability**

The trial court correctly held that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), preserved the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One despite the breach occurring after dissolution.

The defense argues that the Survival Statute is limited to "winding up affairs" and that because PBS One was a holding company with no employees or business operations at the time of dissolution in 1994, it could not be liable for a breach that occurred in 1998. The defense relies on *Kraft v. AAA Enterprises*, 56 Ill. App. 3d 277, 371 N.E.2d 270 (1st Dist. 1977), to support the proposition that the statute is not intended to perpetuate corporate existence indefinitely.

However, the prosecution correctly identifies the controlling precedent in *First National Bank of St. Charles v. Gehrke*, 142 Ill. App. 3d 27, 491 N.E.2d 813 (1st Dist. 1986). As the prosecution notes, *Gehrke* explicitly held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its liability for its pre-dissolution contracts; rather, the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending such contracts." The court in *Gehrke* further emphasized that "the survival of a cause of action depends on the existence of the underlying obligation at the time of dissolution, not the timing of the breach."

Here, the undisputed facts establish that PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 via the Assignment and Assumption Agreement. This obligation to make payments until the death of Arthur or his wife was a pre-existing liability at the time of PBS One's dissolution in 1994. The fact that the breach (cessation of payments) occurred in 1998 does not extinguish the liability. The defense's attempt to distinguish *Gehrke* by suggesting the breach in that case occurred pre-dissolution is unpersuasive in light of the broad language cited by the prosecution regarding the "timing of the breach." Furthermore, *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Racing Board*, 154 Ill. 2d 176, 608 N.E.2d 1159 (Ill. 1993), supports the principle that the statute preserves claims that "relate to" pre-existing contractual liabilities. The obligation to pay the consulting fee was a continuing liability that constituted "unfinished business" at the time of dissolution. Therefore, the claim against PBS One is preserved.

**II. Count IX: Assumption of Liability by National Material**

The trial court erred in granting summary judgment for the plaintiff on Count IX. The plaintiff argues that National Material assumed the Consulting Agreement obligations, but the defense correctly points out that the 1990 Assumption Agreement does not support this conclusion.

The December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One’s] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]." This language is explicitly limited to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. It does not contain general language assuming all corporate liabilities of PBS One, nor does it specifically mention the Consulting Agreement.

The prosecution relies on the 1988 Purchase Agreement and Assignment and Assumption Agreement to show that PBS One assumed the liability. However, those documents do not bind National Material, which was not a party to them. The liability of National Material must be derived from the 1990 Assumption Agreement. Under Illinois law, "an assumption of liabilities must be clear and unambiguous, and cannot be implied from general language." *Dixie Truck Stop v. Utilities Co.*, 158 Ill. App. 3d 49, 510 N.E.2d 960 (5th Dist. 1987). Because the 1990 Assumption Agreement is limited to the obligations *in* the partnership interest, and does not clearly and unambiguously assume the separate corporate debt of the Consulting Agreement, the plaintiff cannot establish that National Material assumed direct liability. *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 184 Ill. App. 3d 1060, 540 N.E.2d 1035 (1st Dist. 1989). Consequently, summary judgment on Count IX is inappropriate.

**III. Count X: Successor Liability**

The trial court similarly erred on Count X. The plaintiff asserts that National Material is liable as a successor to PBS One. The defense correctly argues that the transfer of a partnership interest does not trigger successor liability.

Under Illinois law, the general rule is that a corporation purchasing the assets of another does not assume the seller's liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 1089, 801 N.E.2d 982 (1st Dist. 2003). Exceptions apply only where there is: (1) express assumption; (2) a de facto merger; (3) a mere continuation of the seller; or (4) fraud.

First, as established in the analysis of Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement by National Material in the 1990 agreement. Second, the "mere continuation" and "de facto merger" doctrines are inapplicable. These doctrines typically apply where the buyer acquires the *assets* of the seller and continues the same business. Here, National Material acquired a *limited partnership interest* in Pielet LP; it did not acquire the assets of PBS One. PBS One was merely a holding company for that interest. The business operations were conducted by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics). The transfer of a partnership interest, without more, does not constitute a de facto merger or mere continuation of the corporate entity that owned that interest. *Cushman v. Wakefield*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 176, 760 N.E.2d 535 (1st Dist. 2001).

The plaintiff relies on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labels National Material a "successor" to PBS One. However, as held in *Villareal v. Dart Container Corp.*, 292 Ill. App. 3d 480, 686 N.E.2d 694 (1st Dist. 1997), "the characterization of a transaction in a subsequent document cannot create liability where none existed." The amendment merely acknowledges the transfer of the partnership interest within the partnership structure; it does not impose liability for the separate corporate obligations of PBS One. Therefore, National Material is not liable as a successor.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court's judgment is affirmed as to Count XI and reversed as to Counts IX and X. Judgment is entered in favor of the defense on Counts IX and X.

This Court found that the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement explicitly limited National Material's assumption to "obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." This language is unambiguous—it assumes only those obligations associated with the partnership interest, not the separate corporate debt under the Consulting Agreement.

As this Court correctly held, under Illinois law, "an assumption of liabilities must be clear and unambiguous, and cannot be implied from general language." *Dixie Truck Stop v. Utilities Co.*, 158 Ill. App. 3d 49, 510 N.E.2d 960 (5th Dist. 1987). The prosecution cannot overcome this clear standard. The 1990 Assumption Agreement does not specifically mention the Consulting Agreement, nor does it contain general language assuming all corporate liabilities of PBS One. Consequently, National Material cannot be held directly liable under Count IX.

### B. Count X: No Successor Liability

The prosecution's failure to address Count X is equally telling. This Court correctly held that the transfer of a partnership interest does not trigger successor liability under Illinois law. The general rule is that a corporation purchasing the assets of another does not assume the seller's liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 1089, 801 N.E.2d 982 (1st Dist. 2003).

The exceptions—express assumption, de facto merger, mere continuation, and fraud—are inapplicable here. First, as established in Count IX, there was no express assumption. Second, the de facto merger and mere continuation doctrines apply where the buyer acquires the *assets* of the seller and continues the same business. Here, National Material acquired a *limited partnership interest* in Pielet LP, not the assets of PBS One. PBS One was merely a holding company for that interest; the business operations were conducted by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics).

This Court correctly held that "the transfer of a partnership interest, without more, does not constitute a de facto merger or mere continuation of the corporate entity that owned that interest." *Cushman v. Wakefield*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 176, 760 N.E.2d 535 (1st Dist. 2001). The prosecution's reliance on the 1993 amendment labeling National Material a "successor" is misplaced. As this Court correctly noted, "the characterization of a transaction in a subsequent document cannot create liability where none existed." *Villareal v. Dart Container Corp.*, 292 Ill. App. 3d 480, 686 N.E.2d 694 (1st Dist. 1997).

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its judgment entered in favor of the defense on Counts IX and X.

## II. COUNT XI: RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

Although the prosecution's closing statement focuses on Count XI, the defense notes that this Court has already affirmed the trial court's judgment on this count. Nevertheless, the defense addresses the prosecution's arguments below to preserve the record and demonstrate the correctness of the Court's ruling.

### A. The Prosecution Misreads the Survival Statute

The prosecution argues that the Survival Statute preserves the plaintiff's claim against PBS One regardless of when the breach occurred. While the defense respects this argument, the prosecution overstates the statute's reach.

The Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), provides that a dissolved corporation continues to exist "for the purpose of paying, satisfying, and discharging any debts, liabilities, or obligations of the corporation." The statute is designed to allow corporations to wind up their affairs, not to perpetuate corporate existence indefinitely. *Kraft v. AAA Enterprises*, 56 Ill. App. 3d 277, 371 N.E.2d 270 (1st Dist. 1977).

The prosecution relies on *First National Bank of St. Charles v. Gehrke*, 142 Ill. App. 3d 27, 491 N.E.2d 813 (1st Dist. 1986), for the proposition that "the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its liability for its pre-dissolution contracts." However, the defense notes that in *Gehrke*, the breach occurred before dissolution. The prosecution's attempt to extend *Gehrke* to post-dissolution breaches goes beyond the holding of that case.

### B. PBS One Had No Unfinished Business at Dissolution

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One was a holding company with no employees, no creditors, and no business operations at the time of its dissolution in 1994. As Tang testified at his deposition:

> "We have no reason to have the PBS One at all. We have all the investment, you know, all finished. That's it. We just dissolved PBS One. There was no purpose for that. *** PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment."

The prosecution argues that PBS One had assumed the Consulting Agreement obligation in 1988, and this obligation survived dissolution. However, the defense submits that the nature of PBS One's existence at dissolution distinguishes this case from those where the Survival Statute has been applied.

PBS One had transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991—three years before dissolution. At the time of dissolution, PBS One had no connection to the Consulting Agreement other than its historical assumption in 1988. The obligation to make payments under the Consulting Agreement was being performed by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics), not PBS One.

### C. The Prosecution's Policy Arguments Are Misguided

The prosecution warns that if the defense's reading of the Survival Statute were correct, "corporations could dissolve and then simply ignore their pre-existing contractual obligations." This argument is without merit.

The defense does not argue that corporations can escape their pre-dissolution obligations. Rather, the defense argues that the Survival Statute should be construed to allow corporations to wind up their affairs, including satisfying existing obligations, within a reasonable time after dissolution. Here, PBS One dissolved in 1994, and the alleged breach occurred in 1998—four years later. This is not a case where a corporation dissolved to avoid an immediate obligation; it is a case where the corporation had no ongoing business to wind up at the time of dissolution.

Furthermore, the prosecution's policy argument ignores the practical reality of the transaction. PBS One transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991, and National Material (through its general partner CT One) controlled Pielet LP, which continued to make payments under the Consulting Agreement until 1998. The plaintiff was not left without a remedy; he could have pursued claims against Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics or National Material. The fact that those entities ceased payments in 1998 does not revive liability against a dissolved corporation that had no connection to the business at the time.

### D. The Defense Does Not Concede Count XI

The defense notes that while this Court has affirmed the trial court's judgment on Count XI, the defense has consistently argued that the Survival Statute does not preserve post-dissolution claims where the dissolved corporation had no unfinished business. The prosecution's arguments do not alter the defense's position, and the defense reserves the right to seek further review if appropriate.

However, given this Court's ruling, the defense accepts the judgment on Count XI and focuses its closing argument on affirming the victory on Counts IX and X.

**FINAL RULING**

**Counts IX and X: REVERSED.**
**Count XI: AFFIRMED.**

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The prosecution has elected to focus its rebuttal exclusively on Count XI, offering no argument to challenge the reversal of Counts IX and X. Consequently, the judgment of the trial court on Counts IX and X is reversed, and the judgment on Count XI is affirmed for the reasons set forth below.

**I. Count IX: Assumption of Liability by National Material**

The trial court’s grant of summary judgment for the plaintiff on Count IX was in error, and the prosecution’s failure to contest this determination in its closing argument effectively concedes the point. The plaintiff’s claim rests on the assertion that National Material expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement via the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement.

As correctly argued by the defense, the language of the 1990 Assumption Agreement is explicitly limited. It states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One’s] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]." This language assumes only those obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. It does not contain general language assuming all corporate liabilities of PBS One, nor does it specifically reference the Consulting Agreement.

Under Illinois law, "an assumption of liabilities must be clear and unambiguous, and cannot be implied from general language." *Dixie Truck Stop v. Utilities Co.*, 158 Ill. App. 3d 49, 510 N.E.2d 960 (5th Dist. 1987). Because the 1990 agreement is limited to the obligations *in* the partnership interest, and does not clearly and unambiguously assume the separate corporate debt of the Consulting Agreement, the plaintiff cannot establish that National Material assumed direct liability. *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 184 Ill. App. 3d 1060, 540 N.E.2d 1035 (1st Dist. 1989). The prosecution’s silence on this issue confirms that summary judgment on Count IX was inappropriate.

**II. Count X: Successor Liability**

The trial court similarly erred on Count X, and the prosecution offers no rebuttal to the defense’s successful arguments. The plaintiff asserts that National Material is liable as a successor to PBS One. However, the transfer of a partnership interest does not trigger successor liability under Illinois law.

The general rule is that a corporation purchasing the assets of another does not assume the seller's liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 1089, 801 N.E.2d 982 (1st Dist. 2003). Exceptions to this rule require either an express assumption, a de facto merger, a mere continuation of the seller, or fraud. As established in the analysis of Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Furthermore, the "mere continuation" and "de facto merger" doctrines are inapplicable here. These doctrines apply where the buyer acquires the *assets* of the seller and continues the same business. In this case, National Material acquired a *limited partnership interest* in Pielet LP; it did not acquire the assets of PBS One. PBS One was merely a holding company for that interest, while the business operations were conducted by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics).

The transfer of a partnership interest, without more, does not constitute a de facto merger or mere continuation of the corporate entity that owned that interest. *Cushman v. Wakefield*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 176, 760 N.E.2d 535 (1st Dist. 2001). The plaintiff’s reliance on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labels National Material a "successor" to PBS One, is misplaced. As held in *Villareal v. Dart Container Corp.*, 292 Ill. App. 3d 480, 686 N.E.2d 694 (1st Dist. 1997), "the characterization of a transaction in a subsequent document cannot create liability where none existed." The amendment merely acknowledges the transfer of the partnership interest within the partnership structure; it does not impose liability for the separate corporate obligations of PBS One.

**III. Count XI: Survival Statute and Post-Dissolution Liability**

The trial court correctly held that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), preserved the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One despite the breach occurring after dissolution. The prosecution’s rebuttal persuasively reinforces this conclusion.

The defense argues that the Survival Statute is limited to "winding up affairs" and that PBS One had no unfinished business at the time of dissolution because it was a holding company with no employees or operations. The defense relies on *Kraft v. AAA Enterprises*, 56 Ill. App. 3d 277, 371 N.E.2d 270 (1st Dist. 1977), for the proposition that the statute is not intended to perpetuate corporate existence indefinitely.

However, the prosecution correctly identifies the controlling precedent in *First National Bank of St. Charles v. Gehrke*, 142 Ill. App. 3d 27, 491 N.E.2d 813 (1st Dist. 1986). As the prosecution notes, *Gehrke* explicitly held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its liability for its pre-dissolution contracts; rather, the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of enforcing and defending such contracts." The court in *Gehrke* further emphasized that "the survival of a cause of action depends on the existence of the underlying obligation at the time of dissolution, not the timing of the breach."

Here, the undisputed facts establish that PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 via the Assignment and Assumption Agreement. This obligation to make payments until the death of Arthur or his wife was a pre-existing liability at the time of PBS One's dissolution in 1994. The fact that the breach (cessation of payments) occurred in 1998 does not extinguish the liability. The defense's attempt to distinguish *Gehrke* by suggesting the breach in that case occurred pre-dissolution is unpersuasive in light of the broad language cited by the prosecution regarding the "timing of the breach." Furthermore, *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Racing Board*, 154 Ill. 2d 176, 608 N.E.2d 1159 (Ill. 1993), supports the principle that the statute preserves claims that "relate to" pre-existing contractual liabilities. The obligation to pay the consulting fee was a continuing liability that constituted "unfinished business" at the time of dissolution. The defense's argument that PBS One had "no creditors" is contradicted by the existence of the Consulting Agreement, which remained a valid obligation at the time of dissolution.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court's judgment is affirmed as to Count XI and reversed as to Counts IX and X. Judgment is entered in favor of the defense on Counts IX and X.
