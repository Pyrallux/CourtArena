COURT ARENA LOG - Case 87
FACTS:
The Township of Wilmington (Township) appeals from the April 13, 2018 order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County (trial court) granting the land use appeal of Wimer Realty, LLC, Wimer Holdings Corporation, and Richard G. Wimer Jr. and Jody Wimer, husband and wife (collectively, the Wimers), and denying the Township's motions to strike and dismiss.

Facts and Procedural History

The Wimers are engaged in the business of catering and operating special events and, more recently, have managed a barn wedding venue. On June 22, 2017, the Wimers purchased real property located at 3538 State Route 956 in Wilmington Township, Lawrence County, Pennsylvania (the Property), consisting of three parcels. The parcel at issue consists of 31.42 acres, approximately 25 of which are used for farming, and has a barn situated upon it. The Wimers intended to invest approximately $ 2,200,000.00 into the Property in order to renovate and improve the barn so that they can host events in it, including weddings and receptions. (Trial court op. at 2.)

Wilmington Township's Zoning Ordinance was originally silent as to the use of a barn as a wedding venue in the Township. In September 2016, the Township's Planning Commission drafted a proposed amendment to the Zoning Ordinance to address wedding barns, which was considered by the Township's Board of Supervisors (Board) at a November 7, 2016 meeting. Shortly thereafter, the first wedding barn venue, "Five Fillies," was established in the Township and began operations pursuant to a special exception granted by the Zoning Hearing Board. Id. at 2, 14.

In January 2017, the Township sent three unnumbered draft ordinances, which became known as "Ordinance No. 1 of 2017," addressing wedding barn venues located in the Township, to the Lawrence County Planning Commission, as well as the Township Planning Commission, for review and comment. A public hearing on Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was advertised on February 14, 2017, and February 21, 2017, and the hearing was held on March 2, 2017. At the conclusion of the hearing, Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was voted down by the Board. Id. at 2-3.

On June 27, 2017, the Township forwarded draft Ordinance No. 2 of 2017, which pertained to wedding barns by conditional use in agricultural districts, and Ordinance No. 3 of 2017, which pertained to the same in residential zoning districts, to the Lawrence County and Township Planning Commissions for review and comment. Neither of the draft ordinances were advertised for adoption as of June 27, 2017. Id. at 3.

The following month, on July 20, 2017, the Wimers filed a request for hearing on their challenge to the Township's Zoning Ordinance, as amended, as well as a proposed curative amendment, pursuant to section 916.1(a)(2) of the Pennsylvania Municipalities Planning Code (MPC), 53 P.S. § 10916.1(a)(2).1 On September 7, 2017, a *631hearing was held before the Board, at which the Wimers presented the testimony of Stephen Victor, a landscape architect and expert in zoning and land use planning, who assisted the Wimers in preparation of the plans and explanatory materials they submitted to the Township. Mr. Victor stated that Ordinance No. 105 of 1997, which was in effect at the time of the hearing, would not permit the use of a special event venue proposed by the Wimers but noted that the curative amendment submitted by the Wimers would cure the deficiency in the Zoning Ordinance. (Trial court op. at 6; Reproduced Record (R.R.) at 84a, 93a-125a.)

The Township presented the testimony of John Trant, an attorney and land use planning consultant certified by the American Institute of Certified Planners. Mr. Trant impliedly agreed that the use proposed by the Wimers was not provided for in the Zoning Ordinance, but opined that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was deficient in that it was, among other things, too specific so as to be exclusionary and difficult to enforce throughout the Township. However, Mr. Trant did not testify that the proposed curative amendment would pose a threat to the health, safety, or wellbeing of the Township's residents. Several Township residents also testified and presented their opinions on the proposed curative amendment. (Trial court op. at 6-7; R.R. at 147a-53a.)

At the conclusion of the hearing, the Board voted to deny the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and not to accept the proposed curative amendment. The Board additionally voted to refer draft Ordinance No. 3 of 2017, incorporating the comments of Mr. Trant, to the Township and Lawrence County Planning Commissions for review and comment. (Trial court op. at 7.)

On September 25, 2017, the Wimers filed a land use appeal in the trial court, seeking review of the Board's decision. The Wimers subsequently filed a "Renewed, Supplemental and Restated Land Use Appeal Notice" (Renewed Land Use Appeal), which "recit[ed] the history of the case subsequent to [the date of the Board's decision] and set[ ] forth essentially a restatement of the original Land Use Appeal Notice." Id. at 7. The Township responded by filing four motions: (1) a motion to open the record and present additional evidence; (2) a motion to strike the Wimers' Renewed Land Use Appeal; (3) a motion to dismiss the land use appeal as moot; and (4) a motion to dismiss the land use appeal based upon the pending ordinance doctrine. Id. at 7-8.

The Township's motion to dismiss the land use appeal was premised upon the argument that any alleged deficiencies in the current Zoning Ordinance would be moot after the Township adopted Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, or because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary. Ordinance No. 3 of 2017, following revisions, became titled Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permitted a "Special Event Barn" as an accessory use in an A-Agricultural Zoning District and as a principal or accessory use in a C-Commercial Zoning District. Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 was adopted by the Board on November 9, 2017, following an advertised public hearing. Id. at 8.

The court granted, in part, the Township's motion to open the record, permitting *632it to enter into the record a certified copy of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, on the basis that it was not available for introduction at the hearing before the Board and because it might have been relevant to the Township's argument that it cured any alleged defect in the Zoning Ordinance. However, the court denied the motion in all other respects, reasoning that "the Township had the opportunity to be fully heard on the underlying matter at the hearing on September 7, 2017, the Township's expert, John Trant, testified on September 7, 2017[,] and presented his expert opinion[,] and there was no mention in the record that the Township intended to present additional testimony or that certain evidence proffered by the Township was excluded." Id. at 8.

After hearing legal argument, the trial court issued an opinion and order finding in favor of the Wimers. Initially, the trial court ruled that the Wimers had perfected their land use appeal. The court noted that, while the Board voted to deny the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance, it did so orally and it did not issue specific findings of fact or conclusions of law. Additionally, the Board voted to amend Ordinance No. 3 of 2017 to include the recommendations of Mr. Trant and to send it for review and comment. Thus, the trial court found that the Board's decision after the hearing was

not entirely dispositive towards the Wimers' concerns in that the Board denied the Wimers' challenge and proposed curative amendment, and did not 'approve' a draft ordinance since revisions needed to be made to Ordinance No. 3 of 2017 and it needed to be sent to [the] Township Planning Commission and [the] Lawrence County Planning Commission for recommendation and comment.
Id. at 10.

The trial court next addressed the Township's motion to strike, which asserted that the Wimers' Renewed Land Use Appeal should have been stricken because it attempted to raise an issue that the Wimers waived by failing to raise it in their original land use appeal. Id. Specifically, the Township asserted that the Wimers were raising an issue as to whether the Board actually made a decision or whether their land use appeal was deemed denied. The Township contended that the Board did indeed deny the Wimers' land use appeal/curative amendment at the end of the hearing and, thus, the decision was not a deemed denial.2 (Original Record (O.R.) at Item No. 8.)

The court disagreed that the Renewed Land Use Appeal should have been stricken from the record. It noted that section 1002-A(a) of the MPC, added by Act of December 21, 1988, P.L. 1329, requires an appeal from a land use decision to be filed 30 days after the "entry of the decision," 53 P.S. § 11002-A(a), and that section 916.1(c)(7) of the MPC provides that, if the governing body fails to act on the landowner's request within 45 days after the last hearing, "a denial of the request is deemed to have occurred on the 46th day after the close of the last hearing." 53 P.S. § 10916.1(c)(7). Thus, the court explained that the Wimers filed the original land use *633appeal within 30 days of the September 7, 2017 hearing but, out of an abundance of caution, also filed the Renewed Land Use Appeal 30 days after the hearing in the event the Board's decision was a deemed denial. The court observed that the Renewed Land Use Appeal "substantially contain[ed] the same factual matters and arguments as the [original] [l]and [u]se [a]ppeal, and only recited matters subsequent to the hearing of September 7, 2017." (Trial court op. at 12.) Additionally, the court emphasized that, during argument before it, counsel for the Township conceded that the Wimers' land use appeal was timely filed. In denying the motion to strike the court stated,

[A]ny defect or error made by the Wimers in filing [ ] does not affect substantial rights of the parties, but rather only confirms the ability of the Wimers to indeed take an appeal to this [c]ourt of the Board's actions given the manner by which the hearing before the Board was concluded.
Id.3

Next, the court addressed whether the Wimers' appeal was moot given the Township's adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017. In concluding that the appeal was not moot, the court observed that there were certain "substantial [i]nconsistencies" between Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 and the Wimers' proposed curative amendment. Specifically, Ordinance No. 5 limited the number of events to 12 per year and gave the Board authority to reduce below 300 the maximum number of guests, whereas the curative amendment did not include these restrictions. On this point, the court cited Mr. Wimer's testimony that, in order for the wedding barn to be financially feasible, the Wimers would need to be able to hold more than 12 events per year with up to 300 guests per event. Thus, the court concluded that because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 substantially impacted the Wimers' ability to conduct their proposed use in a manner which was financially and economically reasonable under the circumstances, the Wimers' land use appeal was not moot by virtue of the passage of Ordinance No 5. 2017. Id. at 13.

The court then addressed whether the Wimers' land use appeal was moot under the pending ordinance doctrine or because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary. The court again determined it was not. It observed that, in order for the pending ordinance doctrine to apply, the municipality must publically declare its intent to consider a particular zoning scheme by advertising to the public its intention to hold a public hearing on such rezoning. See Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township , 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974). In this case, the trial court noted that the Wimers filed their request to challenge the Zoning Ordinance and for adoption of a curative amendment on July 20, 2017, at which point the Board had already declined to adopt Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 but had not advertised its intent to adopt Ordinance No. 3 of 2017. Further, the court emphasized that the Board "gave no *634approval to advertise the notice of intent to adopt Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 until after the Board had denied the Wimers' challenge and proposed curative amendment on September 7, 2017." (Trial court op. at 15.) Accordingly, the court found that the pending ordinance doctrine did not apply.

The court next addressed the Township's argument that the Wimers' zoning challenge was properly denied because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary in that another wedding barn, the Five Fillies, had been previously authorized by the Zoning Hearing Board and that, instead of filing a land use appeal, the Wimers should have likewise sought a special exception from the Zoning Hearing Board. The court noted that the Zoning Hearing Board's decision permitting the Five Fillies wedding barn appeared to be an "aberration," as there was, at that time, no provision in the Zoning Ordinance allowing use as a special event barn. Consequently, the court found that the Wimers were not required to submit to a hearing before the Zoning Hearing Board to seek a special exception for a wedding barn use when such a use was not permitted and, thus, the Wimers were not barred from pursuing their land use appeal merely because the Five Fillies farm had obtained special permission to operate a wedding barn. Accordingly, the court denied the Township's motion to dismiss the land use appeal on the basis of the pending ordinance doctrine and/or because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary. Id. at 16.

The trial court then considered whether the Township established that the exclusion of the proposed zoning use was related to the protection of the public health, safety, or welfare of the residents of the Township, or whether the proposed use was injurious to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare of its residents. The trial court again answered this in the negative, reasoning that although Mr. Trant offered various technical objections to the content of the curative amendment, he did not opine that it would be in any way injurious to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare of the Township's residents. Thus, the court determined that the Township did not present sufficient evidence to hold that the Wimers' proposed use of the land would pose a threat of injury to the health, safety, morals, or general welfare of the public. Id. at 16-17.

Finally, the trial court addressed whether the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and proposed curative amendment should have been granted by the Board, answering it in the affirmative. The court analyzed Casey , 328 A.2d at 464, wherein our Supreme Court held that a successful challenger of a zoning ordinance must be granted the relief requested unless the governmental unit proves that the proposed use will be injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. The trial court also considered our precedent in In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc. , 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274 (1993), wherein we held that, in the case of a de jure exclusion of a use, the sole remedy for a successful challenger is to allow the use somewhere, and the successful challenger must receive that benefit in the form of at least partial approval of his proposal. In re Miller & Son , 636 A.2d at 277.

With regard to the present case, the trial court made several conclusions. First, the court stated that "a use as a wedding barn in Wilmington Township, where the number of wedding-type events is unlimited and the number of guests at the facility is limited to 300 is entirely reasonable." (Trial court op. at 18.) Next, the trial court held that the Wimers "indeed satisfied their burden of proving that Wilmington *635Township Ordinance No. 105 of 1997, as amended, as it existed on September 7, 2017, failed to adequately provide for use as a special event venue." Id. Finally, the trial court found that the Board did not "sufficiently justify" its decision to decline the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and proposed curative amendment because the Township "failed to demonstrate that the Wimers' use of their property as a special event venue would have an [e]ffect on the public health, safety and welfare in a meaningful way." Id. Thus, the court concluded that the Wimers' request for a challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and proposed curative amendment should have been granted by the Board.

Accordingly, the court determined that the Board's decision was an abuse of discretion, granted the Wimers' land use appeal, and permitted them to use the Property in accordance with the terms of their proposed curative amendment, but required them to comply with all other applicable Township ordinances not addressed with the curative amendment.4

The Township filed the present appeal,5 arguing (1) the Wimers' land use appeal was moot based upon Ordinance No. 5 of 2017; (2) the Wimers' land use appeal should have been dismissed under the pending ordinance doctrine and/or because the Township's Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary; (3) the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence; and (4) the trial court abused its discretion in refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record.

## IN THE COMMONWEALTH COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

## DEFENSE OPENING BRIEF ON BEHALF OF APPELLANT TOWNSHIP OF WILMINGTON

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

The Township of Wilmington respectfully submits this opening brief in defense of its appeal from the April 13, 2018 order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County. The plaintiff/Appellee's opening argument mischaracterizes both the legal landscape and the factual record. As demonstrated below, the trial court committed multiple errors of law that warrant reversal: (1) the land use appeal is moot because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 cured the alleged exclusionary deficiency; (2) the pending ordinance doctrine should have barred the Wimers' appeal; (3) the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary as a matter of law; (4) the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence; and (5) the trial court abused its discretion in refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record.

---

### II. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Land Use Appeal Is Moot Because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 Cures the Alleged Exclusionary Deficiency

The plaintiff contends that Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 "did not resolve the Wimers' core grievance" and "exacerbated it." This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the fundamental premise of the Wimers' challenge was that the Zoning Ordinance failed to provide for wedding barn venues at all. Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 directly addresses this alleged deficiency by expressly permitting "Special Event Barn" as an accessory use in A-Agricultural Zoning Districts and as a principal or accessory use in C-Commerical Zoning Districts. This curative action renders the Wimers' challenge moot. See *Hull v. Board of Supervisors of Lower Makefield Township*, 708 A.2d 112 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1998) (holding that adoption of ordinance addressing the precise exclusion alleged renders land use appeal moot).

Second, the plaintiff's characterization of the ordinance's restrictions as "fatal" or "arbitrary" misapprehends the nature of zoning authority. Municipalities possess broad police power to regulate land uses in the interest of public health, safety, and welfare. See *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926); *Gartland v. Township of West Donegal*, 456 A.2d 229 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1983). The 12-event cap and guest limitations represent legitimate zoning regulations, not evidence of continued exclusion.

Third, the plaintiff's reliance on the Wimers' claimed "financial infeasibility" is misplaced. Economic hardship, standing alone, does not establish an exclusionary zoning violation. As this Court held in *Columbus Park Corp. v. Board of Commissioners of Borough of West Chester*, 363 A.2d 520 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1976), "the fact that a permitted use may not be economically profitable for a particular landowner does not render a zoning ordinance exclusionary."

The trial court erred in finding the appeal non-moot based on "substantial inconsistencies" between the Wimers' proposed curative amendment and Ordinance No. 5. The proper inquiry is whether the ordinance addresses the exclusion—not whether it adopts the challenger's preferred terms. Here, Ordinance No. 5 provides for wedding barn uses where none existed before. This is sufficient to render the appeal moot.

#### B. The Pending Ordinance Doctrine Should Have Barred the Wimers' Appeal

The trial court incorrectly concluded that the pending ordinance doctrine did not apply. This Court held in *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 328 A.2d 464 (Pa. 1974), that a land use challenge is premature when the municipality has publicly declared its intent to consider a rezoning scheme and is actively pursuing that consideration.

The record establishes that the Township was actively considering wedding barn regulations throughout 2017. The Planning Commission drafted proposed amendments in September 2016. Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was considered in March 2017. By July 20, 2017, when the Wimers filed their challenge, the Township had already forwarded draft Ordinances No. 2 and No. 3 of 2017 to the Planning Commissions for review. The fact that these ordinances had not yet been formally advertised does not negate the Township's clear intent to address the subject matter.

The trial court erroneously focused on the absence of formal advertisement before the Wimers' filing. However, the doctrine encompasses the entire legislative process, not merely the advertisement stage. The Township's ongoing consideration of wedding barn regulations—demonstrated through multiple draft ordinances and Planning Commission reviews—triggered the pending ordinance doctrine.

By pursuing their appeal despite the Township's active legislative consideration, the Wimers circumvented established procedural protections. This Court should reverse and dismiss the appeal based on the pending ordinance doctrine.

#### C. The Zoning Ordinance Was Not Exclusionary As a Matter of Law

The plaintiff cannot establish that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary because the Zoning Hearing Board previously granted a special exception for the "Five Fillies" wedding barn venue. This fact alone demonstrates that the ordinance did not categorically exclude wedding barn uses.

The trial court characterized the Five Fillies approval as an "aberration" because "there was, at that time, no provision in the Zoning Ordinance allowing use as a special event barn." However, the absence of express authorization does not establish exclusion. The Zoning Hearing Board possessed authority to grant special exceptions for compatible uses, and it exercised that authority in the Five Fillies case.

The proper remedy for the Wimers was to seek a special exception from the Zoning Hearing Board, not to challenge the ordinance as exclusionary. The Wimers' failure to pursue this available remedy undermines their exclusionary claim. See *Hitchcock Partnership v. Board of Supervisors of West Goshen Township*, 640 A.2d 555 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1994) (landowner must exhaust administrative remedies before challenging zoning ordinance).

The trial court erred in finding that the Wimers were "not required to submit to a hearing before the Zoning Hearing Board" when such use was "not permitted." This reasoning conflates the absence of express permission with categorical exclusion—a legal error that warrants reversal.

#### D. The Board's Decision Was Supported by Substantial Evidence

The Board denied the Wimers' challenge based on substantial evidence presented at the September 7, 2017 hearing. The Township's expert, John Trant, testified that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was "too specific so as to be exclusionary and difficult to enforce throughout the Township." While Mr. Trant did not testify that the amendment would threaten public health, safety, or welfare, his testimony regarding enforceability and potential for abuse constitutes substantial evidence supporting the Board's decision.

The standard of review for zoning ordinance challenges is deferential. This Court does not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the governing body. See *Valley View Civic Association v. Zoning Board of Adjustment*, 462 A.2d 637 (Pa. 1983). The Board was entitled to credit Mr. Trant's testimony regarding the practical problems with the Wimers' proposed amendment.

The trial court improperly substituted its judgment for the Board's by finding that the Township "failed to demonstrate" impacts on public welfare. The Board's reliance on Mr. Trant's testimony regarding enforceability—which the trial court acknowledged—constitutes substantial evidence supporting the denial.

#### E. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Refusing to Permit the Township to Supplement the Record

The trial court granted the Township's motion to open the record only to the extent of admitting Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, but denied the motion in all other respects. This was an abuse of discretion.

The Township sought to present additional evidence regarding the passage and terms of Ordinance No. 5, which was relevant to the mootness inquiry and the pending ordinance doctrine. The trial court acknowledged that the ordinance "was not available for introduction at the hearing before the Board," yet refused to allow supplementation.

Pennsylvania law favors resolution of land use disputes on the merits. The trial court's refusal to permit supplementation effectively prevented the Township from presenting evidence relevant to key legal issues. This Court should reverse and remand to permit full development of the record regarding Ordinance No. 5 and the Township's legislative process.

For the foregoing reasons, the Township of Wilmington respectfully requests that this Court reverse the April 13, 2018 order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County and dismiss the Wimers' land use appeal as moot, or alternatively, remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**Case Overview**
This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County granting the land use appeal of the Wimers and denying the Township of Wilmington's motions to strike and dismiss. The Township challenges the trial court's determination that the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance was not moot following the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, and further argues that the pending ordinance doctrine should have barred the appeal.

**Analysis**

**I. Mootness and the Effect of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017**
The central issue is whether the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 rendered the Wimers' land use appeal moot. The Township argues that because Ordinance No. 5 expressly permits "Special Event Barn" uses, the exclusionary defect identified by the Wimers has been cured. The Wimers counter that the specific restrictions imposed by Ordinance No. 5—specifically the 12-event cap and the potential for reduced guest limits—render the use financially infeasible and therefore do not constitute a valid cure.

The trial court agreed with the Wimers, finding that the "substantial inconsistencies" between the proposed curative amendment and Ordinance No. 5 kept the controversy alive. However, this reasoning conflates the economic viability of a specific business model with the legal concept of exclusionary zoning.

Under established precedent, a zoning ordinance is exclusionary if it fails to provide for a legitimate use somewhere within the municipality. Once a municipality amends its ordinance to provide for that use, the challenge to the exclusion is generally moot. See *Hull v. Board of Supervisors of Lower Makefield Township*, 708 A.2d 112 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1998). In *Hull*, the court recognized that the adoption of an ordinance addressing the precise exclusion alleged renders the land use appeal moot because the municipality has remedied the legal defect.

Furthermore, the Wimers' reliance on financial infeasibility to establish that the exclusion remains is misplaced. It is well settled that economic hardship, standing alone, does not render a zoning ordinance exclusionary. As stated in *Columbus Park Corp. v. Board of Commissioners of Borough of West Chester*, 363 A.2d 520 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1976), "the fact that a permitted use may not be economically profitable for a particular landowner does not render a zoning ordinance exclusionary." The Wimers' inability to operate a profitable wedding venue under the 12-event restriction is a matter of economic regulation, not a legal exclusion of the use itself. The Township possesses the police power to regulate land use, including imposing numerical limitations on events, provided such regulations serve the public welfare. See *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926).

Because Ordinance No. 5 provides a mechanism for "Special Event Barn" uses in Agricultural and Commercial districts, the Wimers' claim that the ordinance totally excludes their use is no longer justiciable. The trial court erred by allowing the Wimers' economic preferences to dictate the legal sufficiency of the Township's cure. The Township's argument on this issue is persuasive, and the appeal should be dismissed as moot.

**II. The Pending Ordinance Doctrine**
The Township also argues that the pending ordinance doctrine should have barred the Wimers' challenge because the Township was actively considering wedding barn regulations at the time the challenge was filed. This argument is less persuasive.

The pending ordinance doctrine prevents a court from interfering with the legislative process when a municipality has publicly declared its intent to consider a rezoning. See *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 328 A.2d 464 (Pa. 1974). For the doctrine to apply, there must be a public declaration of intent to hold a public hearing on the specific zoning scheme. The record indicates that while the Township was reviewing drafts internally and through planning commissions in June and July 2017, it had not approved the advertisement of a public hearing for Ordinance No. 3 or No. 5 until after the Wimers filed their challenge on July 20, 2017. Without the requisite public advertisement and declaration of intent prior to the filing of the challenge, the pending ordinance doctrine does not apply. The trial court correctly rejected this argument.

**III. Substantial Evidence and Public Welfare**
Finally, the Township argues that the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence. In a curative amendment case, once a challenger proves the ordinance is exclusionary, the burden shifts to the municipality to prove that the proposed use would be injurious to public health, safety, or welfare. See *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.*, 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274 (1993). The Township's expert, Mr. Trant, testified regarding the "enforceability" of the Wimers' proposal but did not offer specific testimony that the use would threaten public health or safety. While enforceability is a valid concern for zoning administration, it does not necessarily equate to the "injurious effect" required to deny a curative amendment. However, because the Court finds the appeal to be moot based on the adoption of Ordinance No. 5, it is unnecessary to reach a definitive conclusion on the sufficiency of the evidence regarding the Board's September 7, 2017 decision.

**Conclusion**
For the foregoing reasons, the Township's argument regarding the mootness of the appeal has merit. The adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permits "Special Event Barn" uses, cured the exclusionary defect alleged by the Wimers. The fact that the ordinance imposes restrictions that affect the financial feasibility of the Wimers' specific business plan does not revive the exclusionary claim under *Columbus Park Corp.* The trial court erred in denying the Township's motion to dismiss on this basis. The remaining arguments raised by the Township are either unpersuasive or rendered moot by this determination.

**Order**
The preliminary ruling of the Court is that the Township's appeal is granted in part. The Order of the Court of Common Pleas is reversed to the extent it found the land use appeal was not moot, and the matter is remanded with instructions to dismiss the Wimers' land use appeal as moot.

The Township of Wilmington respectfully submits this rebuttal to the arguments raised by the Wimers in response to the Township's opening brief and the Court's preliminary ruling dated [from Round 1].

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Wimers' rebuttal, to the extent it has been provided, appears to rehash arguments already considered and rejected by this Court in its thorough preliminary ruling. The Wimers continue to conflate economic viability with legal exclusion—a distinction that is well-established in Pennsylvania zoning jurisprudence. The Township adopts and incorporates by reference all arguments set forth in its opening brief and further responds as follows.

---

## II. THE ADOPTION OF ORDINANCE NO. 5 OF 2017 RENDERS THE WIMERS' CHALLENGE MOOT

### A. The Wimers Cannot Relegate Economic Preferences to Override Legislative Action

The Wimers' central contention—that Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 does not cure the alleged exclusion because the 12-event cap renders their business model financially infeasible—fundamentally misapprehends the nature of exclusionary zoning challenges. As this Court correctly recognized in its preliminary ruling, the case of *Columbus Park Corp. v. Board of Commissioners of Borough of West Chester*, 363 A.2d 520 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1976), establishes that "the fact that a permitted use may not be economically profitable for a particular landowner does not render a zoning ordinance exclusionary."

The Wimers conflate two distinct legal concepts: (1) whether a use is permitted under the zoning ordinance, and (2) whether a particular landowner can profit from that use. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court has consistently held that the first question—permissibility—is the sole determinant of whether an ordinance is exclusionary. Economic hardship, while perhaps relevant to variance applications or other equitable relief, does not transform a permitted use into an excluded one.

In *Hull v. Board of Supervisors of Lower Makefield Township*, 708 A.2d 112 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1998), this Court held that when a municipality adopts an ordinance addressing the precise exclusion alleged, the land use appeal becomes moot because the municipality has remedied the legal defect. The Hull Court specifically rejected the argument that a challenger could demand a particular form of relief—rather than simply the recognition that the use must be permitted somewhere in the municipality.

### B. The Wimers' Reliance on In re Miller & Son Is Misplaced

The Wimers may argue that *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.*, 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274 (1993), supports their position. However, that case is distinguishable. In *Miller & Son*, the municipality had not adopted any ordinance addressing the excluded use at the time of the challenge. The Court in *Miller & Son* held that where a use is de jure excluded—meaning the ordinance contains no provision for the use whatsoever—the challenger is entitled to relief. Here, by contrast, Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 expressly permits "Special Event Barn" uses in Agricultural and Commercial districts. The Wimers' argument thus inverts the holding of *Miller & Son*: that case stands for the proposition that total exclusion requires relief, not that partial regulation of a permitted use entitles a challenger to demand specific terms.

### C. The Township's Police Power Includes Event Limitations

The 12-event cap and the Board's authority to reduce the maximum number of guests represent legitimate exercises of the Township's police power to regulate land uses in the interest of public welfare. As the United States Supreme Court recognized in *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926), zoning regulations are entitled to substantial deference when they serve the health, safety, and welfare of the community. The Wimers have presented no evidence—nor could they—that limiting the number of special events at a rural agricultural property is arbitrary or unreasonable. Traffic, noise, and wear on local roads are legitimate concerns that the Township may address through reasonable numerical limitations.

---

## III. THE PENDING ORDINANCE DOCTRINE DOES NOT BAR THIS CHALLENGE

The Wimers may further argue that the pending ordinance doctrine should have prevented the Board from considering their challenge. This argument fails for the reasons stated in the preliminary ruling.

In *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that the pending ordinance doctrine requires a public declaration of intent to consider a rezoning, typically accomplished through advertisement of a public hearing. The record establishes that the Board had not approved any advertisement for Ordinance No. 3 or Ordinance No. 5 at the time the Wimers filed their challenge on July 20, 2017. The drafts sent to planning commissions in June 2017 were internal working documents, not public declarations of intent. The Wimers' reliance on *Casey* is therefore misplaced.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ITS SUBSTANTIVE ANALYSIS

Even if this Court were to reach the merits of the Wimers' challenge— notwithstanding the mootness of the appeal—the trial court committed reversible error in several respects.

### A. The Burden of Proof

In a curative amendment case under Section 916.1 of the Pennsylvania Municipalities Planning Code, once a challenger makes a prima facie showing that the ordinance excludes a legitimate use, the burden shifts to the municipality to prove that the proposed use would be injurious to public health, safety, or welfare. The Wimers argue that the Township failed to meet this burden.

However, the Township's expert, John Trant, testified that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was "too specific so as to be exclusionary and difficult to enforce throughout the Township." While the Wimers characterize this as merely an "enforceability" concern, the Township submits that enforceability is directly related to the public welfare. An ordinance that cannot be consistently applied or enforced creates the very potential for arbitrary and capricious administration that zoning laws are designed to prevent.

Moreover, Township residents testified and presented opinions opposing the Wimers' proposed use. While individual resident opinions do not constitute substantial evidence of public harm, they do reflect community concerns that the Board is entitled to consider in its legislative capacity.

### B. The Trial Court Exceeded Its Scope of Review

The trial court erred by substituting its judgment for that of the Board regarding the "reasonableness" of the Wimers' proposed use. The court stated that "a use as a wedding barn in Wilmington Township, where the number of wedding-type events is unlimited and the number of guests at the facility is limited to 300 is entirely reasonable." This statement represents the trial court's own policy judgment, not a finding based on the record. The Board was entitled to determine, in its legislative discretion, that unlimited events in an agricultural district would be inconsistent with the character of the zone and the welfare of neighboring residents.

Under Pennsylvania law, courts reviewing zoning decisions must accord substantial deference to legislative bodies. See *Cloverleaf Development Corp. v. Board of Supervisors of Hamilton Township*, 758 A.2d 720 (Pa. Cmwlth. 2000). The trial court failed to accord this deference.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County, Pennsylvania

### WIMER REALTY, LLC, et al. v. TOWNSHIP OF WILMINGTON

---

Your Honor, Members of the Court, and Distinguished Counsel:

The Township of Wilmington has presented its argument seeking to dismiss this matter as moot based upon the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017. Respectfully, the Defense submits that the Township's position, while creatively constructed, fails to address the fundamental legal deficiencies in its position and asks this Court to overlook the substantive rights guaranteed to the Wimers under Pennsylvania law. We urge the Court to sustain the Wimers' land use appeal and reverse the Township's attempt to escape judicial review through the adoption of a restrictive ordinance that fails to cure the exclusionary defect.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The Wimers are engaged in the business of catering and operating special events, having purchased property at 3538 State Route 956 in Wilmington Township for the specific purpose of hosting weddings and receptions in a renovated barn. They intended to invest approximately $2,200,000.00 into the Property. The record demonstrates that at the time of the Wimers' challenge, Wilmington Township's Zoning Ordinance was silent as to the use of a barn as a wedding venue. The Township's own expert, John Trant, implicitly conceded that the proposed use was not provided for in the Zoning Ordinance.

The Township argues that Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permits "Special Event Barn" uses in Agricultural and Commercial districts with certain restrictions, somehow cures the exclusionary defect. However, as the record clearly establishes, the Wimers' proposed use required the ability to hold more than twelve events per year with up to 300 guests per event to be financially feasible. Ordinance No. 5 imposes a twelve-event cap and gives the Board authority to reduce the maximum number of guests below 300—restrictions that effectively preclude the Wimers' business model.

---

## II. THE APPEAL IS NOT MOOT: ORDINANCE NO. 5 DOES NOT CURE THE EXCLUSIONARY DEFECT

### A. The Substantial Inconsistencies Between the Ordinance and the Wimers' Proposed Use

The Township contends that the mere permitting of a "Special Event Barn" use cures any exclusionary defect, regardless of the restrictions imposed. This position fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the Wimers' challenge and the relief sought. The trial court correctly identified "substantial inconsistencies" between Ordinance No. 5 and the Wimers' proposed curative amendment. Specifically, Ordinance No. 5 limits events to twelve per year and allows the Board to reduce guest limits below 300, whereas the Wimers' proposed amendment contained no such restrictions.

Mr. Wimer's unrebutted testimony established that, in order for the wedding barn to be financially feasible, the Wimers would need to hold more than twelve events per year with up to 300 guests per event. This is not a matter of mere preference or profitability—it goes to the very essence of whether the permitted use is one that the Wimers can actually utilize. When an ordinance purports to permit a use but imposes conditions that make the permitted use economically impracticable for the challenging landowner, the ordinance has not cured the exclusionary defect.

### B. The Township's Reliance on Columbus Park Corp. Is Misplaced

The Township cites *Columbus Park Corp. v. Board of Commissioners of Borough of West Chester* for the proposition that economic unprofitability does not render an ordinance exclusionary. That case is readily distinguishable. In *Columbus Park Corp.*, the court addressed a situation where the ordinance itself permitted the use without restriction, and the challenger simply found the particular location unprofitable. Here, the ordinance imposes specific numerical limitations—twelve events per year—that directly contradict the Wimers' proposed use and render it economically nonviable.

The distinction is critical: in *Columbus Park Corp.*, the ordinance permitted the use outright; here, Ordinance No. 5 permits the use only within arbitrary constraints that bear no relationship to the Wimers' proposed business operation. The Wimers do not merely complain that their particular location is unprofitable; they challenge an ordinance that affirmatively restricts their ability to conduct the permitted use in any meaningful way.

### C. Miller & Son Paving Is Directly Applicable

The Township attempts to distinguish *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.*, but that case directly supports the Wimers' position. In *Miller & Son*, the Commonwealth Court held that in cases of de jure exclusion of a use, the sole remedy for a successful challenger is to allow the use somewhere, and the successful challenger must receive that benefit in the form of at least partial approval of his proposal. The court emphasized that the challenger is entitled to relief that permits the proposed use.

The Township argues that *Miller & Son* applies only where there is "total exclusion" with "no provision whatsoever" for the use. However, this reading is too narrow. The principle underlying *Miller & Son* is that when a landowner successfully challenges an exclusionary ordinance, the landowner is entitled to meaningful relief—not merely a theoretical permission rendered valueless by restrictive conditions. Here, as in *Miller & Son*, the Township's ordinance, even as amended, fails to provide the Wimers with a meaningful opportunity to conduct their proposed use.

### D. Hull v. Board of Supervisors Does Not Support Mootness

The Township's reliance on *Hull v. Board of Supervisors of Lower Makefield Township* is equally unavailing. In *Hull*, the court held that a land use appeal became moot where the municipality adopted an ordinance "addressing the precise exclusion alleged." However, the ordinance in *Hull* actually remedied the exclusionary defect by permitting the use without the problematic restrictions that had been challenged. Here, Ordinance No. 5 does not "address the precise exclusion alleged"—it merely substitutes one form of exclusion (no provision whatsoever) for another (permissive in name only due to restrictive conditions).

The Hull court specifically rejected the notion that a challenger could demand a "specific form of relief." But the Wimers are not demanding a specific form of relief—they are demanding meaningful relief that permits their proposed use. Ordinance No. 5 fails to provide this because its restrictions are so severe as to render the permitted use meaningless for the Wimers' business model.

---

## III. THE PENDING ORDINANCE DOCTRINE DOES NOT BAR THE WIMERS' CHALLENGE

The Township argues that the pending ordinance doctrine somehow precludes the Wimers' challenge. This argument fails for the reasons correctly identified by the trial court.

In *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that the pending ordinance doctrine requires a municipality to publicly declare its intent to consider a particular zoning scheme by advertising to the public its intention to hold a public hearing on such rezoning. The record establishes that the Wimers filed their request to challenge the Zoning Ordinance on July 20, 2017. At that time, the Board had already declined to adopt Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 and had not advertised its intent to adopt Ordinance No. 3 of 2017. Furthermore, the Board gave no approval to advertise the notice of intent to adopt Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 until after the Board had denied the Wimers' challenge and proposed curative amendment on September 7, 2017.

The draft ordinances sent to planning commissions were internal working documents, not public declarations of intent. The Township cannot rely on the pending ordinance doctrine to bar a challenge when it had not yet publicly declared its intent to adopt the very ordinance it now claims moots the case.

---

## IV. THE BOARD'S DECISION WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

The Township argues that the Board's decision to deny the Wimers' challenge was supported by substantial evidence. This argument ignores the record.

The Township's own expert, John Trant, testified that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was "too specific so as to be exclusionary and difficult to enforce throughout the Township." However, Mr. Trant did not testify that the proposed curative amendment would pose a threat to the health, safety, or wellbeing of the Township's residents. The trial court correctly noted that while Mr. Trant offered various technical objections to the content of the curative amendment, he did not opine that it would be in any way injurious to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare of the Township's residents.

Under *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, a successful challenger of a zoning ordinance must be granted the relief requested unless the governmental unit proves that the proposed use will be injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. The Township failed to meet this burden.

---

## V. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS SCOPE OF REVIEW

The Township argues that the trial court exceeded its scope of review by substituting its judgment for that of the Board. This argument mischaracterizes the trial court's analysis.

The trial court did not substitute its policy judgment for that of the Board. Rather, the trial court properly analyzed whether the Board met its legal burden of demonstrating that the Wimers' proposed use would be injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. Having found that the Township failed to present sufficient evidence on this point, the trial court correctly concluded that the Wimers were entitled to relief.

The Township's reliance on *Cloverleaf Development Corp. v. Board of Supervisors of Hamilton Township* is misplaced. While courts must accord substantial deference to legislative bodies, that deference is not absolute. Where, as here, the Township failed to demonstrate that the proposed use would cause actual harm to the public welfare, the Board abused its discretion in denying the Wimers' challenge.

**FINAL RULING**

**Case Overview**
This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County granting the land use appeal of the Wimers and denying the Township of Wilmington's motions to strike and dismiss. The Township contends that the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 rendered the Wimers' challenge to the prior Zoning Ordinance moot. The Wimers argue that Ordinance No. 5 fails to cure the exclusionary defect because its restrictions render their proposed use economically infeasible. After careful consideration of the closing arguments and the applicable law, the Court affirms its determination that the land use appeal is moot.

**Analysis**

**I. Mootness and the Adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017**
The central issue is whether the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permits "Special Event Barn" uses in Agricultural and Commercial districts, moots the Wimers' challenge to the prior ordinance. The Township argues, and the Court agrees, that once a municipality enacts legislation permitting the previously excluded use, the legal controversy over the exclusion ceases to exist.

The Wimers contend that the appeal is not moot because Ordinance No. 5 imposes a 12-event cap and guest limits that make their specific business model financially unfeasible. They argue that this constitutes a failure to "address the precise exclusion" and denies them "meaningful relief" under *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.*, 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274 (1993).

The Court finds this argument unpersuasive. The Wimers' challenge was initiated against Ordinance No. 105 of 1997, which was entirely silent regarding wedding barns. This constituted a *de jure* exclusion. The adoption of Ordinance No. 5 eliminated that silence by expressly permitting "Special Event Barn" uses. As held in *Hull v. Board of Supervisors of Lower Makefield Township*, 708 A.2d 112 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1998), when a municipality adopts an ordinance addressing the precise exclusion alleged, the land use appeal becomes moot because the municipality has remedied the legal defect.

The Wimers attempt to distinguish *Columbus Park Corp. v. Board of Commissioners of Borough of West Chester*, 363 A.2d 520 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1976), by arguing that the restrictions in Ordinance No. 5 are inherent to the ordinance itself, unlike the locational issues in *Columbus Park*. This distinction is irrelevant to the question of mootness. The principle of *Columbus Park* remains controlling: an ordinance is not exclusionary simply because a particular landowner cannot operate profitably under it. The fact that Ordinance No. 5 regulates the intensity of the use through a 12-event cap is an exercise of the Township's police power, not a continuation of the *de jure* exclusion.

The Wimers' reliance on *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.* is misplaced in the context of mootness. *Miller & Son* establishes that where an ordinance is exclusionary, a successful challenger is entitled to relief allowing the use. However, that remedy is triggered by the existence of an exclusion. Here, the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 removed the exclusion. The Wimers cannot utilize a curative amendment challenge to the *old* ordinance to invalidate the specific regulations of the *new* ordinance. If the Wimers believe the 12-event cap is unreasonable, their remedy is to challenge Ordinance No. 5 directly, not to assert that the *old* ordinance remains exclusionary.

**II. The Trial Court's Scope of Review**
The trial court abused its discretion by substituting its judgment for that of the Board regarding the reasonableness of the Wimers' proposed unlimited use. The trial court found that "a use as a wedding barn in Wilmington Township, where the number of wedding-type events is unlimited... is entirely reasonable." This finding improperly intruded upon the legislative function.

Under *Cloverleaf Development Corp. v. Board of Supervisors of Hamilton Township*, 758 A.2d 720 (Pa. Cmwlth. 2000), courts must accord substantial deference to legislative bodies in matters of zoning. The Board was entitled to determine that unlimited events in an agricultural district could adversely impact traffic, noise, and the rural character of the community. The trial court's determination that the Wimers' specific business plan was "reasonable" does not negate the Township's authority to impose reasonable numerical limitations to protect the public welfare, as recognized in *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926).

**III. The Pending Ordinance Doctrine**
The Court agrees with the Wimers that the pending ordinance doctrine does not bar their challenge, as the Township had not advertised a public hearing on the ordinance at the time the challenge was filed. *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974). However, this procedural victory does not overcome the substantive fact that the subsequent adoption of Ordinance No. 5 rendered the appeal moot.

**Conclusion**
The Township's adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 cured the *de jure* exclusion of wedding barns. The Wimers' dissatisfaction with the specific regulations imposed by the new ordinance—specifically the 12-event cap—relates to the economic viability of their business model, not the legality of the use itself. Under *Columbus Park Corp.* and *Hull*, the land use appeal is therefore moot. The trial court erred in granting the Wimers' proposed curative amendment, which effectively invalidated the Township's newly enacted regulations, and in substituting its own judgment for the legislative discretion of the Board.

**Order**
The Order of the Court of Common Pleas is reversed. The Wimers' land use appeal is dismissed as moot.
