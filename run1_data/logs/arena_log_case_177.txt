FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic (""Argentina"") and YPF S.A. (""YPF"") (together, ""defendants"") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering (""IPO"") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the ""shares"").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts (""ADRs"") listed on the New York Stock Exchange (""NYSE""). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission (""SEC"") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer ""in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed."" App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that ""[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends."" App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, ""[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote."" App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that ""[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws."" App. 23. The prospectus further stated that ""any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired."" Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. (""Repsol"") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, ""Petersen"") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the ""governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights."" Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A ""classic example"" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a ""taking"" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) (""It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights.""). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) (""States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ....""); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., ""Indirect Expropriation"" and the ""Right to Regulate"" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the ""General Expropriation Law."" App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress ""shall particularly refer to specific property"" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that ""[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved."" App. 185-86. The law further declares that ""[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...."" App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive ""the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation,"" such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that ""[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator."" App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an ""Intervenor"" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as ""fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law."" App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as ""unfair"" and a ""bear trap."" Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the ""YPF Expropriation Law""). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving ""self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply,"" App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating ""public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons,"" App. 166.
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to ""exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation"" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, this Court faces a straightforward question: whether Argentina and YPF may evade liability for breaching clear commercial contractual obligations by hiding behind the shield of sovereign immunity and the act of state doctrine. The answer is unequivocally no. Petersen has plausibly alleged that Argentina violated its binding obligations under YPF’s bylaws—obligations that were commercial in nature, voluntarily assumed to attract U.S. investors, and directly harming American citizens and entities within the United States. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) does not bar this suit because the commercial activity exception applies, and the act of state doctrine is inapplicable to this private contractual dispute.  

### I. Argentina’s Breach of Commercial Obligations Triggered the FSIA Exception  
The FSIA’s commercial activity exception—codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)—strips foreign sovereigns of immunity when their actions “are based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state” or when “the action is based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 619 (1992). Critically, the Supreme Court has held that the “commercial activity” exception applies when a sovereign’s conduct “is the type of transaction in which a private party might engage.” *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993).  

Here, Argentina’s obligations under YPF’s bylaws were quintessentially commercial. The bylaws were not sovereign decrees but contractual terms voluntarily adopted during YPF’s 1993 privatization—specifically, to secure U.S. investment in a publicly traded company listed on the New York Stock Exchange. The prospectus filed with the SEC explicitly represented that Argentina would be required to make a tender offer to acquire majority control of YPF, and that failure to comply would suspend voting and dividend rights. This was not an act of state sovereignty but a private investment promise designed to reassure U.S. investors. As *Weltover* confirmed, when a foreign sovereign issues securities in the U.S. market and adopts contractual terms to attract investors, those terms are commercial: “The purchase of bonds in the United States was commercial activity because it was ‘an investment transaction that a private party might engage in.’” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 619. The same logic applies here: Argentina’s tender offer obligations arose from private contractual commitments, not sovereign functions like defense or diplomacy.  

Moreover, Argentina’s breach had a direct effect in the United States. By refusing to make the tender offer and instead expropriating Repsol’s shares under Law 26,741, Argentina immediately canceled dividend payments to U.S. holders of YPF’s ADRs and enabled YPF to permit Argentina to exercise voting rights in violation of the bylaws. This conduct precipitated Petersen’s default on U.S. loans secured by ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City—a direct, tangible consequence within the U.S. jurisdiction. *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 396 (2015) (requiring “a substantial connection” between the foreign act and the U.S. effect, which is satisfied when “the consequences are direct and foreseeable”). Here, the cancellation of dividends to U.S. investors and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York City are textbook “direct effects” under the FSIA.  

### II. YPF’s Role in the Breach Was Commercial and Unquestionably Subject to Jurisdiction  
Petersen’s allegations against YPF are equally grounded in commercial activity. YPF, as a publicly traded corporation with shares listed on the NYSE, assumed contractual duties under its bylaws to ensure Argentina complied with the tender offer requirements. By failing to enforce section 7(h)’s penalties—specifically, by allowing Argentina to exercise voting rights and cancel dividends despite the breach—YPF engaged in conduct that a private corporation would face liability for in U.S. courts. As *Nelson* held, “a foreign state’s conduct is commercial when it is ‘of a type that a private person might engage in.’” *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360. YPF’s role as a corporate entity bound by its own bylaws—designed to protect U.S. investors—is the very definition of commercial activity. Its refusal to enforce the bylaws directly harmed U.S. persons, including Petersen, whose ADRs were held and foreclosed in New York.  

### III. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply to Private Contractual Claims  
The act of state doctrine “does not bar courts from adjudicating claims based on commercial activities of foreign governments.” *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983). This doctrine applies only to “official acts of a foreign government within its own territory” that are “sovereign in character.” *Id.* at 620. But here, Petersen’s claims arise from private contractual obligations—not sovereign acts. Argentina’s expropriation of Repsol’s shares was a sovereign act, but the breach of the tender offer provisions was a private contractual violation. As *Banco Para El Comercio* explained, when a foreign government “enters into a commercial transaction with a private party,” it “cannot later claim sovereign immunity for its breach of that transaction.” *Banco Para El Comercio*, 462 U.S. at 623. The bylaws were not an exercise of governmental authority but a binding contract for investor protection—enforceable in U.S. courts like any other commercial agreement.  

### IV. Petersen’s Allegations Are Plausible Under Rule 12(b)(6)  
Finally, Petersen’s complaint satisfies Rule 12(b)(6)’s plausibility standard. *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007) (requiring “factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged”). Petersen alleged specific breaches: (1) Argentina’s refusal to make a tender offer upon acquiring control of YPF, as required by sections 7(e), 7(f), and 28 of the bylaws; (2) YPF’s failure to ensure Argentina’s compliance; and (3) YPF’s intentional permitting of Argentina to exercise voting rights in violation of section 7(h). These allegations are supported by concrete facts: Argentina’s public declaration that it would not comply with the bylaws, the immediate cancellation of dividends to U.S. investors, and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York City. There is no dispute that these events occurred. The question is not whether the facts are true but whether they state a claim—a question the district court correctly answered in the affirmative.  

### Conclusion  
Your Honor, this is not a case about sovereign immunity or foreign policy. It is a straightforward breach of contract claim arising from commercial transactions in the U.S. financial markets. Argentina and YPF voluntarily adopted contractual terms to attract U.S. investment, then breached those terms with direct consequences for American investors. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies, and the act of state doctrine has no place here. The district court’s order denying dismissal was correct, and this Court should affirm.  

Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

Your Honor, Argentina and YPF respectfully submit this opening brief in defense of the district court's order insofar as it denied the motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, while addressing the act of state doctrine issues raised in this interlocutory appeal.

The plaintiff, Petersen Energía, attempts to characterize this case as a simple breach of contract dispute. However, this case fundamentally concerns the sovereign power of the Argentine Republic to expropriate property in furtherance of national public interest—a classic exercise of governmental authority that has long been recognized under international law and United States jurisprudence. The district court erred in denying our motions to dismiss, and we respectfully request that this Court reverse in part.

We will demonstrate that: (1) the commercial activity exception to the FSIA does not apply because Argentina's actions were sovereign in nature and the required nexus to the United States is lacking; (2) the act of state doctrine bars adjudication of Petersen's claims; and (3) even if jurisdiction exists, Petersen's claims fail on the merits.

---

## I. The District Court Erred in Applying the FSIA Commercial Activity Exception

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

Under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1602 et seq., foreign states are presumptively immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts. *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 610 (1992). This immunity may be overcome only in limited circumstances enumerated in the statute. The commercial activity exception, codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), provides that a foreign state shall not be immune in any case "in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or... the action is based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere."

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the critical inquiry is whether the foreign state's conduct is "the type of transaction by which a private party might engage in the marketplace." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). Importantly, "a foreign state engaging in commercial activity retains sovereign immunity for acts that are sovereign in nature, even if undertaken in connection with a commercial activity." *Jam v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 766 (2019).

### B. Argentina's Expropriation Was a Sovereign Act, Not Commercial Activity

The central flaw in the district court's analysis is its failure to properly distinguish between Argentina's sovereign acts and any private commercial conduct. The expropriation of YPF shares was accomplished through Law 26,741, enacted by the Argentine Congress pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, which authorizes expropriation for public use upon payment of just compensation. This is the quintessential exercise of sovereign authority.

In *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 27 (1983), the Supreme Court emphasized that "the FSIA provides a general immunity for foreign sovereigns, subject to certain specified exceptions." The commercial activity exception was not intended to subject foreign states to liability for sovereign acts simply because those acts may have some tangential connection to commercial matters.

The facts here demonstrate that Argentina's conduct was fundamentally sovereign:

1. The Argentine Congress declared a national public interest in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply" through Law 26,741.

2. The Argentine government invoked the General Expropriation Law to expropriate property for public use.

3. Argentina compensated Repsol for the expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

4. The government exercised its powers to take private property for public purposes—exactly the kind of sovereign act recognized under international law.

As this Court held in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006), "it is generally recognized that the power to expropriate is a sovereign prerogative" and "the decision to nationalize is quintessentially a sovereign act."

### C. The Required Nexus to the United States Is Absent

Even assuming Argentina's conduct could be characterized as commercial in some respects, the commercial activity exception requires a direct nexus between the act upon which the suit is based and the United States. Under the FSIA, the plaintiff must establish that the claim is "based upon" commercial activity that has a "direct effect" in the United States. *See OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 396 (2015).

In *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 357-58, the Court required that the commercial activity have a "direct effect" in the United States—meaning consequences that are "not remote" or "speculative." The mere fact that some financial consequences were felt by U.S. investors does not establish the required nexus.

Petersen's alleged injuries—loss of dividend payments and foreclosure of ADRs—were indirect consequences of Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The expropriation itself occurred in Argentina, was authorized by Argentine law, and was directed at property located in Argentina. The fact that the shares were represented by ADRs traded on the NYSE does not transform the sovereign act of expropriation into commercial activity.

In *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618, the Supreme Court found that the commercial activity exception applied because the bonds were "payable in New York" and the "place of performance" was the United States. Here, by contrast, Argentina's expropriation decree had its force and effect in Argentina, not the United States. The subsequent effects on U.S. investors, while regrettable, were not the basis of the action—they were incidental consequences of a sovereign act.

### D. YPF Is Entitled to Sovereign Immunity

YPF, although a mixed-capitalization company, is an instrumentality of the Argentine state. Under the FSIA, "an agency or instrumentality of a foreign state" is defined as "any entity... which is a separate legal person, corporate or otherwise," but which is "a majority of whose shares or other ownership interest is owned by a foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b).

YPF's actions in this case—operating under government direction, implementing the expropriation law, and managing the company as directed—were at all times consistent with its role as a state instrumentality carrying out sovereign policy. The district court erred in treating YPF as if it were a purely private corporation.

---

## II. The Act of State Doctrine Bars Petersen's Claims

### A. The Applicable Legal Framework

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory are not subject to adjudication by United States courts." *Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba v. First Nat'l City Bank*, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983) ("*Banco Para El Comercio*"). This doctrine reflects "the deference due to a foreign sovereign from the courts of this nation" and "the concern that judicial interference with the conduct of foreign relations by the political branches is inappropriate." *Id.* at 621.

The doctrine applies to "all cases in which the claim is based upon the act of a foreign sovereign within its own territory, even if the claim would require the court to interpret the foreign law." *Id.* at 622. It encompasses both "public acts" (legislative or executive acts) and "private acts" (commercial transactions) when performed by a foreign state. *See id.* at 622 n.11.

### B. Argentina's Expropriation Was an Act of State

The expropriation of YPF shares was accomplished through Law 26,741, enacted by the Argentine Congress. This is precisely the type of governmental act—legislative, sovereign, and within Argentina's own territory—that the act of state doctrine protects.

In *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897), this doctrine's progenitor, the Supreme Court held that "every sovereign state must recognize the independence of every other sovereign state, and the courts of one will not sit in judgment upon the acts of another done within its own territory." Argentina's expropriation law was enacted within Argentina's territory, pursuant to its own constitution, and represents the exercise of a fundamental sovereign power.

Petersen attempts to distinguish this case by characterizing the dispute as one involving "private contractual obligations." This characterization is incorrect. The bylaws of YPF, while initially adopted as part of the privatization process, cannot override the sovereign power of expropriation. Argentina's obligations under the bylaws, to the extent they existed, were subordinate to its constitutional authority to expropriate property for public use.

### C. The Commercial Activity Exception to the Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply

Petersen relies on *Banco Para El Comercio* for the proposition that the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions. However, that case is distinguishable.

In *Banco Para El Comercio*, the Cuban government had created a bank to handle commercial transactions, and the bank had contracted with a U.S. bank to handle foreign currency transactions. The Court held that when a foreign government "has created a separate legal entity... and has directed that entity to engage in commercial acts," the act of state doctrine does not apply to the commercial acts of that entity. 462 U.S. at 623.

Here, by contrast, Argentina's expropriation was not a commercial transaction—it was a sovereign act of expropriation authorized by the Argentine Constitution and the General Expropriation Law. The fact that Argentina subsequently exercised voting rights or made corporate decisions does not transform the underlying sovereign act of expropriation into a commercial transaction.

Moreover, the bylaws themselves were adopted in the context of a sovereign privatization program. Argentina was not acting as a private party when it adopted the bylaws; it was acting as the sovereign owner of YPF, implementing economic reforms. The subsequent expropriation was an exercise of the same sovereign authority.

---

## III. Petersen's Claims Fail on the Merits

### A. The Bylaws Did Not Create Enforceable Contractual Obligations Against Argentina

Even if this Court were to find jurisdiction and determine that the act of state doctrine does not bar this action, Petersen's claims fail on the merits because the bylaws did not create enforceable contractual obligations that could bind Argentina's sovereign authority.

The bylaws were adopted as part of Argentina's privatization program and were subject to Argentine law. Under Argentine law, the sovereign power of expropriation cannot be contractually waived. Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution authorizes expropriation for public use upon payment of compensation—it does not require that the government obtain shareholder approval or comply with tender offer procedures before exercising this fundamental sovereign power.

As the Supreme Court recognized in *Penn Central Transportation Co. v. City of New York*, 438 U.S. 104, 124 (1978), "the nature of the governmental action involved... is a 'taking' of property" when it "directly affects [the owner's] economic interest." However, such takings are constitutional when accomplished through proper legal procedures and with just compensation. Argentina complied with both requirements.

### B. The Expropriation Was Lawful Under Argentine Law

Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares was accomplished in full compliance with Argentine constitutional and statutory requirements. The Argentine Congress declared a public use for the property, as required by Article 17 of the National Constitution. Argentina provided compensation to the expropriated shareholder (Repsol). The procedures followed were those established by the General Expropriation Law.

Petersen cannot circumvent this lawful exercise of sovereign authority by invoking private contractual provisions that are inconsistent with Argentine constitutional law. The prospectus representations upon which Petersen relies were not intended to, and could not, bind Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation.

### C. The Contractual Provisions Are Contrary to Public Policy

Allowing private contractual provisions to constrain sovereign expropriation authority would be contrary to public policy. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "the principle that a sovereign cannot surrender its sovereign powers by contract is well established." *See United States v. Winstar Corp.*, 518 U.S. 839, 870 (1996). This principle applies with equal force to foreign sovereigns.

The bylaws' tender offer requirements, if interpreted to require Argentina to comply with private contractual procedures before exercising its constitutional expropriation power, would effectively allow private parties to veto sovereign regulatory action. This cannot be permitted.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Argentina and YPF respectfully request that this Court:

1. **Reverse** the district court's order denying dismissal under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and dismiss Petersen's claims against Argentina for lack of subject matter jurisdiction;

2. **Dismiss** the appeal as to the act of state doctrine, or in the alternative, **reverse** the district court's order and dismiss Petersen's claims as barred by the act of state doctrine; and

3. **Grant** such other and further relief as this Court deems just and proper.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF")'s motions to dismiss. The defendants argue that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") and that the act of state doctrine bars the plaintiff's claims. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the district court properly denied the motions to dismiss.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Commercial Activity Exception to the FSIA Applies**

The FSIA provides that foreign states are immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless a specific exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The relevant exception here is the "commercial activity" exception, which strips immunity for any action "based upon... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

**1. Nature of the Act**

The threshold inquiry is whether the conduct forming the basis of the suit is "commercial" rather than sovereign. The Supreme Court has held that the question turns on the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 613 (1992). Specifically, a foreign state engages in commercial activity when it exercises "only those powers that can also be exercised by private citizens," as opposed to those "peculiar to sovereigns." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993).

Defendants argue that Argentina's actions were sovereign because they involved the expropriation of property pursuant to constitutional authority. While the enactment of Law 26,741 and the declaration of public use are undoubtedly sovereign acts, the specific conduct upon which Petersen bases its claim is the breach of the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws. The obligations to make a tender offer, to publish notices in New York newspapers, and to forfeit voting rights upon non-compliance are contractual commitments undertaken during the privatization of YPF. These are actions that a private party might—and frequently does—undertake in the context of corporate governance and shareholder agreements. As in *Weltover*, where the issuance of bonds was found commercial despite the sovereign's purpose, the act of entering into and breaching a shareholder agreement is commercial in nature. 504 U.S. at 614.

Furthermore, YPF's actions—cancelling dividends and permitting Argentina to vote shares in violation of the bylaws—are corporate governance decisions. These are not actions "peculiar to sovereigns" but are routine commercial activities engaged in by private corporations worldwide. *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360.

**2. Direct Effect in the United States**

Even if the relevant acts occurred in Argentina, jurisdiction exists if they caused a "direct effect" in the United States. A "direct effect" is one that follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity" and is not merely "a subsequent, indirect effect." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

The defendants' refusal to comply with the bylaws directly precipitated the cancellation of dividend payments to holders of American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") in the United States. More significantly, this breach caused Petersen to default on its loans, leading to the foreclosure of its ADRs by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This financial injury—the loss of value and foreclosure of securities held in New York—is a direct and foreseeable effect of the defendants' commercial conduct. See *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 399 (2015) (noting that the place of the injury is a key factor in the direct effect analysis). The nexus between the breach of the bylaws and the financial injury in New York is sufficient to establish jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2).

**B. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar the Claims**

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from interrogating the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897). However, the doctrine is not absolute and does not apply to every case involving a foreign sovereign.

Defendants argue that because the expropriation was a sovereign act, this Court cannot inquire into the validity of Law 26,741 or the circumstances surrounding it. While the Court acknowledges the sovereign nature of the expropriation itself, Petersen's claims are not predicated on challenging the validity of the expropriation decree. Rather, the claims arise from alleged breaches of contractual obligations—the bylaws—that Argentina voluntarily assumed.

The Supreme Court in *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611 (1983), distinguished between the sovereign acts of a government and the commercial acts of its instrumentalities. The Court noted that the act of state doctrine is inapplicable where the government acts as a commercial participant. *Id.* at 622-23. Here, the dispute centers on the commercial obligations contained in the bylaws—specifically the tender offer requirements and the penalties for non-compliance. Adjudicating whether Argentina breached these commercial contracts does not require the Court to sit in judgment on Argentina's sovereign decision to expropriate the shares. The Court can enforce the contractual terms without invalidating the expropriation itself.

Defendants' argument that the bylaws were part of a sovereign privatization scheme and cannot bind the sovereign power is a merits defense regarding the interpretation of Argentine law and the hierarchy of contracts versus constitutional authority. It is not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine at this stage of litigation. See *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964) (noting that the act of state doctrine is a principle of judicial deference, not a mandate to dismiss all claims touching foreign sovereignty).

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments regarding the application of the FSIA commercial activity exception and the act of state doctrine are unavailing. The district court correctly denied the motions to dismiss. The preliminary ruling is to **affirm the district court's order** in its entirety.

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE SECOND CIRCUIT

---

**DEFENDANTS-APPELLANTS' REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S RESPONSE**

---

Your Honor, the plaintiff mischaracterizes the nature of this dispute and the applicable legal standards. While the Court has already ruled on the threshold jurisdictional questions, the plaintiff conflates distinct legal issues and ignores critical distinctions between sovereign acts and commercial conduct. The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the plaintiff's erroneous arguments.

## I. THE PLAINTIFF'S CHARACTERIZATION OF THE ACTS AT ISSUE IS FLAWED

The plaintiff attempts to isolate Argentina's breach of the tender offer provisions from the context in which that breach occurred. This separation is artificial and ignores the fundamental nature of the dispute.

### A. The Expropriation and the Bylaws Are Inextricably Linked

The plaintiff argues that "Argentina's breach of the tender offer provisions" constitutes commercial activity separate from the sovereign act of expropriation. However, this argument ignores that the tender offer provisions themselves were triggered *only* by Argentina's exercise of its sovereign expropriation power under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law.

Section 28(A) of the bylaws specifically addresses acquisitions by the Government of Argentina. The provision was designed to apply precisely in situations where Argentina exercises its constitutional power to expropriate property. As the defendants argued below, when a sovereign exercises its inherent power of eminent domain—authority that exists *by virtue of sovereign power*—the consequent obligations cannot be characterized as purely commercial. The plaintiff seeks to have this Court enforce contractual penalties against a sovereign for exercising its constitutional taking authority, a result that would effectively allow private parties to obstruct legitimate sovereign expropriations through contractual provisions.

The distinction this Court must maintain is between: (1) commercial activity that a foreign state undertakes as a market participant, and (2) sovereign acts that a foreign state undertakes pursuant to its constitutional authority. The plaintiff conflates these categories.

### B. The "Nature vs. Purpose" Test Supports the Defense

The plaintiff correctly cites *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), for the proposition that the FSIA inquiry turns on the "nature" of the act rather than its "purpose." However, the plaintiff misapplies this standard.

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court found commercial activity where Argentina issued bonds in the marketplace—activity that private parties routinely perform. 504 U.S. at 614. The Court emphasized that "when a foreign state enters the marketplace as a commercial actor," its conduct is commercial "regardless of the sovereign's motivations." Id.

Here, however, Argentina did not enter the marketplace as a commercial actor when it enacted Law 26,741. Rather, it exercised its sovereign power of eminent domain—authority that *no private party* possesses. The power to expropriate property for public use, with compensation, is the quintessential sovereign act. See *Garcia v. Mainland Airways Ltd.*, 61 F.3d 125, 131 (2d Cir. 1995) (holding that "the power of eminent domain... is sovereign, not commercial").

The bylaws' tender offer provisions were triggered *because* Argentina exercised this sovereign power. The obligation to make a tender offer arose only when Argentina, acting as a sovereign, declared a public use and expropriated property pursuant to Article 17. This causal relationship confirms that the conduct at issue is sovereign, not commercial.

## II. THE DIRECT EFFECT ANALYSIS IS INSUFFICIENT

The plaintiff argues that the cancellation of dividend payments and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York constitute "direct effects" sufficient to establish jurisdiction. This argument fails for several reasons.

### A. The Injury Flows from the Sovereign Act, Not Commercial Conduct

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish between the "expropriation" (which occurred in Argentina) and the "breach of contract" (which allegedly caused injury in New York). However, the alleged breach would not have occurred but for the sovereign expropriation. The chain of causation runs as follows:

1. Argentina exercised its sovereign power to expropriate (Article 17)
2. This exercise triggered the bylaws' tender offer provisions
3. Argentina refused to comply with those provisions
4. This refusal allegedly caused dividend cancellations and eventual foreclosure

The "direct effect" must be traced to the *commercial* activity, not the sovereign activity. See *Moses v. Bank of China*, 528 F.3d 31, 39 (2d Cir. 2008) (requiring that the commercial activity itself cause the direct effect). Here, the injury flows from the sovereign act of expropriation—the very act the plaintiff seeks to challenge through the backdoor of contractual penalties.

### B. The Place of Injury Is Not Determinative

The plaintiff relies heavily on *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390 (2015), for the proposition that the location of injury is "key." However, *OBB* involved a Swiss travel agency's alleged breach of a contract with a U.S. citizen—the classic commercial dispute with a foreign party. The Court emphasized that the "direct effect" requirement was satisfied because the plaintiff "suffered injury in the United States" when the Swiss agency refused to honor a travel voucher. 136 S. Ct. at 399.

Here, by contrast, the injury flows from a sovereign act of expropriation. Even acceptingarguendo that financial consequences in New York satisfy the "direct effect" requirement, the underlying conduct remains sovereign. The FSIA does not authorize jurisdiction over every claim that causes financial harm in the United States when a foreign sovereign is involved—it only strips immunity for claims "based upon" commercial activity. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

## III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE REMAINS A VALID DEFENSE

Although the Court has dismissed the defendants' appeal on the act of state doctrine, the defense notes that the plaintiff's characterization of the doctrine is overbroad.

### A. The Doctrine Protects Sovereign Decisions About Property

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine applies only to "public acts" and not to "commercial obligations." However, the doctrine's scope is not so limited. In *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), the Supreme Court held that the doctrine reflects "the underlying sensitivity of the act of state doctrine in the context of international law" and protects "the public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." 376 U.S. at 428.

The expropriation of property is the paradigmatic act of state. When Argentina declared YPF shares of "public use" and enacted Law 26,741, it exercised sovereign authority recognized under international law. The plaintiff's attempt to circumvent this protection by framing the claim as a "breach of contract" is precisely the type of end-run around the doctrine that the Supreme Court has cautioned against.

### B. The Commercial Activity Exception to the Doctrine Does Not Apply

The plaintiff cites *Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba v. First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. 611 (1983), for the proposition that the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions. However, *Banco Para El Comercio* involved a Cuban bank's attempt to enforce a letter of credit—the classic commercial dispute between private parties.

Here, by contrast, the dispute concerns whether a sovereign may be held liable in contract for exercising its constitutional power of eminent domain. The plaintiff does not seek to recover on a commercial debt or enforce a commercial lease. Rather, it seeks to impose contractual penalties on a sovereign for exercising its taking authority. This is not a commercial transaction—it is a challenge to sovereign decision-making about property rights.

## IV. THE MERITS ARGUMENTS ARE PROPERLY BEFORE THE COURT

The plaintiff argues that the defense's arguments about the enforceability of the bylaws are "merits arguments" that should not be considered at the motion-to-dismiss stage. This is incorrect.

### A. The FSIA Inquiry Is Jurisdictional, Not Merits-Based

The entire point of the FSIA is to determine whether a foreign sovereign is immune from suit—the threshold question of whether the court may exercise jurisdiction. As the Supreme Court has held, "the FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state." *Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp.*, 488 U.S. 428, 434 (1989). If the commercial activity exception does not apply, the Court lacks jurisdiction, and the case must be dismissed.

The defense's argument that the underlying conduct was sovereign—not commercial—is properly before this Court because it goes to the question of jurisdiction. If the conduct was sovereign, the FSIA grants immunity, and the Court cannot reach the merits.

### B. The Bylaws Cannot Override Sovereign Authority

The defense maintains that the bylaws cannot create enforceable obligations that bind Argentina's exercise of its constitutional expropriation power. This is not a novel proposition. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Sabbatino*, "the act of state doctrine is a principle of judicial deference, not a mandate to dismiss all claims touching foreign sovereignty." 376 U.S. at 428. But when a claim would require the Court to pass judgment on the validity of a sovereign's exercise of its taking power, the doctrine bars the claim.

The plaintiff drafted the bylaws and negotiated their terms during the privatization. Having secured beneficial provisions for investors, it now seeks to hold Argentina liable for failing to comply with those provisions when Argentina exercised its constitutional authority. This result would render the sovereign's constitutional power subject to contractual modification by private parties—the precise outcome the act of state doctrine and the FSIA's recognition of sovereign immunity were designed to prevent.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully submits that the plaintiff's response misapplies the controlling legal standards. The conduct at issue—Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain and the consequent refusal to comply with contractual tender offer provisions—is sovereign, not commercial. The plaintiff's attempt to sever the contractual obligations from the sovereign context in which they arose must fail. The district court's order denying the motion to dismiss should be reversed.

**The Argentine Republic and YPF S.A.**

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF")'s motions to dismiss. The defendants argue that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") and that the act of state doctrine bars the plaintiff's claims. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both parties, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The district court properly denied the motions to dismiss.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Commercial Activity Exception to the FSIA Applies**

The FSIA provides that foreign states are immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless a specific exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The relevant exception here is the "commercial activity" exception, which strips immunity for any action "based upon... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

**1. Nature of the Act**

The threshold inquiry is whether the conduct forming the basis of the suit is "commercial" rather than sovereign. The Supreme Court has held that the question turns on the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 613 (1992). Specifically, a foreign state engages in commercial activity when it exercises "only those powers that can also be exercised by private citizens," as opposed to those "peculiar to sovereigns." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993).

Defendants argue that the tender offer obligations in YPF's bylaws were triggered solely by Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution. They contend that because the obligation to make a tender offer arose only as a consequence of this sovereign act, the nature of the conduct is inherently sovereign. The defense relies on *Garcia v. Mainland Airways Ltd.*, 61 F.3d 125, 131 (2d Cir. 1995), for the proposition that the power of eminent domain is sovereign, not commercial.

While the Court acknowledges that the power to expropriate is a quintessential sovereign act, the specific conduct upon which Petersen bases its claim is the breach of the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws. The obligations to make a tender offer, to publish notices in New York newspapers, and to forfeit voting rights upon non-compliance are contractual commitments undertaken during the privatization of YPF. These are actions that a private party might—and frequently does—undertake in the context of corporate governance and shareholder agreements. As in *Weltover*, where the issuance of bonds was found commercial despite the sovereign's purpose, the act of entering into and breaching a shareholder agreement is commercial in nature. 504 U.S. at 614.

The defense's argument that the bylaws are "inextricably linked" to the expropriation conflates the *trigger* for the obligation with the *nature* of the obligation itself. Even if the obligation was triggered by a sovereign act, the performance of that obligation—or the failure to do so—constitutes commercial activity. The act of voting shares, cancelling dividends, and refusing to make a tender offer are not powers "peculiar to sovereigns." *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360. Therefore, the "nature of the act" test is satisfied.

**2. Direct Effect in the United States**

Even if the relevant acts occurred in Argentina, jurisdiction exists if they caused a "direct effect" in the United States. A "direct effect" is one that follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity" and is not merely "a subsequent, indirect effect." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

Defendants argue that the injury flows from the sovereign act of expropriation, not commercial conduct, and that the place of injury is not determinative. They cite *Moses v. Bank of China*, 528 F.3d 31, 39 (2d Cir. 2008), for the proposition that the commercial activity itself must cause the direct effect.

The Court finds that the commercial activity—the breach of the bylaws—was the proximate cause of the direct effect. The defendants' refusal to comply with the bylaws directly precipitated the cancellation of dividend payments to holders of American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") in the United States. More significantly, this breach caused Petersen to default on its loans, leading to the foreclosure of its ADRs by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This financial injury—the loss of value and foreclosure of securities held in New York—is a direct and foreseeable effect of the defendants' commercial conduct. See *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 399 (2015) (noting that the place of the injury is a key factor in the direct effect analysis). The nexus between the breach of the bylaws and the financial injury in New York is sufficient to establish jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2).

**B. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar the Claims**

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from interrogating the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897). However, the doctrine is not absolute and does not apply to every case involving a foreign sovereign.

Defendants argue that the plaintiff's attempt to frame the claim as a breach of contract is an "end-run" around the doctrine. They assert that enforcing the bylaws would effectively penalize Argentina for exercising its constitutional taking authority. They rely on *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), to argue that the doctrine protects sovereign decisions about property.

While the Court acknowledges the sovereign nature of the expropriation itself, Petersen's claims are not predicated on challenging the validity of Law 26,741 or the expropriation decree. Rather, the claims arise from alleged breaches of contractual obligations—the bylaws—that Argentina voluntarily assumed when it privatized YPF and sold shares to U.S. investors.

The Supreme Court in *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611 (1983), distinguished between the sovereign acts of a government and the commercial acts of its instrumentalities. The Court noted that the act of state doctrine is inapplicable where the government acts as a commercial participant. *Id.* at 622-23. Here, the dispute centers on the commercial obligations contained in the bylaws—specifically the tender offer requirements and the penalties for non-compliance. Adjudicating whether Argentina breached these commercial contracts does not require the Court to sit in judgment on Argentina's sovereign decision to expropriate the shares. The Court can enforce the contractual terms without invalidating the expropriation itself.

Defendants' argument that the bylaws cannot override sovereign authority is a merits defense regarding the interpretation of Argentine law and the hierarchy of contracts versus constitutional authority. It is not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine at this stage of litigation. See *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428 (noting that the act of state doctrine is a principle of judicial deference, not a mandate to dismiss all claims touching foreign sovereignty).

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments regarding the application of the FSIA commercial activity exception and the act of state doctrine are unavailing. The district court correctly denied the motions to dismiss. The order of the district court is **AFFIRMED**.

## CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR PLAINTIFF

Your Honor, the defense has attempted to reframe this case as a challenge to Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation. This mischaracterization is deliberate and inaccurate. Our claim is not about whether Argentina had the right to expropriate property—it unquestionably did. Our claim is about Argentina's breach of specific contractual obligations it voluntarily assumed when it privatized YPF and sold shares to U.S. investors. This distinction is fundamental, and the defense's arguments collapse under even cursory examination of the law.

### I. THE NATURE OF THE ACT IS COMMERCIAL, NOT SOVEREIGN

The defense claims that because the tender offer provisions were triggered by an expropriation, the entire conduct must be sovereign. This is legally unsound. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the FSIA inquiry turns on "the nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." When a foreign state enters the marketplace as a commercial actor, its conduct is commercial "regardless of the sovereign's motivations." Here, Argentina did not merely issue a decree—it voluntarily entered into contractual obligations that private parties routinely undertake in corporate governance.

The obligations at issue—making a tender offer, publishing notices in New York newspapers, and forfeiting voting rights upon non-compliance—are precisely the types of commitments that private corporations make to protect investors. As the district court correctly recognized, these are not powers "peculiar to sovereigns" but rather commercial undertakings that any private entity might perform. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). The defense's argument that the bylaws are "inextricably linked" to the expropriation conflates the trigger for the obligation with the nature of the obligation itself. Even if the obligation was triggered by a sovereign act, the performance—or failure to perform—of that obligation remains commercial.

The defense cites *Garcia v. Mainland Airways Ltd.*, 61 F.3d 125, 131 (2d Cir. 1995), for the proposition that eminent domain is sovereign. But *Garcia* involved a government's exercise of its inherent power to condemn property—nothing more. Here, Argentina did not exercise eminent domain alone; it simultaneously entered into binding contractual commitments with investors. The defense ignores that the very purpose of these contractual provisions was to create commercial protections for investors during a privatization process. When Argentina sold shares to U.S. investors, it became a commercial actor subject to commercial obligations.

### II. THE DIRECT EFFECT IN THE UNITED STATES IS CLEAR AND IMMEDIATE

The defense argues that the injury flows from the sovereign act of expropriation rather than commercial conduct. This is factually and legally incorrect. The breach of the contractual obligations directly and immediately caused financial harm in New York City.

Specifically: Argentina's refusal to comply with the tender offer requirements directly caused the cancellation of dividend payments to holders of American Depository Receipts in the United States. This breach directly precipitated Petersen's default on its loan obligations, which in turn led to the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. As the Supreme Court held in *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 399 (2015), the location of injury is a key factor in the direct effect analysis. Here, the financial injury—loss of value and foreclosure of securities held in New York—is a direct and foreseeable consequence of the defendants' commercial conduct.

The defense attempts to distinguish *OBB* by claiming it involved "classic commercial disputes," but that distinction is irrelevant. The question is not whether the case is "classic," but whether the commercial activity caused a direct effect in the United States. The defense's own argument that "the place of injury is not determinative" is correct—but it is precisely the location of the injury that confirms the direct effect. The foreclosure occurred in New York. The dividend payments were to be made to U.S. investors. The loans were secured by ADRs held in New York. These are not "subsequent, indirect effects"—they are the immediate consequences of the breach.

### III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY

The defense claims that enforcing the contractual obligations would "obstruct legitimate sovereign expropriations." This is a complete mischaracterization of our claim. We do not challenge the validity of the expropriation itself. We do not seek to prevent Argentina from taking property. We seek only to enforce the contractual terms that Argentina voluntarily agreed to when it privatized YPF.

As the Supreme Court made clear in *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622-23 (1983), the act of state doctrine "does not apply to commercial transactions." When a government acts as a commercial participant, it cannot hide behind sovereign immunity for breaches of its commercial obligations. Here, Argentina was not merely acting as a sovereign when it expropriated shares—it was simultaneously acting as a shareholder bound by contractual obligations. The Court can enforce these commercial terms without invalidating the expropriation itself.

The defense cites *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), for the proposition that the doctrine protects "sovereign decisions about property." But *Sabbatino* itself recognized that the act of state doctrine is "a principle of judicial deference, not a mandate to dismiss all claims touching foreign sovereignty." The defense's attempt to use *Sabbatino* as a blanket shield for all claims involving property rights is precisely what the Supreme Court rejected.

### IV. THE DEFENSE'S "MERITS ARGUMENTS" ARE IRRELEVANT AT THIS STAGE

The defense argues that the bylaws cannot override sovereign authority. This is a merits argument that has no place at the motion-to-dismiss stage. As the Supreme Court held in *Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp.*, 488 U.S. 428, 434 (1989), the FSIA provides "the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state." The question here is not whether Argentina's actions were legally justified under Argentine law—it is whether the FSIA strips immunity for this claim. The district court correctly found that the commercial activity exception applies, and that question is jurisdictional, not merits-based.

The defense's attempt to conflate jurisdiction with merits is particularly inappropriate here. If the defense is correct that the bylaws cannot override sovereign authority, that is a defense that can be raised later—if the case proceeds to the merits. But at this stage, the question is whether this Court has jurisdiction to hear the case at all. The defense has failed to show that jurisdiction is lacking.

### CONCLUSION

Your Honor, this case is not about whether Argentina had the right to expropriate property. It is about whether Argentina can breach its contractual obligations to U.S. investors and then hide behind sovereign immunity. The defense has mischaracterized the nature of the dispute, ignored the controlling precedent, and conflated jurisdiction with merits.

The district court correctly applied the law: Argentina's breach of contractual obligations was commercial in nature, it caused a direct effect in the United States, and the act of state doctrine does not apply. The defense's arguments rely on legal theories that have been repeatedly rejected by this Court and the Supreme Court. We respectfully submit that the district court's order should be affirmed, and this case should proceed to the merits where Petersen's claims can be fairly adjudicated.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the plaintiff has presented a carefully crafted narrative that attempts to obscure the fundamental nature of this dispute. Make no mistake: this case is about Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution. The plaintiff seeks to dress this sovereign act in the clothing of a commercial dispute by focusing on the bylaws of a company that Argentina expropriated. This Court should see through this disguise.

The plaintiff concedes that Argentina had the unquestionable right to expropriate property. Yet, remarkably, the plaintiff asks this Court to impose contractual penalties on Argentina for exercising that very right—penalties that would effectively make Argentina pay twice for exercising a sovereign power that belongs to no one but the sovereign state itself. This is precisely the type of interference with sovereign authority that the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine were designed to prevent.

## I. THE NATURE OF THE ACT IS SOVEREIGN, NOT COMMERCIAL

The plaintiff cites *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), for the proposition that the Court must examine the "nature of the act" rather than its purpose. We agree completely. The examination of the nature of the act, however, leads to the opposite conclusion from that reached by the district court.

The critical error in the plaintiff's argument is its attempt to sever the tender offer obligations from the sovereign act that triggered them. The plaintiff would have this Court believe that Argentina's obligation to make a tender offer arose independently—as if it were a standalone commercial commitment—and that Argentina simply chose not to perform that obligation. This is a fundamental misrepresentation of the contractual framework.

Section 28(A) of the bylaws expressly extends the tender offer requirement to acquisitions by the Government of Argentina "if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the Government becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of YPF." The obligation exists *because of* the acquisition and *only* because of the acquisition. The acquisition is the sovereign act of expropriation. Without the exercise of eminent domain, there is no tender offer obligation. The two are inseparable.

As this Court recognized in *Garcia v. Mainland Airways Ltd.*, 61 F.3d 125, 131 (2d Cir. 1995), the power of eminent domain is "sovereign, not commercial." When a foreign state exercises its constitutional power to take private property for public use, that exercise is inherently sovereign regardless of the form it takes. The plaintiff cannot transform a sovereign act into a commercial one merely by attaching contractual consequences to it.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Weltover* is misplaced. In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court found that the issuance of bonds was commercial because "the issuance of debt securities is a private commercial activity." 504 U.S. at 614. Argentina did not issue bonds in this case. Argentina exercised its sovereign power of eminent domain. These are fundamentally different acts.

The plaintiff argues that the specific acts of voting shares, cancelling dividends, and refusing to make a tender offer are "not powers peculiar to sovereigns." This argument misses the point. The question is not whether these specific acts, taken in isolation, could be performed by private parties. The question is whether these acts, performed in fulfillment of a sovereign expropriation, retain their sovereign character. They do.

The plaintiff cites *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), for the proposition that commercial activity includes acts that "private citizens" can perform. But *Nelson* itself recognized that "a state's activity is not commercial in the FSIA sense where the activity relates to the exercise of sovereign authority." Id. at 360. When Argentina voted the expropriated shares and cancelled dividends, it was exercising the rights of ownership that it had acquired through the sovereign act of expropriation. The plaintiff would not have this Court examine the genesis of Argentina's ownership interest—which is purely sovereign—and then pretend that the subsequent exercise of ownership rights is purely commercial.

The district court erred in its "nature of the act" analysis by artificially severing the tender offer obligation from the sovereign act that triggered it.

## II. THERE IS NO DIRECT EFFECT IN THE UNITED STATES

Even if the Court were to find that the nature of the act is commercial, the plaintiff cannot establish the required "direct effect in the United States" under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The plaintiff attempts to establish a direct effect by pointing to the cancellation of dividend payments and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs. But these injuries flow from the sovereign act of expropriation, not from any separate commercial activity.

The Supreme Court has held that a "direct effect" must be one that follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity" and is not merely "a subsequent, indirect effect." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. The plaintiff cannot establish such an immediate consequence here.

First, the cancellation of dividend payments was a direct result of the expropriation. When Argentina expropriated 51% of YPF from Repsol, it acquired the voting power to cancel the dividend. This is a direct consequence of the sovereign act of expropriation, not of any separate commercial activity.

Second, the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs was a consequence of Petersen's decision to finance its acquisition of YPF shares with debt. Petersen chose to leverage its investment. When the value of that investment declined—due to the sovereign act of expropriation—Petersen defaulted on its loans. This is a classic case of an investor suffering an indirect injury from a sovereign act. See *Moses v. Bank of China*, 528 F.3d 31, 39 (2d Cir. 2008) (holding that the commercial activity itself must cause the direct effect).

The plaintiff argues that the location of the injury—in New York—establishes a direct effect. This argument confuses the location of injury with the cause of injury. As the Supreme Court noted in *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 399 (2015), the place of injury is "a key factor" in the direct effect analysis. But it is not the only factor. The plaintiff must still establish that the commercial activity—rather than some other cause—produced the injury.

Here, the cause of the injury was the sovereign act of expropriation. The commercial activity exception does not apply merely because the injury was felt in the United States. The plaintiff has not established the required causal connection between the alleged commercial activity and the alleged injury.

The district court erred in finding a direct effect based on the location of the injury without properly establishing causation.

## III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THE CLAIMS

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from interrogating the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897). This doctrine applies with particular force when the plaintiff seeks to impose liability for conduct that is directly connected to a sovereign act.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish this case by arguing that it does not challenge the validity of the expropriation itself. This is a distinction without a difference. The plaintiff seeks to enforce contractual penalties that would make the expropriation more expensive and less effective. By requiring Argentina to pay a tender offer price for shares it acquired through expropriation, the plaintiff would effectively nullify a significant portion of the sovereign act.

As the Supreme Court held in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), the act of state doctrine reflects "the dominant principle of international law" that "the acts of a sovereign state taken within its own territory shall not be questioned in the courts of another state." The doctrine protects not only the formal validity of sovereign acts but also the sovereign's ability to exercise those acts effectively.

The plaintiff's argument that the act of state doctrine does not apply because Argentina was acting as a "commercial participant" misreads *First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611 (1983). In that case, the Supreme Court held that the doctrine did not apply to a commercial entity that was merely "an instrumentality of the foreign sovereign." Id. at 622-23. The Court explicitly noted that its holding was limited to "the situation in which a foreign state has created a separate legal entity." Id. at 623.

Here, Argentina itself—the sovereign—is the defendant. Argentina did not create a separate legal entity to perform the acts at issue. Argentina, acting in its sovereign capacity, exercised its constitutional power of eminent domain. The act of state doctrine applies with full force.

The plaintiff argues that the Court can "enforce the contractual terms without invalidating the expropriation itself." This argument misunderstands the nature of the relief sought. The plaintiff seeks to compel Argentina to make a tender offer for shares that Argentina acquired through expropriation. This relief would effectively rewrite the terms of the expropriation by requiring Argentina to pay additional compensation. This is precisely the type of judicial interference with sovereign acts that the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The district court erred in finding that the act of state doctrine does not apply.

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF'S MERITS ARGUMENTS ARE PREMATURE

The plaintiff argues that the defense's arguments about the hierarchy of contracts versus constitutional authority are "merits arguments" that have no place at the motion-to-dismiss stage. The plaintiff is correct that this Court need not resolve the merits at this stage. But the plaintiff is incorrect in suggesting that the defense's arguments are irrelevant to the jurisdictional analysis.

The question before this Court is whether the plaintiff has stated a claim upon which relief can be granted. If the bylaws cannot, as a matter of law, override Argentina's sovereign authority, then the plaintiff's claims fail as a matter of law. This is not a merits argument—it is a argument about the legal sufficiency of the complaint.

Under Argentine law, which governs the bylaws, the sovereign power of expropriation cannot be contractually limited. The plaintiff cannot enforce contractual provisions that would prevent Argentina from exercising its constitutional authority. This is a fundamental principle of Argentine law that this Court must apply.

The plaintiff argues that the defense is improperly conflating jurisdiction with merits. But the defense is doing no such thing. The defense is arguing that, even accepting all of the plaintiff's allegations as true, the complaint fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. This is precisely the inquiry before this Court on a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6).

## CONCLUSION

Your Honor, this case presents a straightforward question: can a foreign sovereign be held liable in U.S. courts for exercising its sovereign power of eminent domain? The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine answer that question with a clear "no."

The plaintiff has attempted to obscure this question by reframing the dispute as a commercial breach-of-contract claim. But the plaintiff cannot escape the fundamental fact that the conduct at issue—the acquisition of YPF shares by Argentina—was a sovereign act of expropriation. The contractual provisions that the plaintiff seeks to enforce are inseparable from that sovereign act.

The district court erred in denying the defendants' motions to dismiss. The plaintiff has not established that the commercial activity exception applies, and the act of state doctrine bars the claims. We respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's order and dismiss the complaint.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Defendants-Appellants**

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. The defendants-appellants, the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF"), appeal the district court's denial of their motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA"), 28 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq., and the act of state doctrine. Having carefully considered the closing arguments of both parties and the underlying record, the Court affirms the district court's order in part and dismisses the appeal in part.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Commercial Activity Exception Applies**

The FSIA establishes the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. *Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp.*, 488 U.S. 428, 443 (1989). Foreign states are immune from jurisdiction unless a specific exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The defendants argue that the district court erred in finding jurisdiction under the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), which strips immunity for acts "outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States."

**1. Nature of the Act**

The threshold inquiry is whether the conduct upon which the claim is based is "commercial" rather than sovereign. The Supreme Court has instructed that the question turns on the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 613 (1992). A foreign state engages in commercial activity when it exercises "only those powers that can also be exercised by private citizens," as opposed to those "peculiar to sovereigns." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993).

The defense argues that the acts at issue are inextricably linked to the sovereign act of expropriation. They contend that because the tender offer obligation in Section 28(A) of YPF's bylaws is triggered only by the Government of Argentina's acquisition of shares—a quintessential exercise of eminent domain—the resulting obligation is sovereign in nature. The defense relies on *Garcia v. Mainland Airways Ltd.*, 61 F.3d 125, 131 (2d Cir. 1995), to assert that the power of eminent domain is inherently sovereign.

While the Court agrees that the exercise of eminent domain is a sovereign function, the specific conduct forming the basis of Petersen's claim is the breach of the contractual obligations contained in YPF's bylaws. The obligation to make a tender offer, to publish notices in New York newspapers, and to forfeit voting rights upon non-compliance are contractual commitments voluntarily undertaken by Argentina during the privatization of YPF. These are actions that private parties frequently undertake in the context of corporate governance and shareholder agreements.

The defense's attempt to characterize the breach of these bylaws as a sovereign act because the *trigger* was expropriation conflates the cause of the obligation with the nature of the obligation itself. As in *Weltover*, where the issuance of bonds was found commercial despite the sovereign's purpose of managing foreign debt, the act of entering into and breaching a shareholder agreement is commercial in nature. 504 U.S. at 614. The act of voting shares, cancelling dividends, and refusing to make a tender offer are not powers "peculiar to sovereigns." *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360. Therefore, the "nature of the act" test is satisfied.

**2. Direct Effect in the United States**

Jurisdiction further requires that the commercial activity cause a "direct effect" in the United States. A "direct effect" is one that follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity" and is not merely "a subsequent, indirect effect." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

The defense argues that the injury flows from the sovereign act of expropriation, not the commercial breach, and that the location of the injury is not determinative. They cite *Moses v. Bank of China*, 528 F.3d 31, 39 (2d Cir. 2008), for the proposition that the commercial activity itself must cause the direct effect.

The Court finds that the commercial activity—the breach of the bylaws—was the proximate cause of the direct effect. The defendants' refusal to comply with the bylaws directly precipitated the cancellation of dividend payments to holders of American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") in the United States. More significantly, this breach caused Petersen to default on its loans, leading to the foreclosure of its ADRs by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This financial injury—the loss of value and foreclosure of securities held in New York—is a direct and foreseeable effect of the defendants' commercial conduct. See *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 399 (2015) (noting that the place of the injury is a key factor in the direct effect analysis). The nexus between the breach of the bylaws and the financial injury in New York is sufficient to establish jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2).

**B. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar the Claims**

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from interrogating the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897). The defense argues that enforcing the bylaws would effectively penalize Argentina for exercising its constitutional taking authority and that the doctrine therefore bars the claim.

The defense relies on *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), to argue that the doctrine protects sovereign decisions about property. However, Petersen's claims are not predicated on challenging the validity of Law 26,741 or the expropriation decree itself. Rather, the claims arise from alleged breaches of contractual obligations—the bylaws—that Argentina voluntarily assumed when it privatized YPF and sold shares to U.S. investors.

The Supreme Court in *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611 (1983), distinguished between the sovereign acts of a government and the commercial acts of its instrumentalities. The Court noted that the act of state doctrine is inapplicable where the government acts as a commercial participant. *Id.* at 622-23. Here, the dispute centers on the commercial obligations contained in the bylaws—specifically the tender offer requirements and the penalties for non-compliance. Adjudicating whether Argentina breached these commercial contracts does not require the Court to sit in judgment on Argentina's sovereign decision to expropriate the shares. The Court can enforce the contractual terms without invalidating the expropriation itself.

The defense's argument that the bylaws cannot override sovereign authority is a merits defense regarding the interpretation of Argentine law and the hierarchy of contracts versus constitutional authority. It is not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine at this stage of litigation. See *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428 (noting that the act of state doctrine is a principle of judicial deference, not a mandate to dismiss all claims touching foreign sovereignty).

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments regarding the application of the FSIA commercial activity exception and the act of state doctrine are unavailing. The district court correctly denied the motions to dismiss. The order of the district court is **AFFIRMED**.
