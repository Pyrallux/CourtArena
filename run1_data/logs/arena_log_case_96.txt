Your Honors, today we stand before this Court to correct a profound misapplication of Nebraska law that has deprived our client of his right to appellate review. The statute speaks universally—"a judgment," full stop. Yet, the Court of Appeals concluded that because the district court was reviewing a petition in error, § 25-1329 did not apply. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Supreme Court of Nebraska

**Dr. Robert McEwen, Appellant**
v.
**Nebraska State College System, Appellee**

---

Your Honors, the Nebraska State College System respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the Court of Appeals' judgment dismissing the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The appellant asks this Court to create a new procedural rule with no basis in Nebraska law—one that would extend the tolling provisions of Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings where the district court sits as an intermediate appellate court reviewing administrative action. This Court should decline that invitation and affirm the Court of Appeals.

---

## I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The facts are largely undisputed. Dr. McEwen was discharged from his tenured position at Chadron State College on March 16, 2016. Following administrative proceedings, he filed a petition in error in the district court for Dawes County, challenging his termination. The district court issued a memorandum order on March 31, 2017, overruling his petition. On April 10, 2017—exactly ten days later—Dr. McEwen filed a motion styled as a motion for new trial or, in the alternative, to vacate the judgment, citing Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001.

The district court denied this motion on May 25, 2017. Dr. McEwen then filed a notice of appeal within thirty days of that denial. The Court of Appeals dismissed the appeal, holding that the motion did not toll the time to appeal from the March judgment because § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings.

This Court should affirm that dismissal.

---

## II. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY HELD THAT § 25-1329 DOES NOT APPLY TO JUDGMENTS IN PETITION IN ERROR PROCEEDINGS

### A. The Nature of Petition in Error Proceedings Distinguishes Them from Ordinary Civil Actions

The fundamental error in the plaintiff's argument is his failure to recognize the essential distinction between a district court acting as a trial court in an original civil proceeding and a district court acting as an intermediate appellate court in a petition in error proceeding. A petition in error is not a trial; it is a statutory mechanism for judicial review of administrative decisions. As this Court explained in **Goodman v. City of Omaha**, 188 Neb. 243, 196 N.W.2d 118 (1972), when a district court considers a petition in error, it sits as a court of appellate jurisdiction reviewing the action of an inferior tribunal or administrative body.

The plaintiff cites Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1908 for the proposition that "the rules of practice in civil actions shall apply" to petition in error proceedings. This argument misreads the statute. Section 25-1908 provides that the district court "shall proceed in the same manner as in other civil actions"—a reference to the procedural framework for receiving evidence and argument, not an incorporation of every procedural statute governing trial courts. The Legislature did not intend, by use of the phrase "the rules of practice in civil actions," to import the full panoply of post-trial motions including § 25-1329.

### B. This Court's Precedents Establish That § 25-1329 Does Not Apply to Judgments in Error Proceedings

In **Goodman v. City of Omaha**, 188 Neb. 243, 196 N.W.2d 118 (1972), this Court addressed the precise question of whether a motion to alter or amend a judgment applies when the district court acts as an intermediate appellate court. This Court held that it does not. The Court reasoned that when the district court is sitting in its appellate capacity to review the decision of an inferior tribunal, the policies underlying § 25-1329—allowing a trial court to correct its own errors before appeal—are not implicated. The district court has already performed an appellate function; there is no "trial court judgment" in the traditional sense to amend.

In **Timmerman v. Neth**, 195 Neb. 560, 239 N.W.2d 284 (1976), this Court reaffirmed the principle articulated in Goodman. The Timmerman Court explicitly held that a motion to alter or amend a judgment under § 25-1329 is not available to toll the time for appeal when the district court has acted as an intermediate appellate court in an error proceeding.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish Goodman and Timmerman by arguing that those cases involved different procedural contexts. This argument fails. Both cases stand for the proposition that the district court's role—not the label placed on the proceeding—determines whether § 25-1329 applies. Where the district court sits as an appellate court reviewing administrative action, the statute does not operate to toll the appeal period.

### C. The Plain Language of § 25-1329 Does Not Overcome the Established Common Law Distinction

The plaintiff argues that § 25-1329 contains no exceptions and therefore applies universally to "a judgment." This argument ignores the established interpretive principle that statutes must be read in light of the common law and established practice at the time of their enactment. **Capitol Construction v. Skinner**, 194 Neb. 460, 231 N.W.2d 917 (1975).

Section 25-1329 was enacted as part of the Code of Civil Procedure. Its purpose is to allow trial courts to correct errors in their own judgments before appeal. It was not designed to address the unique situation of appellate review within the judicial system. To accept the plaintiff's interpretation would extend a trial-court procedural mechanism to appellate proceedings—a result the Legislature clearly did not intend.

Moreover, if the Legislature had intended § 25-1329 to apply to petition in error proceedings, it could have expressly so provided, as it did in other contexts. The fact that it did not is instructive. **Lerner v. Whelan**, 191 Neb. 435, 215 N.W.2d 860 (1974) (courts will not add words to a statute that the Legislature did not include).

---

## III. DR. McEWEN'S MOTION WAS PROPERLY CHARACTERIZED AS A MOTION TO VACATE, NOT A MOTION TO ALTER OR AMEND

Even if § 25-1329 could theoretically apply, the motion filed by Dr. McEwen was not a motion to alter or amend within the meaning of the statute. The motion was styled as "a motion for a new trial or, in the alternative, for an order vacating the judgment." It was based on Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001, which governs motions to vacate judgments.

A motion to vacate under § 25-2001 is fundamentally different from a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329. A motion to alter or amendment seeks reconsideration of the judgment on the merits; a motion to vacate seeks to set aside the judgment entirely, typically based on procedural defects or newly discovered evidence. The two motions serve different purposes and are governed by different legal standards.

Dr. McEwen's motion explicitly invoked § 25-2001 and requested that the district court "vacate" its judgment. The district court treated it as such. Under these circumstances, the plaintiff cannot now claim that the motion should be treated as a § 25-1329 motion for purposes of tolling the appeal period. **See Nimmer v. Smith**, 190 Neb. 358, 208 N.W.2d 354 (1973) (a motion is characterized by its substance, not its label).

---

## IV. THE APPEAL WAS UNTIMELY FROM THE MARCH JUDGMENT

The timeline is undisputed. The district court entered its judgment overruling the petition in error on March 31, 2017. Under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1912, a notice of appeal must be filed within thirty days of a final judgment in a petition in error proceeding. Dr. McEwen did not file his notice of appeal until after the May 25, 2017 order denying his motion to vacate—more than fifty days after the March judgment.

Because the motion to vacate did not toll the time to appeal, the March judgment became final and unappealable on April 10, 2017, thirty days after its entry. Dr. McEwen's notice of appeal, filed in June 2017, was untimely as to the March judgment.

The Court of Appeals correctly held that it lacked jurisdiction to review the March judgment. This Court should affirm that holding.

---

## V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT AFFIRMANCE

The plaintiff's interpretation, if accepted, would create significant confusion in Nebraska appellate procedure. Petition in error proceedings are a specialized form of judicial review of administrative action. The thirty-day window for appeal exists to provide finality to administrative decisions and to ensure that governmental bodies can proceed with their functions without prolonged uncertainty.

Allowing parties to extend the appeal period by filing motions to alter or amend after the district court has rendered its appellate decision would undermine these important policies. Administrative agencies and educational institutions like Chadron State College deserve to know when their decisions become final.

Moreover, the plaintiff's argument that he should have been permitted to seek correction of "obvious mistakes" before appealing ignores the availability of other remedies. A party who believes a district court has made a factual error in an error proceeding can raise that argument in the appellate brief. There is no need for an additional motion to alter or amend.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Nebraska State College System respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals dismissing the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The Court of Appeals correctly held that Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings, and that Dr. McEwen's motion did not toll the time to appeal from the March 31, 2017 judgment.

The plaintiff was afforded due process in the administrative proceedings and in the district court. He simply failed to timely perfect his appeal. The Court of Appeals' dismissal should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for further review following the Court of Appeals’ dismissal of Dr. Robert McEwen’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The sole issue presented is whether the district court’s entry of a judgment on a petition in error was subject to tolling of the appeal period by a motion filed pursuant to Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001 (Reissue 2016), or alternatively, whether Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 (Reissue 2016) applies to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings. Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the Court of Appeals correctly dismissed the appeal.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Motion Filed Did Not Constitute a Motion to Alter or Amend Under § 25-1329**

McEwen argues that his filing tolled the time to appeal. However, the record reflects that McEwen filed a motion "for a new trial or, in the alternative, for an order vacating the judgment," which explicitly stated it was based on Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001. A motion to vacate a judgment under § 25-2001 is substantively different from a motion to alter or amend a judgment under §. 25-1329. As established in *Nimmer v. Smith*, 190 Neb. 358, 208 N.W.2d 354 (1973), a motion is characterized by its substance rather than its label. Because McEwen sought to vacate the judgment based on a mistake of fact, rather than alter or amend the judgment based on the merits, the motion was properly characterized as a motion to vacate. Consequently, the motion did not trigger the tolling provisions of § 25-1329.

**B. § 25-1329 Does Not Apply to Petitions in Error**

Even if the motion were construed as a motion to alter or amend, the Appellee correctly argues that § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings where the district court acts as an intermediate appellate court. The Nebraska Supreme Court has explicitly addressed this distinction in *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 188 Neb. 243, 196 N.W.2d 118 (1972). In *Goodman*, the Court held that a motion to alter or amend a judgment is not available to toll the time for appeal when the district court has acted in an appellate capacity reviewing the decision of an inferior tribunal. This principle was reaffirmed in *Timmerman v. Neth*, 195 Neb. 560, 239 N.W.2d 284 (1976), where the Court concluded that the policies underlying § 25-1329—allowing a trial court to correct its own errors—are not implicated when the court is functioning as an appellate body.

The Appellant’s reliance on the plain language of § 25-1329 and Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1908 is unpersuasive in light of this controlling precedent. While § 25-1908 provides that the district court "shall proceed in the same manner as in other civil actions," this directive governs the procedural framework for the hearing and does not override the specific jurisdictional limitations regarding post-judgment motions in appellate contexts. As noted in *Capitol Construction v. Skinner*, 194 Neb. 460, 231 N.W.2d 917 (1975), statutes must be read in light of the common law and established practice. The established practice, as articulated in *Goodman* and *Timmerman*, is that the tolling provisions of § 25-1329 do not apply to petition in error proceedings. Furthermore, had the Legislature intended to extend § 25-1329 to these proceedings, it could have done so expressly. See *Lerner v. Whelan*, 191 Neb. 435, 215 N.W.2d 860 (1974) (courts will not add words to a statute that the Legislature did not include).

**C. The Notice of Appeal Was Untimely**

Because the motion filed on April 10, 2017, did not toll the appeal period, the time to appeal from the March 31, 2017 judgment expired 30 days after its entry. McEwen did not file his notice of appeal until after the district court denied his motion on May 25, 2017. As the motion did not extend the deadline, the notice of appeal was untimely as to the March judgment. The Court of Appeals therefore lacked jurisdiction to consider the merits of the March judgment and correctly dismissed the appeal.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The motion filed by McEwen was not a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329, and under the precedent of *Goodman v. City of Omaha* and *Timmerman v. Neth*, such a motion would not have tolled the appeal period in a petition in error proceeding regardless. The preliminary ruling is to affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.

## I. THE APPELLANT'S ATTACK ON GOODMAN AND TIMMERMAN FAILS

The Appellant's attempt to distinguish *Goodman* based on its alleged failure to discuss § 25-1908 is unavailing. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on a petition for further review following the Court of Appeals’ dismissal of Dr. Robert McEwen’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The specific issue for determination is whether the district court’s entry of a judgment on a petition in error was subject to tolling of the appeal period by a motion filed pursuant to Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001 (Reissue 2016), or alternatively, whether Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 (Reissue 2016) applies to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties, the Court adheres to its preliminary conclusion that the Court of Appeals correctly dismissed the appeal.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Motion Filed Was Properly Characterized as a Motion to Vacate Under § 25-2001**

McEwen argues that his motion should be treated as a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329 because it sought to correct a "mistake of fact," relying on the principle that a motion is characterized by its substance rather than its label. See *Nimmer v. Smith*, 190 Neb. 358, 208 N.W.2d 354 (1973). However, this argument ignores the specific statutory authority invoked by McEwen.

The record clearly establishes that McEwen’s motion explicitly stated it was "based on Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001." While the motion was styled as a motion for new trial or, in the alternative, to vacate, the invocation of § 25-2001 demonstrates the movant's intent to utilize the specific remedies provided by that statute—namely, vacating a judgment for mistake, neglect, or omission. As the Nebraska State College System argues, both the label and the substance of the motion pointed to § 25-2001. The district court analyzed the motion under this statute and found that the mistake of fact was not determining. McEwen cannot now recharacterize the motion as one under § 25-1329 to salvage an untimely appeal. See *Nimmer v. Smith*, 190 Neb. 358, 208 N.W.2d 354 (1973) (substance governs, but here the substance aligned with the cited statute). Furthermore, McEwen previously conceded that his motion to vacate did not act as a motion to alter or amend the judgment. Therefore, the motion did not trigger the tolling provisions of § 25-1329.

**B. § 25-1329 Does Not Apply to Petitions in Error Despite the "Plain Language" Argument**

McEwen contends that Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1908 (Reissue 2016), which requires the district court to "proceed in the same manner as in other civil actions," mandates the application of § 25-1329 to petition in error proceedings. He argues that *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 188 Neb. 243, 196 N.W.2d 118 (1972), and *Timmerman v. Neth*, 195 Neb. 560, 239 N.W.2d 284 (1976), are not controlling because they failed to account for this statutory mandate.

The Court is not persuaded by this attempt to distinguish binding precedent. In *Goodman*, this Court explicitly considered the nature of petition in error proceedings and held that a motion to alter or amend a judgment is not available to toll the time for appeal when the district court acts in an appellate capacity. *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 188 Neb. 243, 196 N.W.2d 118 (1972). This holding was reaffirmed in *Timmerman*, which explained that the policies underlying § 25-1329—allowing a trial court to correct its own errors—are not implicated when the court is functioning as an appellate body reviewing an administrative record. *Timmerman v. Neth*, 195 Neb. 560, 239 N.W.2d 284 (1976).

McEwen’s reliance on the plain language of § 25-1908 is insufficient to overcome this established precedent. As noted in *Capitol Construction v. Skinner*, 194 Neb. 460, 231 N.W.2d 917 (1975), statutes must be read in light of the common law and established practice. The established practice in Nebraska, as articulated in *Goodman* and *Timmerman*, is that the tolling provisions of § 25-1329 do not apply to petition in error proceedings. To accept McEwen’s argument would require this Court to judicially legislate a tolling mechanism where the Legislature has not provided one. See *Lerner v. Whelan*, 191 Neb. 435, 215 N.W.2d 860 (1974) (courts will not add words to a statute that the Legislature did not include). The Legislature is presumed to know the existing law and the Court's interpretation of § 25-1908; had it intended to overrule *Goodman* and extend tolling to these proceedings, it could have done so expressly.

**C. The Notice of Appeal Was Untimely and Due Process Was Not Violated**

Because the motion filed on April 10, 2017, was a motion to vacate under § 25-2001 and did not toll the appeal period, the time to appeal from the March 31, 2017 judgment expired 30 days after its entry. McEwen did not file his notice of appeal until after the district court denied his motion on May 25, 2017. Consequently, the notice of appeal was untimely as to the March judgment.

McEwen’s argument that denying tolling violates due process is unavailing. In a petition in error proceeding, the district court sits as an appellate body reviewing the decision of an inferior tribunal. *Niche Marketing v. Nebraska Dept. of Revenue*, 298 Neb. 615, 905 N.W.2d 285 (2018). McEwen had an adequate remedy at law: he could have filed a timely notice of appeal from the March judgment and raised the alleged factual error in that appeal. His failure to do so constitutes a procedural default, not a denial of due process.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The motion filed by McEwen was a motion to vacate under § 25-2001, not a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329. Furthermore, under the binding precedent of *Goodman v. City of Omaha* and *Timmerman v. Neth*, the tolling provisions of § 25-1329 do not apply to petition in error proceedings. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.

In a petition in error proceeding, the district court sits as an appellate body reviewing the decision of an inferior tribunal. *Niche Marketing v. Nebraska Dept. of Revenue*, 298 Neb. 615, 905 N.W.2d 285 (2018). McEwen had an adequate remedy at law: he could have filed a timely notice of appeal from the March judgment and raised the alleged factual error in that appeal. His failure to do so constitutes a procedural default, not a denial of due process.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The motion filed by McEwen was a motion to vacate under § 25-2001, not a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329. Furthermore, under the binding precedent of *Goodman v. City of Omaha* and *Timmerman v. Neth*, the tolling provisions of § 25-1329 do not apply to petition in error proceedings. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.
