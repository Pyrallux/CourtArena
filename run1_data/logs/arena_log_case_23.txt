# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE ARIZONA COURT OF APPEALS

---

### Introduction and Statement of the Case

The prosecution asks this Court to reverse Dickinson's convictions and dismiss the charges with prejudice, arguing that the trial court's declaration of mistrial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The prosecution contends the mistrial was not justified by "manifest necessity" because the court failed to consider less drastic alternatives before terminating the first trial. This Court should affirm the convictions.

---

## I. The Trial Court Properly Declared a Mistrial Because Manifest Necessity Existed

### A. The Standard for Manifest Necessity

The Fifth Amendment protects against double jeopardy, but it "does not guarantee a defendant the right to have his trial proceed regardless of circumstances." *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). Rather, the Constitution requires that "there is a necessity for doing so" before a mistrial may be declared. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971). The trial court must consider alternatives, but it retains "broad discretion" in determining whether manifest necessity exists. *Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506.

The manifest necessity standard "does not require an exhaustive inquiry" or that the trial court "articulate a perfect solution." *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 595, 633 P.2d 366, 372 (Ariz. 1981). Instead, the court must simply exercise "sound discretion" in determining that "the trial cannot continue" without prejudicing the defendant. *Id.*

### B. Manifest Necessity Existed Here

The prosecution minimizes the significance of the surprise note, but the evidence was transformative. Defense counsel told the jury during opening statements that the bike Dickinson sold on Craigslist was *not* the stolen bike because the serial numbers differed. This was the central defense theory. The surprise note—containing a serial number that matched the bike purchased from Dickinson—directly contradicted this opening statement and undermined the entire defense strategy.

In *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 633 P.2d 366 (Ariz. 1981), this Court held that manifest necessity existed where unexpected evidence destroyed the defendant's theory of the case. Here, the note had the same effect. The defense was ambushed with evidence that contradicted its opening statement—a situation that fundamentally compromised the fairness of the proceeding.

The prosecution argues that because it offered to proceed without the note evidence, manifest necessity was lacking. This misapprehends the standard. The prosecution's willingness to forgo evidence does not eliminate the prejudice already inflicted upon the defense. The jury heard the buyer's testimony about the note during cross-examination. Even if the court had later excluded the note, the jury had already received this damaging information. A curative instruction would have highlighted the very evidence the defense wanted suppressed, and the prosecution's brief acknowledgment of an "option" does not constitute a meaningful alternative that the trial court was required to adopt.

### C. The Trial Court Exercised Sound Discretion

The trial court was in the best position to assess the impact of the surprise evidence. The court had observed the proceedings, heard the opening statements, and understood the strategic significance of the serial number evidence. As this Court recognized in *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. at 595, 633 P.2d at 372, "the trial judge is in the best position to evaluate the circumstances" and determine whether manifest necessity exists.

The prosecution cites *State v. Johnson*, 174 Ariz. 383, 850 P.2d 180 (Ariz. 1993), for the proposition that a mistrial is improper when the court does not explore alternatives. However, *Johnson* is distinguishable. In that case, the trial court declared a mistrial based solely on the prosecutor's representation that witness tampering had occurred, without any independent inquiry. Here, by contrast, the court witnessed the surprise disclosure and its impact on the proceedings. The court was not required to conduct a further inquiry when the prejudice was self-evident.

---

## II. Dickinson's Conduct and Failure to Preserve Waived the Double Jeopardy Claim

### A. The Defense Contributed to the Circumstances

Critically, Dickinson's counsel did not object to the testimony that precipitated the mistrial. To the contrary, when asked whether he would move to strike the surprise testimony, defense counsel stated he would *not* do so "because he believed doing so would emphasize it." This strategic decision backfired, but it cannot now form the basis of a double jeopardy claim.

The defense cannot exploit a circumstance it helped create. If defense counsel believed the note was so prejudicial that the trial could not continue, he should have moved to strike or requested a mistrial himself. His strategic choice to let the testimony stand, hoping the jury would not credit it, undermines his subsequent double jeopardy claim.

### B. Dickinson Failed to Move for Dismissal

After the mistrial, Dickinson's counsel told the court he would seek dismissal with prejudice if a mistrial was declared. Yet after the mistrial was granted, defense counsel *did not file* such a motion. This failure is significant.

In *State v. Patton*, 120 Ariz. 386, 586 P.2d 629 (Ariz. 1978), this Court held that a defendant who fails to move for dismissal after a mistrial may be deemed to have accepted the mistrial and waived double jeopardy concerns. Dickinson's counsel had the opportunity to seek dismissal but chose not to pursue it. This waives the claim on appeal.

The prosecution argues that *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 931 P.2d 1046 (Ariz. 1997), holds that a defendant need not move to dismiss to preserve a double jeopardy claim. However, *Mott* involved a fundamentally different situation—a trial court that declared a mistrial over the defendant's objection. Here, Dickinson's counsel's conduct was more equivocal. He did not object to the mistrial; he simply failed to pursue the remedy he had earlier identified.

---

## III. Any Error Was Harmless

Even assuming *arguendo* that the mistrial declaration was improper, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Dickinson was convicted after a second trial that occurred six months after the mistrial. The prosecution presented its case, the defense presented its theory, and the jury convicted.

The double jeopardy protection "is primarily designed to prevent the trauma of a second prosecution for the same offense." *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 607 (1976). Here, Dickinson received a full and fair trial. He was not subjected to repeated prosecutions for the same offense in rapid succession. The second trial occurred only after appropriate procedures were followed, and the verdict was based on the evidence presented.

In *State v. Murray*, 136 Ariz. 132, 664 P.2d 642 (Ariz. 1983), this Court held that a mistrial declaration, even if erroneous, does not automatically require dismissal with prejudice when the defendant receives a fair subsequent trial. The appropriate remedy for an improper mistrial is not always dismissal; it may be review of the subsequent conviction for prejudicial error. Here, Dickinson has not demonstrated that the second trial was unfair or that the verdict was tainted.

---

## IV. The Prosecution's Citations Do Not Support Reversal

The prosecution relies heavily on *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), but that case is distinguishable. In *Jorn*, the trial court declared a mistrial after the prosecutor indicated he wanted to call a witness who had not been properly subpoenaed. The Supreme Court emphasized that the trial court had not explored alternatives and had effectively "sacrificed" the defendant to the prosecutor's convenience. Here, by contrast, the trial court faced genuinely prejudicial surprise evidence that compromised the defense theory. The circumstances were not within the control of either party.

*State v. Johnson*, 174 Ariz. 383, 850 P.2d 180 (Ariz. 1993), is likewise distinguishable. In *Johnson*, the trial court declared a mistrial without any basis in the record. Here, the record demonstrates the prejudicial impact of the surprise note.

*Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28 (1978), does not advance the prosecution's argument. That case addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause attaches before a jury is empaneled. It does not address the circumstances under which a mistrial is justified.

---

## Conclusion

The trial court's declaration of mistrial was justified by manifest necessity. The surprise note directly contradicted the defense theory presented in opening statements, and the defense's own strategic decisions contributed to the circumstances. Dickinson failed to preserve the issue by moving for dismissal after the mistrial, and any error was harmless given the fair subsequent trial. The prosecution has not demonstrated a constitutional violation warranting dismissal with prejudice.

This Court should affirm Dickinson's convictions.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from Dickinson’s convictions following a second trial. The central issue is whether the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during the first trial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Specifically, we must determine whether "manifest necessity" existed to justify the mistrial, or whether the trial court abused its discretion by failing to exhaust less drastic alternatives. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-4033.1.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from being placed twice in jeopardy for the same offense. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). A trial court may declare a mistrial and discharge the jury before verdict only if there is a "manifest necessity" for doing so, or if the defendant consents. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971). The manifest necessity standard is a high one, requiring the trial judge to exercise "sound discretion" and to carefully consider alternatives to terminating the trial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506. The burden of demonstrating manifest necessity rests with the prosecution. *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 595, 633 P.2d 366, 372 (Ariz. 1981).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Manifest Necessity Did Not Exist**

The trial court declared a mistrial after the buyer of the mountain bike testified regarding a surprise note containing serial numbers that contradicted the defense’s theory of the case. While the admission of this evidence was undoubtedly prejudicial to the defense, the record demonstrates that the trial court failed to exhaust less drastic alternatives before terminating the proceedings.

In *State v. Johnson*, 174 Ariz. 383, 385, 850 P.2d 180, 182 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1993), this Court held that "a mistrial is improper when the trial court does not explore alternatives to termination of the trial." Here, the prosecution explicitly identified a viable alternative to mistrial. The prosecutor stated, "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence. It’s not the State’s first priority, because all facts should go to the jury. But, however, it’s an option for the Court."

This offer to proceed without the contested evidence was a clear, less drastic alternative that the trial court failed to adequately consider or adopt. The court did not rule on the admissibility of the note, nor did it attempt to give a curative instruction to the jury to mitigate any prejudice. By jumping immediately to the remedy of mistrial, the court ignored the mandate of *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485, which requires courts to exhaust "less drastic alternatives" before declaring a mistrial.

The defense relies on *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 633 P.2d 366, to argue that the surprise nature of the evidence destroyed the defense theory and necessitated a mistrial. However, *Schad* does not stand for the proposition that a mistrial is automatic whenever surprise evidence is introduced. In *Schad*, the court found manifest necessity after considering the specific circumstances, but here, the prosecution’s willingness to forgo the evidence distinguishes this case. If the State was prepared to continue the trial without the damaging note, the trial could have proceeded without the alleged prejudice. The trial court’s failure to utilize this option constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**B. Dickinson Did Not Waive His Double Jeopardy Claim**

The defense argues that Dickinson waived his double jeopardy claim by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial was declared. We are not persuaded.

In *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 541, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1997), this Court squarely held that "a defendant need not move to dismiss to preserve a double jeopardy claim when the trial court’s declaration of mistrial was without manifest necessity." Because we find that the trial court lacked manifest necessity to declare the mistrial, Dickinson’s failure to file a post-trial motion does not constitute waiver. The error is fundamental and unwaivable, as it strikes at the core of the constitutional protection against successive prosecutions. *Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28, 35 (1978).

The defense’s reliance on *State v. Patton*, 120 Ariz. 386, 586 P.2d 629 (Ariz. 1978), is misplaced. *Patton* involves circumstances where the defendant consents to or invites the mistrial. Here, Dickinson’s counsel expressed a preference to continue the trial and only indicated he would seek dismissal if the court *deemed* a mistrial necessary. The court acted sua sponte, over the defense's preference to proceed. Therefore, the principles of *Patton* do not apply.

**C. The Error Is Not Subject to Harmless Error Analysis**

Finally, the defense argues that any error was harmless because Dickinson received a fair trial six months later. This argument misapprehends the nature of the Double Jeopardy protection. The Double Jeopardy Clause is not merely a guarantee of a fair trial, but a protection against the harassment and burden of multiple prosecutions. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 607 (1976). When a mistrial is declared without manifest necessity, the remedy is dismissal with prejudice, not a review of the subsequent trial for fairness. *State v. Murray*, 136 Ariz. 132, 664 P.2d 642 (Ariz. 1983), does not compel a different result, as the violation of double jeopardy in this context requires the bar of retrial.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial without first exhausting the less drastic alternative of excluding the surprise evidence, an option expressly offered by the prosecution. Consequently, the retrial violated Dickinson’s protection against double jeopardy.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from Dickinson’s convictions following a second trial. The central issue is whether the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during the first trial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Specifically, we must determine whether "manifest necessity" existed to justify the mistrial, or whether the trial court abused its discretion by failing to exhaust less drastic alternatives. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-4033.1.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from being placed twice in jeopardy for the same offense. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). A trial court may declare a mistrial and discharge the jury before verdict only if there is a "manifest necessity" for doing so, or if the defendant consents. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971). The manifest necessity standard is a high one, requiring the trial judge to exercise "sound discretion" and to carefully consider alternatives to terminating the trial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506. The burden of demonstrating manifest necessity rests with the prosecution. *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 595, 633 P.2d 366, 372 (Ariz. 1981).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Manifest Necessity Did Not Exist**

The trial court declared a mistrial after the buyer of the mountain bike testified regarding a surprise note containing serial numbers that contradicted the defense’s theory of the case. While the admission of this evidence was undoubtedly prejudicial to the defense, the record demonstrates that the trial court failed to exhaust less drastic alternatives before terminating the proceedings.

In *State v. Johnson*, 174 Ariz. 383, 385, 850 P.2d 180, 182 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1993), this Court held that "a mistrial is improper when the trial court does not explore alternatives to termination of the trial." Here, the prosecution explicitly identified a viable alternative to mistrial. The prosecutor stated, "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence. It’s not the State’s first priority, because all facts should go to the jury. But, however, it’s an option for the Court."

This offer to proceed without the contested evidence was a clear, less drastic alternative that the trial court failed to adequately consider or adopt. The court did not rule on the admissibility of the note, nor did it attempt to give a curative instruction to the jury to mitigate any prejudice. By jumping immediately to the remedy of mistrial, the court ignored the mandate of *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485, which requires courts to exhaust "less drastic alternatives" before declaring a mistrial.

The defense relies on *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 633 P.2d 366, to argue that the surprise nature of the evidence destroyed the defense theory and necessitated a mistrial. However, *Schad* does not stand for the proposition that a mistrial is automatic whenever surprise evidence is introduced. In *Schad*, the court found manifest necessity after considering the specific circumstances, but here, the prosecution’s willingness to forgo the evidence distinguishes this case. If the State was prepared to continue the trial without the damaging note, the trial could have proceeded without the alleged prejudice. The trial court’s failure to utilize this option constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**B. Dickinson Did Not Waive His Double Jeopardy Claim**

The State argues that Dickinson waived his double jeopardy claim by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial was declared. We are not persuaded.

In *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 541, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1997), this Court squarely held that "a defendant need not move to dismiss to preserve a double jeopardy claim when the trial court’s declaration of mistrial was without manifest necessity." Because we find that the trial court lacked manifest necessity to declare the mistrial, Dickinson’s failure to file a post-trial motion does not constitute waiver. The error is fundamental and unwaivable, as it strikes at the core of the constitutional protection against successive prosecutions. *Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28, 35 (1978).

The State’s reliance on *State v. Patton*, 120 Ariz. 386, 586 P.2d 629 (Ariz. 1978), is misplaced. *Patton* involves circumstances where the defendant consents to or invites the mistrial. Here, Dickinson’s counsel expressed a preference to continue the trial and only indicated he would seek dismissal if the court *deemed* a mistrial necessary. The court acted sua sponte, over the defense's preference to proceed. Therefore, the principles of *Patton* do not apply.

**C. The Error Is Not Subject to Harmless Error Analysis**

Finally, the State argues that any error was harmless because Dickinson received a fair trial six months later. This argument misapprehends the nature of the Double Jeopardy protection. The Double Jeopardy Clause is not merely a guarantee of a fair trial, but a protection against the harassment and burden of multiple prosecutions. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 607 (1976). When a mistrial is declared without manifest necessity, the remedy is dismissal with prejudice, not a review of the subsequent trial for fairness. *State v. Murray*, 136 Ariz. 132, 664 P.2d 642 (Ariz. 1983), does not compel a different result, as the violation of double jeopardy in this context requires the bar of retrial.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial without first exhausting the less drastic alternative of excluding the surprise evidence, an option expressly offered by the prosecution. Consequently, the retrial violated Dickinson’s protection against double jeopardy.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT LACKED MANIFEST NECESSITY TO DECLARE A MISTRIAL

The prosecution's primary contention—that the surprise testimony about the serial-number note constituted manifest necessity justifying the mistrial—fails as a matter of law. The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that a trial court may declare a mistrial only upon a showing of "manifest necessity," a standard requiring the exercise of "sound discretion" and careful consideration of alternatives. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 506 (1978). The burden of demonstrating manifest necessity rests with the prosecution. *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 595, 633 P.2d 366, 372 (Ariz. 1981).

Here, the prosecution cannot carry this burden because they themselves provided the solution that would have eliminated any alleged prejudice. As the record unequivocally establishes, the prosecutor explicitly offered: "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." This was not a mere suggestion—it was a concrete, immediately implementable alternative that would have permitted the trial to continue without the contested evidence. The trial court's failure to adopt this option, or even to meaningfully consider it, constitutes an abuse of discretion under *State v. Johnson*, 174 Ariz. 383, 385, 850 P.2d 180, 182 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1993), which holds that "a mistrial is improper when the trial court does not explore alternatives to termination of the trial."

The prosecution's characterization of the evidence as "irreparably prejudicial" rings hollow when they simultaneously offered to proceed without that very evidence. One cannot claim irreparability while offering the cure. The prosecution cannot now on appeal claim manifest necessity when they explicitly told the trial court that proceeding without the note was viable. This Court correctly found that the trial court "jumped immediately to the remedy of mistrial" without fulfilling its constitutional duty to consider less drastic alternatives as required by *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971).

## II. DICKINSON DID NOT WAIVE HIS DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAIM

The prosecution's waiver argument misreads controlling precedent and would, if adopted, eviscerate the constitutional protection against successive prosecutions. They argue that Dickinson forfeited his double jeopardy claim by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial. However, *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 541, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1997), expressly holds that "a defendant need not move to dismiss to preserve a double jeopardy claim when the trial court's declaration of mistrial was without manifest necessity." Because this Court correctly found no manifest necessity, Dickinson's claim was preserved regardless of any post-trial motion.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Patton*, 120 Ariz. 386, 586 P.2d 629 (Ariz. 1978), is wholly inapposite. *Patton* addresses circumstances where the defendant consents to or actively invites the mistrial. Here, the record demonstrates the opposite: Dickinson's counsel explicitly stated he preferred to continue with the trial and would only seek dismissal "if the court deemed a mistrial necessary." The court acted sua sponte, over the defense's objection. Under these circumstances, *Patton* does not apply.

The prosecution's waiver theory would create a perverse incentive: any defendant whose trial court erroneously declares a mistrial would be required to anticipate the error and file a protective motion to preserve constitutional rights. This Court in *Mott* correctly rejected such an approach, recognizing that double jeopardy protections are fundamental and cannot be forfeited through procedural default when the trial court has acted without constitutional justification.

## III. THE CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATION IS NOT SUBJECT TO HARMLESS ERROR ANALYSIS

The prosecution's final argument—that any error was harmless because Dickinson received a "fair trial" six months later—demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Clause is not merely a guarantee of procedural fairness; it is a substantive protection against the state's power to subject a citizen to multiple prosecutions for the same offense. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 607 (1976).

The violation here occurred at the moment the trial court improperly declared the mistrial without manifest necessity. At that moment, Dickinson was subjected to the burden of a second prosecution—a burden the Constitution expressly prohibits. The subsequent fairness of the second trial is irrelevant to the constitutional injury. As this Court correctly noted, *State v. Murray*, 136 Ariz. 132, 664 P.2d 642 (Ariz. 1983), does not compel a different result, because when a mistrial lacks manifest necessity, the retrial itself violates double jeopardy regardless of the outcome.

The prosecution's harmless error argument asks this Court to transform the Double Jeopardy Clause from a bar against successive prosecutions into merely a procedural safeguard. This Court rejected that reasoning below, and we urge affirmance. The remedy for an unconstitutional retrial is dismissal with prejudice—not a review of whether the second trial was sufficiently "fair." *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. at 595, 633 P.2d at 372.

The prosecution's appeal attempts to relitigate settled questions of constitutional law. They cannot manufacture manifest necessity where they themselves offered the alternative that would have allowed the trial to proceed. The trial court abused its discretion by declaring a mistrial without first considering the prosecution's own offered alternative—exclusion of the surprise evidence. This error was fundamental, it was preserved, and it cannot be cured by a subsequent trial. The retrial violated Dickinson's core constitutional right against successive prosecutions.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from Dickinson’s convictions following a second trial. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during the first trial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Specifically, we must determine whether "manifest necessity" existed to justify the mistrial, or whether the trial court abused its discretion by failing to exhaust less drastic alternatives. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-4033.1.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from being placed twice in jeopardy for the same offense. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). A trial court may declare a mistrial and discharge the jury before verdict only if there is a "manifest necessity" for doing so, or if the defendant consents. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971). The manifest necessity standard is a high one, requiring the trial judge to exercise "sound discretion" and to carefully consider alternatives to terminating the trial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506. The burden of demonstrating manifest necessity rests with the prosecution. *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. 589, 595, 633 P.2d 366, 372 (Ariz. 1981).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Manifest Necessity Did Not Exist**

The trial court declared a mistrial after the buyer of the mountain bike testified regarding a surprise note containing serial numbers that contradicted the defense’s theory of the case. While the admission of this evidence was undoubtedly prejudicial to the defense, the record demonstrates that the trial court failed to exhaust less drastic alternatives before terminating the proceedings.

In *State v. Johnson*, 174 Ariz. 383, 385, 850 P.2d 180, 182 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1993), this Court held that "a mistrial is improper when the trial court does not explore alternatives to termination of the trial." Here, the prosecution explicitly identified a viable alternative to mistrial. The prosecutor stated, "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence. It’s not the State’s first priority, because all facts should go to the jury. But, however, it’s an option for the Court."

This offer to proceed without the contested evidence was a clear, less drastic alternative that the trial court failed to adequately consider or adopt. The court did not rule on the admissibility of the note, nor did it attempt to give a curative instruction to the jury to mitigate any prejudice. By jumping immediately to the remedy of mistrial, the court ignored the mandate of *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485, which requires courts to exhaust "less drastic alternatives" before declaring a mistrial.

The State argues that the surprise nature of the evidence destroyed the defense theory and necessitated a mistrial. However, the State cannot claim the evidence was "irreparably prejudicial" while simultaneously offering to proceed without that very evidence. If the State was prepared to continue the trial without the damaging note, the trial could have proceeded without the alleged prejudice. The trial court’s failure to utilize this option constitutes an abuse of discretion under *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506.

**B. Dickinson Did Not Waive His Double Jeopardy Claim**

The State argues that Dickinson waived his double jeopardy claim by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial was declared. We are not persuaded.

In *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 541, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1997), this Court squarely held that "a defendant need not move to dismiss to preserve a double jeopardy claim when the trial court’s declaration of mistrial was without manifest necessity." Because we find that the trial court lacked manifest necessity to declare the mistrial, Dickinson’s failure to file a post-trial motion does not constitute waiver. The error is fundamental and unwaivable, as it strikes at the core of the constitutional protection against successive prosecutions. *Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28, 35 (1978).

The State’s reliance on *State v. Patton*, 120 Ariz. 386, 586 P.2d 629 (Ariz. 1978), is misplaced. *Patton* involves circumstances where the defendant consents to or invites the mistrial. Here, Dickinson’s counsel expressed a preference to continue the trial and only indicated he would seek dismissal if the court *deemed* a mistrial necessary. The court acted sua sponte, over the defense's preference to proceed. Therefore, the principles of *Patton* do not apply.

**C. The Error Is Not Subject to Harmless Error Analysis**

Finally, the State argues that any error was harmless because Dickinson received a fair trial six months later. This argument misapprehends the nature of the Double Jeopardy protection. The Double Jeopardy Clause is not merely a guarantee of a fair trial, but a protection against the harassment and burden of multiple prosecutions. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 607 (1976). When a mistrial is declared without manifest necessity, the remedy is dismissal with prejudice, not a review of the subsequent trial for fairness. *State v. Schad*, 129 Ariz. at 595, 633 P.2d at 372, confirms this: when a mistrial lacks manifest necessity, the constitutional error cannot be cured by a later trial.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial without first exhausting the less drastic alternative of excluding the surprise evidence, an option expressly offered by the prosecution. Consequently, the retrial violated Dickinson’s protection against double jeopardy.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.
