# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF THE STATE OF HAWAII

### STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee

---

**Defense Response to Plaintiff's Opening Argument**

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence and that this Court should affirm that ruling. The search conducted by Officer Davis and accompanying officers violated the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the warrantless search of Mr. Haili's vehicle falls within any recognized exception to the warrant requirement. The Court should affirm the suppression order.

## I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution contends that the officers' actions were lawful, but the defense submits that the initial stop of Mr. Haili's vehicle was not supported by the requisite level of suspicion under the Fourth Amendment.

Officer Davis stopped the vehicle based solely upon the following information: (1) gunshots had been heard in the area; (2) the shots appeared to come from the unfinished H-3 freeway; and (3) a car was traveling on the unfinished freeway with its headlights on at approximately 10:30 p.m. The officer had no description of the vehicle, no information identifying the occupants, and no observation of any criminal activity directly connected to the vehicle at the time of the stop.

As the Supreme Court held in **Terry v. Ohio**, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a temporary investigative detention must be supported by "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." The prosecution has not established that Officer Davis had such specific and articulable facts. The mere presence of a vehicle on an unfinished freeway at night, without more, does not create a reasonable suspicion that the occupants were engaged in criminal activity. See **State v. Barnes**, 58 Haw. 412, 570 P.2d 575 (1977) (holding that mere presence in an area associated with criminal activity is insufficient to justify an investigative stop).

## II. THE SEARCH EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF A LAWFUL PAT-DOWN

Assuming *arguendo* that the initial stop was valid, the defense submits that the subsequent search of Mr. Haili's vehicle exceeded the scope of any lawful authority.

The prosecution argues that the officers lawfully scanned the vehicle's interior with a flashlight. However, once the four occupants were removed from the vehicle and subjected to a pat-down search—presumably for weapons—the officers had no lawful basis to conduct a warrantless search of the vehicle's interior.

The defense acknowledges that under **Michigan v. Long**, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), officers may search the passenger compartment of a vehicle if they have reasonable suspicion that the vehicle contains weapons. However, the prosecution has not demonstrated such reasonable suspicion here. The officers had already removed all occupants from the vehicle and had presumably completed any weapons pat-down. At that point, there was no ongoing threat justifying a search of the vehicle's interior.

Furthermore, the officers' actions went far beyond a cursory scan. They physically moved the front seat to access the rear floor area, opened a ukulele case, and examined its contents. This was not a minimal intrusion but rather an extensive warrantless search requiring probable cause under the automobile exception.

## III. PROBABLE CAUSE DID NOT EXIST FOR THE VEHICLE SEARCH

The prosecution contends that probable cause existed to search the vehicle. The defense submits this argument fails for several reasons.

First, the mere fact that gunshots were heard in the vicinity does not establish probable cause to believe that the specific vehicle stopped by Officer Davis was connected to the shooting. As the Court held in **Henry v. United States**, 361 U.S. 98 (1959), probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge that are sufficient to warrant a prudent man in believing that an offense has been committed."

Second, the discovery of a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor—visible only after the officers had already improperly scanned the interior—cannot retroactively justify the search. The exclusionary rule prohibits using "fruit of the poisonous tree" to validate an unlawful search. See **Wong Sun v. United States**, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

Third, the prosecution conflates the standard for a Terry frisk with the higher probable cause requirement for a full vehicle search. Even assuming the officers had reason to believe weapons were in the vehicle, the scope of their search exceeded what was necessary for officer safety. See **Ybarra v. Illinois**, 444 U.S. 85 (1979) (holding that a frisk of a person does not automatically justify searching the area beyond that person's immediate control).

## IV. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The prosecution appears to invoke the automobile exception to the warrant requirement, but this exception does not validate the search in this case.

Under the automobile exception, as articulated in **Carroll v. United States**, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), and refined in **Pennsylvania v. Labron**, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), a vehicle may be searched without a warrant if there is probable cause to believe it contains evidence of a crime or contraband. However, probable cause must exist *before* the search is conducted, not as a result of the search.

Here, when Officer Davis first observed the vehicle and decided to stop it, he had no information indicating that the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. The vehicle was simply traveling on the unfinished freeway with its headlights on—a completely innocent activity. The prosecution has not established that probable cause existed at the time of the initial stop or at the time the officers began their interior search.

Moreover, even if probable cause had existed, the officers were not confronted with exigent circumstances requiring immediate action. The vehicle was secured, the occupants were detained, and there was no risk of evidence being destroyed. The officers could have readily obtained a search warrant. See **Coolidge v. New Hampshire**, 403 U.S. 443 (1971) (holding that the automobile exception requires both probable cause and exigent circumstances).

## V. THE PROTECTION AGAINST UNREASONABLE SEARCHES UNDER HAWAII CONSTITUTION

The defense further submits that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides protection at least as broad as the Fourth Amendment, and this Court has historically interpreted our state constitution to provide greater protections than federal minimum standards. See **State v. Hoang**, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987); **State v. Kaluna**, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974).

Even under federal standards, the search in this case was unconstitutional. Under the Hawaii Constitution, the analysis yields the same result.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The search of Mr. Haili's vehicle was conducted without a warrant, without valid consent, and without satisfaction of the automobile exception requirements. The evidence obtained as a result of this unconstitutional search must be excluded, and the suppression order should be upheld.

Respectfully submitted,

**REBUTTAL BY PROSECUTION COUNSEL**  

Your Honor, the defense’s opening statement mischaracterizes the facts, misapplies controlling precedent, and fails to acknowledge that Officer Davis’s actions were fully consistent with the Fourth Amendment. The warrantless scanning of the vehicle’s interior with a flashlight was constitutionally permissible as it constituted a lawful observation under the plain view doctrine, and the totality of circumstances known to the officers at the time established probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. The circuit court’s suppression order was erroneous and must be reversed.  

### I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION  
The defense wrongly asserts that the stop lacked reasonable suspicion under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). However, the security guard’s report—that 6–7 gunshots were heard and a bullet struck a building adjacent to the Kapaa Quarry, with the shots originating from the unfinished H-3 freeway—provided specific, articulable facts directly linking the location to criminal activity. Officer Davis observed a vehicle traveling on that *unfinished* freeway at 10:30 p.m., a location that is not a public thoroughfare and is inherently suspicious for vehicular traffic. As this Court held in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981), reasonable suspicion requires only a "particularized and objective basis" for suspecting criminal activity, and the totality of circumstances—including the timing, location, and nature of the reported crime—supports such a finding. The defense’s reliance on *State v. Barnes*, 58 Haw. 412, 570 P.2d 575 (1977), is inapposite, as *Barnes* involved a vehicle stopped in a public area without any direct connection to a recent crime report. Here, the car was found *exactly where the gunshots originated*, creating an immediate and specific nexus between the vehicle and the crime. This is indistinguishable from *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000), where unprovoked presence in a high-crime area, coupled with flight, supported reasonable suspicion. The unfinished freeway is not merely "associated with criminal activity"—it is the precise location of an ongoing criminal incident.  

### II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN WAS NOT A SEARCH UNDER THE FOURTH AMENDMENT  
The defense erroneously characterizes the flashlight scan as a "warrantless search" requiring probable cause. However, *Texas v. Brown*, 445 U.S. 404 (1981), squarely holds that "illuminating the interior of a vehicle with a flashlight does not constitute a search" when the officer is lawfully present and merely observing what is already visible. In *Texas v. Brown*, officers shone a flashlight into a car during a lawful stop and observed a plastic bag containing heroin in plain view. The Supreme Court emphasized that "the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not violate the Fourth Amendment" because it merely enhances an officer’s natural senses to see what is otherwise exposed to public view. Here, Officer Davis was lawfully positioned at the chain-link fence—60 feet from the vehicle—after a valid stop. His flashlight scan merely revealed the .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor, which was in plain view once illuminated. This is no different from *California v. Ciraolo*, 476 U.S. 207 (1986), where aerial surveillance of a backyard from a public airspace did not constitute a search because the officer observed what was visible to the naked eye. The flashlight did not "intrude" on any privacy interest; it simply made observable evidence visible.  

### III. PROBABLE CAUSE EXISTED BEFORE THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN  
The defense incorrectly claims that probable cause was lacking at the time of the search. Probable cause exists when "the facts and circumstances within the officer’s knowledge are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). Here, the officers knew: (1) gunshots had just been fired from the unfinished H-3 freeway; (2) a bullet struck a building adjacent to the quarry; (3) a vehicle was traveling on that *unfinished* freeway at the exact time and location of the shooting; and (4) the unfinished freeway is not a public roadway where ordinary traffic would operate. This is not a case of "mere presence" in a general area—it is a vehicle observed *at the precise scene of an ongoing violent crime*. As the Court stated in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), probable cause for a vehicle search arises when "there is probable cause to believe that the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime." The totality of circumstances here—gunshots reported from the freeway, a car present on that freeway during the incident—creates a strong inference that the vehicle contains evidence of the shooting. The discovery of the rifle casing during the flashlight scan merely corroborated this probable cause, but it was not the *source* of it. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), does not apply because the flashlight scan was not an unlawful search—it was a lawful observation—and thus the rifle casing was not "fruit of the poisonous tree."  

### IV. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION APPLIES  
The defense argues that the automobile exception under *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), is inapplicable due to a lack of exigency. This is incorrect. The automobile exception requires both probable cause and the inherent mobility of the vehicle. *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), confirms that "if a car is readily mobile and probable cause exists to believe it contains contraband, the Fourth Amendment does not require a warrant." Here, the vehicle was on a freeway—by definition, a mobile environment—and the officers had probable cause to believe it contained evidence of the recent shooting. The fact that the car was temporarily stopped does not negate the exigency; as the Supreme Court held in *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), "the ready mobility of the vehicle itself is sufficient to satisfy the exigency requirement." *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971), is inapplicable because it involved a vehicle seized at a fixed location with no immediate risk of removal, whereas here the vehicle was actively being operated on a public roadway and could have been driven away at any moment.  

### V. HAWAII CONSTITUTIONAL PROTECTIONS DO NOT ALTER THE ANALYSIS  
The defense’s reliance on *State v. Hoang*, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987), is misplaced. While Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution may sometimes provide broader protections, this Court has consistently held that it does not exceed federal standards where the federal analysis is clear. *State v. Kaluna*, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974), confirms that Hawaii’s constitutional protections are "coextensive" with the Fourth Amendment in cases involving vehicle searches. Here, the federal analysis is dispositive: the flashlight scan was not a search, and probable cause existed under *Illinois v. Gates* and *Carroll*. There is no basis to apply a stricter state standard.  

### CONCLUSION  
The defense’s arguments rest on a fundamental misreading of the facts and law. Officer Davis’s flashlight scan was a lawful observation under *Texas v. Brown*, and the totality of circumstances established probable cause for the vehicle search under *Illinois v. Gates* and *Carroll*. The circuit court erred in suppressing the evidence, and this Court should reverse its order.  

Respectfully submitted,

The prosecution's rebuttal fundamentally misapprehends the controlling constitutional principles and distorts the factual record. ### II. THE INITIAL STOP STILL LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution's attempt to establish reasonable suspicion fails because it conflates general suspicion about a location with particularized suspicion regarding a specific vehicle. While the prosecution correctly notes that *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), requires only a "particularized and objective basis" for suspecting criminal activity, the prosecution conflates this standard by arguing that the vehicle's presence at the scene of reported gunshots automatically satisfies this requirement. This is precisely the type of generalized suspicion that *Terry* rejected.

The security guard provided no description of any vehicle or occupants. The guard reported hearing gunshots emanating from the unfinished H-3 freeway area, but this generic information did not identify any particular vehicle. The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981), is misplaced because that case involved suspects who had crossed an international border and were observed behaving suspiciously in a specific manner. Here, by contrast, Officer Davis observed a single vehicle traveling at a "reasonable speed" with "headlights on"—behavior entirely consistent with lawful operation of a vehicle. The prosecution cannot transform an anonymous tip about gunshots into reasonable suspicion to stop every vehicle that happens to be in the vicinity.

The prosecution's comparison to *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000), is inapposite. In *Wardlow*, the defendant was in a high-crime area and fled upon seeing police, conduct that the Court specifically characterized as "an affirmative indication of wrongdoing." Here, there was no flight, no evasive behavior, and no description linking the vehicle to criminal activity. The vehicle was simply traveling on the unfinished freeway—a location the prosecution characterizes as suspicious simply because gunshots were reported nearby. This amounts to guilt by association, which the Fourth Amendment does not permit.

The prosecution's argument that the vehicle was found "exactly where the gunshots originated" is an overstatement of the facts. The security guard reported hearing shots "coming from the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway"—a general area encompassing the entire unfinished section. Officer Davis positioned himself at the chain-link fence barricading the uncompleted end of the freeway and observed the vehicle approaching from a distance. This is not the equivalent of observing a vehicle in the act of discharging a firearm.

The defense maintains that *State v. Barnes*, 58 Haw. 412, 570 P.2d 575 (1977), is controlling. In *Barnes*, the Hawaii Supreme Court held that a stop was unlawful where the officer lacked specific facts connecting the defendant to criminal activity. Here, as in *Barnes*, the stop was based on nothing more than the defendant's presence in an area associated with criminal activity, without any particularized suspicion. This Court should hold that the initial stop was unconstitutional.

### III. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF A LAWFUL FRISK

The prosecution's reliance on *Texas v. Brown*, 445 U.S. 404 (1981), is fundamentally misplaced and does not govern the facts of this case. In *Texas v. Brown*, the officers lawfully stopped the defendant's vehicle for a traffic violation and, while standing outside the vehicle, used a flashlight to observe the interior. The Court held that the flashlight illumination did not transform the observation into a search because the officers were lawfully positioned and the interior was exposed to their view.

The critical distinction this Court must recognize is that in *Texas v. Brown*, the flashlight scan occurred as part of a lawful traffic stop and was contemporaneous with the officer's lawful presence. Here, by contrast, the officers had already completed the following actions before scanning the interior: (1) ordered all four occupants out of the vehicle; (2) conducted pat-down searches of all four occupants; (3) seated the occupants on the ground under guard; and (4) positioned themselves to scan the vehicle's interior with a flashlight. The officers' scan of the interior occurred only after the occupants had been secured and the justification for a *Terry* frisk had expired.

Under *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), an officer may search a vehicle's passenger compartment without a warrant only if the officer possesses reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. Once the four occupants were removed from the vehicle, patted down, and seated on the ground under guard, the immediate threat to officer safety was neutralized. At that point, there was no remaining justification for searching the vehicle's interior. The prosecution fails to address this critical sequence of events and cites no authority for the proposition that officers may conduct a warrantless search of a vehicle's interior after suspects have been secured and the basis for a *Terry* frisk has dissipated.

The prosecution's argument that the flashlight "merely revealed" what was visible is unavailing. The officers did not simply observe the interior from a lawful vantage point while the suspects were still in the vehicle. Rather, they deliberately scanned the interior with a flashlight after the occupants had been removed and secured. This was not a "general observation" but an investigative search conducted after the lawful justification for the initial encounter had ended.

Furthermore, the prosecution's reliance on *California v. Ciraolo*, 476 U.S. 207 (1986), is distinguishable. In *Ciraolo*, the Court held that aerial surveillance of a backyard did not constitute a search because the defendant had no reasonable expectation of privacy in what was visible from public airspace. Here, by contrast, the defendant had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the interior of his vehicle. The prosecution has not argued that the interior was visible from a public vantage point without the use of artificial illumination. The flashlight was used to peer into the vehicle's interior, not to observe what was already exposed to public view.

### IV. PROBABLE CAUSE DID NOT EXIST BEFORE THE SEARCH

The prosecution's argument that probable cause existed before the flashlight scan is circular and unsupported by the facts. Probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge [that] are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). The prosecution's argument rests on the following purported facts: (1) gunshots were reported from the unfinished freeway; (2) a vehicle was traveling on that freeway at 10:30 p.m.; and (3) the freeway is not a public roadway. These facts, taken together, are insufficient to establish probable cause.

The prosecution argues that "a vehicle present on that freeway during the incident" creates "a strong inference that the vehicle contains evidence of the shooting." This argument is precisely the type of generalized suspicion that probable cause does not permit. The mere presence of a vehicle in the vicinity of a crime, without more, does not establish probable cause. The prosecution has not identified any specific evidence linking the defendant's vehicle to the shooting—not a description of the vehicle, not testimony that shots were fired from the vehicle, not shell casings found outside the vehicle, nothing.

The prosecution's reliance on *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), is premature because the automobile exception requires probable cause to exist before the search. The prosecution cannot rely on evidence discovered during an unlawful search to establish the probable cause that would have justified that search. This is the very essence of the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine.

The prosecution argues that *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), does not apply because "the flashlight scan was not an unlawful search." This argument is circular and assumes the very question in dispute. Whether the flashlight scan was a search is the central constitutional question. The defense has demonstrated that it was, and the prosecution has not otherwise established that the scan was lawful. The rifle casing was discovered during an unlawful search and must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree.

---

### V. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The prosecution misapplies the automobile exception by arguing that the vehicle's inherent mobility satisfies the exigency requirement. The automobile exception, as articulated in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), and refined in *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), requires both probable cause and the vehicle's ready mobility. However, the exception also requires that the search be conducted in a manner consistent with the vehicle's mobility—i.e., before the vehicle is secured and its occupants are detained.

In *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), the Court held that the automobile exception applied where the vehicle was in a public parking lot and the officers had probable cause to believe it contained contraband. The Court emphasized that "the ready mobility of the vehicle itself" created the exigency justifying a warrantless search. However, the Court also noted that the vehicle was "parked in a public place" and that "the opportunity to search is fleeting."

Here, the vehicle was not in a state of readiness to be driven away. The four occupants had been removed, patted down, and seated on the ground under guard. The vehicle was stationary, the engine was not running, and the occupants—who presumably had the keys—were secured. There was no risk that the vehicle would be driven away or that evidence would be destroyed. The prosecution's argument that "the vehicle was on a freeway" is unavailing because the vehicle was not in motion at the time of the search; it had been stopped and its occupants had been removed.

The prosecution's reliance on *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971), is instructive. In *Coolidge*, the Court held that the automobile exception did not apply where the vehicle was seized at the defendant's home and there was no exigency requiring immediate action. The Court emphasized that "the automobile exception has no application in this case because the police had ample time to obtain a warrant." Here, similarly, the officers had ample time to obtain a warrant. The vehicle was stationary, the occupants were detained, and there was no immediate risk of evidence being destroyed.

The prosecution's argument that the vehicle "could have been driven away at any moment" is speculative and unsupported by the facts. The occupants had been removed and secured. There is no evidence that the keys were in the ignition or that anyone had access to the vehicle. The prosecution cannot manufacture exigency by speculating about what might have happened.

### VI. HAWAII CONSTITUTIONAL PROTECTIONS SUPPORT SUPPRESSION

The prosecution incorrectly argues that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides no greater protection than the Fourth Amendment. This argument is contrary to the Hawaii Supreme Court's decision in *State v. Hoang*, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987), which recognized that the Hawaii Constitution may provide broader protections than the federal Constitution.

In *Hoang*, the Hawaii Supreme Court held that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution "protects the privacy of the individual against arbitrary governmental intrusion" and that this protection is "independent" of the Fourth Amendment. The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Kaluna*, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974), is misplaced because that case was decided before *Hoang* and does not reflect the current state of Hawaii constitutional law.

Even under federal standards, the evidence must be suppressed. However, to the extent this Court finds the federal analysis to be in doubt, the defense respectfully requests that the Court invoke the independent protections of the Hawaii Constitution to suppress the evidence.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The prosecution has failed to establish that the initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion, that the flashlight scan was a lawful observation under the plain view doctrine, that probable cause existed before the search, or that the automobile exception applies. The search violated the Fourth Amendment and Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution, and the evidence obtained as a result must be suppressed.

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee**

**NO. ** (Appellate Case Number)

**Pursuant to the review of the rebuttal arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, this Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires the Court to determine whether the circuit court erred in granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence obtained during a warrantless search of his vehicle. The State of Hawaii appeals the suppression order. After reviewing the rebuttal arguments, the Court finds that the prosecution has successfully demonstrated that the search fell within recognized exceptions to the warrant requirement.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Stop Was Supported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The defense argues that the initial stop lacked reasonable suspicion because the security guard provided no description of the vehicle or occupants, relying on *State v. Barnes*, 58 Haw. 412, 570 P.2d 575 (1977). However, the prosecution correctly distinguishes *Barnes* by highlighting the specific factual context of the "unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway." Unlike a general public area where a vehicle's presence might be innocuous, an unfinished freeway is a location closed to public traffic.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may stop a vehicle if they have "specific and articulable facts" that suggest criminal activity. Here, Officer Davis received a report of gunshots originating from a specific, limited area—the unfinished freeway. Within minutes, he observed a vehicle traveling in that exact location. As held in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981), reasonable suspicion is based on the "totality of the circumstances" and allows for logical inferences. The inference that a vehicle found on a closed, unfinished freeway immediately after a report of gunfire from that same location is connected to the gunfire is specific and objective. The defense's reliance on *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000), is inapposite because the suspicion here arises from the specific correlation of time and place, not merely presence in a high-crime area. Therefore, the initial stop was lawful.

**B. The Flashlight Scan Was Not a Search**

The defense contends that the flashlight scan constituted a search because it occurred after the occupants were secured and the *Terry* justification had expired. The defense relies on *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), arguing that the search for weapons was no longer justified.

However, the prosecution correctly identifies that the flashlight scan was not a "search" within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment, but rather a lawful observation. Under *Texas v. Brown*, 445 U.S. 404 (1981), the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search if the officer is lawfully present. Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene of a valid *Terry* stop. The fact that the occupants had been secured and seated on the ground does not transform a visual observation of the vehicle's interior from a lawful vantage point into a Fourth Amendment search. The defense's argument that the scan was an "investigative search" conflates the *Long* standard (which permits a search for weapons) with the *Brown* standard (which governs plain view observations). The officers were entitled to look into the vehicle; the use of a flashlight merely facilitated this observation.

**C. Probable Cause Existed Prior to the Search**

The defense argues that probable cause was lacking and that the discovery of the rifle casing was "fruit of the poisonous tree." The Court disagrees.

Probable cause exists when the facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). At the moment Officer Davis observed the vehicle, he knew: (1) gunshots had been fired from the unfinished freeway; (2) the vehicle was present on that specific unfinished freeway; and (3) the freeway was not a public thoroughfare where ordinary traffic would be expected. This combination of facts creates a fair probability that the vehicle contained evidence of the shooting. The discovery of the .22-caliber casing during the flashlight scan corroborated this existing probable cause but was not the sole source of it. Therefore, the doctrine of *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), does not require suppression, as the initial observation was lawful and probable cause predated the discovery of the casing.

**D. The Automobile Exception Applies**

The defense argues that the automobile exception articulated in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), does not apply because the vehicle was stationary and the occupants were secured, citing *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971). The Court is not persuaded.

The automobile exception is based on the inherent mobility of vehicles and the diminished expectation of privacy in automobiles. *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), holds that "if a car is readily mobile and probable cause exists to believe it contains contraband, the Fourth Amendment does not require a warrant." The vehicle was located on a freeway, a mobile environment, and probable cause existed. The fact that the vehicle was temporarily stopped and the occupants detained does not eliminate its inherent mobility or the exigency justifying a warrantless search. *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), confirms that the ready mobility of a vehicle is sufficient to satisfy the exigency requirement, even if the vehicle is parked at the time of the search. *Coolidge* is distinguishable because it involved a vehicle seized at a private residence, not a vehicle on a public highway actively connected to a recent crime.

**E. State Constitutional Protections**

The defense argues that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides broader protection than the Fourth Amendment, citing *State v. Hoang*, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987). However, as noted by the prosecution, this Court has held that Hawaii's protections against unreasonable search and seizure are generally coextensive with the Fourth Amendment in vehicle cases. *State v. Kaluna*, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974). Since the search was valid under federal standards, it is likewise valid under the Hawaii Constitution.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has successfully rebutted the defense's arguments. The initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion based on the specific location of the vehicle relative to the reported crime. The flashlight scan was a lawful observation under *Texas v. Brown*, not a search requiring independent justification. Probable cause existed prior to the search based on the totality of the circumstances, and the automobile exception justified the warrantless search of the vehicle. The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress evidence is **REVERSED**.

The flashlight merely revealed what was otherwise visible—no intrusion occurred. The defense's reliance on *California v. Ciraolo*, 476 U.S. 207 (1986), is irrelevant because that case addressed aerial surveillance; here, the officers observed the interior directly from ground level, where no reasonable expectation of privacy exists for a vehicle on a public highway. The scan was not a search requiring independent justification.

Third, probable cause existed before the flashlight scan. Probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge [that] are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that a crime was being committed," as defined in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). Officer Davis knew: (1) gunshots had been fired from the unfinished H-3 freeway; (2) the freeway was a closed, non-public thoroughfare; and (3) a vehicle was traveling on that exact location at the time of the incident. This combination creates a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained evidence of the shooting. The discovery of the rifle casing corroborated but did not create probable cause; it was merely the product of a lawful observation. The defense's claim of "circular reasoning" is baseless because probable cause arose from the location-specific facts alone—no evidence from the scan was needed to establish it. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), does not apply because the flashlight scan was not an unlawful search.

Fourth, the automobile exception justifies the warrantless search. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), established that the inherent mobility of vehicles creates exigency, permitting warrantless searches when probable cause exists. *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), confirms that "if a car is readily mobile and probable cause exists to believe it contains contraband, the Fourth Amendment does not require a warrant." The vehicle was on a public highway—a mobile environment—and the officers had probable cause. *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), explicitly holds that the "ready mobility" of a vehicle satisfies exigency even when temporarily parked, as long as it is on a public roadway. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971), is inapplicable because it involved a vehicle seized at a private residence; here, the vehicle was on an active public highway during an ongoing crime. The defense's speculation about "no risk of evidence destruction" ignores the Supreme Court's recognition that the automobile exception applies to "the fleeting opportunity to search" when a vehicle is in a public space. The search was lawful.

Finally, Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides no greater protection than the Fourth Amendment in this context. *State v. Kaluna*, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974), holds that Hawaii's constitutional protections for vehicle searches are coextensive with federal standards. While *State v. Hoang*, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987), recognizes independent state constitutional principles, it does not alter this precedent for automobile searches. The search complied with federal law and thus with Hawaii law.

In conclusion, the defense's arguments rest on misreadings of precedent and factual distortions. The stop was justified by the totality of circumstances; the flashlight scan was a lawful observation under *Texas v. Brown*; probable cause existed before the search; and the automobile exception applied. The evidence was lawfully obtained. The suppression order must be reversed to uphold constitutional law and public safety.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## STATE OF HAWAII v. DAVID A. HAILI

---

**Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and in support of the circuit court's original order granting the motion to suppress evidence.** The Defense maintains that the warrantless search of Mr. Haili's vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution. The prosecution's arguments, while artfully constructed, fail to overcome the fundamental constitutional deficiencies in the search of the defendant's vehicle.

---

## I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution asks this Court to find reasonable suspicion based solely on the fact that a vehicle was present on an unfinished freeway shortly after gunshots were reported. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the defense acknowledges the holding in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), which permits brief investigative stops based on specific and articulable facts indicating criminal activity. However, as this Court recognized in *State v. Barnes*, 58 Haw. 412, 570 P.2d 575 (1977), mere presence in a general area where criminal activity has been reported is insufficient to justify a stop. The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Barnes* is unavailing. In *Barnes*, this Court held that a vehicle's presence in a parking lot area where shots had been fired did not justify a stop without additional identifying information. The same principle applies here—the unfinished H-3 freeway, while not open to public traffic, is a location where vehicles engaged in legitimate construction or survey work might reasonably be found. The security guard provided no description of the vehicle, no description of occupants, and no observation of suspicious activity beyond hearing shots.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981), is misplaced. In *Cortez*, the Court emphasized that reasonable suspicion must be based on more than mere hunches and requires "a particularized suspicion." The prosecution argues that the correlation between the location of the vehicle and the reported gunshots creates such a suspicion. However, the defense submits that this conflates mere coincidence with particularized suspicion. Officer Davis had no information connecting the specific vehicle to the shooting—he arrived at the scene within minutes and observed a single car traveling at reasonable speed with headlights on. This is precisely the type of generic suspicion that *Terry* and its progeny prohibit.

Third, the prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000), actually underscores the defense's position. In *Wardlow*, the Court upheld the stop because the defendant was in a high-crime area and engaged in nervous flight. Here, there was no flight, no suspicious behavior, and no particularized description. The defendant was simply driving on a roadway—albeit an unfinished one—at the time of the incident. The prosecution asks the Court to infer guilt from mere presence in a location, but this is precisely the "guilt by association" that constitutional protections forbid.

The Defense submits that the initial stop of the defendant's vehicle was not supported by reasonable suspicion and therefore violated the Fourth Amendment.

---

## II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN CONSTITUTED AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL SEARCH

The prosecution argues that the flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior was not a "search" but merely a lawful observation under *Texas v. Brown*, 445 U.S. 404 (1981). The Defense respectfully disagrees.

In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court held that the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search when the officer is lawfully present in a place where he has a right to be. However, critical to the Court's analysis in *Brown* was the fact that the officer was lawfully positioned to observe the vehicle's interior as part of a lawful traffic stop. Here, the situation is materially different.

First, as argued above, the initial stop was unlawful for lack of reasonable suspicion. An unlawful stop cannot serve as the foundation for any subsequent observation. Under the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine established in *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), evidence obtained as a result of an illegal search must be suppressed. If the initial stop was unlawful, everything flowing from it—including the flashlight scan—must likewise be suppressed.

Second, even assuming arguendo that the initial stop was lawful, the prosecution's characterization of the flashlight scan as a mere "observation" ignores the practical realities of the situation. After the occupants were ordered out of the car and seated on the ground, Officer Davis and the other officers actively scanned the interior of the vehicle with a flashlight. This was not a casual glance from a lawful vantage point—it was an affirmative investigative act. The defense submits that this constitutes a search under *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), which requires independent justification beyond the initial *Terry* stop.

The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Long* fails. In *Long*, the Court held that a search of a vehicle's passenger compartment is justified if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the vehicle contains weapons. Here, there was no such reasonable suspicion at the time of the scan. The officers had secured the four occupants, seated them on the ground, and had one officer guarding them. There was no ongoing threat justifying a weapons search. The prosecution cannot bootstrap the automobile exception—which applies to evidence of crime—onto a *Terry* stop—which requires articulable facts of danger.

The Defense submits that the flashlight scan was an unconstitutional search requiring suppression of all evidence obtained as a result.

---

## III. PROBABLE CAUSE DID NOT EXIST PRIOR TO THE SEARCH

The prosecution argues that probable cause existed before the flashlight scan based on the totality of circumstances. The Defense submits this argument fails for the fundamental reason that the prosecution cannot establish probable cause from facts that were only obtained through the unlawful search.

The prosecution lists the following facts as establishing probable cause: (1) gunshots had been fired from the unfinished freeway; (2) the vehicle was present on that specific unfinished freeway; and (3) the freeway was not a public thoroughfare. The defense submits that these facts, taken individually or collectively, are insufficient to establish probable cause.

Probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge [that] are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). The prosecution's facts establish nothing more than that the defendant's vehicle was present in a location where gunshots had been reported. This is insufficient for probable cause. As the Court held in *Barnes*, "mere presence in an area where criminal activity has occurred is not enough to create probable cause." 58 Haw. at 415, 570 P.2d at 578.

The prosecution's argument that probable cause existed "prior to the search" is fundamentally circular. The prosecution claims that Officer Davis knew the vehicle was on the unfinished freeway—but this knowledge only came from the stop itself. The prosecution claims the vehicle was connected to the gunfire—but this connection was assumed, not established. The prosecution cannot create probable cause out of speculation and then use that speculative probable cause to justify the search.

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States* is instructive, but in the defense's favor. In *Wong Sun*, the Court held that evidence obtained through an unlawful search must be suppressed unless the prosecution can demonstrate that it would have been discovered through independent lawful means. Here, the prosecution cannot make such a demonstration. The rifle casing, ukulele case, modified rifle, and ammunition were all discovered as a direct result of the unlawful flashlight scan. Without that scan, the officers would have had no probable cause to search the vehicle.

The Defense submits that no probable cause existed prior to the search, and all evidence obtained must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree.

---

## IV. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The prosecution argues that the automobile exception justifies the warrantless search. The Defense respectfully submits that this argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the automobile exception requires both probable cause and exigent circumstances. As the Court held in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), the exception applies because of the inherent mobility of vehicles and the possibility that evidence could be removed or destroyed. However, in *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971), the Court emphasized that the automobile exception does not apply when there is no exigency. Here, when the officers searched the vehicle, the four occupants were secured on the ground, guarded by an officer. The vehicle was stationary. There was no risk of the vehicle being driven away, and there was no risk of evidence destruction because the occupants had no opportunity to remove or conceal anything. The prosecution's argument that "inherent mobility" alone suffices ignores the exigency requirement that *Coolidge* and subsequent cases have repeatedly emphasized.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), and *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), is misplaced. In both cases, the vehicles were either mobile or readily mobile at the time of the search. Here, the vehicle was not merely parked—it was stopped in the middle of an unfinished freeway, barricaded from public access, with its occupants secured on the ground. This is not a situation of a vehicle that could be quickly driven away. The prosecution's characterization of the freeway as a "mobile environment" is a legal fiction that finds no support in the case law.

Third, the prosecution's argument that the vehicle was "actively connected to a recent crime" is conclusory. The connection between the vehicle and the shooting was assumed, not established. The prosecution has presented no evidence that the defendant or the vehicle was involved in the shooting. The mere presence of a vehicle on an unfinished freeway after gunshots were heard is insufficient to establish an "active connection" to any crime.

The Defense submits that the automobile exception does not apply because no probable cause existed and no exigent circumstances were present.

---

## V. HAWAII CONSTITUTION PROVIDES INDEPENDENT PROTECTION

Finally, the prosecution argues that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides no greater protection than the Fourth Amendment. The Defense submits that this argument ignores this Court's precedent establishing independent state constitutional protections.

In *State v. Hoang*, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987), this Court held that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides independent protection against unreasonable searches and seizures. The prosecution is correct that *State v. Kaluna*, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974), held that Hawaii's protections are generally coextensive with federal protections in vehicle cases. However, *Hoang* establishes that this Court retains the authority to provide greater protection under the Hawaii Constitution when warranted by the circumstances.

Here, the circumstances warrant greater protection. The search of the defendant's vehicle occurred in the middle of the night on an unfinished, barricaded freeway. The defendant and his passengers were ordered out of the vehicle and seated on the ground. There was no warrant. There was no probable cause. There was no exigency. The defense submits that this Court should exercise its authority under *Hoang* to hold that the Hawaii Constitution provides greater protection in this situation and affirm the circuit court's suppression order.

---

## CONCLUSION

**The Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence.** The prosecution has failed to establish that the initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion, that the flashlight scan was a lawful observation rather than an unconstitutional search, that probable cause existed prior to the search, or that the automobile exception applies. The evidence obtained during the warrantless search of the defendant's vehicle must be suppressed.

The Defense's position is straightforward: the Constitution protects citizens against unreasonable searches and seizures, and the prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the search of Mr. Haili's vehicle fell within any recognized exception to the warrant requirement. The circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress, and that order should be affirmed.

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee**

**NO. ** (Appellate Case Number)

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, this Court issues its final ruling.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires the Court to determine whether the circuit court erred in granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence obtained during a warrantless search of his vehicle. The State of Hawaii appeals the suppression order. After carefully considering the closing arguments of both parties and the factual record provided, the Court finds that the prosecution has met its burden to demonstrate that the search was constitutional under both the Fourth Amendment and Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Stop Was Supported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The defense argues that the initial stop lacked reasonable suspicion, relying heavily on *State v. Barnes*, 58 Haw. 412, 570 P.2d 575 (1977). In *Barnes*, the Hawaii Supreme Court held that a vehicle's presence in a general area where shots had been fired was insufficient to justify a stop without a specific description of the vehicle or occupants. The defense contends that the facts here are indistinguishable, noting that the security guard provided no description of the car or the suspects.

The Court disagrees. The prosecution correctly distinguishes *Barnes* by focusing on the specific location of the vehicle: the "unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway." Unlike the parking lot in *Barnes*, which was a public area where vehicles might lawfully be found, the unfinished freeway was a restricted area barricaded by a chain-link fence. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may stop a vehicle if they have "specific and articulable facts" that suggest criminal activity. The "totality of the circumstances" test outlined in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981), allows officers to draw logical inferences based on the time and place of the observed activity.

Here, Officer Davis received a report of gunshots originating from a specific, closed construction zone. Within minutes, he observed a vehicle traveling within that exact restricted zone. The inference that a vehicle found on a closed freeway immediately after a report of gunfire from that location is connected to the criminal activity is far more than a "hunch" or "mere presence." It is a specific, objective correlation of time and place. The defense's suggestion that the vehicle might have been engaged in legitimate construction work at 10:30 p.m. without lights or identification is speculative. Therefore, the initial stop was lawful.

**B. The Flashlight Scan Was Not a Search**

The defense contends that the flashlight scan constituted an unconstitutional search, characterizing it as an "affirmative investigative act" rather than a casual observation. The defense relies on *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), arguing that because the occupants were secured, the justification for a *Terry* frisk had expired.

The Court finds the prosecution's argument persuasive. The flashlight scan was not a search for weapons under *Long*, but a visual observation under the plain view doctrine. As held in *Texas v. Brown*, 445 U.S. 404 (1981), the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search if the officer is lawfully present. Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene of a valid *Terry* stop. The fact that the occupants had been secured and seated on the ground does not strip the officer of the right to look into the vehicle from a lawful vantage point. The defense's attempt to transform a standard visual inspection into a Fourth Amendment search is rejected. The officers were entitled to observe the interior of the car; the flashlight merely facilitated this observation in the dark.

**C. Probable Cause Existed Prior to the Search**

The defense argues that probable cause was lacking and that the discovery of the rifle casing was "fruit of the poisonous tree," asserting that the prosecution's logic is circular.

The Court disagrees. Probable cause exists when the facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). At the moment Officer Davis observed the vehicle, he possessed critical facts: (1) gunshots had been fired from the unfinished freeway; (2) the vehicle was present on that specific unfinished freeway, a location closed to the public; and (3) the vehicle was the only one present in that restricted area immediately following the report.

This combination of facts creates a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained evidence of the shooting. The defense's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), is misplaced because the initial stop and the subsequent observation were lawful. Furthermore, the discovery of the .22-caliber casing during the flashlight scan provided independent corroboration of the probable cause. The casing was not the product of an unlawful search, but rather a plain view observation that solidified the existing basis to search the vehicle.

**D. The Automobile Exception Applies**

The defense argues that the automobile exception does not apply because the vehicle was stationary and the occupants were secured, citing *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971), for the proposition that exigent circumstances were lacking.

The Court is not persuaded. The automobile exception is based on the inherent mobility of vehicles and the diminished expectation of privacy in automobiles. *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), holds that "if a car is readily mobile and probable cause exists to believe it contains contraband, the Fourth Amendment does not require a warrant." The vehicle was located on a public freeway, a mobile environment, and probable cause existed.

The defense's reliance on *Coolidge* is inapposite. *Coolidge* involved a vehicle seized at a private residence, where the expectation of privacy is significantly higher. Here, the vehicle was on a public highway. Moreover, *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), confirms that the "ready mobility" of a vehicle is sufficient to satisfy the exigency requirement, even if the vehicle is parked at the time of the search. The fact that the occupants were temporarily detained does not eliminate the vehicle's inherent mobility or the exigency justifying a warrantless search.

**E. State Constitutional Protections**

The defense argues that Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution provides broader protection than the Fourth Amendment, citing *State v. Hoang*, 66 Haw. 576, 732 P.2d 161 (1987). While *Hoang* recognizes the authority of this Court to interpret the Hawaii Constitution independently, this Court has previously held that Hawaii's protections against unreasonable search and seizure are generally coextensive with the Fourth Amendment in vehicle cases. *State v. Kaluna*, 55 Haw. 361, 520 P.2d 51 (1974). Since the search was valid under federal standards, it is likewise valid under the Hawaii Constitution. The defense has not provided compelling reasons to deviate from the *Kaluna* standard in this instance.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has successfully rebutted the defense's arguments. The initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion based on the specific location of the vehicle relative to the reported crime. The flashlight scan was a lawful observation under *Texas v. Brown*, not a search requiring independent justification. Probable cause existed prior to the search based on the totality of the circumstances, and the automobile exception justified the warrantless search of the vehicle. The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress evidence is **REVERSED**.
