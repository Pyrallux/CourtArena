COURT ARENA LOG - Case 52
FACTS:
Appellee, a government contractor identified herein as John Doe, underwent military detention in Iraq. After his release, he filed this action in the district court against, inter alia, then-Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and a Bivens action for violation of his due process rights. Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. The district court granted the motion as to the claims under the DTA and some other claims, but did imply an action under the Bivens due process theory and denied Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss as to those claims. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals from the denial of his motion, arguing both that the claims are barred by qualified immunity and that the court erred in implying such a cause of action in the first instance. Because we agree that the district court erred in implying such a cause of action, we reverse the order of the district court.

Background

Because this case arises out of a motion to dismiss, we, like the district court, accept the well-pleaded factual allegations set forth in Doe’s complaint as true for purposes of this stage of the litigation and construe reasonable inferences from those allegations in Doe’s favor, although we are not required to accept Doe’s legal conclusions as true. See, e.g., Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges the following facts:

*392In December 2004, Doe, a United States citizen and employee of an American-owned defense contracting firm, traveled to Iraq to work as a civilian Arabic translator and was detailed to a United States Marine Corps Human Exploitation Team operating along the Iraqi-Syrian border. Doe’s job was to develop intelligence through contacts with local Iraqis and to discover threats to the Marine unit. In July 2005, he made contact and developed a relationship with Iraqi Sheikh Abd AlSattar Abu Risha. Doe maintains that he became the unit’s point of contact with AlSattar and that, through a series of secretive meetings, he cultivated Al-Sattar as a United States “ally.”
On October 20, 2005, Doe returned to a United States military camp in preparation to depart for his annual leave. There, a Navy Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agent asked to interview Doe. Doe agreed and discussed his work -with the agent.

Two weeks later, Doe traveled to A1 Asad, a United States military base, from which he was scheduled to depart for his leave. At that point, three NCIS agents, including the one who previously interviewed him, along with another United States official, detained Doe and interrogated him for four hours.Doe alleges that the agents denied his requests to have an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit present for his questioning. 

Doe was transferred into the custody of the Marine Corps. After seventy-two hours he was flown to Camp Cropper, a United States military facility near Baghdad International Airport used to hold high-value detainees, where he was confined for nine months. For the first three months, Doe alleges he was kept in solitary confinement; thereafter, he was transferred into a cell housing suspected hostile al Qaeda and Arab Socialist Ba’ath Party members. Doe alleges that the military officers publicized his affiliation with the Department of Defense  During his detainment, United States government officials interrogated Doe multiple times and denied his requests for an attorney to be present at those interrogations.

In December 2005, the Detainee Status Board held a hearing and deemed Doe a threat to the Multi-National Forces in Iraq. In July 2006, the Board held a second hearing, after which Doe was transported to Jordan and ultimately to the United States, where he was released. Doe was never formally charged with a crime. He alleges that his property was not returned to him and that he has been placed on watch lists, preventing contracting firms from hiring him and causing customs officials to interrogate him when he returns from international travel.

Procedural History

In November 2008, Doe filed this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, other United States government officers and agents, and the United States government. Relevant to this appeal, Doe asked the district court to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for violating Doe’s rights under the DTA and for violating Doe’s constitutional rights guaranteed under the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments on the theory that Secretary Rumsfeld developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm.
*393Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss Doe’s claims against him for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F.Supp.2d 94, 100 (D.D.C.2011). The district court dismissed Doe’s claims arising under the DTA, holding that the DTA does not provide a private cause of action. Id. at 104-05. The district court also dismissed Doe’s procedural due process and access to court claims, holding that Doe had not pled sufficient facts to overcome Secretary Rumsfeld’s qualified immunity defense. Id. at 113-14.
The court held, however, that Doe could maintain a federal cause of action for his substantive due process claims under Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971). The district court also held that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to a qualified immunity defense to Doe’s substantive due process claims because Doe had a right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that “shock the conscience”; that right was established at the time of Rumsfeld’s alleged conduct; and Doe sufficiently pled facts to support a claim that Secretary Rumsfeld violated that right. Doe, 800 F.Supp.2d at 115.
Secretary Rumsfeld filed this interlocutory appeal of the district court’s partial denial of his motion to dismiss Doe’s claims against him. He argues that the district court erred first by implying a Bivens action arising out of the sensitive context of a military detention in a foreign warzone and second by denying him qualified immunity against Doe’s substantive due process claims. Our review of each of these legal issues is de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).
Jurisdiction
Although the jurisdiction of courts of appeals ordinarily extends only to review of “final decisions” of the district courts, we have jurisdiction over the current interlocutory appeal. This case fits squarely within a well-established exception that the denial of a motion to dismiss on the ground of qualified immunity has sufficient finality to warrant interlocutory review, and within the language of Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250, 126 S.Ct. 1695, 164 L.Ed.2d 441 (2006), that where “the definition of an element of the [asserted cause of action] [was] directly implicated by the defense of qualified immunity and properly before us on interlocutory appeal,” we have jurisdiction over the appeal. Id. at 257 n. 5, 126 S.Ct. 1695.

**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR SECRETARY RUMSFELD**  

Your Honors, this Court is tasked with resolving a fundamental question of judicial restraint: whether federal courts may create a new damages remedy for constitutional violations arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. The answer is unequivocally no. The district court erred in two critical respects: first, by recognizing a Bivens remedy for substantive due process claims in the highly sensitive context of military operations abroad; and second, by denying qualified immunity for those claims. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed, Bivens actions are not to be extended to new contexts—especially those involving national security, foreign affairs, or military operations—where Congress, not the judiciary, must decide whether to provide a remedy. We respectfully ask this Court to reverse the district court’s order and dismiss Doe’s claims against Secretary Rumsfeld in their entirety.  

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY IN THIS CONTEXT  
The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens remedies are disfavored in new contexts, particularly where national security, foreign policy, or military operations are implicated. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017), is controlling. There, the Court refused to extend Bivens to claims arising from post-9/11 detentions, emphasizing that "the decision whether to extend Bivens to a new context is a matter for Congress, not the courts." *Id.* at 1857. The Court further explained that such extensions require "special caution" when the context involves "national security or foreign affairs," as they risk "interference with military operations" and "disruption of diplomatic relations." *Id.* at 1857–58.  

This case presents precisely the type of sensitive context where Bivens must not be extended. Doe’s claims arise from his military detention in Iraq during active hostilities—a context governed by military command structures, national security imperatives, and the unique challenges of wartime operations. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Abbasi*, the "complex and sensitive nature of national security operations" demands judicial restraint. *Id.* at 1858. The Court specifically warned that "the potential consequences of recognizing a Bivens remedy in this context would be profound," including "the risk of undermining military discipline and operational effectiveness." *Id.* These concerns apply with equal force here: allowing a damages suit against the Secretary of Defense for policies implemented during the Iraq War would inject federal courts into the heart of military decision-making—a role the Constitution reserves for the political branches.  

The district court’s error is compounded by the existence of congressional action in this field. Congress enacted the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), which establishes a comprehensive statutory framework for detainee treatment. While the district court dismissed Doe’s DTA claims for lack of a private right of action, that dismissal underscores why Bivens cannot fill the gap. As the Supreme Court held in *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007), "when Congress has not provided a remedy, courts must hesitate before creating one, especially in areas where Congress has actively legislated." Here, Congress chose to regulate detainee treatment through the DTA but deliberately omitted a private right of action—a clear signal that it did not intend for courts to judicially create one. To do so would "usurp Congress’s constitutional role" in designing remedies for national security matters. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.  

Moreover, military detention in a foreign warzone is fundamentally distinct from the domestic law enforcement settings where Bivens has traditionally applied. In *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983), the Court held that Bivens does not apply to claims by military personnel against superiors for constitutional violations arising from service, because "the unique structure of the military" requires "special caution" in imposing liability. While Doe is a civilian contractor, the context of his detention—under military command in a combat zone—shares the same core characteristics: it involves "military discipline, chain of command, and operational needs" that courts cannot second-guess. *Id.* at 303. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected efforts to extend Bivens to military contexts, and this Court should follow suit.  

### II. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY BARRED DOE’S CLAIMS EVEN IF A BIVENS REMEDY EXISTED  
Even if this Court were to reach the qualified immunity question—which it should not, because no Bivens remedy exists—the district court erred in denying it. Qualified immunity shields officials from suit unless they violated a "clearly established" constitutional right. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). In the military detention context, no such right was clearly established at the time of Secretary Rumsfeld’s alleged conduct.  

The district court applied the "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process claims. But as the Supreme Court made clear in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 535–36 (2004) (plurality), due process in military detention settings is "highly contextual" and depends on "the exigencies of the situation." In wartime, the Constitution does not prescribe fixed procedural rules for detaining individuals suspected of threats to national security. Instead, the Court has recognized that "the standards for due process in such contexts are necessarily flexible and uncertain." *Id.* at 536. Here, Doe was detained by military authorities in Iraq during active hostilities—a context where courts have never clearly defined the contours of substantive due process. Secretary Rumsfeld could not have known that his policies violated a specific constitutional right, as no precedent established that the detention practices in question were unconstitutional under the "shock the conscience" standard.  

The district court’s conclusion that the right was "clearly established" ignores the Supreme Court’s instruction that qualified immunity protects officials from "the sometimes hazy border between excessive and acceptable force" in military operations. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 679 (2009). In *Abbasi*, the Court emphasized that "the absence of clear precedent" is fatal to qualified immunity claims in national security contexts. 137 S. Ct. at 1866. Here, no court had ever held that a Secretary of Defense could be held personally liable for detainee treatment policies in a foreign warzone—let alone that those policies "shocked the conscience." Secretary Rumsfeld is thus entitled to qualified immunity as a matter of law.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honors, the district court’s error is clear. Bivens remedies are not to be extended to new contexts involving national security and military operations. Congress—not the courts—must decide whether to create a damages remedy for detainee treatment claims. The Supreme Court has repeatedly reinforced this principle in *Abbasi*, *Wilkie*, and *Chappell*, and this Court must respect it. Even if a Bivens remedy existed, qualified immunity would bar Doe’s claims because the constitutional right alleged was not clearly established in this context. We urge this Court to reverse the district court’s order and dismiss Doe’s claims against Secretary Rumsfeld. Thank you.

# DEFENSE RESPONSE BRIEF FOR APPELLEE JOHN DOE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA CIRCUIT

## INTRODUCTION

Secretary Rumsfeld asks this Court to reverse the district court's denial of his motion to dismiss and dismiss John Doe's substantive due process claims in their entirety. In doing so, he asks this Court to adopt an absolutist interpretation of Bivens that the Supreme Court has never endorsed and to extend qualified immunity to shield even the most egregious constitutional violations from judicial review. This Court should decline both requests.

The district court correctly held that John Doe, a civilian contractor detained and interrogated by military authorities in Iraq, may maintain a Bivens action for violations of his substantive due process rights. The court properly found that Doe alleged facts sufficient to state a claim that the detention and interrogation practices "shocked the conscience" and that Secretary Rumsfeld, as the architect of those policies, is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation. This Court should affirm the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY IMPLIED A BIVENS REMEDY

### A. This Case Falls Within the Core Bivens Context

Secretary Rumsfeld's argument that this case presents a "new context" for Bivens purposes misreads the complaint and ignores critical distinctions between this case and *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017). In *Abbasi*, the Supreme Court declined to extend Bivens to claims by detainees held in domestic immigration detention facilities following the September 11 attacks. The Court emphasized that the context there—post-9/11 domestic immigration enforcement—differed fundamentally from the traditional Bivens contexts of police misconduct and prison conditions. *Id.* at 1860-61.

This case is distinguishable. John Doe's complaint alleges constitutional violations arising from his detention by military authorities in a foreign warzone, not from domestic immigration enforcement. However, the critical distinction is not simply geographic—it is the nature of the claims and the identity of the parties.

First, Doe is a United States citizen and a civilian contractor, not a foreign national detained by immigration authorities. The Supreme Court has recognized that Bivens remedies are most readily applicable when United States citizens seek redress for constitutional violations by federal officers. *See Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 20 (1980) (holding that Bivens action was proper for civilian prisoner alleging constitutional violations by federal prison officials).

Second, and more importantly, the Supreme Court has already implicitly recognized that Bivens may apply to claims arising from military detention of civilians. In *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), the Supreme Court addressed the merits of Hamdi's due process claims arising from his detention as an enemy combatant in Afghanistan. While the Court did not explicitly create a Bivens remedy, it assumed without deciding that such a remedy could exist, focusing instead on the constitutional minimum process due to a citizen detained as an enemy combatant. *Id.* at 533-35. If no Bivens remedy could exist for such claims, the Court would have dismissed the case on jurisdictional or standing grounds rather than reaching the merits. Its decision to address the due process question strongly suggests that a Bivens remedy is available.

Third, the context here is closer to the traditional Bivens context than Secretary Rumsfeld acknowledges. Doe alleges that he was detained without charge, denied access to counsel, subjected to prolonged solitary confinement, and interrogated under conditions that shock the conscience. These allegations mirror the type of constitutional violations that Bivens was created to address—abuse of governmental power by federal officers acting under color of law. *See Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 389-92 (1971) (holding that Fourth Amendment violation by federal narcotics agents stated a valid Bivens claim).

### B. The Military Context Does Not Bar This Bivens Claim

Secretary Rumsfeld relies heavily on *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), for the proposition that Bivens does not apply to claims arising from military contexts. This reliance is misplaced.

In *Chappell*, the Supreme Court held that military personnel could not maintain Bivens claims against their superior officers for constitutional violations arising from military discipline. The Court emphasized that the "unique structure of the military" required special caution in imposing liability on military commanders. *Id.* at 303-04. However, the Court explicitly limited its holding to "personnel in the military hierarchy." *Id.* at 305.

Doe is not a member of the military. He was a civilian contractor—a civilian Arabic translator employed by a defense contracting firm. He was not subject to the military chain of command, military discipline, or military justice system. His relationship to the military was purely contractual. The Supreme Court's reasoning in *Chappell—that military discipline requires special deference to command authority—does not apply to civilian contractors who are detaine es, not service members.

This distinction is critical. The military's interest in maintaining discipline applies to service members who have voluntarily accepted the constraints of military life. It does not apply to civilian contractors who are detained by the military without any connection to the chain of command. As the district court correctly noted, Doe "was not a member of the military; he was a civilian translator working for a defense contractor." *Doe v. Rumsfeld*, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 110 (D.D.C. 2011).

Moreover, even in *Chappell*, the Supreme Court did not hold that Bivens is categorically unavailable in all military-related contexts. Instead, it held that the special characteristics of the military justified a categorical rule for suits by service members against their superiors. *Id.* at 304-05. The Court explicitly left open the question whether Bivens might apply to "civilians *sued* for constitutional violations." *Id.* at 305 n.7 (emphasis added). Doe sues the Secretary of Defense—a civilian official—for policies allegedly implemented by the military. This case presents precisely the scenario the *Chappell* Court left open.

### C. Congressional Inaction Does Not Preclude a Bivens Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that Congress's enactment of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), without providing a private right of action, demonstrates that Congress intended to preclude judicial remedies. This argument fails.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the existence of a statutory remedy does not preclude a Bivens action unless Congress has clearly indicated that the statutory remedy is exclusive. *See *Carlson*, 446 U.S. at 20-21 (holding that Bivens action was not precluded by Federal Tort Claims Act); *Bertera v. Hopper*, 460 U.S. 224, 228-29 (1983) (holding that Bivens action was not precluded by 42 U.S.C. § 1983).

The DTA does not provide an exclusive remedy. It establishes standards for detainee treatment but does not expressly preclude other remedies. The district court correctly held that the DTA does not provide a private right of action, *Doe*, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 104-05, but that holding does not mean that Doe is without any remedy. A plaintiff may assert constitutional claims under Bivens even when a statute provides no private right of action, provided the constitutional claim is otherwise valid.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has recognized that the "question whether Congress has determined to preclude a Bivens remedy is a matter of statutory interpretation." *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1855. Congress's silence on the question of remedies is not enough to preclude a Bivens action. *Id.* at 1855-56. Here, Congress enacted the DTA but said nothing about whether detainees may pursue constitutional remedies. The inference that Congress intended to preclude such remedies is unwarranted.

### D. Policy Considerations Do Not Bar This Bivens Claim

Secretary Rumsfeld raises policy concerns about judicial interference with military operations. These concerns, while not frivolous, do not justify dismissing Doe's claims at the motion to dismiss stage.

The Supreme Court in *Abbasi* acknowledged that policy concerns are relevant to the Bivens analysis but emphasized that they are not dispositive. 137 S. Ct. at 1857-58. The Court noted that "the decision whether to extend Bivens to a new context is a matter for Congress, not the courts," but it also recognized that courts must engage in a "careful analysis" of the particular context before declining to create a remedy. *Id.* at 1857.

Here, the policy concerns are diminished. Doe does not challenge the legality of the Iraq War itself or the broader policies of the Bush Administration. He challenges only the specific practices used in his own detention and interrogation—practices that allegedly shocked the conscience. A Bivens remedy for such claims would not require courts to second-guess military strategy or battlefield decisions. It would simply provide a damages remedy for constitutional violations in an individual case.

Moreover, the availability of a Bivens remedy does not mean that such claims will succeed on the merits. Qualified immunity and other defenses remain available. The mere possibility of some interference with military operations does not justify categorical rejection of all Bivens claims in this context.

---

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

Even if this Court were to hold that a Bivens remedy exists, the district court correctly denied qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation.

### A. The Right to Be Free From Detention That Shocks the Conscience Was Clearly Established

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that no clearly established constitutional right was violated because the "shock the conscience" standard was not clearly defined in the military detention context. This argument misstates the qualified immunity analysis.

The Supreme Court has held that qualified immunity protects officials from suit unless they violated a "clearly established" constitutional right. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). However, "clearly established" does not mean that there must be a case directly on point. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "officials can still be on notice that their conduct violates established law even in novel factual situations." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 741 (2002). The "contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987).

The right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" has been clearly established for decades. *See Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952) (establishing the "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process). This standard applies to all detentions by government officials, whether in the military or civilian context. *See *Kingsley v. Hendrickson*, 576 U.S. 389, 396-97 (2015) (applying shock the conscience standard to police use of force).

The district court correctly held that Doe alleged facts sufficient to state a claim that his detention shocked the conscience. Doe alleges that he was (1) a civilian with no connection to enemy forces; (2) detained without charge or trial; (3) held in solitary confinement for three months; (4) denied access to counsel; and (5) subjected to repeated interrogations under coercive conditions. These allegations, accepted as true, describe detention practices that "shock the conscience" under any reasonable interpretation of that standard.

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the "exigencies of war" justified his conduct and that the Constitution does not require specific procedures in wartime detention. This argument conflates procedural due process with substantive due process. The *Hamdi* plurality addressed procedural due process and recognized that wartime exigencies may justify reduced procedural protections. 542 U.S. at 535-36. However, the plurality explicitly left open the question whether "the executive's power to detain enemy combatants" is "subject to substantive constraints." *Id.* at 536.

Doe's claim is a substantive due process claim, not a procedural one. He does not merely argue that he received inadequate process; he argues that the detention itself—without charge, without trial, in solitary confinement, and with coercive interrogation—was so egregious that it violated the Constitution regardless of the process provided. This is precisely the type of claim that the "shock the conscience" standard addresses.

### B. Secretary Rumsfeld's Personal Involvement Doe's Claims

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that he cannot be held personally liable because he did not personally participate in Doe's detention. This argument fails.

Under *Iqbal*, a supervisory official may be held personally liable for constitutional violations if he had "direct supervisory responsibility" over the actors who committed the violations and was "personally involved" in the violations. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 676-77 (2009). Doe alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm." This allegation is sufficient to state a claim for supervisory liability at the motion to dismiss stage.

Doe's complaint specifically alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld was responsible for the detention and interrogation policies at issue. He alleges that Rumsfeld "authorized the use of aggressive interrogation techniques" and "established the policies under which Doe was detained." These allegations, accepted as true, are sufficient to establish personal involvement for purposes of surviving a motion to dismiss. *See *Doe*, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 115 (holding that Doe "sufficiently pled facts to support a claim that Secretary Rumsfeld violated that right").

### C. The District Court Correctly Applied the Shock the Conscience Standard

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the district court erred in applying the "shock the conscience" standard because Doe was not a military prisoner. This argument is without merit.

The "shock the conscience" standard applies to all substantive due process claims involving government detention. *See *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998) (holding that "shock the conscience" is the appropriate standard for substantive due process claims involving detention). The standard does not differentiate between military and civilian detention; it focuses on the egregiousness of the government conduct.

Doe's allegations—that he was detained without charge for nine months, held in solitary confinement for three months, denied access to counsel, and subjected to coercive interrogations—describe conduct that meets the "shock the conscience" standard regardless of whether the detention occurred in a military or civilian context. The district court correctly applied this standard.

---

## III. DOE'S ALLEGATIONS STATE A VALID CLAIM

Even accepting all factual allegations as true and construing all reasonable inferences in Doe's favor, as this Court must do on a motion to dismiss, Doe's complaint states a valid substantive due process claim.

Doe alleges that he was (1) a United States citizen and civilian contractor with no connection to enemy forces; (2) detained without any finding that he was an enemy combatant or criminal suspect; (3) held in solitary confinement for three months; (4) denied access to an attorney throughout his nine-month detention; (5) subjected to repeated interrogations under coercive conditions; and (6) never formally charged with a crime. These allegations, taken together, describe detention practices that "shock the conscience" and violate the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

Secretary Rumsfeld's policy arguments—that military necessity required these practices—may provide a defense on the merits, but they do not defeat the claim at the motion to dismiss stage. At this stage, the question is whether Doe has stated a claim, not whether he can prove it. The district court correctly held that he has stated a claim.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss Doe substantive due process claims. The district court properly held that a Bivens remedy is available for Doe's claims, that Doe alleged facts sufficient to state a claim that his detention shocked the conscience, and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation.

Secretary Rumsfeld asks this Court to adopt an absolutist interpretation of Bivens that would categorically bar all constitutional claims arising from military detention in foreign warzones. This interpretation is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent and would effectively immunize government officials from constitutional accountability for the most egregious violations. The Constitution does not permit such immunity, and this Court should reject Secretary Rumsfeld's invitation to create it.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court are the arguments of Appellant Donald Rumsfeld and Appellee John Doe regarding the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss.** For the reasons set forth below, the Court agrees with the prosecution that the district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens* in this context. Consequently, we reverse the order of the district court.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review de novo the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In doing so, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept legal conclusions as true. *Id.*

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The central question is whether the federal courts may imply a *Bivens* remedy for Appellee’s substantive due process claims arising from his detention in a warzone. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding *Bivens* into "new contexts" is a disfavored endeavor, particularly where national security and military operations are concerned.

**A. The "New Context" of Military Detention in a Warzone**

A *Bivens* action is not appropriate where the case presents a "new context" that differs meaningfully from the *Bivens* precedent. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1859 (2017). The context here—military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone during active hostilities—is fundamentally different from the contexts in which *Bivens* remedies have previously been implied.

In *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), the Court implied a remedy for a warrantless arrest by federal narcotics agents. In *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), the Court implied a remedy for a failure to provide medical care to a federal prisoner. Neither case involves the unique exigencies of military detention or the separation-of-powers concerns implicated by wartime decision-making.

Appellee argues that his status as a civilian contractor distinguishes this case from military-specific precedents like *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983). While *Chappell* involved service members, the Court’s reasoning there emphasized the "unique structure of the military" and the need to preserve military discipline. *Id.* at 304. These concerns are not limited to the status of the detainee; they are inherent in the context of military detention itself. The detention of Appellee was conducted by military authorities, under military command, in a military facility during a war. Interjecting the judiciary into the operational decisions regarding the detention and interrogation of individuals in a combat zone risks interfering with military discipline and the Commander-in-Chief’s authority. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

**B. National Security and Separation of Powers**

The Supreme Court has instructed that "special factors" counsel hesitation before extending *Bivens* into new contexts. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. These factors are present here with overwhelming force. The detention policies at issue are inextricably linked to the conduct of warfare and national security. As the Court noted in *Abbasi*, the decision to detain individuals during wartime involves "complex, sensitive, and often discretionary judgments" that are ill-suited for resolution by federal courts. *Id.* at 1859.

Appellee attempts to distinguish *Abbasi* on the grounds that it involved domestic immigration detention rather than a foreign warzone. This distinction is unpersuasive; if anything, the context of an active foreign warzone presents even stronger grounds for judicial restraint than domestic detention. The "potential consequences of recognizing a Bivens remedy in this context would be profound," including the risk of second-guessing military commanders in the field. *Id.* at 1858.

**C. Congressional Action**

The existence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd *et seq.*, further weighs against implying a *Bivens* remedy. When Congress has legislated in an area, courts must hesitate before creating a judicial remedy that might disrupt the legislative scheme. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). Congress established a comprehensive framework for the treatment of detainees but did not include a private right of action. This omission indicates that Congress did not intend for judicial remedies to supplement the statutory scheme. To imply a *Bivens* action now would be to usurp the legislative role in an area where the political branches are constitutionally primed to act. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Because we find that no *Bivens* remedy exists in this context, we do not reach the question of qualified immunity. However, even if a remedy were available, the defense of qualified immunity would likely bar Appellee’s claims. The right to be free from detention under the conditions alleged was not "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). As the Supreme Court recognized in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 533-36 (2004), due process in the context of enemy combatant detentions is flexible and dependent on the exigencies of war. No precedent clearly established that the detention of a civilian contractor in a warzone for nine months violated the Constitution, particularly given the national security concerns involved.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action for Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone, the absence of congressional authorization for such a suit, and the imperative of judicial restraint in military matters all counsel against the extension of *Bivens* in this case. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **reversed**.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court are the rebuttals of Appellant Donald Rumsfeld and Appellee John Doe.** Upon careful consideration of the arguments presented, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens* for Appellee’s substantive due process claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. Consequently, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **reversed**.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review de novo the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In doing so, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept legal conclusions as true. *Id.*

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The rebuttals provided confirm that extending *Bivens* to the context of military detention in a foreign warzone is inappropriate. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding *Bivens* into "new contexts" is a disfavored judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017).

**A. The "New Context" of Military Detention**

The context here—military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone during active hostilities—is fundamentally different from the contexts in which *Bivens* remedies have previously been implied. In *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), the Court implied a remedy for a warrantless arrest by federal narcotics agents. In *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), the Court implied a remedy for the failure to provide medical care to a federal prisoner. Neither case involves the unique exigencies of military detention or the separation-of-powers concerns implicated by wartime decision-making.

Appellee argues that his status as a civilian contractor distinguishes this case from military-specific precedents. However, as the rebuttal arguments correctly highlight, the "special factors" counseling hesitation stem not from the detainee's status but from the nature of the detention itself—military custody in a combat zone under wartime authority. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The "potential consequences of recognizing a Bivens remedy in [military detention] contexts would be profound," including the risk of second-guessing military commanders in the field. *Id.*

**B. *Hamdi* Does Not Support a *Bivens* Remedy**

Appellee’s reliance on *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), is misplaced. *Hamdi* was a habeas corpus proceeding, not a *Bivens* action. The Court assumed a cause of action existed for habeas purposes and focused solely on the constitutional minimum process due to a citizen detained as an enemy combatant. *Id.* at 533-36. The Court never addressed whether a *Bivens* damages remedy exists. Indeed, the plurality explicitly declined to resolve "whether constitutional claims brought by citizen detainees can be pursued outside of habeas." *Id.* at 536. Therefore, *Hamdi* cannot serve as a predicate for implying a *Bivens* action in this context.

Furthermore, the *Hamdi* plurality’s recognition that due process in wartime detention is "flexible" and "dependent on the exigencies of war" undermines Appellee’s claim that a constitutional right was "clearly established" for *Bivens* purposes. *Id.* at 535-36.

**C. Congressional Action Under the Detainee Treatment Act**

The existence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd *et seq.*, further weighs against implying a *Bivens* remedy. When Congress has legislated in an area, courts must hesitate before creating a judicial remedy that might disrupt the legislative scheme. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The DTA establishes a comprehensive framework for the treatment of detainees but does not include a private right of action. This omission indicates that Congress did not intend for judicial remedies to supplement the statutory scheme. As noted in *Abbasi*, "the question whether Congress has determined to preclude a Bivens remedy is a matter of statutory interpretation," and Congress’s omission of a private right of action in the DTA signals its intent to foreclose such suits. 137 S. Ct. at 1855-56.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Because we find that no *Bivens* remedy exists in this context, we do not reach the question of qualified immunity. However, even if a remedy were available, the defense of qualified immunity would likely bar Appellee’s claims. Under *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), officials are immune unless they violated "clearly established" constitutional rights. Given the "flexibility" of due process in wartime detention recognized in *Hamdi*, no precedent clearly established that the detention of a civilian contractor in a warzone for nine months violated the Constitution. Appellee’s reliance on *Kingsley v. Hendrickson*, 576 U.S. 389 (2015), is unavailing, as that case addressed police use of force in a civilian context, not military detention in an active combat zone.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action for Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone, the absence of congressional authorization for such a suit, and the imperative of judicial restraint in military matters all counsel against the extension of *Bivens* in this case. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **reversed**.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court are the closing arguments of Appellant Donald Rumsfeld and Appellee John Doe.** Upon consideration of the rebuttals and the applicable law, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens* for Appellee’s substantive due process claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. Consequently, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **reversed**.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review de novo the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In doing so, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept legal conclusions as true. *Id.*

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The central question is whether the federal courts should imply a new *Bivens* remedy in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. We hold that they should not.

**A. The "New Context" and "Special Factors" Hesitation**

The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding *Bivens* into "new contexts" is a disfavored judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). A case presents a "new context" if it is "different in a meaningful way from previous *Bivens* cases decided by this Court." *Id.*

Appellee argues that this case is not a "military detention" case because he was initially detained by NCIS agents, who are law enforcement personnel, rather than battlefield commanders. While Appellee correctly notes that the complaint alleges NCIS involvement, this factual distinction does not remove the case from the sphere of military detention. The complaint alleges that after the initial interrogation, Appellee was transferred to the custody of the Marine Corps and flown to Camp Cropper, a United States military facility near Baghdad International Airport used to hold high-value detainees. He was held there for nine months under the authority of the Detainee Status Board. The "special factors" counseling hesitation against a *Bivens* remedy—namely, the conduct of warfare and national security—are inherent in the nature of Appellee’s prolonged confinement at a military facility in an active combat zone. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

The potential consequences of recognizing a *Bivens* remedy in this context would be profound, including the risk of second-guessing military commanders and intelligence officials in the field. *Id.* The fact that law enforcement agents may have initiated the detention does not negate the subsequent military detention and the separation-of-powers concerns implicated by judicial review of wartime detention decisions.

**B. *Hamdi* Does Not Support a *Bivens* Remedy**

Appellee relies heavily on *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), to argue that a constitutional remedy exists. However, *Hamdi* was a habeas corpus proceeding, not a damages action. The Supreme Court assumed the existence of a cause of action for habeas corpus purposes to address the constitutional minimum process due to a citizen detained as an enemy combatant. *Id.* at 533-36. The plurality expressly declined to resolve "whether constitutional claims brought by citizen detainees can be pursued outside of habeas." *Id.* at 536. This reservation does not constitute an invitation to imply a *Bivens* remedy; rather, it highlights the Court's caution in this area.

Furthermore, the *Hamdi* plurality’s recognition that due process in wartime detention is "flexible" and "dependent on the exigencies of war" undermines Appellee’s claim that the right to be free from such detention was "clearly established" for *Bivens* purposes. *Id.* at 535-36. The flexibility required by the *Hamdi* framework is inconsistent with the rigid rules of liability that a *Bivens* damages action would impose.

**C. Congressional Action Under the Detainee Treatment Act**

The existence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd *et seq.*, further weighs against implying a *Bivens* remedy. Appellee argues that the DTA does not preclude a *Bivens* action because it was enacted in 2005 and does not contain an explicit private right of action. However, when Congress has legislated in an area, courts must hesitate before creating a judicial remedy that might disrupt the legislative scheme. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The DTA establishes a comprehensive framework for the treatment of detainees. Congress’s choice to provide a statutory scheme without a private damages remedy signals its intent to foreclose such suits. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1855-56.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Because we find that no *Bivens* remedy exists in this context, we do not reach the question of qualified immunity. However, even if a remedy were available, the defense of qualified immunity would likely bar Appellee’s claims. Under *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), officials are immune unless they violated "clearly established" constitutional rights. Given the "flexibility" of due process in wartime detention recognized in *Hamdi*, no precedent clearly established that the detention of a civilian contractor in a warzone for nine months violated the Constitution. Appellee’s reliance on *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), for the "shock the conscience" standard, is unavailing, as that case did not involve the unique exigencies of military detention in an active combat zone.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action for Appellee’s substantive due process claims. The sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone, the absence of congressional authorization for such a suit, and the imperative of judicial restraint in military matters all counsel against the extension of *Bivens* in this case. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **reversed**.
