**Opening Argument for the Plaintiff/Prosecution**  

May it please the Court. Today, we ask this Honorable Court to correct a fundamental legal error that denied Van Ness his rightful workers' compensation benefits. The Director of Labor and Industrial Relations erred in denying compensation by misapplying the law governing proximate cause in occupational disease claims. Specifically, the Director incorrectly required that Vog exposure be unique to Van Ness’s workplace to establish a work-related nexus—a standard that has no basis in Hawaii workers’ compensation law. Instead, the law requires only that employment was a contributing cause of the injury, as established by the unitary test for proximate cause. This Court must reverse the Director’s decision because the evidence unequivocally shows that Van Ness’s employment directly contributed to the aggravation of his asthma, as confirmed by medical testimony and the facts of record.  

Under Hawaii law, the "arising out of" requirement for workers’ compensation claims is satisfied if the employment was a contributing cause of the injury. As this Court held in *Kauai Elec. Co. v. Workers’ Comp. Comm’n*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), "[t]he 'arising out of' requirement does not demand that the injury be caused solely by the employment or unique to the workplace; it requires only that employment was a contributing cause." This unitary test applies universally to all workers’ compensation claims, including those involving occupational diseases. It does not require exclusivity or uniqueness of exposure; it requires only that the employment played some role in causing the injury.  

*Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008), confirms this principle. In *Flor*, this Court clarified that for injuries by disease, the test for compensability is limited to determining whether the disease "resulted from the nature of the employment"—meaning the employment must be a significant contributing factor. Crucially, *Flor* does not impose a separate or higher standard for proximate cause; rather, it aligns with the unitary test by requiring only that employment contributed to the disease. The Director’s error here was conflating *Flor*’s "resulting from the nature of employment" standard with an impermissible requirement of workplace uniqueness. The Director stated that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and concluded there was "insufficient evidence" of a nexus. This reasoning is legally unsound. Under the unitary test, it is irrelevant that vog was present island-wide; what matters is whether Van Ness’s employment exposed him to it in a manner that contributed to his injury.  

The record is replete with evidence that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing cause of his aggravated asthma. Van Ness was employed as a technology coordinator at Lahainaluna High School from July 2005 to November 2006. During the severe vog season from late October 2005 through April 2006, he was required to perform his duties on the school’s mountainous campus, which involved repeatedly climbing steep stairs between buildings at varying elevations to service computers. Van Ness testified that this physical exertion—coupled with vog exposure—caused acute respiratory distress: he struggled to breathe while ascending the stairs, experienced "incredibly sharp" stabbing pain from coughing, and required frequent breaks. He exhausted his inhaler supply months before his prescription expired, necessitating multiple pharmacy refills, and his medications failed to control his symptoms.  

Medical evidence further confirms the causal link. Dr. Mathison, who reviewed Van Ness’s complete medical history—including records from 1991 through his 2006 hospitalization—diagnosed "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." Dr. Mathison explicitly stated that "volcanic products" are a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" and that Van Ness’s employer "should not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." Dr. Tom, Van Ness’s treating physician, documented that his symptoms "never completely went away" after December 2005 and noted "vog contributing" to his condition. Dr. Tom also recommended a transfer to Oahu due to "higher vog exposure" on Maui. Crucially, Van Ness’s symptoms improved only after he was transferred to Oahu, where vog exposure was minimal.  

The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s report was misplaced. Dr. Arora conducted his examination without access to Van Ness’s medical records and admitted it would be "unwise to address the referral questions" until those records were provided. In contrast, Dr. Mathison’s opinion was based on a thorough review of all relevant records, including hospitalization details, and directly linked Van Ness’s condition to vog exposure during his employment. Under Hawaii law, the Director must credit competent medical evidence that supports the claim, and Dr. Mathison’s testimony is uncontradicted by any medical expert who reviewed the full record.  

The Director’s error lies in demanding that vog exposure be unique to the workplace—a standard that has never been imposed by this Court or by statute. The unitary test requires only that employment contributed to the injury, and here, Van Ness’s job duties required him to be present on campus during severe vog events, which directly exacerbated his asthma. As *Kauai Elec. Co.* teaches, "if the employment was a contributing cause, it is sufficient for compensation." The Director’s focus on the island-wide presence of vog ignores this elementary principle. Van Ness did not need to prove that vog was absent elsewhere; he needed only to show that his employment exposed him to it in a way that contributed to his injury—and the record proves he did.  

For these reasons, we respectfully request that this Court reverse the Director’s decision and award Van Ness workers’ compensation benefits. The law is clear: employment need not be the sole cause, nor must the hazard be unique to the workplace. It need only be a contributing cause—and the evidence here leaves no doubt that it was. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of the Workers' Compensation Claim of VAN NESS, Claimant-Appellant

---

May it please the Court. The Defense respectfully requests that this Honorable Court affirm the Director's decision denying workers' compensation benefits. The Plaintiff mischaracterizes the Director's reasoning and ignores the substantial evidence supporting the denial. Contrary to the Plaintiff's assertions, the Director did not impose an erroneous "uniqueness" standard. Rather, the Director properly applied the unitary test and found, based on the credible evidence, that the Plaintiff failed to establish that his employment was a contributing cause of his alleged injury. The evidence demonstrated that the vog exposure was general to the entire island of Maui and not peculiar to the Plaintiff's workplace, and the Plaintiff's pre-existing asthma condition was aggravated by factors unrelated to his employment. This Court should affirm the Director's decision.

---

## I. THE DIRECTOR PROPERLY APPLIED THE UNITARY TEST

The Plaintiff incorrectly asserts that the Director imposed a "uniqueness" requirement that has no basis in Hawaii workers' compensation law. This mischaracterization ignores the actual basis for the Director's decision.

In *Kauai Elec. Co. v. Workers' Comp. Comm'n*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), this Court held that the "arising out of" requirement "requires only that employment was a contributing cause." However, this does not mean that any employment exposure to a general environmental condition satisfies the test. The Plaintiff must still establish that his particular employment exposed him to the condition in a manner that contributed to his injury.

The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition." This finding is supported by substantial evidence in the record, including the Plaintiff's own testimony that he lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted "the vog was even worse" than at the school. The Plaintiff testified that he was "not much of an outdoors person" and "there really wasn't much to do there," suggesting he had limited outdoor activities beyond work.

Under the unitary test, the question is whether the employment contributed to the injury. Here, the Director found that the Plaintiff's exposure to vog was general to the island and not attributable to his particular employment conditions. This is a factual determination supported by the record. The Plaintiff confuses the standard by arguing that employment "played some role" in causing the injury. The question is not whether employment played "some role" in the abstract, but whether the particular conditions of employment contributed to the injury beyond general exposure that would have occurred regardless of employment.

---

## II. THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH A NEXUS BETWEEN EMPLOYMENT AND INJURY

The record supports the Director's finding that the Plaintiff failed to establish a nexus between his employment and his alleged injury.

### A. The Vog Exposure Was General to Maui, Not Specific to Employment

The facts demonstrate that the vog was a general environmental condition affecting the entire island of Maui. The Plaintiff testified that there was "a significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006." Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "we experienced some unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding that winter break" and that "on some days the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School." Most significantly, Vice Principal Dennis noted that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where the Plaintiff lived, than at the school.

The Plaintiff was not required to work outdoors in exposed conditions. His position as technology coordinator primarily involved maintaining computers inside school buildings. While he did need to travel between buildings to service computers, this travel was not the type of employment-specific exposure that would distinguish his workplace from any other location on Maui. The vog was present everywhere on the island during the relevant period.

### B. The Physical Exertion of Climbing Stairs Was Not a Special Hazard of Employment

The Plaintiff argues that his job required him to climb steep stairs, which exacerbated his breathing difficulties. However, the Director was entitled to find that this physical exertion was not a special hazard of his employment such that it would establish a nexus between employment and injury.

The Plaintiff was employed as a technology coordinator. The physical exertion of climbing stairs was incidental to his job, not a special hazard unique to his position. The fact that the Plaintiff chose to walk between buildings rather than drive (as he admitted he could do) does not transform this incidental exertion into a compensable employment condition. The Plaintiff testified that "it was easier to drive from the library down, around the campus, up to admin, than to actually walk down there," demonstrating that he had options to reduce his physical exertion.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008), this Court held that for occupational diseases, the test is whether the disease "resulted from the nature of the employment." The nature of the Plaintiff's employment as a technology coordinator did not inherently involve exposure to vog or unusual physical exertion that would cause asthma exacerbation. Dr. Mathison diagnosed the Plaintiff with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." Dr. Mathison's opinion establishes that volcanic pollution generally exacerbates the Plaintiff's asthma, but this does not establish that his particular employment was a contributing cause. Dr. Mathison recommended that the employer "not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." This recommendation applies to any assignment on Maui, not specifically to the Plaintiff's technology coordinator position at Lahainaluna.

The Plaintiff notes that his symptoms improved after transferring to Oahu. However, this temporal correlation does not establish causation. The Plaintiff was transferred in November 2006, after his May 2006 surgery and hospitalization. His improvement could be attributed to his recovery from surgery, his change in residence, or other factors unrelated to his employment.

### C. The Director Was Entitled to Make Credibility Determinations

The Director heard the testimony and reviewed the evidence. Under Hawaii law, the Director is the finder of fact and is entitled to make credibility determinations. The Director chose to credit Dr. Arora's observations about the general nature of vog exposure and the employer's position. This Court should not reweigh the evidence on appeal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The issue presented is whether the Director erred in concluding that Van Ness’s injury did not arise out of and in the course of employment. Specifically, the Court must determine if the Director legally misapplied the standard for compensability by requiring that Van Ness’s exposure to volcanic smog (vog) be unique to his workplace, rather than determining whether his employment was a contributing cause of his injury.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

Under Hawaii law, a claim for workers’ compensation benefits is compensable if the injury "arises out of and in the course of employment." The Hawaii Supreme Court has established a unitary test for proximate cause in this context. In *Kauai Elec. Co. v. Workers’ Comp. Comm’n*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), the Court held that the "arising out of" requirement "does not demand that the injury be caused solely by the employment or unique to the workplace; it requires only that employment was a contributing cause." This standard applies universally to all workers’ compensation claims.

Where the injury is a disease, the compensability analysis is further informed by *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008). In *Flor*, the Court clarified that for injuries by disease, the test is whether the disease "resulted from the nature of the employment," meaning the employment must be a significant contributing factor. *Flor* does not impose a requirement that the environmental conditions causing the disease be exclusive to the workplace, but rather that the nature of the employment exposed the claimant to the risk in a manner that contributed to the injury.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to support a finding that the exposure occurred at the place of employment only. The Director concluded that because the vog was a general environmental condition, there was no nexus between the employment and the injury.

The Court finds that the Director applied an incorrect legal standard. By requiring Van Ness to demonstrate that his exposure to vog occurred "at this place of employment only," the Director imposed a "uniqueness" requirement that is explicitly rejected by *Kauai Elec. Co.* As stated in *Kauai Elec. Co.*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), the law does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace; it requires only that the employment was a contributing cause. The presence of vog island-wide is irrelevant to the inquiry of whether the employment contributed to the aggravation of Van Ness’s condition.

The record demonstrates that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing cause of his respiratory injury. Van Ness testified that his duties as the technology coordinator at Lahainaluna High School required him to repeatedly climb steep stairs and traverse a mountainous campus to service computers. He described the physical exertion as significant, noting that it took "20 minutes" to climb back up to his office compared to five minutes to go down, and that he was forced to stop to catch his breath. This physical exertion occurred simultaneously with his exposure to severe vog at the school.

Medical evidence supports the link between these employment conditions and the injury. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noted that volcanic products are a "well-recognized risk factor." Dr. Tom, Van Ness’s treating physician, documented that his symptoms worsened due to "vog on Maui" and recommended a transfer. While the Defense argues that the "nature of employment" for a technology coordinator is sedentary, the specific facts of this assignment at Lahainaluna required substantial physical exertion in a vog-filled environment. Under *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008), the nature of the employment includes the actual conditions under which the work is performed. The combination of physical exertion and environmental exposure at work satisfied the requirement that the injury resulted from the nature of the employment.

The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s report does not cure this legal error. Dr. Arora’s observation that vog is "not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" is a statement of fact regarding the environment, but it does not address the legal standard of "contributing cause." Furthermore, Dr. Arora lacked access to Van Ness’s medical records at the time of his report, whereas Dr. Mathison’s opinion was based on a comprehensive review of Van Ness’s history.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Director erred as a matter of law by applying a "uniqueness" standard rather than the "contributing cause" standard established by *Kauai Elec. Co.* The evidence shows that Van Ness’s employment required him to exert himself physically in an environment with high levels of vog, which aggravated his asthmatic condition. Therefore, the injury arose out of and in the course of employment.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals of the opening arguments from both the Prosecution and the Defense. The issue presented is whether the Director erred in concluding that Van Ness’s injury did not arise out of and in the course of employment, and whether the evidence compels a finding of non-compensability even under the correct legal standard.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

Under Hawaii law, a claim for workers’ compensation benefits is compensable if the injury "arises out of and in the course of employment." The Hawaii Supreme Court has established a unitary test for proximate cause in this context. In *Kauai Elec. Co. v. Workers’ Comp. Comm’n*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), the Court held that the "arising out of" requirement "does not demand that the injury be caused solely by the employment or unique to the workplace; it requires only that employment was a contributing cause."

Where the injury is a disease, the compensability analysis is further informed by *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008). In *Flor*, the Court clarified that for injuries by disease, the test is whether the disease "resulted from the nature of the employment," meaning the employment must be a significant contributing factor. *Flor* does not impose a requirement that the environmental conditions causing the disease be exclusive to the workplace, but rather that the nature of the employment exposed the claimant to the risk in a manner that contributed to the injury.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to support Van Ness’s claim that his exposure occurred at the place of employment only. The Director concluded that because the vog was a general environmental condition, there was no nexus between the employment and the injury.

The Court agrees with the Prosecution that the Director applied an incorrect legal standard. By requiring Van Ness to demonstrate that his exposure to vog occurred "at this place of employment only," the Director imposed a "uniqueness" requirement that is explicitly rejected by *Kauai Elec. Co.* As stated in *Kauai Elec. Co.*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), the law does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace; it requires only that the employment was a contributing cause.

The Defense concedes this error but argues that the decision should nonetheless be affirmed because, even under the correct "contributing cause" standard, the evidence does not support a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a significant contributing cause of his injury. The Defense contends that the nature of Van Ness’s employment as a technology coordinator was sedentary, and that his respiratory injury was caused by general vog exposure on the island of Maui—particularly at his home in Kihei—rather than by his work duties.

The Court finds that the Defense’s arguments raise genuine issues of material fact that were not resolved by the Director under the correct legal standard. The Director’s finding of "insufficient evidence" was predicated on the erroneous "uniqueness" requirement. The Director did not make findings regarding whether the physical exertion required by Van Ness’s specific duties at Lahainaluna—climbing steep stairs and traversing a mountainous campus—constituted a "significant contributing factor" to his asthma exacerbation under *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008).

The record contains conflicting evidence on this point. Because the Director’s decision was based on an erroneous application of the law, the Court cannot determine whether the Director would have reached the same conclusion had the proper standard been applied.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Director erred as a matter of law by applying a "uniqueness" standard rather than the "contributing cause" standard established by *Kauai Elec. Co.* While the Defense raises valid arguments regarding the nature of the employment and the source of the vog exposure, these arguments present factual questions that were not addressed by the Director under the correct legal framework. Therefore, the injury’s compensability cannot be definitively determined on this record without a new analysis by the Director.

While the Prosecution has highlighted favorable testimony, the Defense respectfully submits that the totality of the evidence does not establish that Van Ness's employment was a "significant contributing factor" to his injury as required by *Flor v. Holguin*. Rather, the evidence demonstrates that Van Ness's respiratory injury resulted from general environmental exposure to vog on the island of Maui—a condition that was not peculiar to his employment—and that his job duties, while requiring some physical activity, were not the cause of his asthma exacerbation.

## II. LEGAL STANDARD

The Defense acknowledges that under *Kauai Elec. Co.*, employment need not be the sole cause of injury; it need only be a contributing cause. However, under *Flor v. Holguin*, where the injury is a disease, the employment must be a "significant contributing factor." This standard requires more than de minimis contribution—it demands that the employment expose the claimant to a risk that meaningfully contributed to the injury.

The Prosecution incorrectly suggests that *Flor v. Holguin* imposes a low threshold. To the contrary, the "significant contributing factor" test requires substantial evidence that the nature of the employment itself—not merely the claimant's presence in a geographic location—contributed to the disease. As this Court recognized in *Flor*, the employment must expose the claimant to risks "resulted from the nature of the employment."

## III. ANALYSIS

### A. The Nature of Van Ness's Employment Was Not a Significant Contributing Factor

The Prosecution repeatedly emphasizes that Van Ness was required to "climb steep stairs" at Lahainaluna. However, the record establishes that Van Ness's position as a technology coordinator was fundamentally sedentary in nature. His primary responsibility was maintaining and repairing computer equipment—a job that does not inherently require strenuous physical activity.

The Prosecution attempts to transform a routine job duty into a hazardous condition by emphasizing the physical geography of the campus. However, the record shows that Van Ness had alternatives. He testified that "it was easier to drive from the library down, around the campus, up to admin, than to actually walk down there." This admission demonstrates that transportation was available, and the choice to walk was exactly that—a choice. The Defense does not dispute that walking up stairs would be exerting for anyone, but the question under workers' compensation law is whether the employment required such exertion as part of its essential duties, not whether the employee chose to exert himself.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, the Hawaii Supreme Court required that the employment itself expose the claimant to a risk that contributed to the injury. The Court emphasized that "the nature of the employment" must be examined. Here, Van Ness was employed as a technology coordinator—not a construction worker, not a landscaper, not a messenger required to deliver packages uphill. His job description involved technical work, not physical labor. The Prosecution has not established that climbing stairs was an essential function of his position; rather, it was a consequence of the school's geography that Van Ness could mitigate through driving.

### B. Vog Exposure Was a General Environmental Condition, Not Specific to the Workplace

The Prosecution argues that because Van Ness's symptoms worsened during his employment on Maui, the employment must be a contributing cause. This logic fails because it ignores the fundamental principle that a claimant must demonstrate a nexus between the employment and the injury, not merely between the injury and the claimant's presence in a particular location.

The record establishes that vog was present across the entire island of Maui. Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness resided. Dr. Mathison's own report noted that Van Ness's symptoms improved when he left Maui—including his trip to Pennsylvania where his symptoms "essentially resolved"—but did not differentiate between vog exposure at work versus at home. The Prosecution cites Dr. Mathison's statement that Van Ness's symptoms worsened "specifically during his work at Lahainaluna," but this must be read in context: Van Ness was working at Lahainaluna *while* he was on Maui. The temporal correlation does not establish causation.

Dr. Mathison's report states that Van Ness's symptoms were triggered by "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and recommends that the employer avoid "assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure." However, this recommendation applies to geographic areas generally, not specifically to the Lahainaluna campus. The doctor did not state that the school was uniquely hazardous or that Van Ness's job duties exacerbated his condition beyond what general exposure would have caused.

The Prosecution argues that Van Ness was transferred to Maui *for employment* and therefore his presence there is attributable to his job. This argument misses the point. The question is not whether the employment brought Van Ness to Maui, but whether his specific work conditions—rather than general environmental conditions common to all residents—contributed to his injury. Under *Kauai Elec. Co.*, the hazard need not be unique to the workplace, but there must still be a nexus between the employment and the injury. Here, the evidence shows that Van Ness's exposure to vog was substantially similar whether he was at work, at home, or anywhere else on the island.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Support a Finding of Employment as a Significant Contributing Factor

The Prosecution relies heavily on Dr. Mathison's report. However, a careful reading of that report undermines rather than supports the claim of compensability. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis has largely been controlled with avoidances and the several-year course of immunotherapy during the early 90s" and that he "had significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke." The doctor attributed Van Ness's symptoms to "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution"—a general environmental condition—not to any workplace-specific factor.

Dr. Mathison's conclusion that Van Ness "can be considered to be disabled by virtue of his asthmatic tendency and susceptibility to the volcanic pollutant effects" does not establish that his employment was a significant contributing factor. The doctor recommended avoiding "assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure"—which includes all of Maui, not just Lahainaluna. This is consistent with the Defense's position that Van Ness's injury resulted from general vog exposure on the island, not from his specific employment conditions.

The Prosecution dismisses Dr. Arora's report as unreliable because he lacked medical records. However, Dr. Arora's observation that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" is nonetheless insightful and accurate. The Defense is not required to rely on a single physician's opinion when the totality of the evidence—including the testimony regarding Van Ness's residence in Kihei and the island-wide nature of vog—supports the conclusion that employment was not a significant contributing factor.

### D. Van Ness's Decision to Live in Kihei Was a Material Factor

The record establishes that Van Ness lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted that "the vog was even worse" than at Lahainaluna. This fact is material because it demonstrates that Van Ness's exposure to vog was not limited to his workplace. He voluntarily chose to live in an area with higher vog concentration, and his symptoms improved when he left Maui entirely.

The Prosecution argues that Van Ness's employment required him to live on Maui. This is not accurate. Van Ness was employed on Maui, but nothing in the record suggests that his employment required him to live in Kihei specifically. He could have lived in other areas of Maui with less vog exposure. His choice to reside in Kihei was a personal decision that contributed to his exposure to volcanic pollutants.

Under workers' compensation principles, the Defense is not liable for injuries that result from personal decisions unrelated to employment. See generally *Kauai Elec. Co.* (requiring that employment be a contributing cause). Here, the evidence suggests that Van Ness's residence in Kihei was at least as significant a factor in his asthma exacerbation as his employment.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The Prosecution's closing argument mischaracterizes the record in several respects.

First, the Prosecution claims that Van Ness "could drive, yes—but only because the school provided no transportation, and he had to choose between exhausting himself or risking his health." This is misleading. The record shows that Van Ness *chose* to walk the stairs, even though driving was an option. He testified that driving was "easier," suggesting he made a conscious decision not to drive. The Prosecution cannot transform a choice into a job requirement.

Second, the Prosecution argues that "the physical exertion required by Van Ness's job—climbing steep stairs daily in a high-vog environment—was a significant contributing factor." This argument fails because it assumes that climbing stairs was an essential job function. The record does not support this assumption. Van Ness's job was technology coordinator—a sedentary position. The stairs were a consequence of the school's geography, not a requirement of the job.

Third, the Prosecution cites Dr. Mathison's statement that Van Ness's symptoms "worsened since moving to Maui due to the higher vog exposure there." However, this statement supports the Defense, not the Prosecution. Dr. Mathison attributed the worsening to "moving to Maui," not to working at Lahainaluna. The Prosecution cannot selectively quote this statement to establish workplace causation when the statement itself points to general geographic exposure.

Fourth, the Prosecution argues that "the Director never made that finding because they were trapped by their own legal error." This is true, but it does not aid the Prosecution. On remand, the Director must make new findings under the correct standard. The Defense is entitled to have those findings based on a fair evaluation of the evidence, not a predetermined conclusion that employment must be a contributing cause.

## V. CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the denial of workers' compensation benefits. Even under the correct legal standard of "significant contributing factor" as established by *Flor v. Holguin*, the evidence does not support a finding that Van Ness's employment was a significant contributing cause of his respiratory injury.

The record demonstrates that Van Ness's asthma exacerbation resulted from general exposure to vog on the island of Maui—a condition that was not peculiar to his workplace. His job duties, while requiring some physical activity, were not the cause of his injury. His decision to live in Kihei, where vog was "even worse," was a personal choice that contributed to his exposure. Dr. Mathison's report attributes Van Ness's symptoms to "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" generally, not to any workplace-specific condition.

The Prosecution has not met its burden of establishing that Van Ness's employment was a significant contributing factor to his injury. The Director's denial should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense. The central issue is whether the Director’s denial of benefits should be reversed and remanded, or if the evidence compels a finding for either party under the correct legal standard.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The applicable legal standard is well-established. Under *Kauai Elec. Co. v. Workers’ Comp. Comm’n*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005), a compensable injury is one that "arises out of and in the course of employment." The "arising out of" requirement is satisfied if the employment was a "contributing cause" of the injury; it does not require that the injury be caused solely by the employment or that the risk be unique to the workplace.

Where the injury is a disease, as is the case here with Van Ness’s asthma exacerbation, the analysis is governed by *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008). In *Flor*, the court held that for an injury by disease to be compensable, the disease must "result from the nature of the employment," meaning the employment must be a "significant contributing factor" in the contraction or aggravation of the disease.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Legal Error Requires Reversal**

The Director denied the claim based on the finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was insufficient evidence to show exposure occurred "at this place of employment only." This reasoning imposes a "uniqueness" requirement that is explicitly rejected by *Kauai Elec. Co.*, 107 Haw. 202, 210, 112 P.3d 671, 679 (2005). The Defense conces this error. Therefore, the Director’s decision cannot stand and must be reversed.

**B. The Evidence Does Not Compels a Finding for Either Party**

The Prosecution argues that the evidence "compels" a finding of compensability because the physical exertion required by Van Ness’s job—climbing stairs at Lahainaluna—was a significant contributing factor to his injury under *Flor v. Holguin*, 118 Haw. 247, 256, 189 P.3d 477, 486 (2008). The Defense counters that the evidence does not compel such a finding, arguing that Van Ness’s job was sedentary, that he had the option to drive rather than climb stairs, and that his exposure to vog was greater at his home in Kihei than at work.

The Court finds that the evidence presents genuine issues of material fact that preclude granting summary judgment for either party. Specifically, there is a factual dispute regarding the "nature of the employment."

1.  **Physical Exertion as a Requirement vs. a Choice:**
    The Prosecution emphasizes the testimony that Van Ness was "required to repeatedly climb up and down the school stairs daily" due to the lack of golf carts and paved roads. However, the Defense highlights Van Ness’s own testimony that "it was easier to drive from the library down, around the campus, up to admin, than to actually walk down there." This creates a factual dispute regarding whether the strenuous physical exertion was an inherent requirement of his employment or a choice made by Van Ness despite available alternatives. Under *Flor*, the Director must determine if the *nature* of the employment—defined by its essential duties rather than the employee’s preferred methods of performing them—was a significant contributing factor.

2.  **Workplace vs. Residential Exposure:**
    The Prosecution relies on Dr. Mathison’s report, which notes that Van Ness’s symptoms worsened during the period of severe vog at Lahainaluna. However, the Defense points to the testimony of Vice Principal Dennis that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness resided. Furthermore, Dr. Mathison’s report links the symptoms to "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" generally and recommends avoiding "assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure," which suggests a geographic risk rather than a risk unique to the specific job duties. The Director must weigh this evidence to determine if the employment conditions were a "significant contributing factor" compared to the general environmental conditions of living on Maui.

Because the Director’s original findings were predicated on the erroneous "uniqueness" standard, the Director did not make the necessary factual findings regarding these disputes under the correct *Flor* standard. The Court cannot determine, as a matter of law, that the evidence compels a finding for the Prosecution, nor can it affirm the Director’s denial given the legal error and the existence of conflicting evidence.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director erred as a matter of law by applying a "uniqueness" standard to the vog exposure. While the Prosecution presents evidence that the physical demands of the job contributed to the injury, the Defense presents evidence that the exertion was optional and that residential exposure was more severe. These are factual questions that must be resolved by the Director under the correct legal framework established by *Kauai Elec. Co.* and *Flor v. Holguin*.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director shall apply the "significant contributing factor" test to determine whether the nature of Van Ness’s employment at Lahainaluna was a substantial cause of his respiratory injury, giving due consideration to the disputed issues of physical exertion and relative exposure.
