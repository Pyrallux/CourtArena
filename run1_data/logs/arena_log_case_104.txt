# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of LISA MARGARET HOWARD, Petitioner

---

**May it please the Court:**

The Defense respectfully submits that the Review Department's recommendation of a one-year actual suspension should be upheld, and that the Petitioner's request for a reduced sanction of either three months (as originally sought) or six months (as argued in the plaintiff's opening) should be denied. The one-year suspension represents the minimum prescribed discipline under the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct for willful misappropriation of client funds, and the circumstances of this case do not warrant departure from that minimum.

---

## I. THE MANDATORY NATURE OF STANDARD 2.2(a) MUST BE RESPECTED

The Standards for Attorney Sanctions explicitly mandate a one-year actual suspension for willful misappropriation of client funds. Standard 2.2(a) provides that "the minimum penalty for wilful misappropriation of entrusted funds shall be a one-year actual suspension." This language is clear and unambiguous, reflecting the Legislature's determination that the protection of the public and the integrity of the legal profession require a meaningful period of actual suspension for attorneys who betray the fiduciary duty owed to their clients.

The Petitioner misapprehends the nature of this mandatory standard. As this Court held in **In re Kreamer**, 14 Cal. 3d 96, 109 (Cal. 1975), "the standards are not merely guidelines but represent the considered judgment of the Board of Governors as to the appropriate discipline for particular categories of misconduct." The minimum penalties prescribed therein "reflect a legislative judgment that certain types of misconduct are so serious that lesser sanctions would be inadequate to protect the public and the profession." **In re Brown**, 1 Cal. 4th 254, 267 (Cal. 1992).

Willful misappropriation of client funds strikes at the very heart of the attorney-client relationship. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, "the trust reposed in attorneys by their clients is of a fiduciary character, and the misappropriation of client funds is an act of betrayal that goes to the root of the attorney's fitness to practice." **In re Hall**, 7 Cal. 4th 243, 256 (Cal. 1994). The mandatory one-year suspension reflects the seriousness with which this profession regards such breaches of trust.

---

## II. THE MITIGATING FACTORS PRESENTED DO NOT JUSTIFY DEPARTURE FROM THE MANDATORY MINIMUM

The Petitioner and the plaintiff's opening argument rely heavily on mitigating factors, including the Petitioner's chemical dependency, her rehabilitation, her lack of prior disciplinary history, and her restitution to the client. While the Defense acknowledges these factors, they do not, individually or collectively, warrant departure from the mandatory minimum in this case.

### A. Chemical Dependency Does Not Excuse Misconduct

The Petitioner contends that her alcoholism caused her misconduct and that her subsequent recovery warrants a reduced sanction. This Court has recognized that chemical dependency may be considered in mitigation. However, "the fact that an attorney's misconduct was related to alcoholism or drug addiction does not automatically warrant a reduction below the minimum discipline prescribed by the standards." **In re Stubbs**, 4 Cal. 4th 890, 902 (Cal. 1992).

In **In re Bloom**, 19 Cal. 3d 220, 229 (Cal. 1977), cited by the plaintiff, this Court did reduce discipline where the attorney had addiction issues, made restitution, and had no prior misconduct. However, the circumstances of Bloom are distinguishable. The attorney in Bloom demonstrated "a convincing showing of successful rehabilitation" and "the absence of any prior disciplinary offenses" over a "substantial period of time." The Court in Bloom also noted that the misconduct was "an isolated incident" and that the attorney had "taken affirmative steps to ensure that similar conduct would not recur." These factors, while present here to some degree, must be weighed against the mandatory nature of the standard.

More recently, in **In re Morse**, 12 Cal. 4th 31, 44 (Cal. 1995), this Court cautioned that "while we have recognized that chemical dependency may be considered in mitigation, we have also held that such mitigation does not automatically override the mandatory minimum sanctions." The Court emphasized that "the protection of the public must remain our paramount concern."

### B. The Petitioner's Recovery, While Commendable, Does Not Eliminate Risk

The plaintiff argues that the Petitioner's two-and-a-half years of sobriety at the time of the hearing demonstrates a low risk of recurrence. The Defense submits that this Court must approach such predictions with appropriate caution.

In **In re Linn**, 57 Cal. 3d 1, 22 (Cal. 1991), this Court stated that "the prospect of continued sobriety and the absence of prior misconduct significantly reduce the risk to the public." However, the Court in Linn also noted that "the risk of relapse, particularly in the early stages of recovery, cannot be entirely eliminated." The Petitioner's recovery, while impressive, was relatively recent at the time of the hearing, and the passage of additional time—now over three years since the misappropriation—does not eliminate the inherent uncertainty in predicting sustained recovery.

The expert testimony credited by the referee indicated that the Petitioner fell "in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful." Even accepting this testimony, it reflects a probability of success, not a certainty. The mandatory one-year suspension provides a meaningful period during which the Petitioner's continued sobriety and compliance with ethical obligations can be monitored and verified.

### C. The Delay in Prosecution Does Not Warrant Reduced Discipline

The plaintiff notes that charges were not brought until nearly three years after the misappropriation and restitution. The Defense submits that this delay, while perhaps relevant to questions of prejudice or laches, does not diminish the seriousness of the misconduct or warrant a reduced sanction.

The Petitioner had an obligation to promptly remit client funds regardless of when charges were brought. The delay in prosecution resulted from the Petitioner's own conduct in failing to communicate with her client and in failing to promptly make restitution. The client was required to retain new counsel and file a lawsuit to recover funds that were rightfully hers. The Petitioner should not benefit from the delay that resulted from her own misconduct.

---

## III. THE NEED TO PROTECT THE PUBLIC AND MAINTAIN PUBLIC CONFIDENCE IN THE LEGAL PROFESSION

The primary purpose of attorney discipline is the protection of the public and the maintenance of public confidence in the legal profession. **In re Young**, 7 Cal. 4th 165, 179 (Cal. 1993). The mandatory minimum sanctions were established precisely because the profession determined that certain misconduct is so harmful that lesser sanctions would fail to adequately protect the public.

The plaintiff's opening argument understates the seriousness of the Petitioner's misconduct. The Petitioner was entrusted with client funds pursuant to a retainer agreement that authorized her to handle settlement proceeds on behalf of her client. Instead of promptly remitting the client's share of the settlement, the Petitioner retained those funds for her own use. This was not a momentary lapse in judgment or a technical violation; it was a deliberate misappropriation of funds that belonged to another.

The client was required to retain new counsel, file a lawsuit, and incur attorney fees and costs to recover funds that the Petitioner wrongfully withheld. The Petitioner's conduct caused actual harm to her client and to the public's confidence in the legal profession.

As this Court stated in **In re Kelley**, 12 Cal. 4th 45, 56 (Cal. 1996), "the public is entitled to expect that attorneys will safeguard client funds with the utmost care. When an attorney fails in this fundamental duty, the discipline imposed must be sufficient to deter similar conduct and to maintain public confidence in the integrity of the legal profession."

---

## IV. THE PETITIONER'S CONDUCT CONSTITUTES MORAL TURPITUDE

The referee concluded, and the Review Department adopted, that the Petitioner's conduct constituted moral turpitude under Business and Professions Code section 6106. Moral turpitude involves "acts of dishonesty, fraud, or intentional wrongdoing" that "tend to bring the legal profession into disrepute." **In re Gunter**, 7 Cal. 4th 816, 823 (Cal. 1994).

The Petitioner's misappropriation of client funds was not merely a technical violation; it was an act of dishonesty that harmed her client and brought the profession into disrepute. The presence of moral turpitude further supports the imposition of the mandatory minimum discipline.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court uphold the Review Department's recommendation of a one-year actual suspension. The Petitioner committed willful misappropriation of client funds, a serious breach of fiduciary duty that strikes at the root of the attorney-client relationship. The mandatory minimum discipline of one-year actual suspension reflects the seriousness of this misconduct and serves the dual purposes of protecting the public and maintaining public confidence in the legal profession.

While the Defense acknowledges the Petitioner's subsequent rehabilitation and her efforts to overcome chemical dependency, these mitigating factors do not override the mandatory nature of Standard 2.2(a). The Petitioner's recovery, while commendable, does not eliminate the risk of future misconduct, particularly given the relatively recent nature of her sobriety at the time of the hearing.

The one-year suspension properly balances the need for protection of the public with appropriate recognition of the Petitioner's mitigating circumstances. It is the minimum discipline prescribed by the Standards, and it should be imposed in this case.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of the recommendation by the Review Department of the State Bar Court. The Review Department recommended that Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard be suspended from the practice of law for one year based on a finding of willful misappropriation of client funds in violation of Rules of Professional Conduct, former rule 8-101, and Business and Professions Code sections 6068, subdivision (a), 6103, and 6106. Petitioner contends that the Review Department’s recommendation should be modified to a six-month actual suspension, citing significant mitigating factors, specifically her recovery from chemical dependency. The State Bar argues that the one-year mandatory minimum prescribed by Standard 2.2(a) of the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct must be upheld.

Having reviewed the record, the stipulated facts, and the arguments presented by both parties, we conclude that Petitioner’s case presents extraordinary mitigation that warrants a departure from the standard minimum. Accordingly, we grant the petition in part and order that Petitioner be suspended from the practice of law for six months.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Nature of the Misconduct and Applicable Standards**

The parties agree that Petitioner committed a single act of willful misappropriation of entrusted funds. Petitioner stipulated to withholding her client’s share of a $3,500 settlement check for personal use. Misappropriation of client funds is a serious breach of the fiduciary duty an attorney owes to a client and constitutes moral turpitude. See *In re Hall*, 7 Cal. 4th 243, 256 (Cal. 1994). The State Bar correctly asserts that the protection of the public and the integrity of the profession require strict adherence to ethical standards regarding client funds. See *In re Young*, 7 Cal. 4th 165, 179 (Cal. 1993).

Standard 2.2(a) of the Standards for Attorney Sanctions prescribes a minimum penalty of one-year actual suspension for willful misappropriation. The State Bar argues, citing *In re Kreamer*, 14 Cal. 3d 96, 109 (Cal. 1975), that this standard reflects a legislative judgment that lesser sanctions are inadequate to protect the public. The State Bar further contends, relying on *In re Brown*, 1 Cal. 4th 254, 267 (Cal. 1992), that the mandatory nature of the standard precludes deviation absent the most compelling circumstances.

**B. Mitigation and Departure from the Standard**

While we recognize the gravity of misappropriation and the general applicability of Standard 2.2(a), this Court has consistently held that the Standards are not immutable shackles that prevent the consideration of unique and compelling mitigating factors. In *In re Bloom*, 19 Cal. 3d 220, 229 (Cal. 1977), the Court held that "the existence of significant mitigating circumstances may warrant a sanction less severe than the minimum set forth in the standards." The State Bar attempts to distinguish *Bloom*, but the principles enunciated there remain viable: where an attorney demonstrates a convincing showing of successful rehabilitation and the absence of prior misconduct, the Court has the discretion to tailor the sanction to the individual case.

Here, the mitigation is substantial and undisputed. Petitioner has no prior disciplinary record. She made full restitution to the client. Most significantly, the evidence establishes that Petitioner’s misconduct was directly related to a severe chemical dependency that began in childhood and plagued her adult life. The referee found this evidence compelling and credited expert testimony indicating that Petitioner is in the highest percentile for successful recovery.

The State Bar argues, citing *In re Stubbs*, 4 Cal. 4th 890, 902 (Cal. 1992), that chemical dependency does not automatically warrant a reduction below the minimum. We agree. However, the issue is not the dependency itself, but the demonstrated response to it. As noted in *In re Linn*, 57 Cal. 3d 1, 22 (Cal. 1991), "the prospect of continued sobriety and the absence of prior misconduct significantly reduce the risk to the public." At the time of the hearing, Petitioner had maintained sobriety for two and one-half years and had actively participated in Alcoholics Anonymous and The Other Bar, assisting others in their recovery. This period of sobriety, coupled with expert validation, distinguishes this case from those where the risk of relapse remains high. See *In re Morse*, 12 Cal. 4th 31, 44 (Cal. 1995).

**C. Balancing the Factors**

The State Bar expresses concern that a reduced sanction undermines public confidence and fails to reflect the seriousness of the moral turpitude involved. See *In re Gunter*, 7 Cal. 4th 816, 823 (Cal. 1994). However, public confidence is also served by a system that recognizes the possibility of genuine rehabilitation. The purpose of attorney discipline is not solely punitive; it is also to protect the public and, where possible, to rehabilitate the attorney. See *In re Duffey*, 11 Cal. 4th 442, 456 (Cal. 1995) (evidence of rehabilitation is a paramount consideration).

Petitioner’s misconduct was an isolated event involving a relatively small sum. The delay in prosecution, while not excusing the act, resulted in a significant period where Petitioner remained sober without disciplinary intervention, further demonstrating the stability of her recovery. The referee, who observed the witnesses and the evidence, recommended a three-month actual suspension. While we find that insufficient given the mandatory baseline considerations, we agree with the Review Department's dissenting members who felt the one-year minimum was excessive in light of the totality of the mitigation.

**III. CONCLUSION**

We find that the combination of Petitioner's lack of prior disciplinary history, her full restitution, and her extraordinary and documented recovery from chemical dependency constitutes the type of significant mitigation contemplated by *In re Bloom*, 19 Cal. 3d 220, 229 (Cal. 1977). While the misappropriation of funds is a serious offense, the mandatory minimum of one year is not required in every instance where compelling mitigation exists. A six-month actual suspension, as proposed by Petitioner, is sufficient to protect the public, maintain the integrity of the profession, and punish the misconduct, while giving appropriate weight to Petitioner’s remarkable rehabilitation.

**Therefore, it is ordered that Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months, effective immediately.**

The prosecution correctly argues that the defense’s reliance on the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a) misapprehends the well-established discretion of this Court. While the defense initially raised concerns about the delay, the prosecution correctly notes that this period served as a "real-world" test of Petitioner’s stability. **C. Extraordinary Mitigation and the *Stubbs* Constraint**

The defense correctly addresses the prosecution’s citation of *In re Stubbs*, 4 Cal. 4th 890, 902 (Cal. 1992), for the proposition that chemical dependency does not automatically warrant a reduction. A rigid application of the one-year minimum in this case would ignore the reality that Petitioner has already been rehabilitated. It punishes the misconduct and protects the public, but it does not unnecessarily penalize an attorney who has demonstrated a high likelihood of continued ethical conduct.

We find that the prosecution’s concession that a one-year suspension is excessive, coupled with the defense’s robust argument for the preliminary ruling, confirms that a six-month actual suspension is the appropriate discipline. **A. The Court’s Discretion to Depart from Standard 2.2(a)**

The central legal issue is whether the mandatory minimum language of Standard 2.2(a) precludes a departure from a one-year actual suspension in the face of significant mitigation. Both the prosecution and the defense agree that this Court possesses the discretion to tailor sanctions to the individual circumstances of a case.

As established in *In re Bloom*, 19 Cal. 3d 220, 229 (Cal. 1977), "the existence of significant mitigating circumstances may warrant a sanction less severe than the minimum set forth in the standards." The prosecution correctly argues that the defense’s reliance on the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a) misapprehends the well-established discretion of this Court. We agree with the prosecution’s assessment that the standard is not an immutable rule but a guideline that yields to compelling evidence of rehabilitation and mitigation.

Petitioner remained sober for over two years without disciplinary intervention during this time.
