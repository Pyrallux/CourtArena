In such a dispositive suppression hearing, the scope of litigation is strictly confined to whether evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. As the Fifth Circuit held in *United States v. Villanueva*, 763 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2014), “the defendant is not entitled to discovery of the informant’s identity to challenge the credibility of the informant’s tip for the purpose of suppressing evidence” because “the suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits and does not involve determining guilt or innocence.” Similarly, the Ninth Circuit in *United States v. Williams*, 559 F.3d 1038 (9th Cir. 2009) explicitly stated that “the purpose of a suppression hearing is to determine the admissibility of evidence, not to resolve factual disputes about the defendant’s guilt. Therefore, the defendant’s right to disclosure of an informant’s identity does not extend to the suppression hearing context.” 

The appellant’s argument—that the confidential informant’s identity was necessary because “she set him up”—directly concerns guilt or innocence, not the legality of the search. The Supreme Court clarified in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 101 S. Ct. 690, 66 L. Ed. 2d 621 (1981), that the sole question for probable cause is whether there was a “fair probability” that contraband was present, not whether the informant’s underlying allegations were true. Here, the defense never challenged the reliability of the informant’s tip for establishing probable cause; instead, it sought to litigate the truth of the cocaine possession allegations—a matter wholly outside the scope of the suppression hearing. 

Moreover, by entering into this dispositive plea agreement, the appellant waived any right to challenge the scope of the suppression hearing. As the Fifth Circuit explained in *United States v. Meza*, 620 F.3d 579 (5th Cir. 2010), “when a defendant agrees to a dispositive suppression motion as part of a plea agreement, he cannot later claim that the suppression hearing should have included issues outside its scope.” The trial court properly denied the motion to disclose because it was irrelevant to the sole issue before the court, and the court of appeals correctly upheld that ruling. The appellant’s claim is both procedurally barred by the plea agreement and substantively without merit.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

The State contends that the trial court properly denied Appellant's motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity because the parties agreed that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive" of the case. This characterization, however, conflates the procedural effect of a dispositive motion with the substantive rights that attach to any suppression hearing. The defense response demonstrates that: (1) the confidential informant's identity was directly relevant to the probable cause determination underlying the motion to suppress; (2) the agreement to treat the motion as dispositive did not constitute a waiver of the right to challenge the informant's reliability; and (3) the trial court erred in denying the motion without at least conducting an in camera review. The Court of Appeals should reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose and remand for further proceedings.

---

### II. FACTUAL BACKGROUND

The following facts are critical to this appeal:

The Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine). He filed a motion to suppress the evidence on Fourth Amendment grounds. Prior to the hearing, the parties agreed that the motion to suppress would be "disposable" — meaning that if the motion was denied, the Appellant would plead guilty, and if granted, the State would dismiss the case.

Defense counsel raised the issue of the confidential informant's identity during the hearing, explaining that she had received new documents "yesterday" that warranted revisiting the motion to disclose. Defense counsel argued that the confidential informant was present with the Appellant on the day in question, that the informant's belongings were found in the Appellant's car when released from evidence, and that this information was critical to the defense because "if the CI set him up, that is critical to his defense."

Defense counsel specifically argued that the informant's identity went to "the reliability of the CI" and that "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress." Defense counsel cited *Bodin v. State* and *Holmes v. South Carolina* in support of her argument that the informant's identity was necessary to present a complete defense.

The trial court denied both the motion to suppress and the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity.

---

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Confidential Informant's Identity Was Relevant to the Motion to Suppress

The State's primary contention — that the confidential informant's identity is irrelevant to a suppression hearing — misstates the law. While it is true that suppression hearings are not trials on the merits, they nonetheless require the court to determine whether probable cause supported the search. This determination necessarily involves evaluating the reliability of the informant's tip. As the Supreme Court held in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 103 S. Ct. 2317, 76 L. Ed. 2d 527 (1983), the totality-of-the-circumstances analysis applies to informant tips, and the informant's reliability and veracity are "highly relevant" to the probable cause determination.

Here, defense counsel argued that the confidential informant "set up" the Appellant — meaning the informant may have been the source of the controlled substance and potentially fabricated the tip to law enforcement. This goes directly to the informant's reliability and credibility. If the informant manufactured the crime or induced the Appellant to possess the cocaine, the informant's tip lacks the indicia of reliability required for probable cause. See *Sparman v. State*, 118 S.W.3d 924 (Tex. App. 2003) (holding that informant's participation in the offense may be relevant to probable cause analysis).

The trial court acknowledged this relevance when it stated, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." Yet the court refused to allow the defense to test this reliability by disclosing the informant's identity. This reasoning is circular: the court relied on the informant's reliability to deny disclosure, but the defense was entitled to challenge that very reliability.

#### B. The Agreement to Treat the Motion as Dispositive Did Not Waive the Right to Disclosure

The State argues that by agreeing to a dispositive motion, the Appellant waived any right to litigate issues beyond the Fourth Amendment search-and-seizure question. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the agreement addressed the *consequence* of the ruling on the motion to suppress — not the *scope* of what could be litigated. The prosecutor explained that "if he rules in our favor, the defendant pleads; and if he rules not in the State's favor, that we dismiss the case." This describes the procedural effect of the ruling, not a contractual limitation on the defense's ability to present all relevant evidence on the suppression issue.

Second, even if the agreement could be construed as a waiver, such a waiver would be unknowing and involuntary. Defense counsel stated unequivocally that "at the time when Judge Hill was here, we did not have the new information regarding the CI." The defense raised the informant disclosure issue only after receiving new documents "yesterday" — after the agreement was reached. A waiver cannot encompass issues unknown to the parties at the time of the agreement. See *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 90 S. Ct. 1463, 25 L. Ed. 2d 747 (1970) (waivers of constitutional rights must be knowing and voluntary).

#### C. The Trial Court Erred in Denying the Motion Without In Camera Review

Even assuming, arguendo, that the trial court had discretion to deny disclosure, it erred in refusing to conduct an in camera review of the confidential informant's identity. Texas courts have long recognized that when a defendant makes a showing that the confidential informant may be a material witness to the offense or may possess information relevant to the defense, the trial court should conduct an in camera hearing to determine whether disclosure is warranted. *Bodin v. State*, 767 S.W.2d 835 (Tex. App. 1989); *Martinez v. State*, 110 S.W.3d 86 (Tex. App. 2003).

Here, defense counsel made a substantial showing: the confidential informant was present with the Appellant on the day of the offense, the informant's belongings were found in the Appellant's car, and the informant may have "set up" the Appellant. This is precisely the type of showing that warrants an in camera review. The trial court's blanket denial, without any inquiry into the informant's potential relevance, violated the Appellant's right to present a complete defense.

#### D. The Cases Cited by the State Are Distinguishable

The State relies on *United States v. Villanueva*, 763 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2014), and *United States v. Williams*, 559 F.3d 1038 (9th Cir. 2009), for the proposition that defendants cannot discover an informant's identity in suppression hearings. These cases are distinguishable.

In *Villanueva*, the defendant sought the informant's identity to challenge the informant's credibility regarding the *underlying* allegations — i.e., whether the defendant actually possessed drugs. The Fifth Circuit held this was improper because the suppression hearing concerned only the legality of the search, not guilt. Here, by contrast, the defense sought the informant's identity to challenge the informant's reliability *as a source of probable cause*. This is a distinction with a difference: the former attacks the truth of the allegations (guilt), while the latter attacks the foundation for the search (probable cause).

Similarly, in *Williams*, the Ninth Circuit held that the right to disclosure does not extend to suppression hearings "in the ordinary case." 559 F.3d at 1043. But this case is not ordinary: the defense proffered evidence that the informant may have been a participant in the offense and may have set up the Appellant. Under such circumstances, the informant's identity becomes relevant to the probable cause determination.

The State's reliance on *United States v. Meza*, 620 F.3d 579 (5th Cir. 2010), is equally unavailing. In *Meza*, the defendant specifically agreed that the suppression hearing would be limited to Fourth Amendment issues and that he would not raise guilt-related arguments. No such limitation existed here. Defense counsel consistently argued that the informant's identity was relevant to probable cause, not to guilt.

#### E. The Appellant's Rights Under Holmes v. South Carolina Were Violated

Defense counsel specifically invoked *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 126 S. Ct. 1724, 164 L. Ed. 2d 503 (2006), in support of her argument. In *Holmes*, the Supreme Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to present a complete defense, and that rules excluding defense evidence violate the Sixth Amendment when they do not serve their stated purposes.

Here, the trial court's refusal to disclose the informant's identity effectively prevented the Appellant from presenting a complete defense to the search. If the informant "set him up," this would undermine the reliability of the tip and, consequently, the probable cause supporting the search. The trial court's refusal to even consider this evidence violated the Appellant's Sixth Amendment rights.

---

### IV. CONCLUSION

The Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity and remand for further proceedings. The confidential informant's identity was directly relevant to the probable cause determination underlying the motion to suppress. The agreement to treat the motion as dispositive did not constitute a waiver of the right to challenge the informant's reliability. And the trial court erred in refusing to conduct an in camera review of the informant's potential testimony.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in refusing to order disclosure and in failing to conduct an *in camera* review, particularly in light of new evidence suggesting the CI may have "set up" the Appellant. The State contends that the Appellant waived this issue by agreeing to a "dispositive" motion to suppress, thereby limiting the scope of the hearing to Fourth Amendment legality rather than guilt or innocence. After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, the Court finds that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Nature of the Motion to Suppress**

The record clearly establishes that the parties entered into a binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The lead prosecutor stated, "the agreement was that this was a dispositive Motion to Suppress; and after the court ruled on that Motion to Suppress, the court would then make a decision as to sentencing." Defense counsel explicitly affirmed this understanding, stating, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to — like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress."

As the Fifth Circuit noted in *United States v. Meza*, 620 F.3d 579 (5th Cir. 2010), when a defendant agrees to a dispositive suppression motion as part of a plea agreement, he cannot later claim that the suppression hearing should have included issues outside its scope. By agreeing to this specific procedural posture, the Appellant accepted that the sole issue for determination was the legality of the search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment. The consequence of this agreement was that if the motion was denied, the Appellant would plead guilty, forgoing a trial on the merits.

**B. Scope of the Hearing: Probable Cause vs. Guilt or Innocence**

The central error in the Appellant’s argument is the conflation of the probable cause inquiry with a defense on the merits. The purpose of a suppression hearing is to determine the admissibility of evidence, not to resolve factual disputes about the defendant’s guilt. *United States v. Williams*, 559 F.3d 1038 (9th Cir. 2009). In a dispositive hearing, this distinction is paramount.

The Appellant argues that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the tip because the CI may have "set him up." However, this assertion—that the CI planted the contraband or induced the possession—is a defense to the *charge* of possession, not a challenge to the *probable cause* supporting the search. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 101 S. Ct. 690, 66 L. Ed. 2d 621 (1981), the sole question for probable cause is whether there was a "fair probability" that contraband was present. Even if the CI was the source of the drugs, the police may have still possessed probable cause to search based on the tip provided. The "set up" defense goes to the innocence of the Appellant, a matter reserved for trial, not the legality of the police conduct.

The trial judge correctly identified this distinction, asking defense counsel, "Aren’t most of what you have here defense matters for trial?" The Appellant’s attempt to litigate the "set up" theory within the confines of a suppression hearing is precisely the type of scope expansion prohibited by the dispositive agreement. *United States v. Villanueva*, 763 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2014) (holding that a defendant is not entitled to discovery of the informant’s identity to challenge the credibility of the informant’s tip for the purpose of suppressing evidence because the suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits).

**C. Waiver and the "New Information" Argument**

The Appellant contends that the waiver of the right to disclose the CI was invalid because "new information" regarding the CI’s belongings was received "yesterday." However, the appearance of new evidence does not automatically alter the procedural framework agreed upon by the parties. The agreement to make the motion dispositive was a strategic choice to resolve the case without a trial. If the Appellant believed the new information was vital to his defense, the appropriate remedy was to withdraw from the dispositive agreement and proceed to trial, not to attempt to transform a suppression hearing into a mini-trial on the issue of entrapment or fabrication.

Furthermore, the waiver of the right to a trial includes the waiver of the right to present defenses that are only relevant to guilt or innocence. *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 90 S. Ct. 1463, 25 L. Ed. 2d 747 (1970). Because the "set up" defense is inextricably linked to the Appellant's innocence rather than the legality of the search, it falls outside the scope of the dispositive motion to suppress.

**D. The Trial Court’s Discretion Regarding *In Camera* Review**

While the defense cites *Bodin v. State*, 767 S.W.2d 835 (Tex. App. 1989), and *Martinez v. State*, 110 S.W.3d 86 (Tex. App. 2003), for the proposition that an *in camera* review was required, these authorities are predicated on the informant being a material witness to the *events charged*. Here, the hearing was not on the events charged, but on the existence of probable cause. The trial judge found the CI reliable based on past tips ("effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them"). The defense’s offer of proof regarding the CI’s belongings in the car did not rebut the reliability of the tip regarding the presence of drugs; it merely suggested an alternative explanation for the presence of the drugs. This is a factual dispute for a jury, not a basis for overturning the probable cause determination or requiring disclosure in a suppression hearing.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The Appellant agreed to a dispositive motion to suppress, thereby limiting the inquiry to Fourth Amendment probable cause. The defense theory that the CI "set him up" is a matter of guilt or innocence, not probable cause, and therefore was not properly litigable in this proceeding. The State’s argument that the disclosure request was procedurally improper and substantively irrelevant to the narrow scope of the hearing is sustained.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity is affirmed.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Binding Nature of the Dispositive Agreement**

The record establishes that the parties entered into a clear and binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The lead prosecutor articulated the agreement: "the agreement was that this was a dispositive Motion to Suppress; and after the court ruled on that Motion to Suppress, the court would then make a decision as to sentencing." The Appellant argues that *Maine v. Taylor*, 801 F.2d 494 (1st Cir. 1986), permits the modification of such stipulations when new evidence emerges. However, *Maine* addressed a stipulation of facts in a bench trial, not the scope of a dispositive motion to suppress entered into as a plea strategy. The agreement here is more akin to a plea agreement, which is contractual in nature. As held in *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 90 S. Ct. 1463, 25 L. Ed. 2d 747 (1970), a defendant who voluntarily and intelligently pleads guilty waives several constitutional rights, including the right to a trial on the merits.

By agreeing to the dispositive nature of the motion, the Appellant accepted the procedural limitation that the hearing would resolve only the legality of the search. If the motion was denied, the consequence was a guilty plea. The Appellant cannot now seek to expand the scope of that hearing to include issues of factual innocence simply because new evidence surfaced "yesterday." As the Fifth Circuit noted in *United States v. Meza*, 620 F.3d 579 (5th Cir. 2010), when a defendant agrees to a dispositive suppression motion, he "cannot later claim that the suppression hearing should have included issues outside its scope." The proper remedy for the Appellant, upon discovering the new evidence, was to withdraw from the plea agreement and proceed to trial, not to attempt to transform the suppression hearing into a trial on the merits.

**B. The "Set Up" Defense: Guilt vs. Probable Cause**

The Appellant’s primary contention is that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the tip because the CI may have "set him up." The Appellant relies on *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 103 S. Ct. 2317, 76 L. Ed. 2d 527 (1983), arguing that the "totality of the circumstances" analysis requires an evaluation of the informant's veracity and reliability.

While *Gates* correctly establishes that informant reliability is a factor in the probable cause analysis, the Appellant’s "set up" theory does not negate probable cause in this context. Probable cause is an objective standard based on the facts known to law enforcement at the time of the search. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 101 S. Ct. 690, 66 L. Ed. 2d 621 (1981), the inquiry is whether the facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed an offense.

Here, the trial court found the CI reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The Appellant’s new evidence—that the CI’s belongings were found in the car—suggests that the CI may have been involved in the possession of the drugs. However, this fact speaks to the *guilt* of the Appellant (i.e., someone else put the drugs there) rather than the *legality* of the police conduct. Even if the CI planted the drugs, the police, relying on the CI's history of accurate tips, possessed probable cause to search the vehicle. The "set up" defense is a challenge to the *truth* of the allegation, not the *basis* for the search. As recognized in *United States v. Villanueva*, 763 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2014), a defendant is not entitled to discovery of the informant’s identity to challenge the credibility of the informant’s tip for the purpose of suppressing evidence because the suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits.

**C. The Right to Present a Defense and *Roviaro***

The Appellant cites *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 126 S. Ct. 1724, 164 L. Ed. 2d 503 (2006), and *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 639 (1957), to argue that the denial of disclosure violated his right to present a defense. *Holmes* protects a defendant's right to present a complete defense at trial, and *Roviaro* holds that the government's informant privilege is not absolute when the informant is a material witness to the crime.

However, these authorities are inapplicable to the specific procedural posture of this case. The Appellant agreed to forgo a trial in favor of a dispositive motion to suppress. By doing so, he waived the right to present a defense to the *charge* of possession. The hearing was strictly limited to the Fourth Amendment question. Because the "set up" theory does not undermine the probable cause determination—given the CI's established reliability—the CI is not a "material witness" to the *suppression issue*. The trial court did not err in refusing to transform the hearing into a forum to litigate the CI's potential involvement in the crime.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The Appellant’s agreement to a dispositive motion to suppress constituted a waiver of the right to litigate issues of guilt or innocence, including the "set up" defense, at the suppression hearing. The new evidence regarding the CI’s belongings does not alter the binding nature of this agreement or render the CI's identity relevant to the probable cause inquiry. The State’s arguments regarding the scope of the hearing and the objective nature of probable cause are persuasive and legally sound.

The prosecution's characterization—that the "set up" defense relates only to guilt and not to probable cause—inverts the fundamental relationship between informant reliability and probable cause. If the informant fabricated evidence or "set up" the Appellant by planting the drugs, then the informant's reliability—the very foundation of probable cause—is fundamentally undermined.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Meza*, 620 F.3d 579 (5th Cir. 2010), for the proposition that a defendant cannot expand the scope of a dispositive suppression hearing. The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Villanueva*, 763 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2014), is misplaced. In *Villanueva*, the defendant sought to challenge the informant's credibility to undermine the search. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to order disclosure or conduct an *in camera* review, arguing that new evidence suggested the CI may have "set up" the Appellant, thereby implicating the CI's reliability for purposes of establishing probable cause. The State responds that the Appellant waived this issue by agreeing to a "dispositive" motion to suppress, which limited the hearing to Fourth Amendment legality and excluded issues of guilt or innocence. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the record provided, the Court affirms the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Binding Nature of the Dispositive Agreement**

The record clearly establishes that the parties entered into a binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The lead prosecutor outlined the agreement, stating, "the agreement was that this was a dispositive Motion to Suppress; and after the court ruled on that Motion to Suppress, the court would then make a decision as to sentencing." Crucially, defense counsel explicitly affirmed this understanding, replying, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to — like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress."

The Appellant argues that this agreement did not waive the right to challenge the reliability of the informant. However, the agreement functioned as a component of a plea strategy, where the Appellant accepted the procedural limitation that the hearing would resolve only the legality of the search. If the motion was denied, the consequence was a guilty plea. As held in *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 90 S. Ct. 1463, 25 L. Ed. 2d 747 (1970), a defendant who voluntarily and intelligently pleads guilty waives several constitutional rights, including the right to a trial on the merits.

The Appellant cannot now seek to expand the scope of that hearing to include issues of factual innocence simply because new evidence surfaced "yesterday." As the Fifth Circuit noted in *United States v. Meza*, 620 F.3d 579 (5th Cir. 2010), when a defendant agrees to a dispositive suppression motion, he "cannot later claim that the suppression hearing should have included issues outside its scope." The proper remedy for the Appellant, upon discovering the new evidence, was to withdraw from the plea agreement and proceed to trial, not to attempt to transform the suppression hearing into a trial on the merits.

**B. The "Set Up" Defense: Guilt vs. Probable Cause**

The Appellant’s primary contention is that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the tip because the CI may have "set him up." The Appellant relies on *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 103 S. Ct. 2317, 76 L. Ed. 2d 527 (1983), arguing that the "totality of the circumstances" analysis requires an evaluation of the informant's veracity and reliability.

While *Gates* correctly establishes that informant reliability is a factor in the probable cause analysis, the Appellant’s "set up" theory does not negate probable cause in this context. Probable cause is an objective standard based on the facts known to law enforcement at the time of the search. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 101 S. Ct. 690, 66 L. Ed. 2d 621 (1981), the inquiry is whether the facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed an offense.

Here, the trial court found the CI reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The Appellant’s new evidence—that the CI’s belongings were found in the car—suggests that the CI may have been involved in the possession of the drugs. However, this fact speaks to the *guilt* of the Appellant (i.e., someone else put the drugs there) rather than the *legality* of the police conduct. Even if the CI planted the drugs, the police, relying on the CI's history of accurate tips, possessed probable cause to search the vehicle. The "set up" defense is a challenge to the *truth* of the allegation, not the *basis* for the search. As recognized in *United States v. Villanueva*, 763 F.3d 411 (5th Cir. 2014), a defendant is not entitled to discovery of the informant’s identity to challenge the credibility of the informant’s tip for the purpose of suppressing evidence because the suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits.

**C. The Right to Present a Defense and *Roviaro***

The Appellant cites *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 639 (1957), and *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 126 S. Ct. 1724, 164 L. Ed. 2d 503 (2006), to argue that the denial of disclosure violated his right to present a defense. *Roviaro* holds that the government's informant privilege is not absolute when the informant is a material witness to the crime.

However, these authorities are inapplicable to the specific procedural posture of this case. The Appellant agreed to forgo a trial in favor of a dispositive motion to suppress. By doing so, he waived the right to present a defense to the *charge* of possession. The hearing was strictly limited to the Fourth Amendment question. Because the "set up" theory does not undermine the probable cause determination—given the CI's established reliability—the CI is not a "material witness" to the *suppression issue*. The trial court did not err in refusing to transform the hearing into a forum to litigate the CI's potential involvement in the crime.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The Appellant’s agreement to a dispositive motion to suppress constituted a waiver of the right to litigate issues of guilt or innocence, including the "set up" defense, at the suppression hearing. The new evidence regarding the CI’s belongings does not alter the binding nature of this agreement or render the CI's identity relevant to the probable cause inquiry. The State’s arguments regarding the scope of the hearing and the objective nature of probable cause are persuasive and legally sound.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity is affirmed.
