# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF WISCONSIN

---

**Defendant-Appellant Lalor respectfully submits this opening response to the State's brief and asserts that the trial court committed reversible error by failing to make an express finding that Lalor is unable to control his behavior, by erroneously admitting the results of a plethysmograph test, and by applying an incorrect burden of proof.**

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The State asks this Court to affirm the commitment order based on its reading of *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784. However, the State's argument conflates statutory interpretation with constitutional requirements and ignores binding precedent establishing that substantive due process demands more than mere prediction of future dangerousness. The trial court erred by failing to make an express finding that Lalor is unable to control his behavior, and this Court should reverse.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Erred by Failing to Make an Express Finding That Lalor Is Unable to Control His Behavior

The State contends that *Laxton* forecloses Lalor's substantive due process argument. This reading is overly broad and ignores critical distinctions between the statutory scheme and constitutional minimum requirements.

In *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), the United States Supreme Court upheld a sexually violent person commitment statute but emphasized that commitment under such schemes requires proof of "mental abnormality" that predisposes the individual to commit sexually violent acts. The Court has consistently recognized that the Constitution requires proof of something more than mere dangerousness—it requires proof of lack of control. As the Court stated in *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407, 413 (2002), "the Constitution does not permit commitment of the type of possible here without any showing of the individual's inability to control behavior."

While this Court in *Laxton* interpreted the Wisconsin statutory scheme to incorporate the inability-to-control element within the "substantial probability" language, *Laxton* did not hold that a trial court may omit an express finding on this critical element. To the contrary, *Laxton* emphasized that the statutory scheme satisfies due process "because the 'substantial probability' standard necessarily includes the inability-to-control element." *Laxton*, 254 Wis. 2d 495 at ¶ 24. This reasoning logically implies that the trial court must actually make such a finding—not merely apply a standard that theoretically encompasses it.

Here, the trial court's written decision found that "Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." This finding predicts future dangerousness but does not expressly find that Lalor is *unable to control* his behavior. These are distinct concepts. One may predict that an individual will likely reoffend without concluding that the individual lacks the capacity to control his impulses. The trial court failed to make the latter finding.

The failure to make an express finding on inability to control is not a technical deficiency. It is a constitutional error. In *Crane*, 534 U.S. at 413-14, the Supreme Court held that "due process requires that the State prove 'lack of control' as a link between the mental abnormality and the dangerousness." While the Court stopped short of requiring "perfect precision," it insisted on "some" lack-of-control finding. The trial court's omission here violates this constitutional mandate.

The State argues that requiring an express finding would create an "artificial distinction." However, the distinction is not artificial—it is constitutional. The Supreme Court in *Crane* drew this distinction explicitly when it required "the absence of the sort of full-blown lack-of-control that would be negating a defense of insanity." The Wisconsin statutory scheme may incorporate this element, but the trial court must actually find it.

**This Court should hold that when a defendant specifically requests an express finding on inability to control, due process requires the trial court to make such a finding, and the failure to do so constitutes reversible error.**

### B. The Trial Court Erred by Admitting the Results of a Plethysmograph Test

The record reflects that the trial court admitted the results of a plethysmograph test over Lalor's objection. This was error.

The plethysmograph is a device that measures sexual arousal patterns in response to stimuli. Its use in criminal proceedings has been highly controversial. In *State v. Bergrstrom*, 2002 WI App 107, 253 Wis. 2d 721, 644 N.W.2d 85, this Court addressed the admissibility of plethysmograph results and held that such evidence must satisfy the requirements of *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), regarding scientific validity and reliability.

The State bears the burden of establishing that the scientific evidence meets the *Daubert* standards. Here, the State failed to present any testimony establishing the general acceptance of plethysmograph testing in the scientific community, the known error rates, or the reliability of the specific test administered to Lalor. The defense expert, Dr. Maskel, testified regarding the problems with the testing instruments used by the State's experts. This testimony highlighted the very concerns that render plethysmograph evidence unreliable.

Even assuming the plethysmograph has some general scientific validity, its admission requires a threshold showing of reliability in the specific case. The trial court made no such finding, and the record does not support admission.

**The erroneous admission of this evidence requires reversal because it cannot be said with fair assurance that the error did not affect the outcome of the proceeding.**

### C. The State Failed to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That Lalor's Mental Disorder Creates a Substantial Probability of Future Dangerousness

The State's burden under Wis. Stat. § 980.05(4) is to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the individual is a sexually violent person. This requires proof that: (1) the individual has been convicted of a sexually violent offense; (2) the individual suffers from a mental disorder; and (3) the mental disorder creates a substantial probability that the individual will commit sexually violent acts in the future.

The record establishes only the first element. While the State presented testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields regarding actuarial instruments and predictions of reoffense, the evidence regarding the second and third elements was insufficient.

Dr. Moore's evaluation assessed Lalor's psychopathy using the PCL-R and assigned a score indicating substantial probability to reoffend. However, psychopathy is not itself a "mental disorder" within the meaning of the statute. The statute requires a "mental disorder" as defined in Wis. Stat. § 980.01(2). The State's experts failed to establish that Lalor suffers from a specific, diagnosable mental disorder as distinct from personality traits associated with psychopathy.

Dr. Fields relied on multiple actuarial instruments, including the Hanson RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG. However, Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed significant problems with these instruments. These tools are designed to predict recidivism generally; they do not measure whether a particular individual lacks the capacity to control his behavior. The instruments assess risk factors but do not establish the causal link between a mental disorder and future dangerousness that the statute requires.

Critically, neither the State nor the defense experts conducted a proper differential diagnosis distinguishing Lalor's personality disorder from other conditions. The evidence failed to establish that Lalor's alleged mental disorder causes or contributes to his dangerousness in a way that renders him unable to control his behavior.

**The evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability he will reoffend. The trial court should have granted Lalor's motion for a new trial.**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the State and Defendant-Appellant Lalor. The primary dispute before this Court concerns whether the trial court's findings satisfied substantive due process requirements under Wisconsin law. Additionally, the defense raises issues regarding the admissibility of scientific evidence and the sufficiency of the evidence to support the commitment. This preliminary ruling addresses the merits of the arguments presented.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Unable to Control" Finding and Substantive Due Process**

The State argues that the trial court did not violate Lalor's substantive due process rights by failing to make an express finding that he is "unable to control" his behavior. The State relies on *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784, which established that the statutory standard of "substantial probability" under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) inherently incorporates the inability-to-control element required by the United States Supreme Court in *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002).

The defense contends that *Laxton* does not permit a trial court to omit an express finding on this element, arguing that a prediction of future dangerousness is distinct from a finding of lack of control. While the defense correctly notes that *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997) and *Crane* require a link between mental abnormality and dangerousness, the Wisconsin Supreme Court in *Laxton* specifically addressed how this requirement is met under state law.

The record shows that the trial court found that "Lalor suffers from a mental disorder" and that "Lalor is dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence" (¶ 9). Under the binding precedent of *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784, this finding of "substantial probability" satisfies the constitutional requirement. The *Laxton* court explicitly rejected the notion that a trial court must use the specific phrase "unable to control" to satisfy due process, reasoning that the statutory language already encompasses this concept. Therefore, the trial court's findings are legally sufficient to withstand the defense's substantive due process challenge.

**B. Admissibility of Plethysmograph Evidence**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by admitting the results of a plethysmograph test, citing *State v. Bergrstrom*, 2002 WI App 107, 253 Wis. 2d 721, 644 N.W.2d 85 and *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). The defense asserts that the State failed to establish the scientific reliability of the test or its specific application to Lalor, noting that Dr. Maskel testified to problems with the testing instruments.

The State's opening argument does not address the admissibility of the plethysmograph evidence, nor does it offer a rebuttal to the defense's *Daubert* challenge. In the absence of a counter-argument from the State defending the trial court's evidentiary ruling, the defense's argument regarding the potential error in admitting this evidence stands unrebutted in this preliminary review.

**C. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The defense further argues that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability of reoffense. The defense distinguishes between "psychopathy" and a "diagnosable mental disorder" and challenges the causal link established by the actuarial instruments.

Again, the State's opening argument is silent on this issue. The State does not provide an analysis of the evidence presented by Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields to counter the defense's assertion that the evidence was insufficient. Consequently, the Court cannot make a determination regarding the sufficiency of the evidence based solely on the State's submission.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the arguments provided, the Court preliminarily rules in favor of the State regarding the substantive due process issue. The trial court's finding that Lalor's mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" of future sexually violent acts satisfies the constitutional requirements articulated in *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784. No separate express finding of inability to control was required.

However, regarding the defense's claims concerning the erroneous admission of plethysmograph evidence and the insufficiency of the evidence, the State has failed to provide any legal argument or factual rebuttal. Therefore, the defense's arguments on those points remain unresolved by the State's current filing.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the State's position on the "unable to control" issue is sustained. The State is directed to address the remaining issues raised by the defense regarding evidentiary rulings and sufficiency of the evidence in subsequent briefings.

## I. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR SUFFERS FROM A "MENTAL DISORDER" AS DEFINED BY WISCONSIN STATUTE § 980.01(2)

The defense acknowledges the holding in Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), that personality disorders may constitute mental abnormalities for purposes of civil commitment. However, the defense respectfully submits that the State has failed to establish the essential causal nexus required by Wisconsin law between any alleged mental disorder and Lalor's future dangerousness.

The State's reliance on Dr. Moore's and Dr. Fields' diagnoses of "psychopathy" misses the critical point. While psychopathy may be recognized as a mental abnormality, the State must still prove that this condition creates a "substantial probability" that Lalor will reoffend. The defense presented compelling testimony from Dr. Lynn Maskel that exposed fundamental problems with the actuarial instruments used by the State's experts.

The instruments relied upon by Dr. Fields—including the PCL-R, the Hanson RRASOR, the MnSOST, the MnSOST-R, and the V-RAG—measure risk factors but do not establish a clinical diagnosis of a mental disorder that causes future dangerousness. As Dr. Maskel testified, these instruments are designed to assess risk, not to diagnose mental disorders. The State has conflated risk assessment with clinical diagnosis, a distinction that is fatal to its case.

The defense further notes that while State v. Richard, 2008 WI 113, 314 Wis. 2d 242, 756 N.W.2d 620, holds that conflicting expert testimony goes to the weight of the evidence, this does not relieve the State of its burden of proving each element beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court cannot simply weigh the competing evidence; the State must carry its entire burden, and the defense submits that it has failed to do so here.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS DO NOT SATISFY SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS UNDER KANSAS V. CRANE

The defense maintains that the trial court's findings are constitutionally insufficient under Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). While the State argues that State v. Laxton, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784, controls this issue, the defense submits that Laxton does not go far enough to satisfy federal constitutional requirements.

In Crane, the Supreme Court explicitly held that civil commitment requires a "nexus" between the mental abnormality and the "inability to control behavior." The Court stated that "the Constitution does not permit commitment of the type possible here without any showing of the required lack of control." The trial court's finding that Lalor's "mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence" does not address Lalor's ability to control his behavior.

The defense respectfully submits that "substantial probability" is not synonymous with "inability to control." A person may pose a substantial probability of reoffending due to various factors—including character defects, anti-social behavior, or lack of treatment—without being unable to control his actions. The trial court's finding addresses dangerousness but does not establish the constitutional "lack of control" element that Crane requires.

The defense recognizes that Laxton interpreted the Wisconsin statute to incorporate the inability-to-control element. However, the specific findings in this case do not demonstrate that Lalor is unable to control his behavior. The State presented no evidence that Lalor lacks volitional control. The expert testimony established risk factors but did not establish that Lalor cannot refrain from committing sexually violent acts.

## III. THE ADMISSION OF PLETHYSMOGRAPH EVIDENCE WAS NOT HARMLESS ERROR

The defense contends that the trial court's error in admitting the plethysmograph evidence was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. While the State argues that other evidence supports the commitment, the defense submits that the plethysmograph evidence was so prejudicial that it infected the entire proceeding.

The plethysmograph measures sexual arousal patterns and is used to assess sexual preferences. Its use in legal proceedings has been widely criticized due to reliability concerns. In this case, the State failed to establish the scientific reliability of this test under Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). The State's failure to present any foundation for the test's reliability renders its admission fundamentally flawed.

The defense further submits that the impact of this evidence cannot be minimized. The trier of fact was presented with evidence suggesting abnormal sexual arousal patterns, which would have prejudiced Lalor in the eyes of the fact-finder. This prejudice cannot be cured by citing other evidence in the record. The commitment determination turned on Lalor's alleged propensity for future sexual violence, and evidence of abnormal sexual arousal directly spoke to this issue.

Under State v. Bergrstrom, 2002 WI App 107, 253 Wis. 2d 721, 644 N.W.2d 85, erroneous evidence requires reversal unless the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The defense submits that the State cannot meet this high burden when the erroneous evidence goes to the very heart of the commitment determination.

## IV. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO THE STATE'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The State argues that the defense's position is "legally unsound" with respect to the mental disorder element. However, the defense notes that the State has not addressed the critical distinction between a diagnosis of psychopathy and proof that this condition causes future dangerousness. The State cannot simply point to a diagnosis and assume that the causal link is established.

The State also argues that Dr. Maskel's testimony "does not undermine this conclusion." The defense disagrees. Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed the limitations of the actuarial instruments, which are precisely the instruments upon which the State relies to establish the "substantial probability" element. If these instruments cannot reliably predict future behavior, then the State's entire case fails.

With respect to the due process argument, the defense submits that the State misreads Crane. The State argues that Crane does not "demand a separate, explicit finding of 'inability to control.'" However, Crane does require a "nexus" between mental abnormality and lack of control. The trial court's finding does not establish this nexus. The defense is not arguing for specific language; it is arguing that the finding as made does not satisfy constitutional requirements.

## V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's commitment order. The State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability of future dangerousness. The trial court's findings do not satisfy the substantive due process requirements of Crane. And the admission of plethysmograph evidence, which the State failed to establish as scientifically reliable, was not harmless error.

The defense recognizes that civil commitment serves important public safety interests. However, the Constitution imposes limits on the State's power to detain individuals. These limits exist because the consequences of wrongful commitment are severe—individuals lose their liberty based on predictions of future behavior. The State must meet its burden, and the evidence in this case does not support a finding that Lalor should be committed.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and dismiss the petition for commitment.

**Counsel for Defendant-Appellant Lalor**

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has considered the closing arguments submitted by the State and Defendant-Appellant Lalor. This final ruling addresses the remaining challenges regarding the sufficiency of the evidence to support the commitment, the substantive due process requirements under *Kansas v. Crane*, and the alleged error regarding the admission of plethysmograph evidence.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The defense argues that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor suffers from a "mental disorder" as defined by Wis. Stat. § 980.01(2). Specifically, the defense contends that the State conflated "risk assessment" with "clinical diagnosis," relying on actuarial instruments that measure risk rather than a mental disorder that causes future dangerousness. The defense points to the testimony of Dr. Maskel, who exposed problems with the testing instruments, to argue that the State's burden was not met.

The Court is not persuaded. The State presented expert testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields establishing that Lalor suffers from psychopathy, a condition that qualifies as a "mental abnormality" under *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997). While the defense correctly notes that actuarial instruments assess risk, Dr. Fields testified that Lalor's "mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act." This testimony established the requisite causal link between the disorder and the dangerousness.

The defense's argument regarding the limitations of the testing instruments goes to the weight of the evidence, not its sufficiency. Under *State v. Richard*, 2008 WI 113, 314 Wis. 2d 242, 756 N.W.2d 620, appellate courts must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations unless a witness is incredible as a matter of law. The trial court credited the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over that of Dr. Maskel. The defense has not demonstrated that the State's experts were incredible as a matter of law. The trial court's finding that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder is supported by credible evidence in the record.

**B. Substantive Due Process**

The defense argues that the trial court's findings failed to satisfy the "unable to control" requirement of *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). The defense contends that a finding of "substantial probability" is not synonymous with "inability to control" and that the trial court's specific findings did not demonstrate the constitutional nexus.

The Court rejects this argument. *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784, is binding precedent in this jurisdiction. The *Laxton* court explicitly held that the statutory finding of "substantial probability" under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) inherently satisfies the constitutional requirement of a link between mental abnormality and an inability to control behavior. The trial court made the specific finding required by the statute: that "Lalor is dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." Under *Laxton*, this finding is legally sufficient to meet the *Crane* standard. The defense's attempt to distinguish *Laxton* based on the factual adequacy of the nexus is unavailing; the legal sufficiency of the finding itself controls.

**C. Admissibility of Plethysmograph Evidence**

The defense argues that the admission of the plethysmograph evidence was not harmless error, asserting that the evidence was highly prejudicial and went to the heart of the commitment determination. The State concedes that the trial court erred in admitting the evidence without a *Daubert* foundation but argues that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Court agrees with the State. The record indicates that Dr. Fields relied on multiple other sources in reaching her conclusion, including the PCL-R, the RRASOR, the MnSOST, the MnSOST-R, and the V-RAG. Furthermore, the trial court's findings were based on the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, as well as the uncontroverted facts of Lalor's prior convictions for sexually violent offenses in 1984 and 1992. Given the substantial volume of other actuarial and clinical evidence presented, and the trial court's reliance on that evidence, the admission of the plethysmograph was harmless error beyond a reasonable doubt. The State met its burden of proving the elements of the commitment independent of this specific evidence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court's findings of fact regarding the sufficiency of the evidence are supported by the record and are entitled to deference under *State v. Richard*, 2008 WI 113, 314 Wis. 2d 242, 756 N.W.2d 620. The trial court's legal conclusions satisfy the substantive due process requirements articulated in *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 495, 647 N.W.2d 784. Finally, the admission of the plethysmograph evidence, while erroneous, was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court committing Lalor as a sexually violent person is affirmed.
