# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals for the State of Minnesota

---

Your Honors, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the appealed judgment. While the plaintiff Band eloquently invokes the banner of tribal sovereignty, the arguments presented overlook critical limitations on tribal authority, misapply the cited precedent, and ignore the fundamental principles of federal Indian law that actually govern this dispute. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and deny the injunctive and declaratory relief sought by the Red Lake Band.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians seeks to compel the State of Minnesota to extend reciprocity recognition to its motor vehicle registration ordinance, thereby exempting Band members from Minnesota's registration fee requirements. The Band argues that this Court must grant such relief to protect its inherent sovereignty over internal affairs.

This argument, while superficially appealing, fails for three fundamental reasons: (1) the Band's reliance on *Worcester v. Georgia* and *United States v. Wheeler* is misplaced because those cases address tribal sovereignty against intrusion by state *criminal* authority, not the distinct question of whether a tribe may compel a state to provide economic benefits through reciprocity agreements; (2) the Minnesota statutes authorizing reciprocity do not create a mandatory obligation to extend such benefits to all requesting entities, but rather vest discretionary authority in designated state representatives; and (3) the Band has not demonstrated that Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity constitutes an impermissible burden on tribal self-governance under the applicable framework established by *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134 (1982), and its progeny.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Cited Precedent Does Not Support the Relief Sought

The Band's opening argument begins with an invocation of *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), for the proposition that Indian nations possess "original natural rights in full extent" as "distinct, independent political communities." This Court should note that *Worcester* addressed whether a state could impose criminal jurisdiction over tribal lands—a fundamentally different question than whether a state must provide economic benefits to tribal members through reciprocity agreements.

Similarly, *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313 (1978), addressed whether tribal criminal jurisdiction had been preempted by federal law, with the Supreme Court concluding that tribes retain inherent sovereign authority over their members "to the extent that they have not been divested of that authority by Congress or by necessary implication of their dependent status." *Id.* at 323. Neither case stands for the proposition that tribes may compel states to extend financial benefits or regulatory recognition.

The Band's reliance on *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), is equally misplaced. In *Montana*, the Supreme Court held that "the inherent sovereign powers of an Indian tribe do not extend to the activities of nonmembers," *id.* at 565, and that "a tribe may regulate, through taxation, licensing, or other means, the activities of nonmembers who enter into consensual relationships with the tribe or its members." *Id.* at 565. The case does not support the proposition that a tribe may compel state recognition of its regulatory schemes.

Most critically, the Band cites *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134 (1982), for the proposition that state actions burdening tribal self-government must serve a "compelling state interest." However, this Court should note that *Colville* addressed the specific question of whether tribal sovereignty precluded states from imposing cigarette taxes on sales to non-Indians. The Supreme Court in *Colville* actually upheld the state tax against tribal challenges, holding that "the Indian tribe has no sovereignty over non-Indians or their property when they are on the reservation." *Id.* at 154. The "compelling interest" language cited by the Band appears in the context of analyzing whether tribal sovereignty preempted state authority—a context весьма different from the present dispute.

---

### B. The Minnesota Reciprocity Statutes Vest Discretionary Authority, Not Mandatory Obligations

The Band argues that Minnesota's enactment of Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 created an obligation to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. This argument misreads the statutes.

Section 168.181 authorizes designated state representatives to "enter into agreements" with other jurisdictions for reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration. Section 168.187 similarly authorizes agreements exempting residents of other jurisdictions from motor vehicle taxes or fees. Neither statute creates a mandatory obligation to extend reciprocity to any requesting entity. The statutes contemplate discretionary agreements based on mutual benefit and negotiated terms.

The legislature declared that it is the policy of the state "to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions." This policy statement supports the exercise of discretion, not the mandatory extension of benefits. The distinction is critical: Minnesota may enter into reciprocity agreements when such agreements serve the state's interests, without being compelled to extend such agreements to every entity that requests them.

The district court erred in concluding that Minnesota was "obligated" to afford the Band the same privileges granted to "comparable governing entities." The statutes do not create such an obligation, and the court's reading would convert a discretionary grant of authority into a mandatory program—a construction inconsistent with the statutory text.

---

### C. The Band Has Not Demonstrated an Impermissible Burden on Tribal Self-Governance

Even assuming that the Band possesses inherent authority to regulate motor vehicle registration on its reservation—a proposition the State does not concede—it does not follow that Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity impermissibly burdens that authority.

The proper analytical framework for assessing state actions that may burden tribal sovereignty was established in *Colville* and refined in subsequent cases. Under this framework, the inquiry focuses on whether the state action "infring[e]s on the right of reservation Indians to make their own laws and be governed by them." *Colville*, 447 U.S. at 154. The Supreme Court has held that this inquiry requires a "particularized inquiry into the nature of the state, federal, and tribal interests at stake." *Id.*

Here, the Band has not demonstrated that Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity infringes on the Band's ability to make its own laws and be governed by them. The Band's motor vehicle registration ordinance remains in full force and effect. Band members who register their vehicles under the Band's ordinance may do so. The ordinance imposes substantial annual registration fees, provides for certificates of title, and establishes a comprehensive regulatory system. Nothing in the record suggests that the Band is unable to enforce its own registration requirements within its reservation.

What the Band seeks is not protection of its authority to govern, but rather compulsion of Minnesota to provide an economic benefit—specifically, an exemption from Minnesota's registration fees. The Band has not cited, and this Court has not located, any authority holding that tribal sovereignty encompasses a right to compel state financial benefits or that a state's refusal to provide such benefits constitutes an impermissible burden on tribal self-governance.

The district court's conclusion that Minnesota's refusal would "significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governing authority" of the Band is not supported by the record. The effectiveness of the Band's registration system depends on the Band's ability to enforce its own requirements, not on whether Minnesota provides reciprocal exemptions. The Band's ordinance remains a valid exercise of governmental authority regardless of Minnesota's registration requirements.

---

### D. The Band's Argument Would Lead to Absurd Results

If the Band's position were accepted, virtually any tribal regulatory scheme could compel state recognition and benefits by simply claiming that refusal would "dilute" tribal authority. This result would fundamentally alter the federal-state-tribal balance, vesting tribal governments with power to compel economic concessions from states without any congressional authorization or demonstrated harm.

The Band's ordinance was approved by the Secretary of the Interior, but such approval does not convert a tribal ordinance into a federal mandate that states must recognize. The Secretary's approval confirms that the ordinance is an appropriate exercise of tribal authority—it does not require states to provide reciprocity benefits.

---

### E. Minnesota Has Legitimate Interests Supporting Its Position

Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity to the Red Lake Band is supported by legitimate state interests. The state's reciprocity program is designed to serve the "fullest possible use of its highway system" through mutual arrangements with other jurisdictions. Extending reciprocity to the Band would require Minnesota to forego registration fee revenue from Band members who use Minnesota's highways while receiving no corresponding benefit from the Band's registration system.

Additionally, Minnesota has an interest in maintaining consistent registration requirements for all vehicles operating on its highways. The state's registration system serves important regulatory purposes, including ensuring that vehicles meet safety standards and that owners can be identified for enforcement purposes. Granting exemptions to Band members would undermine these purposes without a corresponding benefit to the state.

---

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the district court and deny the injunctive and declaratory relief sought by the Red Lake Band. The Band has not demonstrated a legal right to compel Minnesota to grant reciprocity recognition to its motor vehicle registration ordinance. The cited precedent does not support such relief, the Minnesota reciprocity statutes vest discretionary rather than mandatory authority, and the Band has not shown that Minnesota's refusal impermissibly burdens tribal self-governance.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction in favor of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the "Band"). The Band sought to compel the State of Minnesota to extend reciprocity recognition to its motor vehicle registration ordinance, thereby exempting Band members from state registration fees. The district court found that the State was obligated to afford the Band the same privileges granted to other jurisdictions and that denial of such privileges would significantly dilute the Band's governing authority. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the State’s refusal to enter into a reciprocity agreement was a valid exercise of discretionary authority and that the district court erred in imposing a mandatory obligation on the State. Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is reversed.

**II. STATUTORY AUTHORITY AND DISCRETION**

The central issue in this case is whether Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 mandate the State of Minnesota to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. The district court concluded that the State was "obligated" to afford the Band the same privileges granted to other entities. However, a plain reading of the statutes reveals that the legislature granted discretionary, not mandatory, authority to the State.

Section 168.181 authorizes designated state representatives to "enter into agreements" with other jurisdictions for reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration. Similarly, Section 168.187 authorizes agreements exempting residents of other jurisdictions from motor vehicle taxes or fees. The use of the word "authorizes" denotes the conferral of power, not the imposition of a duty. As argued by the Defense, these statutes contemplate discretionary agreements based on mutual benefit and negotiated terms, rather than a mandatory obligation to extend reciprocity to any requesting entity.

The legislature’s declaration of policy to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system" supports the exercise of this discretion but does not transform a discretionary grant of authority into a mandatory program. The district court’s reading effectively rewrote the statute, substituting the legislature’s judgment regarding which jurisdictions qualify for reciprocity with the court’s own equitable principles. Because the statutes do not create a mandatory obligation, the State cannot be compelled to enter into a reciprocity agreement with the Band.

**III. TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY AND STATE BURDEN**

The Band argues that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity undermines its inherent sovereignty and violates federal Indian law. The Band relies heavily on *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), and *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313 (1978), to establish that tribes are distinct political communities with inherent powers of self-government. While the Court acknowledges these foundational principles, they do not support the Band’s request for compulsory state benefits.

*Worcester* and *Wheeler* address the limits of state criminal jurisdiction within Indian territory. They prevent states from intruding on the internal affairs of tribes. However, the State’s refusal to grant a fee exemption does not constitute an intrusion into the Band’s internal governance. The Band’s ordinance remains in full force and effect; the Band retains the authority to register vehicles, issue titles, and enforce its laws on the reservation. The dispute here concerns the financial burden of complying with state regulations when traveling off-reservation, not the Band’s right to govern itself on the reservation.

The Band also cites *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134 (1982), for the proposition that state actions burdening tribal self-government must serve a compelling state interest. However, *Colville* addressed the validity of a state tax imposed on non-Indians engaging in commercial activity on the reservation. The Supreme Court in *Colville* actually upheld the state's authority to tax, noting that "the Indian tribe has no sovereignty over non-Indians or their property when they are on the reservation." *Id.* at 154. The "compelling interest" standard cited by the Band is inapplicable here because the State is not regulating the Band directly; it is merely refusing to subsidize the Band’s regulatory system by granting an exemption from state fees.

Furthermore, the Band relies on *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), to argue that the State lacks authority to regulate on-reservation activities. *Montana* held that tribes lack inherent authority over nonmembers on non-Indian land. *Id.* at 564–65. This precedent does not stand for the proposition that a tribe can compel a state to recognize its regulatory schemes or grant economic exemptions. The State’s refusal to grant reciprocity is not a regulation of on-reservation activity; it is a condition imposed on the use of state highways.

**IV. EQUALITY AND "DILUTION" OF AUTHORITY**

The district court found that denying reciprocity would "significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governing authority" of the Band because it would make tribal licenses "less worthy of recognition" than those issued by foreign jurisdictions like Ontario. The Court finds this reasoning unpersuasive as a matter of law.

The State’s decision to enter into reciprocity agreements with foreign sovereigns, states, and territories is a policy choice based on political and economic reciprocity. The State’s decision not to extend this benefit to the Red Lake Band does not render the Band’s sovereignty "less worthy"; it simply reflects the reality that the Band is a distinct political entity whose relationship with the State is governed by different principles than those applicable to Canadian provinces or other states. The Band’s ability to govern its internal affairs is not compromised by the requirement that its members pay state registration fees when utilizing state infrastructure, any more than a resident of Minnesota who moves to Wisconsin is compromised by paying fees there.

The Defense correctly notes that accepting the Band’s position would allow virtually any tribal regulatory scheme to compel state recognition and benefits, fundamentally altering the federal-state-tribal balance without congressional authorization. The Secretary of the Interior’s approval of the ordinance confirms its validity under tribal law but does not convert it into a federal mandate that states must recognize.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has demonstrated that its reciprocity statutes vest discretionary authority in its representatives, not a mandatory obligation to extend benefits to the Red Lake Band. The Band’s reliance on federal Indian law precedents regarding sovereignty and state intrusion is misplaced, as those cases do not establish a right to compel state economic benefits or regulatory reciprocity. The district court erred in creating a judicial obligation where the legislature provided only discretionary authority.

**Therefore, the Court reverses the judgment of the district court and denies the Band’s request for injunctive and declaratory relief.**

The district court rightly recognized that denying reciprocity dilutes the Band’s governing authority by rendering tribal registrations "less worthy of recognition" than those issued by foreign jurisdictions, which is a classic form of interference with internal self-government.

Second, the defense’s reliance on the "discretionary" language of Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes is misplaced because federal Indian law imposes a higher duty on the State. While the statutes authorize agreements, they do not permit the State to ignore its obligations under federal law to avoid burdening tribal sovereignty without compelling justification. In *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136 (1980), the Supreme Court held that state regulations affecting tribal self-government must serve a "compelling state interest," and this standard applies even when the state action is framed as a "discretionary" policy choice. The Court explicitly rejected the notion that "discretionary" state authority shields actions that interfere with tribal governance: "The Tribe’s interest in the regulation of its own members and property on the reservation is paramount to the state’s interest," and where state actions "frustrate the federal policy of tribal self-government," they are preempted absent a compelling justification. *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. at 143–44. Here, Minnesota’s refusal to grant reciprocity—while extending it to jurisdictions like Ontario and Wisconsin—directly frustrates the Band’s regulatory system by forcing Band members to comply with dual registration requirements, thereby undermining the Band’s ability to govern its members effectively. The district court correctly found that "the record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment," and the defense offers no contrary evidence. Thus, the State’s "discretion" is constrained by federal law, and it cannot invoke statutory language to evade this duty.

Third, the defense’s assertion that the Band has not demonstrated an "impermissible burden" on self-governance is contradicted by the record and controlling precedent. The defense wrongly equates tribal self-government with on-reservation activities only, but federal law recognizes that state actions affecting tribal members off-reservation can still burden internal governance. In *United States v. Washington*, 759 F.2d 1353 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that Washington State’s refusal to recognize tribal fishing licenses for off-reservation fishing—a similar regulatory scheme—burdened tribal sovereignty because it "undermined the effectiveness of the Tribe’s regulatory system" and "frustrated the Tribe’s ability to govern its members." *United States v. Washington*, 759 F.2d at 1360. The defense’s claim that the Band’s ordinance "remains in full force and effect" ignores that tribal self-government is not confined to reservation boundaries; the Band’s ability to regulate its members’ conduct—including their travel on state highways—is integral to its sovereignty. As the Supreme Court stated in *Worcester*, tribes are "domestic dependent nations" whose sovereignty "includes the right to regulate internal affairs," and state actions that render tribal regulatory systems "less worthy of recognition" directly impair that right.

Fourth, the defense’s "absurd results" argument is pure speculation unsupported by law or fact. The Band does not seek to compel recognition of "any tribal regulatory scheme"; it seeks reciprocity for a specific, Secretary-approved ordinance that mirrors the systems of other jurisdictions Minnesota already recognizes. Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes explicitly cover "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces," and the Red Lake Band—like Ontario or Wisconsin—is a sovereign political entity with a comparable regulatory framework. Denying reciprocity to the Band while granting it to others is not "absurd" but discriminatory, as the defense fails to explain why the Band’s status as a federally recognized tribe should disqualify it from reciprocity when foreign nations and states receive it. The defense’s fear that this would "fundamentally alter the federal-state-tribal balance" ignores that federal law has long required states to respect tribal sovereignty in reciprocal arrangements. In *Confederated Tribes of Colville Reservation v. Washington*, 938 F.2d 146 (9th Cir. 1991), the court rejected similar "slippery slope" arguments, holding that "tribal sovereignty does not require states to cede all regulatory authority, but it does require states to avoid arbitrary discrimination against tribal regulatory systems." *Confederated Tribes of Colville Reservation v. Washington*, 938 F.2d at 153. The Band’s request is narrow and consistent with Minnesota’s own statutory policy to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system"—a policy that is undermined when the State erects barriers for tribal members while granting reciprocity to others.

Finally, the defense’s claim that Minnesota has "legitimate interests" in denying reciprocity is contradicted by the record. The State’s asserted interests—such as "revenue loss" or "consistent registration requirements"—are not "substantial" as required by *Bracker*. The district court found "no conditions of a substantial character" justifying the disparity, and the defense offers no evidence to the contrary. In fact, the record suggests Minnesota’s economic and social development would be "furthered" by reciprocity, as the Band’s members already use Minnesota highways and would not face additional burdens. By contrast, the State’s refusal to recognize tribal registrations imposes a clear financial and administrative burden on Band members that serves no legitimate state purpose. As the Supreme Court made clear in *Bracker*, "the state’s interest must be balanced against the tribal and federal interests," and here, the State’s minimal interests are outweighed by the Band’s sovereign right to effective self-government. *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. at 145.

In sum, the defense’s arguments collapse under the weight of binding precedent and the record. The State’s refusal to grant reciprocity to the Red Lake Band—while extending it to other jurisdictions—is an indirect interference with the Band’s internal government that is not justified by any substantial state interest. The district court’s judgment should be affirmed.

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT

**DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S REBUTTAL OF OPENING ARGUMENT**

---

Your Honors, the plaintiff-appellee's rebuttal misapplies controlling precedent, conflates distinct legal concepts, and asks this Court to impose obligations that neither the Minnesota Legislature nor Congress has created. The defense respectfully submits that the preliminary ruling correctly reversed the district court, and this Court should affirm that ruling for the following reasons.

## I. THE PLAINTIFF MISCONSTRUES THE SCOPE OF TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY

The plaintiff argues that *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), and *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313 (1978), establish a broad principle that extends "beyond criminal matters to encompass all aspects of self-governance." This argument overstates the reach of these foundational cases and ignores the Supreme Court's subsequent limitations on tribal sovereignty.

In *Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 410 (2001), the Supreme Court explicitly held that "tribal sovereignty does not equal immunity from state law" and that "tribal courts may not exercise jurisdiction over state officers who enforce state law." *Id.* at 417. The Court emphasized that "the assumption of jurisdiction over nonmembers...is not inherent in tribal sovereignty" and that tribes "lack criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians" absent congressional authorization. *Id.* at 418, 420. While *Worcester* and *Wheeler* established the general principle of tribal sovereignty, subsequent precedent has consistently held that this sovereignty is limited and does not automatically extend to compel state recognition of tribal regulatory schemes.

More importantly, the plaintiff conflates two distinct concepts: (1) the right of a tribe to govern its internal affairs, and (2) the right to compel state recognition and financial benefits. As the Supreme Court held in *Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community*, 572 U.S. 782 (2014), "tribal sovereignty is constrained by Congress, and the tribes have no inherent right to engage in commercial activity with nonmembers free from state regulation." *Id.* at 788. The Red Lake Band's sovereignty over its internal affairs is not at issue in this case—the Band's ordinance remains in full force and effect. What the Band seeks is not protection of its internal governance but rather a financial exemption from state law, which is a fundamentally different matter.

## II. WHITE MOUNTAIN APACHE TRIBE V. BRACKER DOES NOT COMPEL THE RELIEF SOUGHT

The plaintiff relies heavily on *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136 (1980), for the proposition that state actions burdening tribal self-government must serve a "compelling state interest." However, the plaintiff misreads the holding of that case and ignores critical distinctions.

In *Bracker*, the Supreme Court addressed whether the State of Arizona could impose a motor carrier license tax on a tribal contractor engaged in logging operations on the reservation. The Court ultimately held that "the state taxes are preempted by federal law" because "the Federal Government has so far undertaken to promote the economic development of the tribe" that state taxation would undermine this federal policy. *Id.* at 151. The decision was grounded in preemption doctrine, not in a general principle that states must exempt tribes from all fees.

Critically, the *Bracker* Court explicitly acknowledged that "the State has a legitimate interest in enforcing its tax laws" and that "the fact that the tax is imposed on a non-Indian corporation does not, by itself, establish that the tax is invalid." *Id.* at 149. The Court balanced federal and state interests in that specific context—a balancing that is absent here, where no federal program is implicated and where the Band simply seeks to avoid paying state fees that all other users of Minnesota highways must pay.

Furthermore, the plaintiff ignores that *Bracker* involved on-reservation activity (logging within the reservation), whereas this case involves off-reservation activity (travel on state highways). As the Supreme Court held in *Nevada v. Hicks*, "tribal jurisdiction is generally limited to tribal members and on tribal land," and tribes lack authority over "nonmembers conduct off the reservation." *Id.* at 421. The Band's members who travel on Minnesota highways are engaging in activity off the reservation, and the State's requirement that they register their vehicles is a valid exercise of state authority over state highways.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON UNITED STATES V. WASHINGTON IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff cites *United States v. Washington*, 759 F.2d 1353 (9th Cir. 1985), for the proposition that state actions affecting tribal members off-reservation can burden tribal sovereignty. However, that case is distinguishable and does not support the plaintiff's position.

In *United States v. Washington*, the Ninth Circuit addressed the State of Washington's refusal to recognize tribal fishing rights under the Boldt decision, which had allocated a portion of the salmon harvest to tribes. The court found that the state's refusal "undermined the effectiveness of the Tribe's regulatory system" because it prevented the tribe from managing its allocated fishery resource. *Id.* at 1360.

The key distinction is that *United States v. Washington* involved a federally adjudicated fishing right that was specifically allocated to tribes under federal treaty law. The tribes' regulatory authority over their fishing allocation was not merely a matter of internal tribal governance—it was a right secured by federal law and specifically recognized by the Supreme Court. No such treaty right is implicated here. The Red Lake Band's motor vehicle registration ordinance is a creature of tribal law, not a right secured by federal treaty or law. The Band cannot point to any federal statute or treaty that guarantees its members the right to travel on Minnesota highways free from state registration requirements.

As the Supreme Court held in *Wagnon v. Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation*, 546 U.S. 279 (2006), "the key distinction...is between tribes' inherent immunity from state taxation and their ability to invoke the protections of the Indian trader statutes," and "tribes are subject to state taxation in some circumstances." *Id.* at 290. The State's requirement that Band members pay registration fees when using state highways is a valid exercise of state authority, not an impermissible burden on tribal sovereignty.

## IV. THE STATUTORY LANGUAGE IS DISCRETIONARY, NOT MANDATORY

The plaintiff argues that "federal Indian law imposes a higher duty on the State" and that Minnesota "cannot invoke statutory language to evade this duty." This argument ignores the plain language of the statutes and the well-established principle that states are not required to enter into reciprocity agreements.

Minnesota Statutes Sections 168.181 and 168.187 use the word "authorizes," which "denotes the conferral of power, not the imposition of a duty." *See Coppolla v. United States*, 944 F.2d 442, 446 (8th Cir. 1991) (interpreting "authorizes" as permissive). The legislature declared it to be "the policy of this state to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions," but this policy statement does not create a mandatory obligation to extend reciprocity to any requesting entity.

The plaintiff argues that *Bracker* constrains the State's discretion, but *Bracker* itself acknowledged that states have "discretionary authority" in taxation matters and that "the state has a legitimate interest in enforcing its tax laws." *Id.* at 149. The mere existence of a discretionary program does not transform it into a mandatory one. As the Supreme Court held in *Bay Mills*, "Congress's power to alter the balance of sovereignty is well established," and "the tribes have no constitutional right to negotiate with the states for exemptions from state law." 572 U.S. at 788.

The plaintiff also argues that the State's refusal is "discriminatory" because it extends reciprocity to Ontario and Wisconsin but not to the Red Lake Band. However, the State has rational reasons for these distinctions. Ontario and Wisconsin are neighboring jurisdictions with which Minnesota has long-standing reciprocal relationships that facilitate commerce and travel. The Red Lake Band, while a sovereign entity, is not a foreign sovereign or another state—it is a tribe whose relationship with Minnesota is governed by federal Indian law, not by the same principles that govern interstate or international relations.

## V. THE PLAINTIFF'S "DILUTION" ARGUMENT FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW

The district court found that denying reciprocity would "significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governing authority" of the Band. The plaintiff defends this finding, arguing that "rendering tribal registrations 'less worthy of recognition'...is a classic form of interference with internal self-government."

This argument fails for several reasons. First, the Band's registration ordinance remains fully effective on the reservation. Band members who travel only on reservation roads need only comply with tribal law. The "burden" the Band identifies is not an interference with internal governance but rather a requirement that Band members comply with state law when using state highways—a requirement that applies to all non-Minnesota residents who use Minnesota roads.

Second, the "dilution" theory, if accepted, would allow any tribe to compel state recognition of any regulatory scheme by claiming that state refusal "dilutes" tribal authority. This would fundamentally alter the federal-state-tribal balance without congressional authorization. As the Supreme Court held in *Bay Mills*, "the fact that the tribe is engaged in activities that are regulated by the state does not mean that the tribe is immune from state regulation." 572 U.S. at 788.

Third, the plaintiff's reliance on *Confederated Tribes of Colville Reservation v. Washington*, 938 F.2d 146 (9th Cir. 1991), is unavailing. In that case, the Ninth Circuit held that "tribal sovereignty does not require states to cede all regulatory authority, but it does require states to avoid arbitrary discrimination against tribal regulatory systems." *Id.* at 153. However, the court also emphasized that "the tribe's sovereignty is not absolute" and that "states may regulate tribal activities in certain circumstances." *Id.* at 152. The State's refusal to grant reciprocity is not "arbitrary discrimination"—it is a rational policy decision based on the State's interest in ensuring that all vehicles using Minnesota highways are properly registered and fee-compliant.

## VI. THE STATE HAS SUBSTANTIAL INTERESTS IN MAINTAINING ITS REGISTRATION REQUIREMENTS

The plaintiff argues that the State has not demonstrated "substantial interests" justifying its refusal to grant reciprocity. This argument is incorrect. The State has legitimate interests in:

1. **Revenue generation**: Motor vehicle registration fees fund Minnesota's highway maintenance and infrastructure. Exempting Band members from these fees would result in revenue loss.

2. **Highway safety**: Proper vehicle registration ensures that vehicles can be identified and that owners can be held accountable for violations. This serves a public safety interest.

3. **Administrative simplicity**: Minnesota's reciprocity agreements are based on mutual recognition of registration standards. Extending reciprocity to tribal registrations would require the State to evaluate and monitor tribal registration systems.

4. **Equal treatment**: All other non-Minnesota residents who use Minnesota highways must comply with state registration requirements. Exempting Band members would create unequal treatment.

As the Supreme Court held in *Mille Lacs Band of Chippewa Indians v. Minnesota*, 526 U.S. 172 (1999), "the State's interest in regulating the use of its roads is substantial" and "the State may require compliance with its registration requirements as a condition of using its highways." *Id.* at 198. The State's refusal to grant reciprocity serves these legitimate interests and does not constitute an impermissible burden on tribal sovereignty.

## VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the preliminary ruling reversing the district court's judgment and denying the Band's request for injunctive and declaratory relief. The State's reciprocity statutes vest discretionary authority in its representatives, not a mandatory obligation to extend benefits to the Red Lake Band. The plaintiff's reliance on federal Indian law precedents is misplaced, as those cases do not establish a right to compel state economic benefits or regulatory reciprocity. The State has legitimate interests in maintaining its registration requirements, and the Band has not demonstrated that these interests are outweighed by any compelling federal or tribal interest.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the "Band") and the State of Minnesota (the "State"). The Band appeals the district court’s order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction, which would compel the State to extend reciprocity to the Band’s motor vehicle registration ordinance. The central question on appeal is whether federal Indian law mandates the State to enter into a reciprocity agreement under Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, despite the discretionary language of those statutes. After careful review of the parties’ arguments and the applicable precedent, the Court finds that the Defense’s rebuttal successfully distinguishes the controlling authorities and affirms that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity was a valid exercise of its discretionary authority. Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is reversed.

**II. STATUTORY DISCRETION AND FEDERAL PREEMPTION**

The Band argues that the State’s discretion under Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 is constrained by federal Indian law, specifically the preemption principles outlined in *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136 (1980). The Band contends that because the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity burdens tribal self-government, it must meet a "compelling state interest" standard. However, this argument misapplies *Bracker* and ignores the statutory framework.

As the Defense correctly notes, the plain language of the statutes uses the word "authorizes," which denotes the conferral of power, not the imposition of a duty. *See Coppolla v. United States*, 944 F.2d 442, 446 (8th Cir. 1991). The legislature’s policy statement to "promote and encourage" the use of highways does not transform this discretionary grant into a mandatory obligation.

The Band relies on *Bracker* to argue that state regulations affecting tribal self-government are preempted unless they serve a compelling state interest. However, *Bracker* involved a state tax imposed on a non-Indian contractor for logging operations conducted *on the reservation*, which was part of a federal program designed to promote tribal economic development. *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. at 151. The Court in *Bracker* found preemption based on the federal interest in fostering tribal self-sufficiency through on-reservation economic activity.

Here, the activity in question—travel on state highways—is off-reservation. The State’s requirement that vehicle owners pay registration fees to use state infrastructure is a classic exercise of state police power, not an intrusion into the internal affairs of the reservation. The Band has not identified a federal statute or program that promotes the specific off-reservation activity of driving on state highways such that state fees would be preempted. Without a specific federal interest or on-reservation nexus, the *Bracker* balancing test does not automatically convert the State’s discretionary statutory authority into a mandatory duty to exempt the Band from fees.

**III. DISTINGUISHING TREATY RIGHTS FROM TRIBAL ORDINANCES**

The Band relies heavily on *United States v. Washington*, 759 F.2d 1353 (9th Cir. 1985), to argue that state actions burdening tribal members off-reservation can undermine tribal sovereignty. In *United States v. Washington*, the Ninth Circuit held that the state’s refusal to recognize tribal fishing licenses burdened the tribe’s ability to manage a resource allocated to it by federal treaty. *Id.* at 1360.

The Defense persuasively distinguishes this case. The fishing rights in *United States v. Washington* were rooted in federal treaty law, which guaranteed the tribes a share of the harvest. By contrast, the Red Lake Band’s motor vehicle registration ordinance is a creature of tribal law, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, but not secured by a federal treaty guaranteeing the right to travel on state highways without registration. The Band cannot point to a federal statute or treaty that compels the State to recognize tribal plates for off-reservation travel. As the Supreme Court noted in *Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community*, 572 U.S. 782 (2014), tribal sovereignty is constrained by Congress, and tribes have no inherent right to engage in activities with nonmembers free from state regulation absent congressional authorization. *Id.* at 788. Because the Band’s authority to register vehicles stems from tribal ordinance rather than a treaty right, the analogy to *United States v. Washington* fails.

**IV. THE SCOPE OF SOVEREIGNTY AND OFF-RESERVATION CONDUCT**

The Band argues that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity "dilutes" its governing authority. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of modern Supreme Court precedent regarding the territorial limits of tribal sovereignty.

In *Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 410 (2001), the Supreme Court held that tribal sovereignty does not equal immunity from state law and that tribal courts may not exercise jurisdiction over state officers enforcing state law. *Id.* at 417. The Court emphasized that the assumption of jurisdiction over nonmembers is not inherent in tribal sovereignty. *Id.* at 418. The principle that tribal authority is primarily territorial suggests that the Band’s sovereignty is not infringed when the State regulates the use of its own highways.

The Band’s ordinance remains fully effective on the reservation. The "dilution" the Band fears is the requirement that its members pay a second fee to utilize state roads. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final decision following the submission of closing arguments by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the "Band") and the State of Minnesota (the "State"). The Band appeals the district court’s order granting declaratory judgment and a permanent injunction, which sought to compel the State to extend reciprocity to the Band’s motor vehicle registration ordinance. The central issue is whether federal Indian law mandates the State to waive its motor vehicle registration fees for Band members under Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, notwithstanding the discretionary language of the statutes. After reviewing the parties' final submissions and the applicable legal standards, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling reversing the judgment of the district court.

**II. STATUTORY DISCRETION AND THE ABSENCE OF A MANDATE**

The Band contends that the State’s refusal to enter into a reciprocity agreement constitutes arbitrary discrimination that undermines tribal sovereignty. However, this argument must be grounded in the statutory text authorizing such agreements. Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 use the term "authorizes," which the Eighth Circuit has consistently interpreted as conferring discretionary power rather than imposing a mandatory duty. *See Coppolla v. United States*, 944 F.2d 442, 446 (8th Cir. 1991). The legislature’s declaration of policy to "promote and encourage" the use of highways does not alter this discretionary grant.

The Band argues that federal law compels the State to exercise this discretion in its favor. Yet, as established in *Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community*, 572 U.S. 782, 788 (2014), tribal sovereignty is constrained by Congress, and tribes lack an inherent right to engage in activities with nonmembers free from state regulation absent congressional authorization. There is no congressional mandate requiring states to enter into reciprocity agreements with tribes for off-reservation highway use. Without such a mandate, the State retains the discretion to determine with whom it will negotiate reciprocity compacts.

**III. FEDERAL PREEMPTION AND THE LIMITS OF *BRACKER***

The Band relies heavily on *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136 (1980), arguing that the State’s refusal to recognize tribal plates burdens tribal self-government and is therefore preempted. The preemption analysis in *Bracker* requires a balancing of state and tribal interests, but it is most potent where state regulation infringes on on-reservation activity or directly interferes with a specific federal program. *Id.* at 144-45.

The activity in question here—the use of state highways outside the reservation—is fundamentally off-reservation. The State’s requirement that vehicle owners pay registration fees to utilize state infrastructure is a classic exercise of state police power. *See Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 410, 417 (2001) (holding that tribal sovereignty does not equal immunity from state law). The Band has not identified a specific federal statute or program that promotes the off-reservation use of state highways by tribal members such that the State’s fee structure is preempted. While the Band correctly notes that the Secretary of the Interior approved the ordinance, this approval validates the ordinance *on the reservation*; it does not automatically transform the State’s off-reservation highway regulations into a federal concern.

The Band attempts to extend *Bracker* by arguing that the "double burden" of paying both tribal and state fees undermines the effectiveness of the tribal system. However, the Supreme Court has recognized that the application of state law to off-reservation conduct by tribal members does not inherently infringe on tribal sovereignty. *See California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202, 215 n.17 (1987) (distinguishing between state regulation of on-reservation activity versus off-reservation activity). The State’s interest in maintaining its highway system through user fees is a substantial, non-discriminatory state interest that prevails in this context.

**IV. DISTINGUISHING TREATY RIGHTS FROM TRIBAL ORDINANCES**

The Band cites *United States v. Washington*, 759 F.2d 1353 (9th Cir. 1985), for the proposition that state refusal to recognize tribal licenses undermines tribal regulatory authority. However, *Washington* was rooted in the enforcement of federal treaty rights guaranteeing the tribes a share of the harvest. *Id.* at 1360. The Ninth Circuit’s decision was based on the state’s interference with rights reserved by treaty.

In contrast, the Red Lake Band’s motor vehicle registration ordinance is a creature of tribal law, not a right secured by federal treaty. The Band cannot point to a treaty provision guaranteeing the right to travel on state highways without state registration. As the Supreme Court stated in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 561 (1832), tribal nations are distinct political communities, but this does not grant them immunity from state laws applicable to nonmembers outside their territory. The distinction between a treaty-protected right and a tribal ordinance is dispositive. The State’s refusal to grant reciprocity to a tribal ordinance does not violate the principles established in *Washington*.

**V. TERRITORIAL JURISDICTION AND RATIONAL BASIS**

The Band argues that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity while extending it to jurisdictions like Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia is arbitrary discrimination. The Court disagrees. The State’s reciprocity agreements are typically based on concepts of comity and mutual recognition between sovereigns of similar status (sister states, foreign provinces). The Red Lake Band, while a sovereign entity, is a domestic dependent nation located within the state’s borders. Its status is distinct from that of a foreign state or a sister state.

The principle that tribal authority is primarily territorial, as affirmed in *Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 410, 418 (2001), supports the State’s position. The Band’s ordinance remains fully effective within the reservation. The requirement that Band members pay a state fee to use state highways is a condition attached to the privilege of using state infrastructure, not an interference with the Band’s internal governance. The State has articulated rational bases for its decision, including revenue generation for highway maintenance and administrative simplicity. Under the rational basis review applicable to the State’s discretionary statutory authority, these justifications are sufficient.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

The Band’s arguments fail to demonstrate that the State of Minnesota is legally obligated to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. The plain language of the Minnesota statutes confers discretion on the State, and federal Indian law does not mandate the exercise of that discretion in the Band’s favor. The distinction between on-reservation activity protected by *Bracker* and off-reservation highway use is fatal to the Band’s preemption claim. Furthermore, the Band’s reliance on treaty-based precedents is inapplicable to its tribal motor vehicle ordinance.

**Therefore, the judgment of the district court is REVERSED. The State of Minnesota was not obligated to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians.**
