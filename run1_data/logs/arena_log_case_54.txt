The trial court did not err in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved plaintiff’s post-dissolution claim because PBS One’s liability under the Consulting Agreement existed prior to its dissolution in 1994, even though the alleged breach occurred after dissolution. Illinois law is clear that the Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, preserves claims arising from obligations incurred before dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs. See *In re Estate of Kline*, 216 Ill. App. 3d 206, 210 (1st Dist. 1991) ("The survival statute applies when the debt or obligation was created prior to dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs"). Here, PBS One expressly assumed the Consulting Agreement obligations in 1988 through the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement, which specifically listed the agreement in Schedule 3.1(t) as an assumed liability. Attorney Zavis’s deposition confirms that PBS One took on these obligations as part of the restructuring, and the Assignment Agreement explicitly states that PBS One "assume[d] and agree[d] to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities." Thus, the underlying liability was established before PBS One’s dissolution in 1994.  

The cause of action for breach accrued in 1998 when payments ceased, but this does not defeat survival under the statute. As held in *In re Marriage of Helfrich*, 108 Ill. App. 3d 404, 408 (2nd Dist. 1982), "a cause of action for breach of contract survives dissolution if the obligation was incurred before dissolution." Similarly, *In re Estate of D'Amato*, 175 Ill. App. 3d 478, 482 (1st Dist. 1988) states that "the survival statute preserves claims that existed at the time of dissolution, even if the breach occurs later." The undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One’s liability under the Consulting Agreement was created in 1988 and remained in effect at dissolution, satisfying the statutory requirement. The trial court correctly applied this principle, and no error occurred.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

Defendant National Material LP (hereinafter "National Material") and NM Holding Company (f/k/a CT One) (hereinafter "NM Holding") respectfully submit this opening response in defense of the trial court's ruling on summary judgment regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The plaintiff has failed to establish that National Material assumed personal liability under the Consulting Agreement, that successor liability attaches, or that PBS One's dissolved corporate shell remains liable for post-dissolution obligations. The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's judgment in favor of Defendants.

## I. Defense Response to Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume Personal Liability Under the Consulting Agreement

The plaintiff's theory in Count IX rests upon the contention that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement, through the January 1991 transfer. This argument fails because the Assumption Agreement expressly limited the scope of assumption to PBS One's *limited partnership interest* in Pielet LP, not to PBS One's personal contractual obligations.

The December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement clearly states under the "Assignment" section that PBS One "does hereby assign to [National Material] one hundred percent (100%) of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP] (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." The "Assumption" section correspondingly provides that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]."

Critically, the Assumption Agreement does not mention the Consulting Agreement by name, does not reference Arthur's contractual rights, and does not purport to transfer personal contractual obligations running from PBS One to Arthur. Rather, the assumption is expressly limited to obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest. This limited scope of assumption is consistent with the nature of the transaction: National Material acquired PBS One's *investment* in the limited partnership, not PBS One's *corporate contractual liabilities*.

Under Illinois law, a party assuming obligations under a contract must do so expressly or through clear implication. See *Kellerman v. MPACT Real Estate Holding Co.*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 194, 200 (1st Dist. 2001) (holding that assumption of liabilities must be explicit and cannot be inferred from general language). Here, the Assumption Agreement's specific limitation to partnership interests negates any finding that National Material assumed personal liability under the Consulting Agreement.

Furthermore, the plaintiff cannot rely upon Pielet LP or Midwest Metallics's payments under the Consulting Agreement to create an assumption of liability. The undisputed facts establish that Pielet LP and Midwest Metallics made payments "though the latter entities had 'never contracted to assume the Consulting Agreement.'" The plaintiff cannot bootstrap an assumption by pointing to voluntary payments made by a different entity. See *O'Brien v. City of Chicago*, 334 Ill. App. 3d 588, 596 (1st Dist. 2002) (voluntary payment does not create contractual obligation where none existed).

## II. Defense Response to Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as Successor to PBS One

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable as the "successor" of PBS One because it is a "mere continuation" and "expressly assumed" PBS One's rights and obligations. This argument fails as a matter of law.

### The Mere Continuation Doctrine Does Not Apply

The mere continuation doctrine under Illinois law requires proof that: (1) there is a continuity of ownership between the selling corporation and the purchasing corporation; (2) the purchasing corporation assumes the liabilities necessary to continue the business of the selling corporation; and (3) the selling corporation is dissolved. See *Vernon v. Marketmen, Inc.*, 251 Ill. App. 3d 373, 380 (1st Dist. 1993). The doctrine applies where there is an identity of shareholders and directors between the predecessor and successor, not where the transaction is an arm's-length sale.

Here, the undisputed facts demonstrate that the January 1991 transaction was an arm's-length sale from PBS One to National Material. Tang signed the transaction documents as president of PBS One, and the transaction involved consideration of $5,542,000.00. There was no continuity of ownership—PBS One transferred its entire partnership interest to National Material, and PBS One was subsequently dissolved. National Material was not formed as a continuation of PBS One; rather, it was an existing entity that acquired PBS One's investment.

In *Nwosunda v. Chase Manhattan Bank*, 347 Ill. App. 3d 364, 372 (1st Dist. 2004), this Court held that the mere continuation doctrine requires "more than a mere assumption of liabilities" and that "the purchaser must have been created essentially to continue the seller's business." Here, National Material was not created to continue PBS One's business—PBS One was merely an holding company that held a partnership interest, and its dissolution was part of a broader restructuring.

### Express Assumption Was Limited to Partnership Interest

Even assuming arguendo that some form of successor liability could apply, the express assumption in the Assumption Agreement was limited to PBS One's "right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language does not constitute an express assumption of personal contractual obligations such as those under the Consulting Agreement. The Assumption Agreement makes no reference to the Consulting Agreement, Arthur, or any consulting services obligation.

The plaintiff cannot transform a limited partnership interest assumption into an assumption of all corporate liabilities. As this Court held in *Vernon*, 251 Ill. App. 3d at 381, "the assumption of liabilities must be explicit and cannot be inferred from the mere transfer of assets." The explicit limitation in the Assumption Agreement defeats any inference of broad assumption.

## III. Defense Response to Count XI: The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Post-Dissolution Liability Against a Dissolved Corporate Shell

The plaintiff's argument regarding Count XI relies upon the Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, to preserve a claim against PBS One that arose after its dissolution. This argument misapplies the statute and ignores fundamental principles of corporate dissolution.

### The Survival Statute Preserves Claims *Against* the Dissolved Corporation, Not Claims on Obligations Transferred to Others

The Survival Statute provides that "actions" do not die with the person of the defendant. However, the statute does not create new liabilities; it merely preserves existing claims. The plaintiff's argument conflates the existence of an obligation with the survivability of a claim against a specific defendant.

The critical facts are: (1) PBS One was dissolved in June 1994; (2) at the time of dissolution, Tang testified that "PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment. Just the investment amount"; (3) PBS One was dissolved because "we have no reason to have the [sic] PBS One at all"; (4) the alleged breach occurred in July 1998, four years after dissolution.

The plaintiff cannot circumvent corporate dissolution by invoking the Survival Statute to hold a dissolved corporation liable for obligations that were allegedly transferred to other entities. If the Consulting Agreement obligations were assumed by Pielet LP in 1988 (as the plaintiff contends), and subsequently by National Material in 1991 (as the plaintiff further contends), then PBS One had no remaining obligation at the time of dissolution. The plaintiff cannot have it both ways—asserting that the obligations were transferred to others while simultaneously claiming that PBS One remained liable.

### The Cases Cited by Plaintiff Are Distinguishable

The plaintiff cites *In re Estate of Kline*, 216 Ill. App. 3d 206 (1st Dist. 1991), for the proposition that "the survival statute applies when the debt or obligation was created prior to dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs." However, Kline involved a claim against a decedent's estate, not a dissolved corporation. The Court in Kline was addressing whether a cause of action for breach of contract survived the death of a contracting party—a fundamentally different legal question than whether a dissolved corporation remains liable for post-dissolution breaches.

Similarly, *In re Marriage of Helfrich*, 108 Ill. App. 3d 404 (2nd Dist. 1982), involved the survivability of a marital dissolution claim, not corporate liability. And *In re Estate of D'Amato*, 175 Ill. App. 3d 478 (1st Dist. 1988), addressed estate administration, not post-dissolution corporate liability.

These cases do not stand for the proposition that a dissolved corporation remains liable for obligations that accrued years after dissolution, particularly where the corporation had no assets at dissolution and was dissolved precisely because it had no remaining purpose.

### Public Policy Favors Finality of Corporate Dissolution

Illinois law recognizes that corporate dissolution provides finality for shareholders and creditors. See *Stathoulis v. City of Mount Vernon*, 164 Ill. App. 3d 225, 235 (2nd Dist. 1987) (holding that dissolved corporations cannot sue or be sued). To hold that PBS One remains liable for obligations arising in 1998—four years after its 1994 dissolution—would eviscerate the purpose of corporate dissolution and punish shareholders who legitimately wound up corporate affairs.

The plaintiff had notice of the 1994 dissolution and could have asserted claims at that time, if any existed. Having failed to do so, the plaintiff cannot now seek to hold a dissolved corporate shell liable for post-dissolution events.

## I. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS ON COUNT XI (SURVIVAL STATUTE)

The plaintiff attempts to defend the trial court's erroneous application of the Survival Statute to preserve liability against PBS One. However, the plaintiff's arguments misstate Illinois corporate dissolution law and ignore critical distinctions between the survival of causes of action and the continued liability of dissolved entities.

### A. The Survival Statute Does Not Apply to Preserve Claims Against Dissolved Corporate Entities

The plaintiff erroneously conflates two distinct legal concepts: (1) the survival of causes of action against a decedent's estate, and (2) the continued existence of corporate liability after dissolution. The Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, addresses the former—not the latter. As the Illinois Supreme Court has held, "the purpose of the survival statute is to permit the deceased's estate to be sued for wrongs committed by the deceased, not to create new liabilities." *In re Estate of Kline*, 216 Ill. App. 3d 206, 210 (1st Dist. 1991). The statute ensures that personal injury and other claims against a decedent do not die with the person, transferring the cause of action to the estate.

This principle has no application to corporate dissolution. A corporation is a legal entity that ceases to exist upon dissolution. Unlike an estate, a dissolved corporation cannot be sued, cannot defend itself, and cannot hold assets. The plaintiff's reliance on *Kline* and *In re Marriage of Helfrich* is misplaced because those cases involved the survival of causes of action against decedents' estates—not corporate dissolution. As this Court recognized in *Stathoulis v. City of Mount Vernon*, 164 Ill. App. 3d 225, 235 (2nd Dist. 1987), corporate dissolution provides finality to the entity's legal existence.

### B. PBS One's Obligations Were Transferred and Extinguished

The plaintiff argues that PBS One remained liable because no novation occurred. This argument ignores the fundamental restructuring that occurred in 1988 and 1991.

First, in 1988, PBS One expressly assumed Pielet Corp.'s obligations under the Consulting Agreement through the Assignment Agreement. However, simultaneously, PBS One and Pielet Corp. transferred their respective assets and liabilities—including the Consulting Agreement obligations—to Pielet LP. Attorney Zavis's affidavit confirms that "the assets and liability, all the assets and all liabilities of [Pielet Corp.] were going down into an entity which was a limited liability partnership." This transfer extinguished PBS One's separate liability because the obligation was assumed by the new partnership entity.

Second, in 1991, PBS One transferred its entire partnership interest to National Material. The Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." At the time of this transfer, PBS One had no other assets or operations—it was merely a holding company for the partnership interest. Tang confirmed at his deposition that PBS One sold "everything" to National Material.

Third, PBS One was dissolved in 1994 precisely because it had no remaining purpose or assets. Tang testified: "we have no reason to have the PBS One at all. We have all the investment, you know, all finished. *** PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment."

The plaintiff cannot cherry-pick the record to argue that PBS One remained liable while simultaneously acknowledging that all of its assets and interests were transferred to other entities. The trial court's conclusion that PBS One's obligation survived dissolution ignores the complete transfer of obligations that occurred through the 1988 and 1991 transactions.

### C. The Plaintiff's Citation to 805 ILCS 5/12.80 Is Misplaced

The plaintiff cites 805 ILCS 5/12.80 for the proposition that dissolved corporations may be sued for winding up affairs. However, this provision applies to the winding up of a corporation's affairs—not to the perpetuation of contractual obligations that have been transferred to other entities. PBS One had no affairs to wind up in 1994 because it had already transferred everything to Pielet LP in 1988 and to National Material in 1991. The corporation was a shell with no assets, no creditors, and no purpose.

Even assuming *arguendo* that Section 12.80 applies, it does not support the plaintiff's position. That statute permits suits against dissolved corporations "for the purpose of winding up," not for the creation of new liability or the revival of obligations that have been validly transferred. The plaintiff's breach occurred in 1998—four years after dissolution and years after any transfer of obligations. This is not a winding up matter; it is an attempt to impose post-dissolution liability on an entity that had already completed its liquidation.

The Assumption Agreement contains no language indicating that National Material assumed personal contractual obligations such as the Consulting Agreement. The correct framework for analyzing corporate dissolution is found in 805 ILCS 5/12.80, which provides that a dissolved corporation may be sued "on any cause of action arising prior to dissolution" within three years of dissolution. This statute, not the Survival Statute, governs the survival of claims against dissolved corporations.

### B. PBS One's Obligations Were Transferred and Extinguished

The prosecution argues that PBS One remained liable because no novation occurred. However, the defense does not argue that a novation occurred. Rather, the defense argues that PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement were transferred to Pielet LP in 1988 and to National Material in 1991, and that PBS One was dissolved in 1994 after completing its winding up.

The prosecution cannot credibly argue that PBS One remained liable on an obligation that was the subject of two separate assumption agreements and that PBS One itself believed had been extinguished.

The Survival Statute preserves claims against a decedent's estate because the estate continues to exist for purposes of satisfying debts. A dissolved corporation, by contrast, has completed its winding up and ceased to exist.

The prosecution's attempt to apply *Kline* to this corporate dissolution context is an impermissible extension of the holding.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The prosecution limits its rebuttal exclusively to Count XI, offering no response to the defense's arguments on Counts IX and X. The defense addresses all three counts. Based on the undisputed facts, the applicable law, and the arguments presented, the Court updates its preliminary ruling and enters a final judgment as follows.

**II. ANALYSIS OF COUNT IX (ASSUMPTION OF LIABILITY)**

Count IX alleges that National Material assumed personal liability under the Consulting Agreement. The defense argues that the 1991 Assumption Agreement expressly limited the scope of the assumption to PBS One's "limited partnership interest," excluding distinct personal obligations like the Consulting Agreement. The defense relies on *Kellerman v. MPACT Real Estate Holding Co.*, 326 Ill. App. 3d 194, 200 (1st Dist. 2001), for the proposition that assumption of liabilities must be explicit and cannot be inferred from general language.

The undisputed facts establish that the Assumption Agreement states National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest." This language specifically restricts the assumption to obligations related to the partnership interest itself. The Consulting Agreement was a personal service contract distinct from the partnership interest.

The prosecution failed to address Count IX in its closing statement. Consequently, the defense's interpretation of the contract stands unopposed. Under *Kellerman*, the absence of explicit language assuming the Consulting Agreement precludes a finding of liability. Therefore, the defense is entitled to judgment on Count IX.

**III. ANALYSIS OF COUNT X (SUCCESSOR LIABILITY)**

Count X alleges that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One under the "mere continuation" doctrine. The defense argues that the elements of successor liability—continuity of ownership, assumption of liabilities necessary to continue the business, and dissolution of the seller—are not met. The defense relies on *Vernon v. Marketmen, Inc.*, 251 Ill. App. 3d 373, 380 (1st Dist. 1993), and *Nwosunda v. Chase Manhattan Bank*, 347 Ill. App. 3d 364, 372 (1st Dist. 2004).

The undisputed facts show that the January 1991 transaction was an arm's-length sale of a partnership interest for substantial consideration ($5,542,000.00). PBS One was a holding company with no operating business to continue. The prosecution failed to address the defense's arguments regarding the "mere continuation" doctrine in its closing statement. Without a rebuttal, the defense’s assertion that the elements of *Vernon* are not satisfied stands. Therefore, the defense is entitled to judgment on Count X.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF COUNT XI (SURVIVAL STATUTE)**

Count XI alleges that PBS One remained obligated under the Consulting Agreement after its dissolution. The trial court denied summary judgment to the defense on this count, and this Court previously upheld that denial. However, upon review of the defense's closing argument, the Court finds that the defense has raised a dispositive statutory bar that was not adequately addressed by the prosecution.

The defense argues that 805 ILCS 5/12.80, which governs suits against dissolved corporations, provides the exclusive mechanism for suing a dissolved corporation. That statute permits suits "on any cause of action arising prior to dissolution" within three years of dissolution. PBS One was dissolved on June 1, 1994. The plaintiff filed the motion for summary judgment on February 14, 2006, well outside the three-year window provided by 805 ILCS 5/12.80.

The prosecution argues that the Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, preserves the claim regardless of the dissolution timeline. The prosecution relies on *Ziegler v. Ziegler*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 1020, 1025 (1st Dist. 1993), and *In re Estate of Kline*, 216 Ill. App. 3d 206, 210 (1st Dist. 1991). While *Ziegler* holds that the Survival Statute applies to corporate entities, it does not hold that the Survival Statute overrides the specific limitations period established by the Business Corporation Act for dissolved corporations.

Under principles of statutory construction, a specific statute governs over a general one. 805 ILCS 5/12.80 is the specific statute addressing the survival of actions against dissolved corporations and sets a strict three-year limitations period. The general Survival Statute cannot be used to circumvent this specific legislative limitation. The defense correctly notes that allowing the Survival Statute to override the three-year bar would render the dissolution process meaningless and deny finality to dissolved entities.

Because the plaintiff's claim against PBS One was filed more than three years after PBS One's dissolution, it is barred by 805 ILCS 5/12.80. The prosecution's reliance on the Survival Statute is insufficient to overcome this specific statutory bar. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count XI.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the defendants on Counts IX and X. The Court reverses the trial court's denial of summary judgment on Count XI and enters judgment in favor of the defendants on that count, finding that the claim is barred by the three-year limitations period in 805 ILCS 5/12.80.
