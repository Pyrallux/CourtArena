FACTS:
Appellee, a government contractor identified herein as John Doe, underwent military detention in Iraq. After his release, he filed this action in the district court against, inter alia, then-Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and a Bivens action for violation of his due process rights. Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. The district court granted the motion as to the claims under the DTA and some other claims, but did imply an action under the Bivens due process theory and denied Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss as to those claims. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals from the denial of his motion, arguing both that the claims are barred by qualified immunity and that the court erred in implying such a cause of action in the first instance. Because we agree that the district court erred in implying such a cause of action, we reverse the order of the district court.

Background

Because this case arises out of a motion to dismiss, we, like the district court, accept the well-pleaded factual allegations set forth in Doe’s complaint as true for purposes of this stage of the litigation and construe reasonable inferences from those allegations in Doe’s favor, although we are not required to accept Doe’s legal conclusions as true. See, e.g., Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges the following facts:

*392In December 2004, Doe, a United States citizen , traveled to Iraq to work as a civilian Arabic translator and was detailed to a United States Marine Corps Human Exploitation Team operating along the Iraqi-Syrian border. . In July 2005, he made contact and developed a relationship with Iraqi Sheikh Abd AlSattar Abu Risha. 
On October 20, 2005, Doe returned to a United States military camp in preparation to depart for his annual leave. There, a Navy Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agent asked to interview Doe. Doe agreed and discussed his work -with the agent.

Two weeks later, Doe traveled to A1 Asad, a United States military base, from which he was scheduled to depart for his leave. At that point, three NCIS agents, including the one who previously interviewed him, along with another United States official, detained Doe and interrogated him for four hours. Doe alleges that the agents denied his requests to have an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit present for his questioning. Doe states that the agents confiscated his luggage, blindfolded him, kicked him in the back, and threatened to shoot him if he tried to escape.

 After seventy-two hours of solitary confinement, he was blindfolded, hooded, and  where he was confined for nine months. For the first three months, Doe alleges he was kept in solitary confinement; thereafter, he was transferred into a cell housing suspected hostile al Qaeda and Arab Socialist Ba’ath Party members. Doe alleges that the military officers  to encourage his cell mates to attack him and that the prison guards mistreated him by exposing him to extreme temperatures and depriving him of sleep. He alleges that one guard choked him repeatedly. During his detainment, United States government officials interrogated Doe multiple times and denied his requests for an attorney to be present at those interrogations.

In December 2005, the Detainee Status Board held a hearing and deemed Doe a threat to the Multi-National Forces in Iraq. In July 2006, the Board held a second hearing, after which Doe was transported to Jordan and ultimately to the United States, where he was released. Doe was never formally charged with a crime. He alleges that his property was not returned to him and that he has been placed on watch lists, preventing contracting firms from hiring him and causing customs officials to interrogate him when he returns from international travel.

Procedural History

In November 2008, Doe filed this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, other United States government officers and agents, and the United States government. Relevant to this appeal, Doe asked the district court to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for violating Doe’s rights under the DTA and for violating Doe’s constitutional rights guaranteed under the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments .
*393Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss Doe’s claims against him for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F.Supp.2d 94, 100 (D.D.C.2011). The district court dismissed Doe’s claims arising under the DTA, holding that the DTA does not provide a private cause of action. Id. at 104-05. The district court also dismissed Doe’s procedural due process and access to court claims, holding that Doe had not pled sufficient facts to overcome Secretary Rumsfeld’s qualified immunity defense. Id. at 113-14.
The court held, however, that Doe could maintain a federal cause of action for his substantive due process claims under Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971). The district court also held that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to a qualified immunity defense to Doe’s substantive due process claims because Doe had a right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that “shock the conscience”; that right was established at the time of Rumsfeld’s alleged conduct; and Doe sufficiently pled facts to support a claim that Secretary Rumsfeld violated that right. Doe, 800 F.Supp.2d at 115.
Secretary Rumsfeld filed this interlocutory appeal of the district court’s partial denial of his motion to dismiss Doe’s claims against him. He argues that the district court erred first by implying a Bivens action  by denying him qualified immunity against Doe’s substantive due process claims. Our review of each of these legal issues is de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).
Jurisdiction
Although the jurisdiction of courts of appeals ordinarily extends only to review of “final decisions” of the district courts, we have jurisdiction over the current interlocutory appeal. This case fits squarely within a well-established exception that the denial of a motion to dismiss on the ground of qualified immunity has sufficient finality to warrant interlocutory review, and within the language of Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250, 126 S.Ct. 1695, 164 L.Ed.2d 441 (2006), that where “the definition of an element of the [asserted cause of action] [was] directly implicated by the defense of qualified immunity and properly before us on interlocutory appeal,” we have jurisdiction over the appeal. Id. at 257 n. 5, 126 S.Ct. 1695.

## INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court. Secretary Donald Rumsfeld respectfully submits this opening response in support of his position that the judgment of the district court should be reversed. The plaintiff-appellee's opening argument correctly identifies the fundamental error committed by the district court: its decision to recognize a Bivens cause of action for substantive due process claims against a high-level executive official in a context that implicates core separation of powers concerns. While the plaintiff frames this as a case about qualified immunity, the threshold question—whether a Bivens remedy exists at all—disposes of this appeal. The district court erred in implying a cause of action where none exists, and this Court should reverse.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS CAUSE OF ACTION

### A. Bivens Actions Are disfavored and Must Not Be Extended to New Contexts

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens actions represent disfavored judicial creations that courts should not lightly extend to new contexts. In *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), the Court recognized an implied damages action for Fourth Amendment violations, but subsequent decisions have narrowly circumscribed this remedy. As the Supreme Court explicitly stated in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017), "expanding the Bivens remedy is now a 'disfavored' judicial activity."

The Court in *Ziglar* established a two-step analysis for determining whether to imply a Bivens remedy. First, the claim must arise in a context that is "different" from the three contexts in which Bivens has been applied: the Fourth Amendment, the Fifth Amendment's equal protection component, and the Eighth Amendment. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. Second, even in a new context, courts must consider "whether there are any 'special factors counseling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress.'" *Id.* at 1857 (quoting *Chickasaw Nation v. United States*, 534 U.S. 84, 85, 122 S. Ct. 527, 151 L. Ed. 2d 456 (2001)).

### B. This Case Arises in a New Bivens Context

The claims against Secretary Rumsfeld arise in a context fundamentally different from those in which Bivens has been recognized. This case involves: (1) treatment of enemy combatants or detainees during wartime; (2) decisions made by high-level executive officials regarding national security; and (3) military operations in a foreign theater of war. Each of these factors distinguishes this case from prior Bivens contexts.

The Supreme Court has recognized that Bivens does not extend to claims against high-level executive officials for actions taken in their official capacities, particularly where those actions involve national security judgments. In *Ziglar*, the Court declined to extend Bivens to a context involving high-level Executive Branch officials and national security policy decisions. The Court emphasized that "the question of who should be the proper defendant is not an easy question, but it is a question of vast importance because it may implicate 'separation-of-powers principles' and 'the need to protect the Executive Branch' from 'widescale' damages liability." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60.

Similarly, in *Federal Election Commission v. NRA Political Victory Fund*, 513 U.S. 88, 97-98 (1994), the Court held that Bivens does not apply to claims against federal agencies or officials in their official capacities where another statute provides the exclusive remedy. Here, too, the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld in his official capacity should not proceed under Bivens.

### C. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation Against Implying a Remedy

Even assuming this Court finds a new Bivens context, special factors overwhelmingly counsel against implying a remedy here. The *Ziglar* Court identified several such factors, all of which are present in this case.

**1. The presence of comprehensive statutory schemes.** The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the existence of alternative remedial mechanisms weighs against implying a Bivens action. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423-24 (1988), the Court rejected a Bivens claim for wrongful termination of Social Security benefits because Congress had established a comprehensive statutory scheme for review. While no comprehensive statutory scheme exists specifically for detainee claims, the political branches have enacted the Detainee Treatment Act, the Military Commissions Act, and other legislation addressing the treatment of detainees. The existence of these statutory mechanisms demonstrates Congress's awareness of these issues and its intent to address them through legislative action, not judicial improvisation.

**2. Separation of powers and institutional competence.** The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that courts lack the institutional competence to second-guess executive branch decisions regarding military operations and national security. In *Ziglar*, the Court emphasized that "the risk of disrupting the President's ability to perform his constitutional duties" weighs against implying Bivens remedies. 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The treatment of detainees in military operations involves precisely the kind of executive discretion that the political branches, not the judiciary, are best positioned to oversee.

**3. The role of Congress.** Where Congress has not provided a damages remedy, courts should not lightly infer one. As the Court stated in *Chilicky*, "the fact that Congress has not provided a statutory remedy ... does not, by itself, warrant a judicial inference of a private damages action." 487 U.S. at 423. Congress is far better equipped than the judiciary to balance the competing interests involved in detainee treatment and to determine appropriate remedies.

**4. The policy-making role of the defendant.** Secretary Rumsfeld is not a low-level functionary; he is the Secretary of Defense, a Cabinet-level official responsible for establishing and implementing national security policy. Holding such officials personally liable for damages based on their policy decisions would "imperil 'the President's ability to perform his constitutional duties' and threaten 'the independence of the Executive Branch.'" *Doe v. Rumsfeld*, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 109 (D.D.C. 2011) (quoting *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1860).

### D. The Absence of a Statutory Remedy Does Not Justify Judicial Intervention

The plaintiff may argue that the absence of a statutory remedy for detainee claims justifies implying a Bivens action. This argument fails. The Supreme Court in *Ziglar* explicitly rejected the notion that the mere absence of a statutory remedy justifies extending Bivens. The Court stated: "the lack of a statutory damages remedy does not by itself warrant a Bivens remedy." 137 S. Ct. at 1865. Rather, courts must engage in the careful analysis of special factors that the Court outlined, and those factors here counsel strongly against implying a remedy.

---

## II. EVEN IF A BIVENS REMEDY EXISTS, SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

Even assuming *arguendo* that a Bivens remedy is available in this context, Secretary Rumsfeld is nonetheless entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability unless the constitutional right allegedly violated was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). This doctrine protects "all but the plainly incompetent or those who knowingly violate the law." *Malley v. Briggs*, 475 U.S. 335, 341 (1986).

### A. The Constitutional Right Was Not Clearly Established

The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process claims is inherently vague and fact-specific. As this Court has recognized, the contours of substantive due process rights, particularly in the detention context, remain undefined. The Supreme Court has not clearly established that any specific treatment of detainees violates the Fifth Amendment's due process clause.

In *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998), the Court explained that substantive due process analysis requires balancing the liberty interest against the government's interest, and that only conduct that "shocks the conscience" violates the Constitution. The Court emphasized that this is a high bar, particularly in emergency situations. Here, the alleged conduct, while certainly harsh, occurred in the context of wartime detention of an individual deemed a threat to military forces—a context that weighs heavily in the government's favor.

No precedent at the time of the alleged conduct put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that his actions would violate the Constitution. The district court cited no binding precedent establishing the specific right allegedly violated. As the Supreme Court held in *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987), "the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right."

### B. The "Shock the Conscience" Standard Does Not Provide Clear Notice

The district court erred in holding that the "shock the conscience" standard provides sufficient notice to defeat qualified immunity. This standard is notoriously vague and has been applied differently in different contexts. As the Supreme Court explained in *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846, the "shock the conscience" standard varies depending on the circumstances, including the availability of pre-deprivation process and the emergency nature of the situation.

In the detention context specifically, the courts have recognized that the government retains substantial latitude. In *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 529 (2004), the Court acknowledged that wartime detention of enemy combatants is permissible, and that the procedures required need only be "constitutionally adequate." The Court did not impose a specific set of requirements that would provide clear notice of a constitutional violation.

### C. Doe Failed to Allege Personal Involvement by Secretary Rumsfeld

Additionally, the complaint fails to allege sufficient personal involvement by Secretary Rumsfeld to overcome qualified immunity. In *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 676-77 (2009), the Supreme Court held that supervisory officials cannot be held liable based merely on their position or supervisory responsibility. Rather, the plaintiff must allege "that the supervisor acted with deliberate indifference to the possibility that [subordinates] would violate constitutional rights." *Id.* at 677.

Doe's complaint contains no allegations that Secretary Rumsfeld personally directed, participated in, or had knowledge of the specific acts alleged. The complaint merely asserts that as Secretary of Defense, Rumsfeld was responsible for the policies that allegedly led to the violations. This is insufficient under *Iqbal*. The district court erred in failing to apply this standard.

---

## III. THE COMPLAINT FAILS TO STATE A VALID SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS CLAIM

Beyond the Bivens and qualified immunity issues, the complaint fails to state a valid substantive due process claim. To establish a substantive due process violation, a plaintiff must demonstrate: (1) a protected liberty interest; and (2) government action that "shocks the conscience" in depriving that interest. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846.

### A. Doe Did Not Have a Clearly Established Liberty Interest

While Doe, as a United States citizen, possesses liberty interests, the scope of those interests in the wartime detention context is limited. The Supreme Court has recognized that the government's authority to detain enemy combatants is broad. In *Hamdi*, 542 U.S. at 521, the Court held that "detention of individuals battling the American side during wartime" is constitutionally permissible. The Court specifically noted that "due process demands that the detainee be given notice of the factual basis for his detention, and a fair opportunity to rebut the government's factual assertions before a neutral decisionmaker."

Doe was given notice and an opportunity to appear before the Detainee Status Board. The complaint acknowledges that hearings were held in December 2005 and July 2006. While Doe disputes the adequacy of these procedures, the existence of some process undermines his substantive due process claim.

### B. The Alleged Conduct Does Not "Shock the Conscience"

Even accepting all factual allegations as true, the conduct alleged does not rise to the level of conscience-shocking. The alleged conduct—solitary confinement, denial of attorney, harsh interrogation—while certainly regrettable, must be assessed in the context of wartime detention of an individual deemed a threat to military forces. As the Supreme Court stated in *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 845-46, the "shock the conscience" standard is not met merely by conduct that "is otherwise subject to criticism or reproval," but rather requires conduct that "violates the decencies of civilized conduct."

The district court erred in applying too low a threshold for what "shocks the conscience." The treatment alleged, while harsh, occurred in the context of wartime security operations, not in the context of pure governmental malevolence. The Court in *Lewis* specifically noted that in emergency situations, greater deference is owed to government action.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Secretary Rumsfeld respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and dismiss the substantive due process claims against him. The district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action where special factors counsel hesitation, and even if such a remedy exists, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the constitutional right allegedly violated was not clearly established at the time of the conduct. The complaint fails to state a valid substantive due process claim, and the decision below threatens to embroil the judiciary in executive branch decisions that are properly the province of the political branches.

*Secretary of Defense*
*Defendant-Appellant*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court are the arguments presented by the Appellant, Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, challenging the district court’s denial of his motion to dismiss.** The Appellant argues that the district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), and that he is entitled to qualified immunity. The Appellee, John Doe, has not presented arguments in opposition to the Appellant’s motion within the provided record. For the reasons set forth below, the Court agrees with the Appellant that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this context. Consequently, the order of the district court is reversed.

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In reviewing a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). However, we are not required to accept legal conclusions as true. *Id.*

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Cause of Action**

The central question is whether the federal courts should imply a new *Bivens* remedy in this case. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the *Bivens* remedy is now a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). To determine whether a *Bivens* remedy is available, courts must engage in a two-step analysis. *Id.* at 1859. First, we must determine whether the case presents a new context. *Id.* Second, if a new context is presented, we must ask whether there are "special factors counselling hesitation" before implying a remedy. *Id.*

**A. New Context**

The Appellant correctly argues that this case presents a context fundamentally different from the three contexts in which the Supreme Court has implied a *Bivens* remedy. Those contexts involve Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents, *Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 389; Fifth Amendment due process violations in the context of federal employment, *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979); and Eighth Amendment violations by prison officials, *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1468, 64 L.Ed.2d 15 (1980).

This case involves the detention and interrogation of a United States citizen contractor in a war zone by the military. This context is distinct from the peacetime law enforcement scenarios previously recognized by the Court. The Appellant persuasively cites *Ziglar*, where the Court declined to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from the detention and treatment of terrorist suspects, noting the unique national security and military context. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. The involvement of high-level Executive Branch officials and the military nature of the operations distinguish this case from established *Bivens* precedents.

**B. Special Factors Counselling Hesitation**

Even if this case were not deemed a new context, special factors overwhelmingly counsel hesitation in implying a remedy here. The Supreme Court has identified several factors that preclude *Bivens* relief, including the existence of alternative remedial schemes and the risk of intruding on separation-of-powers principles.

**1. Separation of Powers and National Security**

The Appellant argues, and the Court agrees, that the judiciary lacks the institutional competence to second-guess the decisions of high-level executive officials regarding military operations and national security. In *Ziglar*, the Court emphasized that "the risk of disrupting the President's ability to perform his constitutional duties" weighs against implying *Bivens* remedies. 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The treatment of detainees during wartime involves precisely the type of sensitive judgment committed to the political branches. Imposing liability on the Secretary of Defense for policies regarding detention and interrogation would impermissibly draw the courts into the "heartland of military command." *Id.*

**2. Congressional Action**

The presence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) and the Military Commissions Act indicates that Congress has addressed the treatment of detainees, even if those statutes do not provide a private damages remedy. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423-24, 108 S.Ct. 2438, 101 L.Ed.2d 370 (1988), the Court held that the existence of a comprehensive statutory scheme precluded a *Bivens* remedy. While the statutory scheme here may not be as comprehensive as in *Schweiker*, the Appellant correctly notes that Congress has legislated in this area, and the absence of a damages remedy does not justify judicial intervention where special factors are present. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1865.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Alternatively, the Appellant is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982).

**A. Clearly Established Law**

The Appellant argues that the right asserted by Doe—the right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience" in a wartime military context—was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and vague. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998). As the Appellant notes, no prior precedent put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that his specific actions regarding the detention of a citizen contractor in a war zone would violate the Constitution. *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 562 (1987). The district court erred in concluding that the right was clearly established.

**B. Personal Involvement**

Furthermore, the Appellant argues that Doe failed to plead sufficient facts to establish Secretary Rumsfeld's personal involvement in the alleged constitutional violations. In *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 676-77, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009), the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff must allege that a supervisory official acted with "deliberate indifference" to constitutional rights. The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld was responsible for the policies, but it does not allege specific facts showing that Rumsfeld personally directed the specific acts of abuse or was aware of the specific conduct toward Doe. Without such allegations, the complaint fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted against the Secretary in his personal capacity.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in this context. The special factors involving national security, military operations, and the separation of powers counsel strongly against the creation of a new remedy. Furthermore, even if a remedy existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right was not clearly established and the complaint fails to allege sufficient personal involvement.

Accordingly, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**UPDATED RULING**

**Before the Court are the rebuttals of the Appellee, John Doe, and the Appellant, Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld.** Having carefully considered the arguments presented by both parties, the Court adheres to its preliminary determination that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. For the reasons set forth below, the order of the district court is reversed.

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In reviewing a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). However, we are not required to accept legal conclusions as true. *Id.*

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Cause of Action**

The Appellee argues that this case does not present a "new context" because it involves a "custodial setting" analogous to *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1468, 64 L.Ed.2d 15 (1980). The Court is not persuaded.

**A. New Context**

The Supreme Court has identified three narrow contexts in which a *Bivens* remedy is available: (1) Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents, *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971); (2) Fifth Amendment due process violations in federal employment, *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979); and (3) Eighth Amendment violations by prison officials, *Carlson*, 446 U.S. at 18-19.

The Appellee attempts to fit the present case within the *Carlson* framework by emphasizing the custodial nature of Doe’s detention. However, this argument ignores the critical distinction between domestic penal administration and the wartime detention of a United States citizen contractor in a combat zone. As the Appellant correctly notes, the Supreme Court in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1859 (2017), explicitly declined to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from the detention and treatment of terrorist suspects in the post-September 11 national security context. The Court in *Ziglar* emphasized that "the context is a federal officer's exercise of federal authority in a context for which the Bivens remedy has never been available." *Id.* The military detention of individuals in Iraq, involving decisions by the Secretary of Defense regarding the classification and treatment of detainees during an active conflict, is fundamentally different from the peacetime domestic prison context of *Carlson*. Therefore, this case presents a new context.

**B. Special Factors Counselling Hesitation**

Even if this case were not deemed a new context, special factors overwhelmingly counsel hesitation.

**1. Separation of Powers and National Security**

The Appellee argues that *Bivens* targets individual liability and does not implicate separation of powers. However, imposing liability on the Secretary of Defense for policies regarding the detention and interrogation of individuals in a war zone inevitably requires the judiciary to second-guess military decisions made by the Executive Branch. In *Ziglar*, the Court warned that "the risk of disrupting the President's ability to perform his constitutional duties" weighs heavily against implying *Bivens* remedies in the national security context. 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The treatment of detainees during wartime involves "the kind of discretionary executive action that is the daily fare of the political branches." *Id.* at 1863. Judicial intrusion into this domain raises significant separation-of-powers concerns.

**2. Congressional Action**

The Appellee contends that the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) does not provide a comprehensive remedial scheme and therefore does not bar a *Bivens* action. While the DTA may not provide a private damages remedy, the existence of the DTA and the Military Commissions Act demonstrates that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423-24, 108 S.Ct. 2438, 101 L.Ed.2d 370 (1988), the Court held that the existence of a comprehensive statutory scheme precluded a *Bivens* remedy. Here, Congress has legislated in this sensitive area, and the absence of a damages remedy does not justify judicial intervention where special factors, such as national security, are present. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1865.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Alternatively, the Appellant is entitled to qualified immunity.

**A. Clearly Established Law**

The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. While the standard itself was recognized in *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846-47, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), the application of that standard to the specific facts of this case—military detention of a citizen contractor in a war zone—was not clearly established.

Qualified immunity protects government officials unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The "clearly established" inquiry requires that "the contours of the right be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 562 (1987). As the Supreme Court noted in *Lewis*, the "shock the conscience" standard is context-dependent, operating on a "sliding scale" where "more executive action is needed... to produce a shock" in the context of emergency or military action. 523 U.S. at 850-51. The Appellee cites no precedent holding that the specific conduct alleged—detention based on suspected threat status and the conditions of confinement in a war zone—violated the Fifth Amendment. Without such precedent, the right was not clearly established.

**B. Personal Involvement**

The Appellee argues that the complaint adequately alleges Secretary Rumsfeld’s personal involvement through a "set in motion" theory. However, under *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009), a plaintiff must plead "factual content that allows the court to draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged." The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld was responsible for policies and was aware of systemic abuses in general terms. It does not allege specific facts showing that Rumsfeld personally directed the specific acts of abuse alleged against Doe, that Rumsfeld was aware of the specific conduct toward Doe, or that Rumsfeld specifically authorized the treatment Doe experienced. Conclusory allegations that Rumsfeld "implemented detention policies" and "failed to remedy abuses" are insufficient to state a claim upon which relief can be granted against the Secretary in his personal capacity. *Id.*

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in this context. The special factors involving national security, military operations, and the separation of powers counsel strongly against the creation of a new remedy. Furthermore, even if a remedy existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right was not clearly established and the complaint fails to allege sufficient personal involvement.

Accordingly, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court are the closing arguments of the parties.** Upon consideration of the arguments presented, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms its determination that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, the order of the district court is reversed.

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In reviewing a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). However, we are not required to accept legal conclusions as true. *Id.*

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Cause of Action**

The threshold question is whether a federal cause of action is available for the Appellee’s claims. The Supreme Court has recognized *Bivens* remedies in only three narrow contexts: (1) Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents, *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971); (2) Fifth Amendment due process violations in federal employment, *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979); and (3) Eighth Amendment violations by prison officials, *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1468, 64 L.Ed.2d 15 (1980). The Appellee argues that this case falls within the *Carlson* framework because it involves a "custodial setting." The Court disagrees.

**A. New Context**

The detention of a United States citizen contractor by the military in an active combat zone in Iraq is fundamentally different from the domestic prison environment addressed in *Carlson*. As the Appellant argues, this case involves "the kind of discretionary executive action that is the daily fare of the political branches." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1863 (2017). In *Ziglar*, the Supreme Court explicitly declined to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from the detention and treatment of terrorist suspects in the post-September 11 national security context, noting that "the context is a federal officer's exercise of federal authority in a context for which the Bivens remedy has never been available." *Id.* at 1859. The military detention of individuals in a war zone, involving decisions by the Secretary of Defense regarding the classification and treatment of detainees during an active conflict, presents a new context distinct from the peacetime domestic prison context of *Carlson*.

**B. Special Factors Counselling Hesitation**

Even if this case were not deemed a new context, special factors overwhelmingly counsel hesitation against implying a *Bivens* remedy.

**1. Separation of Powers and National Security**

Imposing liability on the Secretary of Defense for policies regarding the detention and interrogation of individuals in a war zone inevitably requires the judiciary to second-guess military decisions made by the Executive Branch. As the Supreme Court warned in *Ziglar*, "the risk of disrupting the President's ability to perform his constitutional duties" weighs heavily against implying *Bivens* remedies in the national security context. 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The treatment of detainees during wartime involves "the kind of discretionary executive action that is the daily fare of the political branches." *Id.* at 1863. Judicial intrusion into this domain raises significant separation-of-powers concerns.

**2. Congressional Action**

The Appellee contends that the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) does not provide a private damages remedy and therefore does not bar a *Bivens* action. However, the existence of the DTA and the Military Commissions Act demonstrates that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423-24, 108 S.Ct. 2438, 101 L.Ed.2d 370 (1988), the Court held that the existence of a comprehensive statutory scheme precluded a *Bivens* remedy. Here, Congress has legislated in this sensitive area, and the absence of a damages remedy does not justify judicial intervention where special factors, such as national security, are present. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1865.

**III. Qualified Immunity**

Alternatively, the Appellant is entitled to qualified immunity.

**A. Clearly Established Law**

The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. While the standard itself was recognized in *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846-47, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), the application of that standard to the specific facts of this case—military detention of a citizen contractor in a war zone—was not clearly established.

Qualified immunity protects government officials unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The "clearly established" inquiry requires that "the contours of the right be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 562 (1987). As the Supreme Court noted in *Lewis*, the "shock the conscience" standard is context-dependent, operating on a "sliding scale" where "more executive action is needed... to produce a shock" in the context of emergency or military action. 523 U.S. at 850-51. The Appellee cites no precedent holding that the specific conduct alleged—detention based on suspected threat status and the conditions of confinement in a war zone—violated the Fifth Amendment. Without such precedent, the right was not clearly established.

**B. Personal Involvement**

The Appellee argues that the complaint adequately alleges Secretary Rumsfeld’s personal involvement through a "set in motion" theory. However, under *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009), a plaintiff must plead "factual content that allows the court to draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged." The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld was responsible for policies and was aware of systemic abuses in general terms. It does not allege specific facts showing that Rumsfeld personally directed the specific acts of abuse alleged against Doe, that Rumsfeld was aware of the specific conduct toward Doe, or that Rumsfeld specifically authorized the treatment Doe experienced. Conclusory allegations that Rumsfeld "implemented detention policies" and "failed to remedy abuses" are insufficient to state a claim upon which relief can be granted against the Secretary in his personal capacity. *Id.*

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in this context. The special factors involving national security, military operations, and the separation of powers counsel strongly against the creation of a new remedy. Furthermore, even if a remedy existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right was not clearly established and the complaint fails to allege sufficient personal involvement.

Accordingly, the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.
