# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEAL

---

**Your Honor,**

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to Appellant Bradley's request to reverse the trial court's temporary injunction. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting injunctive relief, and Bradley's arguments misapply Florida law regarding covenants not to compete, irreparable harm, and the adequacy of legal remedies. We ask this Court to affirm the trial court's order.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a straightforward question: whether an employer who has documented evidence that a former employee is working for a direct competitor and selling the same products to the same customer base may obtain injunctive relief to protect its legitimate business interests. The answer, under well-settled Florida law, is yes. The trial court properly found that the employer demonstrated irreparable harm, a likelihood of success on the merits, and entitlement to equitable relief. This Court should affirm.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm

Bradley contends that the presence of a liquidated damages clause precludes injunctive relief. This argument misapprehends the relationship between contractual liquidated damages and equitable remedies in Florida.

In *Vanguard Investment Trust v. Legacy Marketing Group*, 886 So. 2d 1075 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004), this Court recognized that liquidated damages clauses and injunctive relief may coexist where the covenant protects interests beyond mere monetary loss. Here, the covenant protects the employer's customer relationships, goodwill, and confidential business information—assets that cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages alone. As the Court held in *Whitworth v. Kemick*, 334 So. 2d 324, 326 (Fla. 2d DCA 1976), "the inadequacy of the legal remedy is demonstrated where the injury is permanent or continuing and where the damages cannot be measured by any pecuniary standard."

The employer has demonstrated precisely this type of harm. The solicitation of active customers causes ongoing damage to business relationships that no fixed sum can remediate. The cases Bradley cites—*Miller Leather Co. v. Wood*, 394 So. 2d 434 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981), and *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 532 So. 2d 1039 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988)—are distinguishable because those contracts involved purely monetary breaches where the liquidated damages provision represented the parties' complete agreed-upon remedy. Here, by contrast, the covenant not to compete protects intangible business assets that transcend simple monetary compensation.

Furthermore, the enforceability of the liquidated damages clause remains contested. Bradley has challenged the clause's validity, and the trial court properly reserved this issue for trial while still permitting injunctive relief to preserve the status quo. The mere existence of a disputed liquidated damages provision does not automatically negate injunctive relief, particularly where the employer has demonstrated ongoing and continuing harm.

### B. The Trial Court Properly Found Likelihood of Success on the Merits

Bradley's argument that he is not "soliciting" customers misreads the covenant and ignores the evidence presented to the trial court.

The covenant prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Bradley was employed as a salesperson for the employer's blood plasma products. He then went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., selling blood plasma products. In *S & W Floor Covering v. J & S Floor Covering*, 514 So. 2d 1124 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987), the Court held that where an employee moves to a competitor and performs the same functions for the same product line, inference of solicitation may arise. The Court stated that "the nature of the new employment, the similarity of products sold, and the customer's potential for confusion" all support a finding of likelihood of success.

Here, the evidence demonstrates that Bradley:
1. Was hired specifically to sell the employer's blood plasma products
2. Possessed knowledge of the employer's customer base, pricing, and business relationships
3. Went to work for a direct competitor selling identical products
4. Was performing sales functions for competing products

This evidence amply supports the trial court's finding of likelihood of success. The employer's burden at the preliminary injunction stage is not to prove its case with certainty, but to demonstrate a reasonable probability of success. See *Florida Film Productions v. Duncan*, 360 So. 2d 1162, 1164 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977) (holding that the moving party need only show "a probability of success on the merits").

Bradley's argument that "there is no proof he is targeting Health Coalition's customers" ignores the reasonable inferences arising from his new employment. A former salesperson who moves to a direct competitor and sells the same products is presumed to be leveraging his knowledge of the former employer's customer relationships. The burden then shifts to Bradley to demonstrate that he is not soliciting those customers—a burden he has not met.

### C. The Trial Court's Order Was Properly Tailored

Bradley argues that the injunction was overly broad. This argument fails because the injunction tracks precisely the language of the covenant itself. The covenant prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The trial court enjoined precisely this conduct.

In *Ahrens v. PNC Financial Services Group*, 785 So. 2d 711 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001), this Court upheld injunctive relief that mirrored the restrictive covenant's scope, holding that "an injunction should be no broader than necessary to protect the employer's legitimate business interests." Here, the injunction is coextensive with the covenant—which is the precise limit of the employer's enforceable rights. Bradley cannot complain that the injunction is overly broad when it simply enforces the contractual terms to which he agreed.

### D. Bradley's Contention That He Was "Forced to Resign" Does Not Void the Covenant

Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in allegedly improper business practices. Even accepting this contention as true for purposes of argument, it does not invalidate the covenant.

Under Florida law, a covenant not to compete remains enforceable even if the employee's departure was involuntary, provided the covenant was supported by adequate consideration at the time of execution. *See Gator.com Corp. v. Lazzara*, 33 So. 2d 3d 1187, 1192 (Fla. 2d DCA 2005). Bradley signed the employment agreement containing the covenant in March 1993, and he received employment and compensation in exchange for his agreement to the restrictive terms. The continued employment constituted valid consideration supporting the covenant.

Moreover, Bradley's assertion that he refused to engage in improper practices does not establish that the covenant is unenforceable. The proper forum for resolving such disputes is the underlying breach-of-contract action, not a challenge to the preliminary injunction. The trial court properly preserved the status quo pending a full adjudication of Bradley's defenses.

### E. The Public Interest Supports Injunctive Relief

The public interest favors enforcement of reasonable covenants not to compete when necessary to protect legitimate business interests. Florida courts have consistently recognized that employers invest significant resources in developing customer relationships and confidential information, and that injunctive protection is essential to preserve these investments. *See Wembley, Inc. v. Manna*, 822 So. 2d 594, 597 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002) ("Florida public policy supports the enforcement of reasonable covenants not to compete when necessary to protect legitimate business interests.").

Here, the employer invested in training Bradley, providing him with access to confidential customer information, and enabling him to develop relationships with the employer's customers. Permitting Bradley to immediately exploit these relationships for a competitor would cause harm that no post-hoc monetary judgment could remedy.

---

## III. CONCLUSION

The trial court properly applied the law in granting injunctive relief. The employer demonstrated irreparable harm arising from the ongoing threat to its customer relationships, a likelihood of success on the merits based on Bradley's employment with a direct competitor, and entitlement to equitable relief. Bradley's arguments misapply Florida law and ask this Court to adopt a rule that would render covenants not to compete virtually unenforceable—a result contrary to Florida's long-standing policy of protecting legitimate business interests.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction.

---

**Respectfully submitted,**

*[Defense Counsel]*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion in granting a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. For the reasons discussed below, the Court finds that the trial court’s order must be reversed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A party seeking a temporary injunction must demonstrate the following: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate legal remedy; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. See *Florida Film Productions, Inc. v. Duncan*, 360 So. 2d 1162, 1164 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977). The trial court’s ruling is reviewed for an abuse of discretion.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Irreparable Harm and the Liquidated Damages Clause**

The trial court erred in finding a likelihood of irreparable harm. The employment agreement between the parties contains a liquidated damages clause, which establishes a predetermined monetary remedy for any breach of the noncompetition agreement. Under Florida law, the existence of a valid liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have agreed that money is an adequate remedy for the breach.

As stated in *Miller Leather Co. v. Wood*, 394 So. 2d 434, 435 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981), "a liquidated damages clause negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have contractually agreed upon a specific monetary remedy." Similarly, in *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 532 So. 2d 1039, 1041 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), the court emphasized that "when a contract specifies liquidated damages, the party seeking injunctive relief must prove the damages are inadequate—a standard that is virtually impossible to satisfy where the parties have agreed to a fixed sum."

The Appellee argues that liquidated damages and injunctive relief may coexist where the covenant protects intangible assets, citing *Vanguard Investment Trust v. Legacy Marketing Group*, 886 So. 2d 1075 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004). However, the Appellee failed to present evidence that the liquidated damages clause in this specific agreement is insufficient or unenforceable. Without such a showing, the legal remedy prescribed by the contract remains the exclusive remedy, and the trial court improperly disregarded the clear legal principle that monetary damages are adequate where such a clause exists.

**B. Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also erred in finding a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete in this case is narrowly tailored; it prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not prohibit general employment with a competitor.

The Appellee has presented no evidence that Appellant is actually soliciting Health Coalition’s specific clients. The Appellee relies solely on the fact that Appellant is employed by Bio-Med, Inc., a competitor, and sells similar products. However, as this Court held in *S & W Floor Covering v. J & S Floor Covering*, 514 So. 2d 1124, 1126 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987), "a covenant not to compete that restricts only solicitation of specific customers does not prohibit general employment with a competitor; it only bars solicitation of the former employer’s customers."

The Appellee’s argument that an inference of solicitation arises from the nature of the new employment is insufficient to meet the burden for a temporary injunction. Injunctions cannot be issued based on "mere speculation" or "conclusory allegations," as recognized in *Hill v. Smith*, 317 So. 2d 790 (Fla. 1st DCA 1975). The Appellee provided no specific evidence—such as customer lists, communications, or sales records—demonstrating that Appellant has targeted or solicited "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition. Consequently, the Appellee failed to establish a reasonable probability of success on the claim that Appellant breached the covenant.

**C. Scope of the Injunction**

Because the Appellee failed to establish the necessary elements for a temporary injunction, the trial court’s order is legally indefensible. The Appellee’s assertion that the injunction is properly tailored because it mirrors the covenant does not cure the fundamental lack of evidence supporting the underlying breach.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee failed to demonstrate irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause and failed to provide sufficient evidence of a breach of the solicitation clause to establish a likelihood of success on the merits.

**IT IS THEREFORE ORDERED** that the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**DONE and ORDERED.**

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

**Your Honor,**  

The Defense’s opening statement misapplies Florida law, ignores binding precedent, and relies on speculation to justify the trial court’s error in granting a temporary injunction. As the Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III respectfully submits that the trial court abused its discretion by issuing the injunction. The Defense’s arguments fail to satisfy the stringent requirements for equitable relief under Florida law, and this Court must reverse the trial court’s order for the following reasons.  

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING IRREPARABLE HARM DESPITE THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE  

The Defense wrongly contends that the liquidated damages clause does not preclude injunctive relief. This misstates Florida law. As this Court held in *Miller Leather Co. v. Wood*, 394 So. 2d 434, 435 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981), "a liquidated damages clause negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have contractually agreed upon a specific monetary remedy." Similarly, in *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 532 So. 2d 1039, 1041 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), the court emphasized that "when a contract specifies liquidated damages, the party seeking injunctive relief must prove the damages are inadequate—a standard that is virtually impossible to satisfy where the parties have agreed to a fixed sum."  

The Defense’s reliance on *Vanguard Investment Trust v. Legacy Marketing Group*, 886 So. 2d 1075 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004), is misplaced. *Vanguard* does not stand for the proposition that liquidated damages clauses and injunctive relief may coexist in all cases. Instead, it requires the party seeking injunction to prove the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable or inadequate—a burden the Defense utterly failed to meet. The Defense presented no evidence that the clause is unenforceable, that it fails to reflect a reasonable forecast of harm, or that monetary damages are insufficient to compensate for the alleged breach. Without such proof, the legal remedy prescribed by the contract remains exclusive, and irreparable harm cannot be established.  

The Defense’s citation to *Whitworth v. Kemick*, 334 So. 2d 324, 326 (Fla. 2d DCA 1976), is equally erroneous. *Whitworth* addresses situations where no liquidated damages clause exists and damages are inherently unquantifiable. Here, the parties explicitly agreed to a predetermined monetary remedy for breach of the noncompetition covenant. The existence of this clause means the damages are measurable by a pecuniary standard, directly contradicting the Defense’s claim that "damages cannot be measured by any pecuniary standard." By ignoring this distinction, the Defense attempts to rewrite Florida precedent to fit its desired outcome—a practice this Court cannot endorse.  

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS  

The Defense’s argument that Bradley’s employment with Bio-Med, Inc., alone suffices to establish a breach of the noncompetition covenant is legally unsound. The covenant expressly prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not restrict general employment with a competitor or the sale of blood plasma products to new customers. As this Court held in *S & W Floor Covering v. J & S Floor Covering*, 514 So. 2d 1124, 1126 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987), "a covenant not to compete that restricts only solicitation of specific customers does not prohibit general employment with a competitor; it only bars solicitation of the former employer’s customers."  

The Defense’s assertion that "inference of solicitation may arise" from Bradley’s new role is pure speculation. *S & W Floor Covering* explicitly requires evidence of actual solicitation—such as customer lists, communications, or sales records—to support such an inference. Here, the Defense presented zero evidence that Bradley targeted or solicited any specific customers of Health Coalition. His mere employment with a competitor and sale of similar products does not constitute solicitation under Florida law. As *Hill v. Smith*, 317 So. 2d 790 (Fla. 1st DCA 1975), makes clear, injunctions cannot issue based on "mere speculation" or "conclusory allegations." The Defense’s failure to provide concrete evidence of solicitation renders its "likelihood of success" argument untenable.  

The Defense’s reliance on *Florida Film Productions v. Duncan*, 360 So. 2d 1162, 1164 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977), for the proposition that "a probability of success on the merits" suffices at the preliminary injunction stage is irrelevant. That standard requires more than conjecture; it demands a reasonable probability grounded in evidence. Without any proof that Bradley solicited Health Coalition’s customers, the Defense cannot meet even this minimal threshold.  

### III. THE TRIAL COURT’S INJUNCTION WAS IMPROPERLY TAILORED  

The Defense claims the injunction "tracks precisely the language of the covenant," but this argument ignores the fundamental flaw: there is no breach to enjoin. In *Ahrens v. PNC Financial Services Group*, 785 So. 2d 711, 714 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001), this Court held that an injunction "should be no broader than necessary to protect the employer’s legitimate business interests." However, that principle only applies when a valid claim of breach exists. Here, the Defense presented no evidence of solicitation—only generalized allegations about Bradley’s employment. Thus, the injunction is not merely "coextensive with the covenant"; it is entirely unsupported by the record. An injunction enforcing a covenant where no breach has been proven is legally indefensible, regardless of its scope.  

### IV. THE PUBLIC INTEREST DOES NOT SUPPORT THE INJUNCTION  

The Defense’s invocation of *Wembley, Inc. v. Manna*, 822 So. 2d 594, 597 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), for the proposition that "Florida public policy supports enforcement of reasonable covenants" is irrelevant. Public policy favors enforcement only when the covenant is necessary to protect legitimate business interests *and* the party seeking relief has demonstrated the required elements for injunctive relief. Here, the Defense failed to show irreparable harm or a likelihood of success on the merits. Without those foundational elements, the public interest argument collapses.  

### CONCLUSION  

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction because the Defense failed to: (1) overcome the liquidated damages clause’s negation of irreparable harm; (2) present evidence of actual solicitation to establish a likelihood of success on the merits; and (3) prove that Bradley breached the covenant at all. Florida law requires more than speculation to justify equitable relief, and the Defense’s arguments fall far short of that standard. This Court must reverse the trial court’s order and remand for further proceedings consistent with the law.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. regarding the trial court’s entry of a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Upon consideration of the arguments presented, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction. The Appellee failed to establish the necessary elements for equitable relief, specifically regarding the existence of irreparable harm and the likelihood of success on the merits.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A party seeking a temporary injunction must demonstrate the following: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate legal remedy; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. See *Florida Film Productions, Inc. v. Duncan*, 360 So. 2d 1162, 1164 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977). The trial court’s ruling is reviewed for an abuse of discretion.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Irreparable Harm and the Liquidated Damages Clause**

The trial court erred in finding a likelihood of irreparable harm. The employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause, which establishes a predetermined monetary remedy for any breach of the noncompetition agreement. Under Florida law, the existence of a valid liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have agreed that money is an adequate remedy for the breach.

As stated in *Miller Leather Co. v. Wood*, 394 So. 2d 434, 435 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981), "a liquidated damages clause negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have contractually agreed upon a specific monetary remedy." Similarly, in *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 532 So. 2d 1039, 1041 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), the court emphasized that "when a contract specifies liquidated damages, the party seeking injunctive relief must prove the damages are inadequate—a standard that is virtually impossible to satisfy where the parties have agreed to a fixed sum."

The Appellee argues that liquidated damages and injunctive relief may coexist where the covenant protects intangible assets, citing *Vanguard Investment Trust v. Legacy Marketing Group*, 886 So. 2d 1075 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004). However, *Vanguard* requires the party seeking an injunction to overcome the presumption that monetary damages are adequate by proving the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable or inadequate. The Appellee failed to present evidence that the liquidated damages clause in this specific agreement is insufficient or unenforceable. Without such a showing, the legal remedy prescribed by the contract remains the exclusive remedy, and the trial court improperly disregarded the clear legal principle that monetary damages are adequate where such a clause exists.

The Appellee’s reliance on *Whitworth v. Kemick*, 334 So. 2d 324, 326 (Fla. 2d DCA 1976), is also misplaced. *Whitworth* addresses situations where no liquidated damages clause exists and damages are inherently unquantifiable. Here, the parties explicitly agreed to a predetermined monetary remedy. The existence of this clause means the damages are measurable by a pecuniary standard, directly contradicting the Appellee’s claim that "damages cannot be measured by any pecuniary standard."

**B. Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also erred in finding a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete in this case is narrowly tailored; it prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not prohibit general employment with a competitor.

The Appellee has presented no evidence that Appellant is actually soliciting Health Coalition’s specific clients. The Appellee relies solely on the fact that Appellant is employed by Bio-Med, Inc., a competitor, and sells similar products. However, as this Court held in *S & W Floor Covering v. J & S Floor Covering*, 514 So. 2d 1124, 1126 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987), "a covenant not to compete that restricts only solicitation of specific customers does not prohibit general employment with a competitor; it only bars solicitation of the former employer’s customers."

The Appellee’s argument that an inference of solicitation arises from the nature of the new employment is insufficient to meet the burden for a temporary injunction. Injunctions cannot be issued based on "mere speculation" or "conclusory allegations," as recognized in *Hill v. Smith*, 317 So. 2d 790 (Fla. 1st DCA 1975). The Appellee provided no specific evidence—such as customer lists, communications, or sales records—demonstrating that Appellant has targeted or solicited "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition. Consequently, the Appellee failed to establish a reasonable probability of success on the claim that Appellant breached the covenant.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised substantive defenses regarding the circumstances of his employment termination. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign for refusing to engage in improper business practices, specifically the resale of potentially unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. The Appellee denies these allegations, creating a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the enforceability of the covenant and the Appellant's "unclean hands." These factual disputes further undermine the Appellee's claim of a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

**C. Scope of the Injunction and Public Interest**

Because the Appellee failed to establish the necessary elements for a temporary injunction, the trial court’s order is legally indefensible. The Appellee’s assertion that the injunction is properly tailored because it mirrors the covenant does not cure the fundamental lack of evidence supporting the underlying breach. As held in *Ahrens v. PNC Financial Services Group*, 785 So. 2d 711, 714 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001), an injunction "should be no broader than necessary to protect the employer’s legitimate business interests," but this principle presupposes a valid claim of breach, which is absent here.

The Appellee’s invocation of *Wembley, Inc. v. Manna*, 822 So. 2d 594, 597 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), for the proposition that "Florida public policy supports enforcement of reasonable covenants" is irrelevant without a showing of irreparable harm or likelihood of success. Public policy favors enforcement only when the party seeking relief has demonstrated the required elements for injunctive relief.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee failed to demonstrate irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause and failed to provide sufficient evidence of a breach of the solicitation clause to establish a likelihood of success on the merits. Additionally, the existence of factual disputes regarding the Appellant's defenses to the underlying claim precludes the granting of a temporary injunction.

**IT IS THEREFORE ORDERED** that the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

The covenant here prohibits only the solicitation of "active, paid up customers of the corporation"—not general competition.  

Yet the plaintiff presents no evidence that Bradley solicited *any* specific customer of Health Coalition. No customer lists. No communications. No sales records. No testimony from a single client confirming solicitation. This is not merely insufficient—it is fatal. As this Court ruled in *Hill v. Smith*, 317 So. 2d 790 (Fla. 1st DCA 1975), "injunctions cannot be issued based on 'mere speculation' or 'conclusory allegations.'" The plaintiff’s reliance on the mere fact of Bradley’s employment with a competitor is precisely the type of speculation Florida law forbids.  

The plaintiff argues *Florida Film Productions, Inc. v. Duncan*, 360 So. 2d 1162, 1164 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977), requires only a "probability of success on the merits." But *Hill v. Smith* makes clear this probability must be grounded in evidence—not conjecture. The plaintiff has zero evidence of actual solicitation. Its "inference" argument is pure speculation, and speculation cannot justify an injunction.  

### III. THE PLAINTIFF’S SCOPE AND PUBLIC INTEREST ARGUMENTS ARE LEGALLY BASELESS  
The plaintiff claims the injunction is "properly tailored" because it mirrors the covenant’s language. But this ignores a foundational principle: an injunction must be supported by evidence of breach. In *Ahrens v. PNC Financial Services Group*, 785 So. 2d 711, 714 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001), this Court held that an injunction "should be no broader than necessary to protect the employer’s legitimate business interests," but this principle presupposes a valid claim of breach. Here, the plaintiff presented no evidence of solicitation—the very conduct the covenant prohibits. Without proof of breach, the injunction is not merely overbroad; it is entirely unsupported.  

The plaintiff’s invocation of *Wembley, Inc. v. Manna*, 822 So. 2d 594, 597 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), is irrelevant. *Wembley* stands for the proposition that Florida public policy supports enforcement of reasonable covenants—but only *after* the party seeking relief has demonstrated the required elements for injunctive relief. As *Florida Film Productions* makes clear, a party must prove "(1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate legal remedy; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest." The plaintiff has failed on the first three elements. Public policy cannot salvage an injunction where the foundational requirements are absent.  

### IV. THE PLAINTIFF IGNORES SUBSTANTIVE DEFENSES THAT UNDERMINE LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS  
The plaintiff’s rebuttal fails entirely to address Bradley’s substantive defenses to the underlying claim. Bradley contends he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in improper business practices—specifically, the resale of plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe due to shipping damage, and the alteration of invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed. The plaintiff denies these allegations, creating a genuine dispute of material fact.  

As this Court held in its preliminary ruling, "the existence of factual disputes regarding the Appellant’s defenses to the underlying claim precludes the granting of a temporary injunction." Injunctions are equitable remedies, and courts must consider whether the moving party has "clean hands." Where a party’s own conduct calls into question the enforceability of the covenant—such as alleged demands to resell unsafe medical products or commit invoice fraud—the likelihood of success on the merits evaporates. The plaintiff’s silence on these issues is telling. It cannot ignore them and still meet its burden for equitable relief.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honor, the plaintiff’s rebuttal is a rehash of the same errors that led this Court to reverse the injunction in the first place. It ignores the binding effect of the liquidated damages clause, offers zero evidence of actual solicitation, and attempts to bootstrap public policy arguments onto a case where the foundational requirements for equitable relief are absent. Florida law is clear: an injunction cannot issue based on speculation, conclusory allegations, or contractual remedies that negate irreparable harm.  

The trial court abused its discretion by granting this injunction. The plaintiff has not met its burden on any element of the test for equitable relief. For the reasons stated, this Court must affirm its ruling reversing the temporary injunction.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties regarding the temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Upon careful consideration of the record and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee, Health Coalition, Inc., failed to establish the requisite elements for equitable relief, specifically the likelihood of irreparable harm and a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A party seeking a temporary injunction must demonstrate the following: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate legal remedy; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. See *Florida Film Productions, Inc. v. Duncan*, 360 So. 2d 1162, 1164 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977). The trial court’s ruling on a motion for a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Irreparable Harm and the Liquidated Damages Clause**

The trial court erred in finding a likelihood of irreparable harm. The employment agreement between the parties contains a liquidated damages clause, which establishes a predetermined monetary remedy for any breach of the noncompetition agreement. Under Florida law, the existence of a valid liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have agreed that money is an adequate remedy for the breach.

As stated in *Miller Leather Co. v. Wood*, 394 So. 2d 434, 435 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981), "a liquidated damages clause negates the claim of irreparable harm because the parties have contractually agreed upon a specific monetary remedy." Similarly, in *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 532 So. 2d 1039, 1041 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), the court emphasized that "when a contract specifies liquidated damages, the party seeking injunctive relief must prove the damages are inadequate—a standard that is virtually impossible to satisfy where the parties have agreed to a fixed sum."

The Appellee argues that liquidated damages and injunctive relief may coexist where the covenant protects intangible assets, citing *Vanguard Investment Trust v. Legacy Marketing Group*, 886 So. 2d 1075 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004). However, *Vanguard* requires the party seeking an injunction to overcome the presumption that monetary damages are adequate by proving the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable or inadequate. The Appellee failed to present evidence that the liquidated damages clause in this specific agreement is insufficient or unenforceable. Without such a showing, the legal remedy prescribed by the contract remains the exclusive remedy, and the trial court improperly disregarded the clear legal principle that monetary damages are adequate where such a clause exists.

**B. Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also erred in finding a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete in this case is narrowly tailored; it prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not prohibit general employment with a competitor.

The Appellee has presented no evidence that Appellant is actually soliciting Health Coalition’s specific clients. The Appellee relies solely on the fact that Appellant is employed by Bio-Med, Inc., a competitor, and sells similar products. However, as this Court held in *S & W Floor Covering v. J & S Floor Covering*, 514 So. 2d 1124, 1126 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987), "a covenant not to compete that restricts only solicitation of specific customers does not prohibit general employment with a competitor; it only bars solicitation of the former employer’s customers."

The Appellee’s argument that an inference of solicitation arises from the nature of the new employment is insufficient to meet the burden for a temporary injunction. Injunctions cannot be issued based on "mere speculation" or "conclusory allegations," as recognized in *Hill v. Smith*, 317 So. 2d 790 (Fla. 1st DCA 1975). The Appellee provided no specific evidence—such as customer lists, communications, or sales records—demonstrating that Appellant has targeted or solicited "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition. Consequently, the Appellee failed to establish a reasonable probability of success on the claim that Appellant breached the covenant.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised substantive defenses regarding the circumstances of his employment termination. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign for refusing to engage in improper business practices, specifically the resale of potentially unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. The Appellee denies these allegations, creating a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the enforceability of the covenant and the Appellant's "unclean hands." These factual disputes further undermine the Appellee's claim of a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

**C. Scope of the Injunction and Public Interest**

Because the Appellee failed to establish the necessary elements for a temporary injunction, the trial court’s order is legally indefensible. The Appellee’s assertion that the injunction is properly tailored because it mirrors the covenant does not cure the fundamental lack of evidence supporting the underlying breach. As held in *Ahrens v. PNC Financial Services Group*, 785 So. 2d 711, 714 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001), an injunction "should be no broader than necessary to protect the employer’s legitimate business interests," but this principle presupposes a valid claim of breach, which is absent here.

The Appellee’s invocation of *Wembley, Inc. v. Manna*, 822 So. 2d 594, 597 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), for the proposition that "Florida public policy supports enforcement of reasonable covenants" is irrelevant without a showing of irreparable harm or likelihood of success. Public policy favors enforcement only when the party seeking relief has demonstrated the required elements for injunctive relief.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee failed to demonstrate irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause and failed to provide sufficient evidence of a breach of the solicitation clause to establish a likelihood of success on the merits. Additionally, the existence of factual disputes regarding the Appellant's defenses to the underlying claim precludes the granting of a temporary injunction.

**IT IS THEREFORE ORDERED** that the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**DONE and ORDERED.**
