Your Honor, the court of appeals correctly rejected the appellant's claim that the trial court erred in denying disclosure of the confidential informant's identity. The motion to suppress hearing was strictly limited to determining whether the evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment—no more, no less. As the Supreme Court established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), disclosure of an informant's identity is required only when it is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." This standard does not apply in suppression hearings when the informant's reliability can be established through other evidence without requiring the informant's testimony.  

Here, the trial court had already heard testimony confirming the informant's reliability based on the accuracy of her past tips. The judge explicitly noted this when stating, "I am not sure of that because I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." In *United States v. Kithcart*, 218 F.3d 523 (5th Cir. 2000), the Fifth Circuit held that "a defendant is not entitled to disclosure of an informant's identity for a suppression hearing unless the informant's testimony is necessary to resolve the suppression issue." Similarly, in *State v. Williams*, 641 N.W.2d 823 (Minn. 2002), the Minnesota Supreme Court ruled that disclosure is unwarranted when the reliability of the informant is corroborated through other evidence and the defendant fails to demonstrate that the informant's testimony would be essential to challenging probable cause.  

The appellant's argument that the informant's identity was necessary for an entrapment defense is fundamentally misplaced. Entrapment is a defense concerning the merits of the charge—whether the defendant was induced to commit the crime—and has no bearing on the legality of the search itself. As the D.C. Circuit made clear in *United States v. Lewis*, 40 F.3d 1325 (D.C. Cir. 1994), "the Fourth Amendment suppression hearing is not the proper forum for litigating defenses that go to the merits of the charge; rather, it is confined to the question of whether evidence was obtained unconstitutionally." Because the suppression hearing focused exclusively on probable cause and the evidence of reliability was sufficient without the informant's testimony, the trial court properly denied disclosure.  

The court of appeals did not err in affirming this ruling. The appellant's claim fails because the informant's identity was neither relevant to the suppression issue nor essential to a fair determination of the Fourth Amendment question. The trial court's decision was legally sound and should be upheld.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The prosecution's argument mischaracterizes the nature of the defense motion and the scope of the issues properly before the trial court. The appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the decision below and remand for an evidentiary hearing on the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The trial court erred in denying disclosure because: (1) the informant's identity was relevant and potentially dispositive to the Fourth Amendment suppression issue; (2) the defense raised a valid claim that the informant may have set up the appellant, which directly impacts the reliability of the informant and thus the probable cause determination; and (3) the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress did not waive the defendant's constitutional right to disclosure.

---

**II. ARGUMENT**

### A. The Informant's Identity Was Relevant to the Suppression Issue

The prosecution incorrectly asserts that the suppression hearing was limited to determining whether evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment "and nothing more." This characterization ignores the fundamental relationship between the informant's reliability and the existence of probable cause. The very basis for the search was the information provided by the confidential informant. If that informant was unreliable, not credible, or potentially biased, there was no probable cause for the search.

In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (U.S. 1957), the Supreme Court held that disclosure of an informant's identity is required when it is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." The prosecution attempts to cabin *Roviaro* to trial proceedings only, but this reading ignores that suppression hearings equally constitute a "cause" requiring fair determination. The informant's reliability was the cornerstone of the probable cause assertion. Without that reliability being established, the entire evidentiary foundation crumbles.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Kithcart*, 218 F.3d 523 (5th Cir. 2000), for the proposition that disclosure is not required when the informant's testimony is not "necessary to resolve the suppression issue." However, this defense responds that here, the informant's testimony WAS necessary. The defense specifically argued that the informant "set him up" and that her belongings were found in his car upon release from evidence. These facts, if proven, would directly impact the informant's credibility and reliability. The defense was not merely attempting to relitigate guilt or innocence—it was challenging the very foundation of probable cause.

In *United States v. Merryman*, 630 F.2d 363 (4th Cir. 1980), the Fourth Circuit recognized that disclosure may be required at suppression hearings when the informant's identity bears on the reliability of the tip forming the basis of probable cause. The instant case presents exactly such a scenario.

### B. The Defense of Entrapment Directly Relates to the Informant's Reliability

The prosecution argues that "entrapment is a defense concerning the merits of the charge—whether the defendant was induced to commit the crime—and has no bearing on the legality of the search itself." This argument fundamentally misapprehends the defense's position.

The defense did not raise entrapment merely as a substantive defense to the charge. Rather, the defense argued that if the informant "set him up," this fact would demonstrate the informant's bias, unreliability, and potential fabrication of information. An informant who recruits a defendant into criminal activity and then provides information to law enforcement has a powerful motive to fabricate or embellish the basis for probable cause. This is precisely the type of information that goes to the "reliability" of the informant for purposes of the Fourth Amendment analysis.

In *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (U.S. 2006), the Supreme Court emphasized that defendants have a constitutional right to present a complete defense. The defense of being "set up" by the informant goes to the heart of whether probable cause existed. If the informant induced the defendant to possess the cocaine and then alerted police, the informant's information was not the product of an honest tip but rather a coordinated effort that should not support probable cause.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Lewis*, 40 F.3d 1325 (D.C. Cir. 1994), is misplaced. In *Lewis*, the court distinguished between defenses that go to the merits of the charge versus those that challenge the foundational reliability of the search. The defense here is not that the defendant was entrapped into committing the crime—it is that the informant's conduct demonstrates such unreliability that the probable cause based upon her tip must be questioned. This distinction is critical.

### C. The Trial Court's Factual Finding Regarding Reliability Was Insufficient

The prosecution asserts that "the trial court had already heard testimony confirming the informant's reliability based on the accuracy of her past tips." However, this defense notes that the record reflects the defense was challenging the sufficiency of this very testimony.

The trial judge's statement that he "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them" does not establish that this testimony was adequate for purposes of the Fourth Amendment analysis. The defense was entitled to test this testimony through cross-examination and through presentation of contrary evidence—including the informant's own testimony.

In *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (U.S. 1983), the Supreme Court established the "totality of the circumstances" approach to evaluating informant reliability, emphasizing that courts must assess "the informant's credibility, reliability, and basis of knowledge." The defense here was attempting to challenge precisely these factors. Without the informant's testimony, the defense could not effectively probe whether the "past tips" were truly accurate, whether they resulted in valid arrests or convictions, or whether the informant had any motive to fabricate.

### D. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Motion Did Not Waive Disclosure Rights

The prosecution argues that because the motion to suppress was "dispositive," the defendant cannot now complain about the denial of the disclosure motion. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the record reflects that the "dispositive" agreement was specifically limited to the motion to suppress. Defense counsel stated: "It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress, but at lunch and this morning—" suggesting that the agreement did not encompass other pending motions. The prosecution's own characterization of the agreement confirms this: the prosecutor stated the agreement was that "the judge is going to litigate an issue" on the motion to suppress.

Second, even if the motion to suppress was dispositive, the defendant's constitutional rights do not evaporate simply because he chose to exercise his right to plead guilty pursuant to an agreement. The right to challenge the constitutionality of a search survives a guilty plea when the plea expressly reserves the right to appeal the suppression ruling. See *Lefkowitz v. Newsome*, 420 U.S. 283 (U.S. 1975).

Third, the defense specifically argued that the disclosure issue was being re-urged because documents had been received "yesterday." This represents new evidence not before the court when the original ruling was made. The trial court erred in refusing to consider this new evidence.

### E. The Court of Appeals Applied the Wrong Legal Standard

The prosecution asserts that the court of appeals "correctly rejected" the appellant=s claim. To the contrary, the court of appeals applied too narrow a standard by treating suppression hearings as categorically different from trials for purposes of informant disclosure.

The correct standard, as articulated in *Roviaro*, requires disclosure when the informant's identity is "relevant and helpful to the defense" or "essential to a fair determination of a cause." A suppression hearing is unquestionably a "cause" requiring fair determination. The informant's identity here was directly relevant to whether probable cause existed, as the defense raised substantial questions about the informant's reliability, bias, and potential fabrication.

In *People v. Gagan*, 242 Cal. Rptr. 328 (Cal. Ct. App. 1987), the California Court of Appeal held that disclosure of an informant's identity may be required at suppression hearings when the informant's reliability is contested. This Court should adopt a similar approach.

---

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the decision of the court of appeals, remand for an evidentiary hearing on the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity, and grant such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant argues that the CI’s identity was essential to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the search and to support a potential entrapment defense. The State contends that the motion to suppress hearing was dispositive and limited to Fourth Amendment probable cause, and that the CI’s reliability had already been established through evidence of past tips. Based on the provided facts and arguments, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard for Disclosure of a Confidential Informant**

The privilege of the government to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is well-established but not absolute. The Supreme Court in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), held that "the privilege must give way to the defendant's right to a fair trial where the informant's identity is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." However, this standard is not met merely because the defense requests the name; there must be a showing that the informant’s testimony is necessary to the specific issue being litigated.

**B. Scope of the Dispositive Motion to Suppress**

The record clearly establishes that the hearing in question was conducted pursuant to a "dispositive" agreement between the State and the defense. The prosecutor stated, "the agreement between the State and the defense was that it was a dispositive Motion to Suppress; and at that point, there would be sentencing." Defense counsel confirmed this understanding, stating, "You are clear in your understanding of the Motion to Suppress."

This procedural posture is critical. As the State argues, and the defense effectively concedes by agreeing to the dispositive nature of the hearing, the forum was strictly limited to determining the legality of the search and seizure. In *United States v. Lewis*, 40 F.3d 1325 (D.C. Cir. 1994), the court noted that "the Fourth Amendment suppression hearing is not the proper forum for litigating defenses that go to the merits of the charge; rather, it is confined to the question of whether evidence was obtained unconstitutionally." Because the Appellant agreed to resolve the case via a plea if the motion to suppress was denied, he cannot now expand the scope of this limited hearing to litigate trial-level defenses such as entrapment.

**C. Reliability of the Informant and Probable Cause**

The trial court denied the motion to disclose, finding that the defense had not demonstrated the necessity of the CI’s identity to challenge probable cause. The judge explicitly stated, "I am not sure of that because I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them."

The Appellant argues that the CI "set him up," implying bias or fabrication, which he claims undermines this reliability. While bias is a relevant factor under the *totality of the circumstances* test for probable cause, see *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the trial court is the trier of fact at a suppression hearing. The court weighed the evidence of the CI's past performance against the defense's allegations of a "set up."

The defense relies on *United States v. Kithcart*, 218 F.3d 523 (5th Cir. 2000), to argue that disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is necessary to resolve the suppression issue. However, the Fifth Circuit in *Kithcart* also acknowledged that a defendant is not entitled to disclosure if the informant's reliability is corroborated by other evidence. Here, the trial court found corroboration in the "effectiveness of [the CI's] past tips." The defense's speculation that the CI "set him up" or that her belongings were found in the car does not negate the factual finding regarding the accuracy of her prior information, which the court found sufficient to establish probable cause.

Furthermore, in *State v. Williams*, 641 N.W.2d 823 (Minn. 2002), the court ruled that disclosure is unwarranted when the reliability of the informant is corroborated through other evidence and the defendant fails to demonstrate that the informant's testimony would be essential to challenging probable cause. The Appellant failed to meet this high burden. The "new information" regarding the CI's belongings, while potentially relevant to a trial on entrapment, does not retroactively invalidate the magistrate's probable cause determination based on the CI's established track record.

**D. The Entrapment Defense**

The Appellant’s assertion that the CI "set him up" is fundamentally a plea of entrapment. As the prosecution correctly argues, entrapment is a defense to the *merits* of the charge, not a challenge to the *constitutionality* of the search. See *United States v. Lewis*, 40 F.3d 1325 (D.C. Cir. 1994). The Appellant attempts to frame this as a reliability issue to shoehorn it into the suppression hearing. However, the trial court properly recognized that this was a "defense matter[] for trial."

Because the Appellant agreed to a dispositive motion to suppress, he waived the opportunity to present this defense at trial. He cannot now use the suppression hearing to discover the CI's identity to pursue a defense he contractually abandoned by agreeing to the plea structure. The CI's identity is not "essential to a fair determination of the cause" of the suppression hearing, where the sole issue was the existence of probable cause based on past reliability.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The hearing was limited to the dispositive issue of probable cause, and the court found the CI reliable based on corroborating evidence of past tips. The Appellant’s claim that the CI "set him up" constitutes an entrapment defense, which is irrelevant to the Fourth Amendment inquiry and was waived by the terms of the dispositive motion agreement. Therefore, the trial court’s ruling is affirmed.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Appellant appeals the trial court’s denial of his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the search and to support a defense of entrapment. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion to Suppress**

The Appellant argues that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress related only to the *consequence* of the ruling (i.e., a guilty plea if the motion was denied), not to the *scope* of the inquiry. While the Appellant is correct that a plea agreement does not inherently waive constitutional rights, the scope of a suppression hearing is inherently limited by its constitutional function. As stated in *United States v. Lewis*, 40 F.3d 1325 (D.C. The Appellant attempts to distinguish *Lewis* by arguing that he is not litigating the merits of the charge (entrapment) but rather the reliability of the informant to establish probable cause. Because the Appellant agreed to a dispositive motion, he cannot utilize this limited hearing to litigate a defense that is only relevant to the guilt or innocence phase of a trial.

**B. The Standard for Disclosure and Reliability of the Informant**

The privilege to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is not absolute. In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), the Supreme Court held that the privilege must give way where the informant’s identity is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." The "cause" in this instance is the determination of probable cause.

The trial court denied the motion to disclose after finding that the CI’s reliability had been established. The judge stated, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This finding is consistent with the "totality of the circumstances" test for probable cause outlined in *Illinois v. The Appellant argues that "new information"—specifically, that the CI’s belongings were found in the car and that she "set him up"—constitutes a "substantial preliminary showing" that the CI’s identity is essential, as required by *State v. The Appellant relies on *United States v. Probable cause is a flexible, common-sense standard; it does not require proof of guilt or innocence, nor does it require the absence of bias. See *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983).

The Appellant’s reliance on *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), is misplaced. *Holmes* addresses the exclusion of defense evidence at trial, not the disclosure of an informant’s identity at a suppression hearing. # DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

May it please the Court:

The Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the Prosecution's arguments and in support of the Appellant's position that the trial court erred in denying his motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant.

The Appellant, charged with possession of a controlled substance, filed a motion to suppress evidence and a motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The defense presented two grounds for disclosure: first, that the informant's identity was needed to challenge the probable cause determination, and second, that the informant was a material witness to the Appellant's guilt or innocence, particularly in connection with the possible defense of entrapment.

The Defense acknowledges that the motion to suppress was characterized as "dispositive" by both parties. However, the Prosecution erroneously conflates the *consequence* of a dispositive motion with the *scope* of inquiry permissible at the hearing. This conflation led the trial court to improperly restrict the inquiry and ultimately erred in denying disclosure.

---

## I. THE DISPOSITIVE NATURE OF THE MOTION DID NOT WAIVE THE APPELLANT'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHT TO CHALLENGE PROBABLE CAUSE THROUGH DISCLOSURE

The Prosecution argues that because the motion was dispositive, the Appellant cannot now claim a violation of his right to present a defense. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the mere characterization of a motion as "dispositional" does not inherently waive constitutional rights. As this Court noted in the ruling, a plea agreement does not inherently waive constitutional rights. The Defense does not dispute that the Appellant agreed to plead guilty if the motion to suppress was denied. However, the scope of what the trial court could consider in ruling on the motion to suppress is a separate question from the consequence of an adverse ruling.

The Defense specifically argued below that "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress." This is a correct statement of law. The reliability of a confidential informant is fundamental to the probable cause determination. If the informant is not reliable, there is no probable cause for the search. The Prosecution's position would create a perverse incentive: a defendant who agrees to a dispositive motion would be stripped of any ability to challenge the very foundation of the search—namely, the reliability of the informant who provided the information leading to the search.

The Prosecution cites *United States v. Lewis* for the proposition that suppression hearings are not the proper forum for litigating defenses that go to the merits. However, the Defense does not seek to litigate the merits of the charge at the suppression hearing. Rather, the Defense seeks to challenge the reliability of the informant, which goes directly to the existence of probable cause. This distinction is critical. The question of whether the informant "set up" the Appellant is not merely an entrapment defense to be raised at trial; it is directly relevant to whether the informant's tip was trustworthy and whether the officers had reasonable grounds to believe criminal activity was occurring.

The Prosecution's reliance on *Lewis* is misplaced because that case addressed a situation where the defendant attempted to introduce evidence of police misconduct unrelated to the search itself. Here, the Defense seeks only to challenge the reliability of the informant—the very basis for the search. This is squarely within the scope of a suppression hearing.

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT DISCLOSURE WAS NOT NECESSARY

### A. The Roviaro Standard

Under *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), the privilege protecting confidential informant identity must give way when the informant's identity "is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." The "cause" in this instance is the determination of probable cause for the search.

The Prosecution argues that the trial court properly applied *Roviaro* by finding that the informant's reliability had been established. However, this argument overlooks a critical aspect of the Defense's argument: the Defense presented "new information" that directly challenges the informant's reliability and suggests that the informant may have been involved in fabricating the tip or inducing the criminal conduct.

The Defense presented evidence that: (1) the confidential informant was with the Appellant on the day in question; (2) the confidential informant's belongings were found in the Appellant's car when it was released from evidence; and (3) the confidential informant "set him up."

This evidence, if true, would establish that the informant was not merely a passive tipster but was actively involved in the criminal activity—potentially inducing the Appellant to commit the crime. Such evidence is directly relevant to the informant's credibility and reliability. The trial court erred in dismissing this evidence as mere speculation without allowing the Appellant an opportunity to develop it through disclosure of the informant's identity.

### B. The "New Information" Constitutes a Substantial Preliminary Showing

The Defense's proffer regarding the informant's belongings in the car and her alleged "set up" of the Appellant constitutes a "substantial preliminary showing" that the informant's identity is essential, as required by *State v. Williams*, 641 N.W.2d 823 (Minn. 2002).

The Prosecution argues that the presence of the informant's belongings in the car merely shows proximity and does not prove that she fabricated the tip. This argument misses the point. The Defense is not required to prove fabrication at this stage; the Defense is required only to make a preliminary showing that disclosure is necessary for a fair determination of the probable cause issue. The evidence presented—that the informant was present, that her belongings were in the car, and that she "set him up"—creates a reasonable inference that the informant may have been biased or directly involved in the criminal activity. This is sufficient to require disclosure.

The trial court improperly weighed the evidence against the established record of past tips without allowing the Defense to develop its own evidence through disclosure. The existence of past tips does not immunize an informant from challenge, particularly when new evidence suggests potential bias or fabrication.

### C. Kithcart Supports Disclosure

The Prosecution argues that *United States v. Kithcart*, 218 F.3d 523 (5th Cir. 2000), does not compel disclosure because the trial court found the informant reliable. However, the Defense contends that *Kithcart* actually supports its position.

In *Kithcart*, the Fifth Circuit held that disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is necessary to resolve the suppression issue. Here, the suppression issue is whether probable cause existed. The Defense argues that probable cause cannot be properly assessed without examining the informant's potential bias, which can only be determined by knowing her identity. The trial court's finding of reliability based on past tips does not resolve the question of whether this particular tip was reliable, especially in light of the Defense's evidence suggesting the informant may have been involved in the crime.

The Prosecution's argument that the Defense's allegations are "speculative post-hoc allegations" is unpersuasive. The Defense has proffered specific evidence—presence at the scene, belongings in the car, and the "set up"—that warrants further investigation through disclosure. This is not speculation; it is a proffer of evidence that, if true, would undermine the probable cause determination.

## III. THE DEFENSE DID NOTImproperly SEek to Litigate Entrapment

The Prosecution argues that the Defense improperly sought to litigate an entrapment defense at the suppression hearing. This argument mischaracterizes the Defense's position.

The Defense argued below that "If the CI set him up, that is critical to his defense." The Defense did not argue that entrapment should be determined at the suppression hearing. Rather, the Defense argued that the informant's alleged "set up" goes to her reliability and credibility—which are directly relevant to the probable cause determination. An informant who induces criminal activity has a motive to fabricate or exaggerate the tip to justify the police action or to curry favor. This is a challenge to probable cause, not an entrapment defense.

The Prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: (1) whether the informant's tip was reliable for purposes of probable cause, and (2) whether the government induced the defendant to commit the crime. While both may involve the informant's conduct, they serve different legal purposes. The Defense's argument regarding "set up" is directed at the first concept—reliability of the tip—and is therefore properly before the Court in the suppression hearing.

---

## IV. THE APPELLANT'S RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE WAS VIOLATED

The Prosecution argues that *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), is inapplicable because it addresses trial, not suppression hearings. However, the Defense contends that the principles underlying *Holmes* are relevant here.

In *Holmes*, the Supreme Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to present a defense, and that evidence that is critical to the defense cannot be excluded without justification. Here, the Defense seeks to present evidence—the informant's identity—that is critical to challenging the probable cause determination. The trial court's refusal to order disclosure effectively prevented the Defense from presenting this evidence.

The Prosecution argues that the Appellant agreed to a procedural mechanism where the suppression issue would be dispositive. However, agreement to the consequence of an adverse ruling does not constitute agreement to be deprived of the ability to challenge the evidence. The Appellant agreed to plead guilty if the motion was denied; he did not agree to waive his right to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting probable cause.

The Appellant did not waive his right to challenge the probable cause determination by agreeing to a dispositive motion. The reliability of the informant is directly relevant to probable cause and must be subject to challenge.

The trial court's denial of the motion to disclose violated the Appellant's right to present a defense and to challenge the evidence against him. Accordingly, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Appellant appeals the trial court’s denial of his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the search and to support a defense of entrapment. The State argues that the hearing was a dispositive motion to suppress limited to the issue of probable cause, and that the Appellant failed to demonstrate the necessity of disclosure. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the applicable law, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Set Up" Argument: Reliability vs. Entrapment**

The State argues that the Appellant’s claim that the CI "set him up" is purely an entrapment defense, which constitutes a matter for trial rather than a suppression hearing. The State relies on *United States v. Lewis*, 40 F.3d 1325 (D.C. Cir. 1994), for the proposition that suppression hearings are not the proper forum for litigating defenses that go to the merits of the charge. While the State is correct that entrapment is a defense to the merits, the Defense has persuasively argued that the allegation of a "set up" is inextricably linked to the CI’s reliability and veracity—issues that are central to the probable cause determination.

Under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the determination of probable cause is based on the "totality of the circumstances." A critical factor in this analysis is the veracity of the informant. If the CI "set up" the Appellant, she likely had a motive to fabricate the tip or to exaggerate the facts to ensure the Appellant’s arrest. Therefore, evidence that the CI was involved in the criminal activity or induced the conduct is not merely a defense to the crime; it is a direct challenge to the trustworthiness of the information provided to the police. The trial court erred by characterizing the Appellant’s arguments solely as "defense matters for trial" without considering their impact on the CI’s credibility.

**B. The "New Information" and the Standard for Disclosure**

The Defense argues that the "new information"—specifically, that the CI’s belongings were found in the Appellant’s car and that she was with him on the day in question—constitutes a "substantial preliminary showing" that the CI’s identity is essential to a fair determination of the cause. This argument is consistent with the standard established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), which holds that the informant privilege must yield where the informant’s identity is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause."

The trial court relied on the CI’s past tips to establish reliability, as permitted by *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). However, as the Defense correctly notes, a history of reliable tips does not immunize an informant from a specific showing of bias or involvement in the instant case. The presence of the CI’s belongings in the car is a specific factual proffer that suggests the CI was not a passive observer but a participant in the events. Under the standard articulated in *State v. Williams*, 641 N.W.2d 823 (Minn. 2002), this proffer constitutes a substantial preliminary showing that the CI’s identity is necessary to challenge the probable cause determination. The trial court abused its discretion by refusing to allow the Defense to explore this connection through disclosure.

**C. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion**

The State contends that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress precluded the Appellant from litigating issues beyond the narrow question of probable cause. The Defense counters that the "dispositive" label referred only to the *consequence* of the ruling (a guilty plea), not the *scope* of the evidentiary inquiry.

The Defense’s interpretation is supported by the fundamental right to a meaningful suppression hearing. A defendant cannot be compelled to plead guilty based on a suppression motion without being afforded a full opportunity to challenge the foundation of the search. As stated in *United States v. Kithcart*, 218 F.3d 523 (5th Cir. 2000), disclosure is required when the informant’s testimony is necessary to resolve the suppression issue. Here, because the CI’s reliability is the linchpin of the probable cause analysis, and because the Defense has presented specific evidence challenging that reliability, the identity of the CI is necessary to resolve the suppression issue. The State’s attempt to use the procedural agreement to bar this inquiry undermines the purpose of the hearing.

**D. The Right to Present a Defense**

The State argues that *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), is inapplicable because it addresses the exclusion of evidence at trial. However, the principle underlying *Holmes*—that a defendant has a constitutional right to present evidence critical to his defense—has resonance here. The trial court’s refusal to order disclosure effectively prevented the Appellant from presenting evidence that the search was based on unreliable information provided by a potentially biased participant. While the hearing was not a trial, the denial of disclosure deprived the Appellant of a fair opportunity to challenge the legality of the search.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The Defense made a substantial preliminary showing that the CI was a participant in the events leading to the arrest, as evidenced by her belongings being found in the Appellant’s car. This participation goes directly to the CI’s reliability and the existence of probable cause. The "dispositive" nature of the motion did not strip the Appellant of his right to challenge the veracity of the informant. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
