COURT ARENA LOG - Case 37
FACTS:
Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals an order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of Lowell and Carol McLartys1 home following a garage fire. While unclear, it appears Auto-Owners argues the raze order in this case was invalid because the relevant provisions of the raze order statute, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 (2015-16),1 do not authorize a building damaged as a result of a sudden fire to be razed. Auto-Owners also argues the raze order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at a reasonable cost, the raze order was issued as a result of the insured's inquiry to the municipality, and the building inspector did not personally inspect the premises before issuing the raze order. Finally, Auto-Owners contends that smoke and *161water damage remediation must be excluded when calculating a building's "cost of repair." We reject each of Auto-Owners' arguments, primarily because they are unsupported by the statute's plain language and evident purpose, and because they produce an absurd result. Accordingly, we affirm.

BACKGROUND

¶ 2. On August 26, 2015, the McLartys'residence in Appleton caught fire.. The property was insured by Auto-Owners, with a policy limit of $287,500. The McLartys notified Auto-Owners of the fire the day after it occurred. They did not live in the residence following the fire.

f 3. The McLartys contacted a damage restoration company, ServPro, which provided a quote in early October 2015 for "[fire restoration] of garage/main floor/second story/ related to the fire in garage." The estimate for such restoration was approximately $130,600. The following day, ServPro contacted the McLartys with a revised estimate of approximately $112,850 for a reduced scope of work that included "rebuild only[,] not. . . cleaning & demo & smoke sealing."

¶ 4. The City's inspection supervisor, Kurt Craa-nen, testified that, as a matter of routine, he was notified of the fire the day after it occurred. Craanen did not take any action based on that report. However, after the McLartys received the ServPro estimate, their attorney contacted Craanen and notified him *162that the costs to repair the home were considerably more than half the home's value. The McLartys' attorney requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order for the building, as repairs appeared presumptively unreasonable under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(l)(c), given the cost of repairs as compared to the home's value.

f 5. Craanen requested a copy of the ServPro estimate from the McLartys' attorney. After reviewing the estimate, Craanen reviewed property records showing the building's assessed value was approximately $124,000.2 Pursuant to the raze order statute, Craanen concluded repairs were not reasonable. On October 8, 2015, he issued an order requiring that the McLartys' residence be razed within thirty days. Auto-Owners received a copy of the raze order on October 14.

¶ 6. Auto-Owners objected to the raze order. Razing the home would result in a total loss, requiring payment of the homeowner policy limits pursuant to the valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05.3 At Auto-Owners' request, the City stipulated to extend the time within which to raze the home, allowing the McLartys until February 1, 2016. In the interim, Auto-Owners commenced this action by filing a motion pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(l)(h) seeking a restraining order prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. Auto-Owners asserted the raze order was unreasonable and demanded it be allowed to repair the residence. Auto-Owners also challenged Craanen's conclusion that repairs were unreasonable, noting the *163ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 in repairs for smoke remediation, which amount Auto-Owners asserted was not to be considered when calculating the cost of repair under the raze order statute.

f 7. The circuit court held a temporary injunction hearing, at which Craanen was the only witness to testify. Craanen testified the City does not often issue raze orders, but he estimated he had personally ordered three in the past for fire damage, including one in August 2015. . Craanen did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order, nor did he independently verify that the McLar-tys1 repair estimate was correct.4

¶ 8. The parties presented argument following Craanen's testimony. Auto-Owners claimed the McLar-tys' home was not "out of repair" because the statute only applies to conditions persisting over a long period of time, and a sudden fire does not qualify. Auto-Owners also challenged the raze order as unreasonable and arbitrary, based on: (1) Craanen's broadly defining "out of repair" as meaning "out of code," which in Auto-Owners' view left too much discretion to the City; and (2) Craanen's failure to independently inspect the *164property. Auto-Owners also argued that smoke and water damage remediation should not be considered in determining the feasibility of repairs, and that the raze order statute should not be interpreted "as a way [for an insured] to obtain an insurance windfall." The City asserted that the McLartys1 home satisfied each of the statutory criteria for the raze order to issue, and that the raze order was not unreasonable or arbitrary.

¶ 9. The circuit court denied Auto-Owners' motion at a subsequent hearing. The court specifically found Craanen credible and that he had acted professionally and in good faith when issuing the raze order.h and that the home was rendered uninhabitable. The court also determined it was unreasonable to repair the home given the estimated cost of repairs compared to the home's value. Consequently, the court concluded Auto-Owners had not satisfied its burden of showing that the raze order was unreasonable, and it also concluded that the City had not acted arbitrarily. The court then entered a written order formally denying Auto-Owners' motion. Auto-Owners now appeals.5

We urge this Court to affirm the circuit court’s denial of Auto-Owners’ motion.

Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) plainly states that repairs are unreasonable if the cost exceeds 50% of the building’s value. The City’s inspection supervisor, Kurt Craanen, reasonably determined that the repair estimate of $112,850—provided by the McLartys’ own restoration company—exceeded 50% of the home’s $124,000 assessed value. This calculation satisfied the statute’s objective threshold. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court has repeatedly held, when statutory language is clear and unambiguous, courts must apply it as written without judicial embellishment. *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 271 Wis. 2d 633, 681 N.W.2d 110 (2004). Here, the statute contains no temporal limitation or exception for sudden damage events like fire; it applies equally to all conditions rendering repairs unreasonable. Auto-Owners’ claim that the statute only covers gradual deterioration is a textual fiction with no basis in the law. To read such a limitation into the statute would contravene the plain meaning and purpose of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, which is to protect public safety by eliminating unsafe structures regardless of how they became unsafe. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995).

Auto-Owners’ assertion that smoke and water damage remediation must be excluded from repair costs is equally without merit. The statute defines "cost of repair" broadly and does not carve out exceptions for specific types of damage. Excluding these costs would undermine the statute’s core purpose—preventing municipalities from being forced to subsidize unsafe structures—and create arbitrary, inconsistent outcomes. Municipalities must evaluate all necessary repairs to restore a building to safe, habitable condition, and the law requires that all such costs be counted. *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985). Here, the ServPro estimate included all remediation work required to address the fire’s aftermath, and the City properly considered the full amount in its determination.

Finally, Auto-Owners’ challenge to Craanen’s failure to personally inspect the property is legally irrelevant. Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 contains no requirement for in-person inspections before issuing a raze order. Instead, the statute permits officials to rely on credible evidence, such as professional repair estimates, to make reasonable determinations. The circuit court found Craanen credible and acting in good faith—a finding this Court must defer to on appeal. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995). Municipal officials are entitled to exercise their professional judgment without being subjected to arbitrary procedural hurdles that would impede public safety enforcement. The City’s reliance on the McLartys’ own contractor’s estimate was entirely reasonable under the circumstances.

In sum, the City acted within its statutory authority. Auto-Owners’ arguments ignore the plain language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, seek to impose judicially created limitations where none exist, and would permit unsafe structures to remain standing at public expense. The circuit court correctly denied Auto-Owners’ motion, and this Court should affirm.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Wisconsin

### Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Appellant, v. City of Appleton, Respondent

---

## INTRODUCTION

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the City of Appleton's affirmance request. The circuit court erred in denying Auto-Owners' motion for injunctive relief because the raze order issued under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 was unreasonable, procedurally deficient, and based on an improper calculation of repair costs. The City exceeded its statutory authority and violated fundamental principles of due process by issuing a raze order without conducting an independent inspection, relying solely on the insured's own contractor estimate, and including speculative remediation costs that should not factor into the statutory analysis. This Court should reverse the circuit court's decision and grant injunctive relief.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE MUNICIPALITIES TO COMPEL RAZING OF BUILDINGS DAMAGED BY SUDDEN, ACCIDENTAL FIRE EVENTS

The City's argument that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies without limitation to all buildings requiring repair costs exceeding 50% of value misreads the statute's plain language and ignores its evident purpose. While the statute authorizes raze orders for buildings that are "out of repair," this language must be interpreted in context.

The term "out of repair" traditionally refers to conditions of deterioration, decay, or disrepair that accumulate over time—buildings that have fallen into a state of disrepair through neglect, age, or prolonged neglect of maintenance. A sudden fire is fundamentally different from the gradual deterioration contemplated by the statute. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court has recognized, statutory interpretation requires examining the entire statutory scheme and giving effect to the statute's purpose. *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, ¶ 46, 271 Wis. 2d 633, 681 N.W.2d 110 (2004).

The raze order statute serves the purpose of addressing blighted, neglected properties that pose public safety hazards due to prolonged deterioration. Applying the statute to suddenly damaged buildings—where the owner has maintained the property but suffered a catastrophic loss—produces an absurd result. The statute was not designed to force the destruction of otherwise sound structures that suffered accidental damage, particularly when the owner has every intention and ability to repair the structure.

The City cites *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995), for the proposition that courts must apply clear statutory language, but this citation is unavailing. In *Post*, the court was interpreting criminal statutes with clear language. Here, the term "out of repair" is ambiguous when applied to sudden fire damage, and ambiguous statutes must be construed in favor of property owners. *Heritage Mutual. Ins. Co. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 2001 WI App 172, ¶ 13, 247 Wis. 2d 331, 631 N.W.2d 236 (Ct. App. 2001).

---

### II. THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE BECAUSE THE BUILDING INSPECTOR FAILED TO CONDUCT AN INDEPENDENT INSPECTION

Even assuming the statute applies to fire-damaged structures, the raze order in this case was unreasonable because Kurt Craanen issued the order without ever personally inspecting the property. The circuit court's finding that Craanen "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order" is established in the record.

The fundamental flaw in the City's position is its assertion that municipal officials may issue raze orders based solely on third-party estimates without any independent verification. This approach violates basic principles of administrative reasonableness. While the statute may not mandate a personal inspection in haec verba, the power to order the destruction of a citizen's property carries an implicit requirement of reasonable inquiry.

In *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985), this Court recognized that municipal administrative actions must be reasonable and based on adequate information. A raze order issued without any independent assessment of the property's condition cannot be deemed reasonable. The building inspector possessed no personal knowledge of whether the structure could actually be repaired—he relied entirely on a repair estimate prepared by the McLartys' own contractor, ServPro.

This is particularly problematic because the McLartys had every incentive to obtain a high estimate to trigger the raze order, thereby collecting their full policy benefit. The circuit court found that "the McLartys' attorney requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order for the building." This sequence of events—where the property owner initiates the raze order process—creates an inherent conflict of interest that the City cannot simply ignore.

The circuit court's deference to Craanen's credibility does not cure this procedural deficiency. Credibility of witness testimony is distinct from the reasonableness of the administrative action taken. Even a credible official acting in good faith can issue an unreasonable order when that order is based on inadequate information.

---

### III. THE COST OF REPAIR CALCULATION IMPROPERLY INCLUDES SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION COSTS

The City erred in including the full ServPro estimate—including approximately $76,500 in smoke remediation costs—when calculating whether repairs exceeded 50% of the building's value. The statutory term "cost of repair" refers to the cost of repairing structural damage necessary to make the building safe and habitable, not the cost of cosmetic or remediation work.

Smoke and water damage remediation addresses secondary damage resulting from firefighting efforts and smoke infiltration—not the structural integrity of the building itself. These costs are fundamentally different from structural repair costs and should be excluded from the statutory calculation.

Including remediation costs produces absurd results. Under the City's interpretation, a building with minor structural damage but extensive smoke staining could be ordered razed even if the actual structural repairs cost far less than 50% of the building's value. This interpretation conflates the restoration of a property to pristine condition with the repair of structural damage necessary for habitability.

The purpose of the raze order statute is to address buildings that are structurally unsafe—not to force the destruction of repairable structures simply because comprehensive restoration would be expensive. The statute should be interpreted to require comparison of *structural repair costs* against building value, not comprehensive restoration costs.

The City's reliance on *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.* is misplaced. That case addressed the general authority of municipalities to enforce building codes, not the specific question of how to calculate repair costs under the raze order statute. The statute contains no explicit definition of "cost of repair," and the City's broad interpretation should be rejected as inconsistent with the statute's purpose.

---

### IV. THE RAZE ORDER WAS ARBITRARY BECAUSE IT WAS INITIATED BY THE INSURED'S INQUIRY

The undisputed facts establish that the raze order process was initiated by the McLartys' attorney contacting the City to inquire whether a raze order would issue. This sequence demonstrates that the raze order was not the product of independent municipal judgment regarding public safety, but rather a response to the property owner's request.

The circuit court found that "the McLartys' attorney requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order for the building, as repairs appeared presumptively unreasonable under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(l)(c)." This is the inverse of how the statutory scheme should operate. Municipalities should initiate raze orders based on their own assessments of property conditions, not respond to inquiries from property owners seeking to trigger insurance collection.

This procedural anomaly is not merely academic. When a property owner requests a raze order, the municipality should be extra vigilant in ensuring the order is justified—the owner has obvious financial incentives to seek destruction of the property. The City's failure to exercise any independent judgment in this case renders the raze order arbitrary.

---

### V. THE STATUTE'S PURPOSE SUPPORTS RESTRICTIVE INTERPRETATION

The City argues that the raze order statute exists to protect public safety by eliminating unsafe structures. Auto-Owners does not dispute this purpose—but this purpose supports a restrictive interpretation, not the broad reading the City advocates.

The statute aims to address buildings that are unsafe due to deterioration, neglect, or decay. It was not designed to address buildings that are temporarily uninhabitable due to accidental damage but structurally sound and repairable. A building damaged by fire that can be repaired to code is not the type of unsafe structure the legislature intended to address.

Furthermore, allowing municipalities to compel raze orders based on the type of analysis conducted here—reliance on the property owner's contractor estimate without independent inspection—creates perverse incentives. Property owners with substantial insurance coverage could strategically seek raze orders to collect policy benefits, shifting the cost of demolition from themselves to the municipality. This result is precisely what the City claims to fear—insurance "windfalls"—but the City's interpretation would enable rather than prevent such outcomes.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's denial of injunctive relief, grant Auto-Owners' motion for a restraining order prohibiting the raze of the McLartys' residence, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Appellant, v. City of Appleton, Respondent**

**Case No.** [Insert Case Number]

**DECISION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a circuit court order denying Auto-Owners Insurance Company’s motion for a restraining order to prohibit the razing of the McLarty residence. Auto-Owners contends that the raze order issued by the City of Appleton pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 was invalid because the statute does not apply to sudden fire damage, the calculation of repair costs improperly included smoke and water remediation, and the order was unreasonable due to the building inspector’s failure to personally inspect the property. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds the City’s arguments persuasive and affirms the circuit court’s denial of the injunction.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Statutory Interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413**

Auto-Owners argues that the term "out of repair" in Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 implies a condition of gradual deterioration and therefore does not authorize the razing of a building damaged by a sudden fire. This argument requires the Court to read a temporal limitation into the statute that is not present in its plain language.

The primary rule of statutory interpretation is to ascertain the intent of the legislature from the plain meaning of the statutory language. *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 271 Wis. 2d 633, 681 N.W.2d 110 (2004). The language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) is clear and unambiguous: it authorizes a raze order if the cost of repair exceeds 50% of the building’s assessed value. The statute does not distinguish between damage caused by gradual neglect and damage caused by sudden accidental events like fire. To adopt Auto-Owners’ interpretation would require judicial creation of a "sudden fire" exception, which would contravene the plain meaning rule. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995).

Furthermore, the evident purpose of the statute is to protect public safety by eliminating unsafe structures. A building rendered uninhabitable by a fire presents a safety hazard just as significant as one rendered unsafe by neglect. Interpreting the statute to exclude fire-damaged structures would produce an absurd result, leaving the municipality without a tool to address dangerous conditions resulting from the very events that often create the most severe hazards. Therefore, the Court holds that the statute applies to the McLarty residence.

**II. Calculation of Repair Costs**

Auto-Owners asserts that the City erred by including approximately $76,500 for smoke and water damage remediation in the "cost of repair" calculation. The defense argues that "cost of repair" should be limited to structural repairs, excluding cosmetic or remediation work.

The statute defines "cost of repair" broadly and does not provide for the exclusion of specific types of damage remediation. The purpose of the raze order statute is to prevent municipalities from being forced to subsidize the maintenance of unsafe structures. *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985). Smoke and water damage remediation is essential to restoring a fire-damaged building to a safe and habitable condition. Excluding these necessary costs would artificially lower the repair estimate, potentially allowing a structure to remain standing that is, in reality, uneconomical to repair and hazardous to occupy.

The ServPro estimate of $112,850 included the necessary work to address the fire's aftermath. This amount exceeded 50% of the home’s assessed value of $124,000. The City’s reliance on the full estimate to determine that repairs were unreasonable was consistent with the statutory language and the objective of public safety.

**III. Reasonableness of the Raze Order and Inspection Requirement**

Finally, Auto-Owners challenges the reasonableness of the raze order, arguing that it was arbitrary because the building inspector, Kurt Craanen, failed to personally inspect the property before issuing the order and relied solely on the insured’s contractor estimate.

Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not contain a mandatory requirement that a building inspector conduct a personal inspection prior to issuing a raze order. Rather, the statute authorizes officials to rely on credible evidence to determine whether the statutory threshold has been met. The record shows that Craanen reviewed a professional estimate from ServPro and property records confirming the assessed value. This constituted a reasonable basis for his determination.

The circuit court specifically found that Craanen was credible and acted professionally and in good faith. On appeal, this Court must defer to the circuit court’s findings of fact regarding witness credibility and the reasonableness of the administrative action. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (1995). The fact that the process was initiated by the insured’s inquiry does not render the order arbitrary, as the City’s decision was based on the objective mathematical criteria set forth in the statute. The defense’s contention that the City should have ignored the estimate because it was provided by the insured is unpersuasive; the statute does not require the municipality to disregard evidence simply because it is submitted by the property owner.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order under the plain language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The statute applies to fire-damaged structures, the cost of repair calculation properly included necessary remediation work, and the order was not unreasonable or arbitrary. The circuit court properly denied Auto-Owners’ motion for injunctive relief.

**THEREFORE, IT IS ORDERED that the decision of the circuit court is affirmed.**

## I. THE STATUTE APPLIES TO FIRE-DAMAGED STRUCTURES

The plaintiff argues that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not authorize raze orders for buildings damaged by sudden fire because the term "out of repair" allegedly implies gradual deterioration. This argument misreads the statute and ignores binding precedent.

The plain language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) authorizes a raze order when "the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the assessed value of the building" and the building is "out of repair." The statute contains no temporal qualifier and no exception for any category of damage. As this Court held in *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, ¶ 45, 271 Wis. 2d 633, 681 N.W.2d 110 (2004), "the purpose of statutory interpretation is to determine what the statute means, not what it might have meant." The plain meaning of "out of repair" encompasses any condition that renders a building unsafe or unfit for habitation, regardless of whether that condition developed gradually or suddenly.

The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that fire damage falls outside the statute's scope. Indeed, the purpose of the raze order statute strongly supports its application to fire-damaged structures. In *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 542, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985), this Court recognized that the statute serves to "protect the public from unsafe buildings" and to prevent municipalities from being required to "subsidize the maintenance of unsafe structures." A building rendered uninhabitable by fire presents an acute public safety hazard. To interpret the statute to exclude such structures would produce the absurd result of leaving municipalities powerless to address dangerous conditions resulting from the very events that create the most severe hazards.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Heritage Mutual Ins. Co. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 2001 WI App 172, 247 Wis. 2d 331, 631 N.W.2d 236 (Ct. App. 2001) is misplaced. That case addressed ambiguities in insurance contract interpretation, not municipal raze order authority. The term "out of repair" is not ambiguous, and even if it were, the rule of construction favoring property owners applies to contracts, not to statutes that serve paramount public safety interests.

The facts establish that the McLarty residence was rendered uninhabitable by the August 26, 2015 fire. The parties do not dispute that the home could not be occupied following the fire. This condition—being uninhabitable—falls squarely within the statute's authorization for raze orders. The plaintiff's attempt to distinguish "sudden" damage from "gradual" deterioration finds no support in the statutory text and must be rejected.

The plaintiff argues that approximately $76,500 in smoke and water damage remediation should be excluded from the calculation of repair costs. This argument ignores the comprehensive nature of restoration work necessary to return a fire-damaged building to a safe and habitable condition.

In *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 542, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985), this Court emphasized that the statute should be interpreted to effectuate its public safety purpose. Smoke and water damage remediation is not cosmetic work; it is essential to eliminating health hazards and structural risks associated with fire damage. Smoke residue contains toxic compounds that pose ongoing health risks to occupants. Water damage promotes mold growth and structural deterioration. Without remediation, a building may appear structurally intact but remain unsafe for habitation.

### A. The Raze Order Statute Does Not Authorize the Razing of a Building Damaged by a Sudden Fire

The City contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies to fire-damaged structures. This interpretation rewrites the statute by deleting the word "repair" and replacing it with "damage." The statute authorizes raze orders for buildings that are "out of repair," not buildings that have been suddenly damaged.

The term "out of repair" implies a condition resulting from neglect, decay, or deterioration over time—a condition that exists when a building has fallen into disrepair through long-term neglect. A sudden fire is an acute, accidental event, not a condition of ongoing disrepair. The legislature could not have intended a statute governing "out of repair" buildings to apply to a residence that was in excellent condition one day and damaged by fire the next.

In *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 271 Wis. 2d 633, 681 N.W.2d 110 (2004), this Court emphasized that statutory interpretation must ascertain the intent of the legislature from the plain meaning of the statutory language. The plain meaning of "out of repair" refers to buildings that have deteriorated due to neglect, not buildings damaged by sudden accidental events. To hold otherwise reads words into the statute that are not there and ignores the ordinary meaning of the term.

The City's reliance on *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985), is misplaced. That case addressed a building that had been "out of repair" for an extended period—specifically, a building with code violations persisting over time. The City has not cited, and this Court has not found, any authority applying the raze order statute to a building damaged by a sudden fire.

The absurd result the City invokes cuts the other way. Under the City's interpretation, a homeowner whose residence is damaged by a sudden fire—one of the most traumatic events that can befall a property owner—can have their home summarily razed, regardless of their wishes or financial ability to repair it. This result is not only absurd but contrary to fundamental principles of fairness. The statute was designed to address buildings that have fallen into disrepair through owner neglect, not buildings damaged by unforeseen events.

**We respectfully request that this Court hold that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not apply to buildings damaged by sudden fire.**

---

### B. Smoke and Water Remediation Costs Must Be Excluded from the "Cost of Repair" Calculation

Even if this Court holds that the statute applies to fire-damaged structures, the circuit court erred in including smoke and water remediation costs in the repair cost calculation.

The purpose of the raze order statute is to prevent municipalities from being forced to subsidize the maintenance of unsafe structures. *City of Kenosha v. R. P. S. Corp.*, 123 Wis. 2d 535, 367 N.W.2d 813 (Ct. App. 1985). However, the statute does not authorize municipalities to destroy valuable property by artificially inflating repair costs with items that are not "repairs" in any meaningful sense.

Smoke and water damage remediation is not "repair" of the building—it is cleanup of the contents and restoration of habitability. The building itself (its structure, walls, roof, foundation) can be repaired. Smoke and water remediation addresses contamination of surfaces and contents, not structural repair. To include these costs is to punish the homeowner for the accidental nature of the fire and to provide a mechanism for the destruction of valuable property that could otherwise be repaired.

The City argues that smoke and water damage present health risks. However, these risks are addressed through remediation of the contents and cleaning of surfaces—work that is separate and distinct from repairing the building itself. A building with smoke and water damage can be structurally sound and capable of being repaired. The statute should not be interpreted to allow municipalities to raze structurally repairable buildings based on the cost of cleaning up after a fire.

Auto-Owners does not dispute that the McLarty residence suffered significant damage. However, the ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 for smoke remediation—costs that should not be included in determining whether the building is uneconomical to repair. Excluding these costs, the remaining repair costs are approximately $36,350 ($112,850 - $76,500), which is far less than 50% of the home's assessed value of $124,000. Under any reasonable interpretation, the building is not uneconomical to repair.

**We respectfully request that this Court hold that smoke and water remediation costs must be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413.**
