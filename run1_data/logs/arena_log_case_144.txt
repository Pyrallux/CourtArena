Discussion

[14] The issue is whether the trial court erred or abused its discretion in permitting Casey's statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. Generally, we review the trial court's ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State , 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State , 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We may affirm a trial court's decision regarding the admission of evidence if it is sustainable on any basis in the record. Barker v. State , 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998), reh'g denied . A trial court "has the duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted" before it, Garcia v. State , 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), and "is given wide latitude of discretion in carrying out [its] duties." Pitman v. State , 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

[15] Casey cites Ind. Evidence Rule 403 and asserts that the unsolicited presentation of the duplicitous and cumulative evidence deprived him of his right to a fair trial. He argues that Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 outlines strict circumstances when a jury may be entitled to receive duplicitous or cumulative evidence and none of the circumstances were present in his case.

[16] The State argues the trial court properly exercised its discretion by replaying Casey's statement to police in response to jury questions. It asserts that the trial court reasonably exercised its discretion to replay the evidence based on the multiple jury questions asking about the content of the already played video and other concerns regarding audio problems and redaction. The State also contends that Casey has not shown substantial prejudice.

[17] Initially, we note that Casey did not specifically mention Ind. Evidence Rule 403 in his objection to the replaying of the video at trial. Ind. Evidence Rule 403 provides: "The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." To the extent the objection by Casey's counsel that the replaying was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over," falls under Rule 403, we will address the issue.3 Transcript Volume II at 45.

[18] The trial court replayed Casey's recorded statement due to multiple questions from the jury and a juror's inability to hear. We have previously held that, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State , 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied . We cannot say that Casey has demonstrated that replaying his statement to police would not aid the jury.  Under these circumstances, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion. See Chambers v. State , 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981) (holding that, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it); see also Harris v. State , 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995) (holding that the trial court did not abuse its discretion by sending exhibits and a tape player to the jury room when the jury began its deliberations where the audio taped confession served to aid the jury in its attempt to understand to what extent defendant participated in the charged crimes and how the crimes were committed, and there was little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court).

**DEFENDANT-APPELLANT'S BRIEF IN RESPONSE TO STATE'S ARGUMENT**

---

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's argument fails to address the central issue in this appeal: whether the trial court abused its discretion by replaying the *entire* recorded statement when only specific portions were at issue. While the prosecution correctly notes that trial courts possess broad discretion in managing proceedings, that discretion is not unlimited, and the circumstances here demonstrate that the trial court's decision exceeded the boundaries of reasonable judicial management.

## I. THE REPLAY EXCEEDED WHAT WAS NECESSARY TO ADDRESS JUROR CONCERNS

The prosecution attempts to justify the replay by characterizing it as a direct response to jury questions. However, a careful examination of the record reveals that the replay was grossly disproportionate to the concerns raised. The prosecution cites *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), for the proposition that jurors must be able to hear testimony to perform their duties. This proposition is correct, but it does not support replaying the *entire* video when only *portions* were in question.

The record demonstrates that specific juror questions were posed: questions about whose bedroom at 48 North Euclid, what time the tip was received, and how long it takes to get a warrant. *See* Transcript Volume II at 41, 44. One juror specifically indicated she could not hear *Casey's answer regarding whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid*. Id. at 44. Rather than addressing these specific concerns—which could have been resolved by replaying only the relevant portions or having the detective simply answer the questions—the trial court replayed the *entire* video. This overbreadth is precisely what *Indiana Evidence Rule 403* guards against: the needless presentation of cumulative evidence.

The defense argued at trial that the replay was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Transcript Volume II at 45. This characterization is accurate. The prosecution's reliance on *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), is misplaced. In *Chambers*, the Indiana Supreme Court upheld the replay of a tape "so all of the jurors could hear it"—a limited replay to address a specific deficiency. Here, the replay went far beyond mere audibility; it gave the prosecution a second opportunity to present its entire case through the defendant's own recorded words.

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON INDIANA CODE § 34-1-21-6 IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 permitted the replay because the jury requested clarification. However, even under the prosecution's reading of the statute, the replay exceeded the scope of any request. The statute permits juries to view evidence "upon their request," but the request here was for clarification on specific points—not for a repeat performance of the entire statement.

The prosecution claims that "none of the statute's narrowly defined exceptions permitting such evidence were met" is "factually incorrect." To the contrary, it is the prosecution's argument that is factually incorrect. The jury did not request the replay of the entire video; they asked specific questions that could have been answered in less cumulative ways. The trial court's decision to replay the entire video was an exercise of its own discretion that went beyond what any party or the statute required.

## III. THE PROSECUTION FAILS TO DEMONSTRATE THE ABSENCE OF PREJUDICE

The prosecution asserts that "Casey has not shown substantial prejudice." This misplaces the burden. Under *Roche v. State*, 690 N.E.2d 1115 (Ind. 1997), the defense is entitled to reversal if the trial court's decision was "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Here, the facts demonstrate that:

1. The video was played once during the State's case-in-chief;
2. The jury had already heard the content of the statement through Detective Leitze's testimony;
3. The defense presented its case, including Casey's testimony that he claimed ownership because he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail";
4. The prosecution then replayed the *entire* video in rebuttal.

This sequence is significant. The replay occurred *after* the defense had presented its case and specifically *after* Casey had explained his statement. The replay thus served not merely to inform the jury, but to reinforce the prosecution's version of events after the defense had attempted to explain them. This is precisely the kind of cumulative presentation that *Rule 403* guards against—evidence that risks unfair prejudice by virtue of its repetition.

The prosecution cites *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522 (Ind. 1995), for the proposition that sending exhibits to the jury room is permissible. However, *Harris* is distinguishable. In *Harris*, the court emphasized that "little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court." Here, the risk was not merely that the jury would "misuse" the evidence, but that the repeated playing would amplify its impact. The prosecution's characterization of the replay as merely "aiding" the jury ignores the reality that repetition inures to the benefit of the party offering the evidence—the prosecution.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT'S DISCRETION WAS ABUSED

The prosecution correctly notes that trial courts have "wide latitude of discretion" in managing proceedings. *Garcia v. State*, 517 N.E.2d 402 (Ind. 1988); *Pitman v. State*, 436 N.E.2d 74 (Ind. 1982). However, this discretion must be exercised reasonably. The trial court had options less prejudicial than replaying the entire video: it could have had Detective Leitze answer the specific questions, played only the relevant portions, or provided a limiting instruction. Instead, it chose the most cumulative option available.

The prosecution argues that the replay was necessary because the video was the "best evidence" of Casey's statement. This argument proves too much. If the video was the best evidence, it was already before the jury. Replaying it did not add new evidence; it merely repeated evidence already presented. This is the very definition of cumulative evidence.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court find that the trial court abused its discretion in replaying Casey's recorded statement. The replay was not justified by the jury's questions, which could have been addressed through less cumulative means. The replay served no purpose other than to allow the prosecution to reinforce its case after the defense had presented its explanation of the statement. This Court should reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in permitting Casey’s recorded statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. We review the trial court’s ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). A trial court has the duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted before it and is given wide latitude of discretion in carrying out those duties. Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988); Pitman v. State, 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

The trial court replayed Casey’s recorded statement in response to multiple questions from the jury and a specific juror’s inability to hear portions of the testimony. The record indicates that jurors submitted questions regarding the content of the video, and one juror explicitly noted that she missed Casey’s answer regarding whose bedroom the gun was found in because she could not hear. We have previously held that, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied. Under these circumstances, the trial court’s decision to replay the video was a reasonable exercise of its discretion to ensure juror comprehension.

Casey argues that the replay was unsolicited and violated Indiana Evidence Rule 403 by needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. We find these arguments unpersuasive. The replay was directly responsive to the jury's inquiries and the identified audibility issues; thus, it was not unsolicited. Regarding the claim of cumulative evidence, while the replay was undoubtedly cumulative of the testimony already heard, Indiana Evidence Rule 403 permits exclusion only if the probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice or the needless presentation of cumulative evidence. Here, the probative value of ensuring the jury could fully hear and understand the defendant's statement outweighed the concerns of cumulation. As held in Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981), "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. Furthermore, in Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995), the court held that providing evidence to the jury to aid their understanding involves little risk of misuse or undue weight where the evidence has already been played in open court.

The defense further contends that the replay constituted an abuse of discretion because it occurred after the defense presented its case, specifically during rebuttal, thereby reinforcing the prosecution's case after Casey's explanation. This argument is not supported by the facts provided. The record shows that the video was replayed during the trial immediately following the sidebar discussions regarding the juror's inability to hear, and *before* the State rested and Casey testified. See Transcript Volume II at 54 (video played); id. at 56 (Casey testified). Therefore, the defense's claim of prejudice based on the timing of the replay in rebuttal is factually incorrect.

Finally, the defense argues that the trial court should have utilized less prejudicial means, such as replaying only specific portions. However, the trial court is afforded wide latitude in managing its proceedings. Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d at 405. The decision to replay the entire video, rather than edited segments, falls within the ambit of that discretion, particularly where the court sought to ensure the juror could hear the answer in context without confusion.

Based on the foregoing, we cannot say that the trial court’s decision to replay the video was clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. The trial court acted within its discretion to address the jury's needs and ensure a fair and comprehensible proceeding.

**THEREFORE, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the evidence for a second viewing. The State's arguments are sustained.**

## II. The Replay Exceeded What Was Necessary to Address Juror Concerns

The prosecution argues that the replay was justified because one juror "could not hear" and "jurors submitted multiple questions about the video's content." This argument fails for several reasons.

State*, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997), reversal is only warranted when the trial court’s decision is “clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances.” Here, the trial court’s decision was eminently reasonable. Jurors submitted specific questions about the recorded statement, including one juror who explicitly stated she missed Casey’s answer regarding whose bedroom the shotgun was in. Transcript Volume II at 44. When a juror reports hearing difficulties, the trial court has a duty to ensure comprehension. As this Court stated in *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), “it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony” for jurors to properly perform their duty. This principle applies equally to recorded evidence, regardless of whether the testimony was live or pre-recorded.  

The defense argues the court should have replayed only the specific portion the juror missed or had the detective answer directly. But the trial court is best positioned to determine the most effective way to resolve juror confusion. As *Garcia v. State*, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), recognizes, trial courts have “wide latitude of discretion in carrying out [their] duties.” The court reasonably determined that partial playback risked distorting the context of Casey’s statement. A trial court’s decision to replay the full recording to avoid misrepresentation is a reasonable exercise of its gatekeeping function under the Indiana Rules of Evidence.  

The defense also misapplies Indiana Evidence Rule 403. Rule 403 permits exclusion only when probative value is “substantially outweighed” by cumulative evidence. But here, the probative value of replaying the statement for jury comprehension was not substantially outweighed by any cumulative effect. *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981), directly controls this issue. There, the Court held that “at most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence” but the trial court did not err in allowing the replay to ensure all jurors could hear it. The fact that the statement was central to the prosecution’s case does not transform a reasonable effort to ensure juror comprehension into reversible error. Similarly, *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526–527 (Ind. 1995), confirms that replaying evidence already presented in open court carries “little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight” to it.  

The defense’s timing argument—that replaying before Casey’s testimony reinforced the prosecution’s case—is baseless. The evidence was properly admitted, and Casey later testified and explained his statement, providing the jury with the full context of his “scared and confused” reasoning. The trial court’s admonishment to weigh all evidence fairly was already incorporated in the jury instructions, as noted in the transcript. Transcript Volume II at 54. This sequence ensured the jury heard both sides before deliberation.  

Finally, the defense has not demonstrated substantial prejudice. As *Joyner v. State*, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997), requires, reversal demands a showing that the trial court’s decision was “clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances.” The defense has not met this burden. The replay addressed a specific juror’s inability to hear, and Casey’s own testimony provided the defense’s explanation. No reasonable juror would have misunderstood the evidence given the full context.  

In conclusion, the trial court’s decision was a reasonable exercise of its discretion to ensure the jury could fully understand the evidence. The defense’s arguments fail to meet the standard for reversal under *Roche* and *Joyner*. We ask the Court to affirm the trial court’s judgment and uphold Casey’s conviction.

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The defense agrees with the prosecution that this Court reviews the trial court's ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997).

However, the defense emphasizes that this standard does not immunize all trial court decisions from scrutiny. A trial court has wide latitude of discretion in managing proceedings, but that discretion must be exercised within the bounds of the Indiana Rules of Evidence, particularly Indiana Evidence Rule 403, which governs the exclusion of cumulative evidence.

## IV. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion Under Indiana Evidence Rule 403

Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides: "The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence."

The defense submits that the replay of Casey's recorded statement constituted "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence" under Rule 403. The video had already been played in full during the State's case-in-chief. The jury had already heard the entire statement. The only issue raised by the jury was that one juror missed a specific portion regarding whose bedroom the shotgun was in.

The defense does not dispute that the trial court has discretion to address juror confusion. However, the exercise of discretion must be reasonable and proportionate to the problem being addressed. Here, the problem was narrow—a single juror's inability to hear a specific portion of the video. The trial court's solution—replaying the entire video—was grossly disproportionate to this limited problem.

The prosecution argues that the trial court reasonably determined that partial playback risked distorting the context of Casey's statement. However, this argument ignores the practical reality that the court could have employed numerous less intrusive alternatives, including: (1) replaying only the specific portion the juror missed; (2) having Detective Leitze answer the question directly, as the defense suggested at sidebar; (3) providing the jury with a transcript of the relevant portion; or (4) simply instructing the jury that the shotgun was found in a bedroom at 48 North Euclid.

The trial court's decision to replay the entire video—approximately how many minutes of recorded content—simply to address a single question about whose bedroom the shotgun was in constitutes the very definition of "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence" under Rule 403.

### B. The Timing of the Replay Increased the Prejudice to Casey

The defense notes that the prosecution incorrectly states in its closing that "the video was replayed before Casey testified, correcting the factual assertion made in its initial opening argument." To the contrary, the defense maintains that the video was replayed *before* Casey testified, as the prosecution now acknowledges.

This timing is critically important. The video was replayed during the State's case-in-chief, before Casey had an opportunity to present his defense. At that point, the only evidence the jury had heard regarding ownership of the shotgun was Detective Leitze's testimony that Casey "claimed ownership" of the shotgun. By replaying the video before Casey could explain his statement, the trial court effectively reinforced the prosecution's most damaging evidence before the defense could present its counter-narrative.

The prosecution argues that this timing was not prejudicial because Casey later testified and explained his statement. This argument misses the point. The prejudice occurred because the jury heard the damning statement twice—once during the State's case-in-chief and again, still before hearing any defense evidence—rather than once, followed by Casey's explanation. The cumulative effect of hearing the statement twice, before hearing Casey's explanation, undermined his ability to contextualize his statement and suggest innocent explanations.

The prosecution cites no authority for the proposition that replaying evidence before the defendant testifies eliminates any prejudice from the replay. Such a rule would eviscerate Rule 403's protection against cumulative evidence, as the prosecution could always argue that any prejudice was "cured" by subsequent defense testimony.

### C. The Prosecution's Reliance on Chambers v. State Is Misplaced

The prosecution relies heavily on Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981), for the proposition that replaying a tape does not constitute reversible error. However, the defense submits that Chambers is distinguishable and does not control the outcome of this case.

In Chambers, the Court held that "at most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. Id. However, the Chambers Court did not conduct a Rule 403 analysis and did not address whether the replay was "needlessly" cumulative. Moreover, the Chambers case did not involve the specific timing concerns present here—the replay in Chambers occurred in the context of a full trial where both sides had presented their evidence.

More importantly, the defense distinguishes Chambers on a critical ground: in Chambers, ownership of the weapon was not in dispute. The defendant in Chambers did not claim that the weapon belonged to someone else. Here, however, Casey consistently and unequivocally testified that the shotgun belonged to his girlfriend, Tara Hamilton, not to him. The central issue at trial was ownership of the shotgun—whether Casey possessed the firearm unlawfully.

In this context, replaying Casey's statement in which he claimed ownership of the shotgun was far more prejudicial than in Chambers, where the ownership issue was not contested. When the very fact being disputed—ownership—is reinforced through cumulative evidence, the prejudice is substantially greater.

The prosecution argues that "the fact that the evidence was central to the prosecution's case does not transform a reasonable effort to ensure juror comprehension into reversible error." This argument proves too much. If evidence is "central" to the prosecution's case, then replaying it cumulatively creates the greatest risk of prejudice. The prosecution cannot escape the cumulative evidence analysis by emphasizing how important the evidence was to their case.

### D. The Prosecution's Reliance on Harris v. State Is Also Misplaced

The prosecution also cites Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995), for the proposition that replaying evidence already presented in open court carries little risk of misuse or undue weight. However, Harris is distinguishable.

In Harris, the Court held that the trial court did not abuse its discretion by sending exhibits and a tape player to the jury room when the jury began its deliberations. The Court noted that the audio taped confession "served to aid the jury in its attempt to understand to what extent defendant participated in the charged crimes and how the crimes were committed." Id. at 526-527. Importantly, the Harris Court also noted that "there was little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court." Id.

However, Harris involved the jury's use of evidence during deliberations, after both sides had presented their evidence and the case was submitted. The replay here occurred during the State's case-in-chief, before the defense had presented any evidence. This timing distinction is critical.

Moreover, the Harris Court emphasized that the evidence was provided to the jury to "aid" their understanding. Here, the jury had already heard the evidence once and understood it—the only issue was that one juror had missed a small portion. The replay was not necessary to "aid" understanding; it was unnecessary reinforcement of evidence already properly considered.

### E. The Defense's Objection Was Sufficient to Preserve the Issue

The prosecution argues that Casey did not specifically mention Indiana Evidence Rule 403 in his objection to the replaying of the video at trial. However, the record reflects that defense counsel stated that the replay was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Transcript Volume II at 45.

Indiana courts have long held that a party need not use precise magical words to preserve an issue for appeal. It is sufficient that the trial court was aware of the grounds for the objection. Here, defense counsel's objection on the basis that the replay was "cumulative" was sufficient to invoke Rule 403 and alert the trial court to the issue.

The prosecution's attempt to parse the specificity of the objection is unavailing. The defense clearly argued that the replay was cumulative, which is the precise language of Rule 403. The trial court was aware of the basis for the objection and ruled on it. The issue is preserved.

## V. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Standard of Review

The prosecution argues that reversal is only warranted when the trial court's decision is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." While this is the correct standard, the prosecution asks this Court to apply it in a manner that effectively eliminates any meaningful review. The defense submits that the trial court's decision *was* clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances.

The facts show: (1) the video was properly played once during the State's case-in-chief; (2) the jury had heard the entire statement; (3) only one juror missed a specific portion regarding whose bedroom; (4) the trial court had multiple less intrusive options available; (5) the trial court chose the most intrusive option—replaying the entire video; (6) this replay occurred before the defense could present its case; and (7) the central issue at trial was ownership of the shotgun, which made the cumulative replay particularly prejudicial.

Under these facts, the trial court's decision to replay the entire video was not a reasonable exercise of discretion. It was an abuse of discretion.

### B. The Prosecution's Argument Regarding Juror Comprehension Is Overstated

The prosecution argues that the trial court had a duty to ensure the jury could hear the testimony, citing Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). However, Lewis stands for the unremarkable proposition that jurors must be able to hear testimony to perform their duties. It does not stand for the proposition that the entire piece of evidence must be replayed when a juror misses a small portion.

In Lewis, the Court reversed the conviction because the trial court refused to allow a witness to repeat testimony that the jury had difficulty hearing. The Court held that "it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Id. However, the remedy in Lewis was to have the witness repeat the specific testimony—not to replay an entire video.

Here, the appropriate remedy was not to replay the entire video, but to have Detective Leitze answer the specific question or to replay only the specific portion the juror missed. The trial court's choice of the most intrusive remedy available was not required by Lewis and was an abuse of discretion.

### C. The Prosecution's Argument Regarding Partial Playback Is Without Merit

The prosecution argues that partial playback "risks distorting the meaning or context of the statement." This argument is speculative and unsupported by the record. The prosecution has not identified any specific way in which replaying only the relevant portion would have distorted the meaning of Casey's statement.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument proves too much. By this logic, no partial playback would ever be permissible, and any time a jury had a question about a portion of a video, the entire video would have to be replayed. This cannot be the law, and the prosecution cites no authority for such a proposition.

The trial court is certainly entitled to discretion in determining how to address juror questions. But that discretion must be exercised reasonably. Replaying the entire video to address a single question about a specific detail was not a reasonable exercise of that discretion.

### D. The Prosecution's Argument Regarding Substantial Prejudice Is Incorrect

The prosecution argues that Casey has not shown substantial prejudice. The defense submits that the cumulative effect of the replay, combined with its timing before Casey's testimony and the centrality of the evidence to the prosecution's case, constitutes substantial prejudice.

The prosecution argues that any prejudice was cured by Casey's subsequent testimony. But this ignores the fundamental unfairness of the situation: the jury heard Casey's statement claiming ownership of the shotgun *twice* before hearing his explanation that he was scared, confused, and did not want his girlfriend to go to jail. The damage was done before Casey ever took the stand.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument assumes that the jury would completely disregard the first two impressions created by the video and give equal weight to Casey's explanation. This assumption is unrealistic. The human tendency is to give greater weight to evidence heard first and heard most often. By replaying the video before Casey testified, the trial court allowed the prosecution's evidence to dominate the jury's initial impressions.

## VI. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and remand for a new trial. The trial court abused its discretion in replaying Casey's recorded statement to police for the jury a second time. The replay constituted "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence" under Indiana Evidence Rule 403, as the trial court had numerous less intrusive alternatives available to address the juror's hearing difficulty. The timing of the replay—before Casey could present his defense—substantially prejudiced his right to a fair trial.

The prosecution has not met its burden of demonstrating that the trial court's decision was within the bounds of reasonable discretion. The defense has demonstrated that the trial court's decision was clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. **FINAL RULING**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in permitting Casey’s recorded statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. We review the trial court’s ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). A trial court has the duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted before it and is given wide latitude of discretion in carrying out those duties. Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988); Pitman v. State, 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

The defense argues that the trial court abused its discretion under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 by replaying the entire video, asserting that the probative value of addressing one juror’s hearing difficulty was substantially outweighed by the danger of needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. The defense contends that the trial court should have employed less intrusive means, such as replaying only the specific portion regarding the bedroom or having the detective answer the question directly. Furthermore, the defense argues that the timing of the replay—occurring before Casey testified—substantially prejudiced Casey by reinforcing the prosecution's case-in-chief before the defense could present its counter-narrative.

We acknowledge the defense's arguments regarding the proportionality of the trial court's remedy and the timing of the replay. However, we are not persuaded that the trial court's decision constitutes an abuse of discretion. The trial court acted in response to a specific juror's inability to hear Casey's answer regarding the bedroom, a fact confirmed by the record. Transcript Volume II at 44. We have previously held that, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied. The trial court was faced with a juror who explicitly stated she could not hear a portion of the evidence. Under these circumstances, the court had a duty to take reasonable steps to ensure the juror comprehended the evidence.

The defense argues that the trial court should have replayed only a portion of the video. However, the trial court is in the best position to determine the most effective way to clarify juror confusion without misleading the jury or distorting the context of the statement. See Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d at 405. The prosecution correctly notes that partial playback carries a risk of taking the statement out of context. The trial court's decision to replay the entire recording to avoid this risk was a reasonable exercise of its gatekeeping function. The defense's proposed alternatives, while perhaps less intrusive, were not the only reasonable options available to the court.

Regarding the defense's argument that the replay was "needlessly cumulative" under Rule 403, we find the trial court's decision sustainable on this basis. In Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981), the Court held that, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. The defense attempts to distinguish Chambers by noting that ownership was not disputed there, whereas it was disputed here. While factual distinctions exist, the holding in Chambers regarding the permissibility of replaying a tape to ensure audibility remains persuasive authority. The fact that the evidence was central to the prosecution's case does not automatically transform a reasonable effort to ensure juror comprehension into reversible error.

The defense also relies on Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995), to argue that the replay was improper because it occurred during the State's case-in-chief rather than during deliberations. In Harris, the court held that sending exhibits to the jury room involved little risk of misuse because the evidence had already been played in open court. Id. Here, the video had already been played once. The replay occurred in open court, under the supervision of the trial court, not in the unmonitored environment of the jury room. The trial court also provided an admonishment to the jury, which the defense acknowledged was already included in the instructions. Transcript Volume II at 54.

Finally, the defense argues that the timing of the replay—before Casey testified—created substantial prejudice. While the replay occurred before Casey could explain his statement, Casey did testify subsequently and provided his explanation that he was scared and confused. The jury had the opportunity to hear both the statement and the defense's explanation before deliberating. The defense has not demonstrated that the trial court's decision was "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d at 390. The trial court acted within its discretion to address a legitimate impediment to the jury's ability to perform its function.

**THEREFORE, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the evidence for a second viewing. The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.**
