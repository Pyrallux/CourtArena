## I. THE PLAINTIFFS' INTERPRETATION OF SECTION 4 IS CONTRARY TO CONSTITUTIONAL TEXT AND PRECEDENT

### A. The Plain Language of Section 4 Does Not Support Plaintiffs' Argument

Proposition 13's section 4 provides that "any special tax measure . . . shall not be effective . . . unless approved by two-thirds of the voters . . . voting on the measure . . . in any city, county, or special district." The critical term at issue—"special district"—must be interpreted in accordance with its established meaning at the time Proposition 13 was adopted.

As the California Supreme Court explained in *Richmond*, the term "special district" in California law refers to entities that possess the power to levy property taxes. The majority opinion explicitly held that "the term 'special district' as used in section 4 refers to those entities which possess the power to levy a property tax." *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 207. This interpretation is grounded in the text of section 4 itself, which specifically addresses "special taxes" in the context of local governmental entities.

In *Richmond*, the California Supreme Court addressed the precise question presented here: whether an entity created by the Legislature without property tax authority constitutes a "special district" under section 4. The term "special district" has a well-established meaning in California law. As defined in Government Code section 16271, a special district is "an area of land created by the legislative act of a local agency formation commission, or by the Legislature . . . for the purpose of providing designated services to a specific area within the district." Critically, the statutory definition of special districts encompasses those entities that possess property tax authority. The Agency, by contrast, possesses no property tax authority and cannot be classified as a special district under any accepted definition.

The plaintiffs' "functional equivalence" argument conflates two distinct concepts: the governmental function performed and the taxation authority possessed. Section 4 focuses on the latter, not the former. The constitutional text refers to "special taxes" imposed by "special districts"—it does not refer to governmental functions performed by entities that lack property tax authority.

In *Richmond*, the majority explicitly rejected a similar functional approach, stating that the "nature of the entity" rather than "the function it performs" determines whether it is a special district. *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 207. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the validity of a sales tax ordinance enacted by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency). The central issue is whether the Agency constitutes a "special district" under article XIII A, section 4 of the California Constitution (Proposition 13), thereby requiring a two-thirds voter approval for the imposition of special taxes. Having reviewed the opening arguments of both parties, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Binding Precedent and the Definition of "Special District"**

The dispositive legal authority in this matter is *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197. In *Richmond*, the California Supreme Court explicitly interpreted the term "special district" as used in section 4 of Proposition 13. The Court held that "the term 'special district' as used in section 4 refers to those entities which possess the power to levy a property tax." *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 207.

The Agency in the present case was created by the Legislature under Government Code sections 26250–26285. As the defense correctly notes, and the facts unambiguously show, the Agency possesses no authority to levy real property taxes. Government Code section 26283 explicitly states that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax." Because the Agency lacks the power to levy a property tax, it falls outside the definition of a "special district" as established by the majority holding in *Richmond*.

**B. The "Functional Equivalence" Argument and Stare Decisis**

The prosecution urges this Court to disregard the strict textual definition of "special district" in favor of a "functional equivalence" test, relying heavily on the dissenting opinion in *Richmond*. The prosecution argues that the Agency is a legislative vehicle designed to circumvent the supermajority vote requirement of Proposition 13, a concern raised by Chief Justice Bird in her dissent. *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 214 (Bird, C.J., dissenting).

However, this Court is bound by the majority holding in *Richmond*, not the dissent. The majority in *Richmond* directly addressed and rejected the argument that the Legislature could circumvent section 4 by creating entities without property tax authority. The Court stated, "We are unpersuaded by the dissent's speculation that our construction will encourage the Legislature to circumvent section 4." *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 207. The majority further concluded that the proper remedy for any perceived loophole lies with the electorate or the Legislature, not through judicial reinterpretation of the constitutional text. *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 208.

To adopt the prosecution's "functional equivalence" argument now would require this Court to overrule or significantly distinguish the binding precedent of *Richmond* based on policy considerations that the *Richmond* court already considered and rejected. The defense correctly argues that such a move would violate principles of stare decisis and create uncertainty in the application of section 4. The Agency's lack of "significant governmental discretion" regarding the expenditure of funds, while factually accurate, does not alter the Agency's fundamental lack of property tax authority, which is the controlling legal characteristic under *Richmond*.

**C. Application of Section 4 and the Validity of the Tax**

Because the Agency is not a "special district" under the definition provided in *Richmond*, the supermajority voter approval requirement of section 4 does not apply to the taxes it imposes. The tax ordinance at issue was approved by 50.8 percent of the voters, which satisfies the simple majority requirement established by the Legislature in the enabling act (Gov. Code, §§ 26271, 26273).

The prosecution's fear that this ruling renders Proposition 13's protections a "dead letter" for non-property taxes is a policy argument for the voters or Legislature, not a basis for this Court to ignore the plain language of the Constitution and binding precedent. As the *Richmond* majority noted, section 4 is limited in its application to entities with property tax power. *Richmond*, 31 Cal.3d at 208.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the binding precedent of *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4 because it lacks the power to levy property taxes. Consequently, the two-thirds voter approval requirement for special taxes does not apply. The tax ordinance, approved by a simple majority vote, is valid.

The Court of Appeal correctly reversed the trial court's judgment. The preliminary ruling is to **AFFIRM** the judgment of the Court of Appeal.

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the judgment of the Court of Appeal, which was affirmed by this Court in its updated ruling. However, the prosecution's argument asks this Court to do what our judicial system does not permit: to disregard the explicit holding of the California Supreme Court in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] in favor of the dissenting opinion from that same case. This this Court cannot do.

---

## I. The Undisputed Facts Establish That the Agency Falls Outside the Scope of Section 4

The facts of this case are largely undisputed and, critically, they are determinative of the legal issue presented. The record establishes the following:

First, the Agency was created by the California Legislature in 1987 through Government Code sections 26250-26285 for the express purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and courthouse facilities in San Diego County.

Second, the enabling legislation granted the Agency no tax power other than the authority to impose a supplemental sales tax of one-half of one percent throughout the County. Government Code section 26283 explicitly provides that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax."

Third, and most critically, the Agency possesses no power to levy a property tax. This fact is undisputed and goes to the very heart of the legal analysis.

Fourth, the tax ordinance was submitted to the County's voters and approved by 50.8 percent of those voting—a bare majority, but a majority nonetheless.

These facts, as found by the courts below and confirmed by this Court, establish the precise scenario contemplated by the *Richmond* majority: an entity created by the Legislature that possesses no property tax authority but does possess the power to levy a sales tax.

---

## II. The Holding of *Richmond* Is Binding and Dispositive

### A. The Majority Opinion in *Richmond* Constitutes binding Authority

The prosecution urges this Court to adopt the reasoning of the dissent in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. This the defense respectfully submits this Court cannot do. The dissent in *Richmond*, authored by Chief Justice Bird, represents the views of two justices who dissented from the majority's holding. It does not constitute binding precedent; it represents the losing side of the judicial decision.

The majority in *Richmond* explicitly addressed and rejected the precise policy arguments now raised by the prosecution. The Court stated: "We are unpersuaded by the dissent's speculation that our construction will encourage the Legislature to circumvent section 4 by creating entities that lack property tax authority but possess the power to levy other taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941].

The majority further explained the constitutional text itself: "the term 'special district' as used in section 4 refers to those entities which possess the power to levy a property tax." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941].

This Court, in its updated ruling, correctly recognized that it is bound by this explicit holding. The Agency lacks property tax authority. Therefore, the Agency is not a "special district" under section 4. The two-thirds voter approval requirement is inapplicable.

### B. The Prosecution's Functional Equivalence Argument Must Fail

The prosecution argues that this Court should look to the function and purpose of the Agency rather than its formal legal powers. According to the prosecution, because the Agency was created solely to impose a tax and has no independent purpose beyond collecting revenue for justice facilities, it should be treated as the functional equivalent of a special district subject to section 4.

This argument, while superficially appealing, is precisely the argument that the *Richmond* majority considered and rejected. The majority stated: "We are unpersuaded by the dissent's speculation that our construction will encourage the Legislature to circumvent section 4 by creating entities that lack property tax authority but possess the power to levy other taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941].

To adopt the prosecution's functional equivalence test would require this Court to overrule or effectively nullify the holding of the California Supreme Court. This this Court cannot do. The principle of stare decisis demands that we follow binding precedent, even when we might disagree with the policy underlying that precedent.

However, the *Richmond* majority explicitly addressed this concern and concluded that the remedy lies with the Legislature, not the judiciary.

The majority stated: "The proper remedy for any perceived loophole in section 4 lies with the electorate or the Legislature, not through judicial reinterpretation of the constitutional text." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. It is not the role of the judiciary to rewrite the constitutional text to close a loophole that the Supreme Court has explicitly left open.

## III. Rebuttal of the Prosecution's Specific Arguments

### A. The Prosecution's "Textbook Example" Characterization

The prosecution characterizes this case as "the textbook example" of the evasion that the dissent in *Richmond* predicted. The majority wrote: "We are unpersuaded by the dissent's speculation that our construction will encourage the Legislature to circumvent section 4 by creating entities that lack property tax authority but possess the power to levy other taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941].

The prosecution's argument is essentially that because the predicted evasion has come to pass, this Court should now adopt the dissent's reasoning. But this turns the doctrine of stare decisis on its head. The fact that a predicted harm has materialized does not authorize the judiciary to disregard binding precedent.

### B. The "Substance Over Form" Argument

The prosecution argues that the Constitution demands "substance over form" and that this Court should look to the functional reality of the Agency rather than its technical legal characterization. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the *Richmond* majority explicitly considered and rejected a similar argument. The majority concluded that the text of section 4—"cities, counties and special districts"—must be interpreted according to its plain meaning, which refers to entities possessing property tax authority.

Second, the prosecution's "substance over form" argument, if accepted, would require this Court to create a new test for determining when an entity is a "special district" under section 4. This new test would necessarily involve judicial assessments of an entity's "discretion," "purpose," and "function"—standards that are inherently vague and subject to manipulation. The *Richmond* majority wisely declined to create such a test, and this Court should decline as well.

Third, the prosecution's argument conflates two distinct concepts: the validity of a tax and the procedure for approving that tax. Even assuming that the Agency serves a public purpose (which the defense does not dispute), this does not answer the question of what voter approval procedure applies. The Constitution draws a distinction between general taxes and special taxes, and between different types of governmental entities. This Court is bound to apply the constitutional text as interpreted by the California Supreme Court.

The prosecution's complaint also asserted that the tax violated Government Code sections 53720-53730, added by Proposition 62. The Court of Appeal concluded that application of the statutory supermajority voter approval provision in Government Code section 53722 to the Agency would be improper as an attempted local tax referendum.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense. This case presents a fundamental question of statutory and constitutional interpretation: whether the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency) constitutes a "special district" under article XIII A, section 4 of the California Constitution, thereby triggering the supermajority voter approval requirements of Proposition 13. The prosecution argues that the Agency is a legislative subterfuge designed to circumvent the will of the voters, urging this Court to adopt a functional analysis based on the dissenting opinion in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197. The defense maintains that the Agency is exempt from the supermajority requirement because it lacks the power to levy property taxes, relying on the binding majority holding in *Richmond*. For the reasons stated below, the Court adheres to its previous ruling and affirms the judgment of the Court of Appeal.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Binding Precedent of *Richmond* and the Doctrine of Stare Decisis**

The prosecution requests that this Court disregard the majority holding in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 in favor of the dissenting opinion authored by Chief Justice Bird. The prosecution characterizes the Agency as the "textbook example" of the evasion the dissent predicted. However, this Court is bound by the principle of *stare decisis* to follow the majority opinion of the California Supreme Court.

In *Richmond*, the Supreme Court explicitly defined the term "special district" for purposes of section 4. The Court held: "the term 'special district' as used in section 4 refers to those entities which possess the power to levy a property tax." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207. The Court further noted that the agency in that case, like the Agency here, was created by the Legislature and possessed no power to levy property taxes. *Id.*

The prosecution’s argument that the Agency should be treated as a "special district" due to its function and purpose constitutes a request to overrule *Richmond*. The majority in *Richmond* anticipated the policy concerns raised by the prosecution and explicitly rejected them. The Court stated, "We are unpersuaded by the dissent's speculation that our construction will encourage the Legislature to circumvent section 4 by creating entities that lack property tax authority but possess the power to levy other taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207. Because the Agency lacks the power to levy a property tax, it falls outside the definition of a "special district" as a matter of law. To adopt the prosecution's functional equivalence test would require this Court to substitute its judgment for that of the Supreme Court and disregard the principle of stare decisis.

**B. The Remedy for Perceived Loopholes Lies with the Legislature**

The prosecution contends that the Agency’s creation—specifically its lack of discretionary governmental power and its sole reliance on a sales tax—constitutes an unconstitutional evasion of the supermajority requirement. While the Court acknowledges the force of the prosecution's policy argument, the legal remedy for such a loophole is not judicial reinterpretation of the Constitution.

The *Richmond* majority directly addressed the separation of powers implications of this issue. The Court concluded: "The proper remedy for any perceived loophole in section 4 lies with the electorate or the Legislature, not through judicial reinterpretation of the constitutional text." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208. The Legislature created the Agency under Government Code sections 26250–26285 and authorized a simple majority vote for the tax ordinance (Gov. Code, §§ 26271, 26273). While the prosecution characterizes this as an evasion, the *Richmond* court has instructed that such structural decisions are within the Legislature's purview, provided the entity does not possess property tax authority. It is not the role of the judiciary to rewrite the Constitution to close a loophole that the Supreme Court has left open, particularly when the text of section 4 is clear regarding the entities to which it applies.

**C. Application of Section 4 to the Agency**

The facts are undisputed that the Agency possesses "no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax" (Gov. Code, § 26283) and no power to levy property taxes. Under the controlling precedent of *Richmond*, this absence of property tax authority is determinative. Consequently, the supermajority vote requirement of section 4 does not apply to the taxes imposed by the Agency.

The tax ordinance was approved by 50.8 percent of the voters, satisfying the simple majority requirement set forth in the enabling legislation. The prosecution’s assertion that this result undermines Proposition 13 is a political argument that must be taken to the voters or the Legislature, as indicated by the Supreme Court in *Richmond*.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution’s closing argument fails to overcome the binding precedent established by the California Supreme Court. The Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4 because it lacks the power to levy property taxes. The two-thirds voter approval requirement is therefore inapplicable. The tax ordinance, approved by a simple majority vote, is valid.

The judgment of the Court of Appeal is **AFFIRMED**.
