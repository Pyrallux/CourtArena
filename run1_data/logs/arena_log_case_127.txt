# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals, State of Arizona

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The defense respectfully submits that the family court correctly applied *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), and that this Court should affirm the ruling that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1). This defense will demonstrate that: (1) A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read gender-neutrally under *McLaughlin*; (2) even if the statutory presumption applies, the evidence fully supports the family court's finding that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting Oakley's parental status; and (3) the policy underpinnings of the statute support its application to same-sex partners who intended to co-parent.

---

## I. A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) Must Be Applied Gender-Neutrally

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Supports Gender-Neutral Reading

The plaintiff contends that the legislature's use of masculine terms "man" and "father" demonstrates clear intent to limit the presumption to men. However, this argument ignores the fundamental principle that Arizona law prohibits gender-based discrimination in parental rights determinations. The statute's purpose—establishing parentage for children born within a marriage—applies with equal force to same-sex marriages following *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), which legalized such marriages nationwide.

As this Court held in *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122, "the legislature is presumed to enact statutes with awareness of existing law and to intend for them to operate in harmony with that law." Arizona's marriage equality provisions, A.R.S. § 25-101 et seq., recognize same-sex marriages as legally valid. Reading A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to exclude spouses in same-sex marriages would create an absurd result: a child born to an opposite-sex couple via artificial insemination would have a presumed parent, while a child born to a same-sex couple under identical circumstances would not. Courts must avoid interpretations that produce absurd or illogical results. *See State v. Mahaney*, 193 Ariz. 554, 557 ¶ 12, 975 P.2d 108, 111 (App. 1999).

### B. *McLaughlin v. Jones* Is Binding Precedent

The plaintiff urges this Court to reject *McLaughlin* as wrongly decided, arguing that it improperly rewrote the statute. This argument fails for three reasons.

First, *McLaughlin* is binding precedent that this Court must follow. *See State v. Brown*, 183 Ariz. 364, 366, 903 P.2d 1099, 1101 (App. 1995) ("One panel of this Court may not overrule another panel's decision absent an intervening change in the law by the Arizona Supreme Court or this Court sitting en banc.").

Second, the *McLaughlin* court properly applied principles of statutory construction. It recognized that the legislature's use of gender-specific terms must be read in context with Arizona's broader anti-discrimination policies. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The court noted that "the legislature has demonstrated its ability to use explicitly gender-specific language when it intends to do so," and the absence of such explicit limitation in § 25-814(A)(1) indicated the statute should apply gender-neutrally. *Id.*

Third, the plaintiff mischaracterizes *McLaughlin* as judicial legislation. To the contrary, the court simply applied established principles of statutory interpretation to fulfill the statute's purpose: ensuring children born into marriages have legally recognized parents. *See A.R.S. § 25-814 (Purpose).*

### C. The Statutory Scheme Supports Application to Oakley

Arizona's parentage statutes must be read in conjunction with the Uniform Parentage Act, which Arizona has adopted. The Act's fundamental policy is that "the parent-child relationship shall not be determined on the basis of race, sex, marital status, or national origin of the natural parent." A.R.S. § 25-812. This policy strongly supports reading § 25-814(A)(1) gender-neutrally.

Moreover, the fertility clinic document that Turner signed expressly contemplated that a child born through artificial insemination would be the legal child of "recipient's husband or partner if applicable." This language demonstrates that the clinic—and presumably the parties—understood that a non-biological partner could be a legal parent. The plaintiff cannot now claim the statute precludes such recognition when the parties themselves structured their arrangement expecting Oakley would have parental rights.

---

## II. Alternatively, Turner Is Equitably Estopped from Rebutting Oakley's Parental Status

Even if this Court were to find any ambiguity in the statutory analysis, the family court's alternative holding—that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental status—independently supports affirmance. The evidence before the family court overwhelmingly established the elements of equitable estoppel.

### A. Turner's Conduct Created and Relied Upon a Representation

The facts demonstrate that Turner actively represented to Oakley, to the medical community, and to the public that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as sole custodian of C.T. if Turner died. This is a clear representation that Turner intended Oakley to have parental rights and responsibilities. *See McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 565-66 ¶ 18, 382 P.3d at 123 (finding will beneficiary provision supported equitable estoppel).

Turner also posted on social media referring to Oakley as C.T.'s "mother." These public representations were not isolated incidents but part of a consistent pattern of treating Oakley as C.T.'s parent. The family court heard testimony from both parties confirming Turner's representations that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent.

### B. Oakley Relied on Turner's Representations to Her Detriment

Oakley relied on Turner's representations by actively participating in the conception process, being present at C.T.'s birth, cutting the umbilical cord, and being listed on the birth certificate. Oakley formed an emotional bond with C.T. and functioned as a parent from birth. To now deny Oakley parental status would cause tremendous harm to both Oakley and C.T., who has known Oakley as a parent since birth.

### C. The Policy Behind Equitable Estoppel Supports This Application

Equitable estoppel in parentage cases serves the paramount interest of the child. *See In re Marriage of Allen*, 168 Ariz. 31, 37, 800 P.2d 135, 141 (App. 1990) ("The welfare of the child is the primary consideration in all custody proceedings."). C.T. has been raised in a family where both Turner and Oakley were represented as parents. Stripping Oakley of parental status would cause profound psychological and emotional harm to a child who has known no other arrangement.

The family court correctly applied *McLaughlin*'s framework, which found equitable estoppel appropriate where "the parties had entered into an agreement guaranteeing equal parenting rights, her wife was a beneficiary of her will, and her wife had been a de facto parent of the child." *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 565-67 ¶ 23, 382 P.3d at 124. The facts here are remarkably similar: Turner and Oakley intended to raise C.T. together as co-parents, Turner named Oakley in her will, and Oakley functioned as a de facto parent from birth.

## III. The Factual Record Supports the Family Court's Ruling

The family court conducted an evidentiary hearing at which both Turner and Oakley testified. The court made express findings that the evidence showed Turner and Oakley "intended to raise the child together as co-parents." This Court defers to the family court's factual findings if supported by substantial evidence. *See In re Marriage of Walpole*, 185 Ariz. 312, 315, 915 P.2d 1233, 1236 (App. 1996).

The evidence before the family court included:

1. **Turner's will**: Drafted with the express purpose of granting Oakley custody if Turner died, demonstrating Turner's intent that Oakley have parental rights.

2. **Social media posts**: Turner publicly referred to Oakley as C.T.'s "mother," representing to the world that Oakley was a parent.

3. **Birth certificate**: Turner voluntarily listed Oakley on C.T.'s birth certificate in the section designated "father," affirmatively representing to the state that Oakley was C.T.'s parent.

4. **Testimony**: Both Turner and Oakley testified about Turner's representations that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent.

5. **Active co-parenting**: Oakley was present at C.T.'s birth, cut the umbilical cord, and participated in the artificial insemination process.

This evidence more than satisfies the burden for establishing equitable estoppel. The family court correctly concluded that Turner was estopped from now claiming Oakley has no parental rights.

## IV. The Plaintiff Misapplies the Cited Authorities

The plaintiff cites *State v. Superior Court*, 145 Ariz. 268, 701 P.2d 532 (1985), for the proposition that courts must apply clear statutory language without judicial alteration. However, this principle has limits. When a statute's plain language leads to absurd results or conflicts with other statutory provisions, courts must interpret the statute to avoid such results. *See State v. Mahaney*, 193 Ariz. at 557 ¶ 12, 975 P.2d at 111. Reading § 25-814(A)(1) to exclude same-sex spouses produces an absurd result: children born to same-sex marriages via artificial insemination would be fatherless under law, while those born to opposite-sex marriages would have presumed parents.

Similarly, the plaintiff's reliance on *State v. Smith*, 180 Ariz. 34, 881 P.2d 1142 (App. 1994), and *State v. Williams*, 175 Ariz. 98, 854 P.2d 131 (1993), is misplaced. Those cases addressed statutes where the legislature clearly intended gender-specific application. Here, by contrast, the *McLaughlin* court properly found that the statute's purpose—establishing parentage for children of married couples—applies equally to same-sex marriages recognized under Arizona law.

---

## V. Conclusion

The family court correctly applied *McLaughlin v. Jones* and properly found that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental status. The evidence overwhelmingly establishes that Turner and Oakley intended to co-parent C.T., that Turner represented to Oakley and the world that Oakley was C.T.'s parent, and that Oakley relied on those representations to her detriment.

To reverse the family court's ruling would punish Oakley for her good-faith reliance on Turner's representations and would harm C.T. by severing a parent-child relationship that has existed since birth. This Court should affirm the family court's ruling and hold that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1).

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the family court's determination that Appellee Oakley is a presumed parent of C.T. under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and that Appellant Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting this presumption. After reviewing the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable law, the Court affirms the family court's ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Family Court Properly Applied Binding Precedent Regarding A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)**

The Prosecution argues that the family court erred by applying A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to Oakley, a woman, contending that the statute’s gender-specific language ("man" and "father") limits its application solely to men. The Prosecution relies on principles of plain meaning statutory construction, citing *State v. Superior Court*, 145 Ariz. 268, 701 P.2d 532 (1985), and *State v. Williams*, 175 Ariz. 98, 854 P.2d 131 (1993), to assert that courts lack the authority to rewrite unambiguous statutory language.

However, the Prosecution’s argument fails to account for the procedural posture of this case and the binding authority of prior appellate decisions. The family court’s reconsideration was explicitly guided by the decision of another panel of this Court in *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016). In *McLaughlin*, this Court held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

It is a fundamental tenet of appellate procedure that one panel of the Court of Appeals cannot overrule a decision of another panel. *State v. Brown*, 183 Ariz. 364, 366, 903 P.2d 1099, 1101 (App. 1995). Because *McLaughlin* is binding precedent, the family court was obligated to follow its holding that the presumption of paternity statute applies gender-neutrally. The Prosecution invites this Court to disregard *McLaughlin* and effectively legislate from the bench by reinstating a gender-specific interpretation, which would violate the principles of stare decisis. Therefore, the family court did not commit reversible error by applying *McLaughlin*.

Furthermore, the Defense correctly notes that interpreting the statute in a gender-exclusive manner would lead to absurd results inconsistent with the legal recognition of same-sex marriage established in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015). As held in *State v. Mahaney*, 193 Ariz. 554, 557 ¶ 12, 975 P.2d 108, 111 (App. 1999), courts must avoid interpretations that produce absurd or illogical results. Denying legal parentage to a spouse in a same-sex marriage while granting it to a spouse in an opposite-sex marriage—despite identical intent and circumstances—would be such an absurd result.

**B. The Family Court’s Finding of Equitable Estoppel Is Supported by Substantial Evidence**

Alternatively, the family court ruled that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of parentage. The Defense argues, and the record supports, that Turner engaged in conduct creating a clear representation of Oakley’s parental status, upon which Oakley relied to her detriment.

The evidence presented at the evidentiary hearing demonstrated that Turner and Oakley intended to raise C.T. together as co-parents. Turner drafted a will explicitly naming Oakley as the sole custodian of C.T. in the event of Turner’s death. Additionally, Turner listed Oakley on C.T.’s birth certificate and publicly referred to Oakley as C.T.’s "mother" on social media. These actions constitute clear representations of parental rights.

Oakley relied on these representations by actively participating in the artificial insemination process, attending medical appointments, being present at the birth, and cutting the umbilical cord. She has functioned as a parent to C.T. since birth. To allow Turner to now deny Oakley’s parental status would cause significant detriment to both Oakley and the child, disrupting the parent-child bond that has formed.

This Court has previously affirmed that the welfare of the child is the primary consideration in custody proceedings. *In re Marriage of Allen*, 168 Ariz. 31, 37, 800 P.2d 135, 141 (App. 1990). The family court’s finding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental status is supported by substantial evidence and is consistent with the equitable estoppel analysis applied in *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 565-67 ¶¶ 16-23, 382 P.3d at 123-24.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution misapprehends the nature of appellate review. When the Arizona Supreme Court grants a petition for review, it does not automatically vacate the Court of Appeals decision; rather, it grants the Court of Appeals decision prospective review by the supreme court. The Court of Appeals decision remains the law of the case unless and until reversed by the higher court. As this Court held in State v. Brown, 183 Ariz. 364, 366, 903 P.2d 1099, 1101 (App. 1995), the principle of stare decisis requires adherence to final decisions of other panels of the Court of Appeals.

McLaughlin was decided by this Court in 2016. Although the Arizona Supreme Court granted review in 2017, the Court of Appeals decision has not been reversed. The McLaughlin panel's holding—that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally—remains good law and binds this panel absent an intervening change in law by a higher court. The Prosecution's request that this Court disregard McLaughlin as "erroneously decided" runs afoul of the well-established principle that one panel of the Court of Appeals cannot overrule the decision of another panel. State v. Brown, 183 Ariz. at 366, 903 P.2d at 1101.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on State v. Williams Is Misplaced

The Prosecution cites State v. Williams, 175 Ariz. 98, 854 P.2d 131 (1993), for the proposition that courts must apply the plain meaning of unambiguous statutory text. The doctrine of equitable estoppel prevents a party from asserting a position contrary to their prior conduct if another party reasonably relied on that conduct to their detriment. In re Marriage of Allen, 168 Ariz. 31, 37, 800 P.2d 135, 141 (App. 1990). This doctrine is applicable in family law proceedings where a parent has represented to another person that they are a co-parent and that person has relied on those representations to their detriment.

The Prosecution’s assertion that estoppel cannot "create" rights misses the mark. Given the constitutional imperative for equal treatment and the overwhelming evidence of Turner’s conduct, the doctrine of equitable estoppel applies to prevent Turner from disadvantaging Oakley and the child.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution’s arguments regarding the finality of *McLaughlin* are well-taken, but they do not save the appeal. The constitutional principles articulated in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), compel a gender-neutral reading of the presumption of paternity statute. Furthermore, the family court’s finding of equitable estoppel is supported by substantial evidence and independently justifies the ruling. The family court’s decision is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** affirming the decision of the family court.
