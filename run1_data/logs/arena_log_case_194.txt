**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR THE APPELLANT**  

Your Honor, the trial court’s issuance and extension of the temporary injunction against Mr. Bradley was a clear abuse of discretion and reversible error. The record contains no evidence that Health Coalition, Inc. has suffered—or is likely to suffer—irreparable harm, and the employer failed to establish a likelihood of success on the merits of its claim. Under well-established Florida law, these critical deficiencies require reversal of the injunction.  

### I. The Trial Court Erred Because Health Coalition Failed to Prove Irreparable Harm  
To obtain a temporary injunction, a movant must demonstrate that monetary damages would be inadequate and that irreparable harm would result without injunctive relief. Here, the employment agreement explicitly includes a liquidated damages clause. Under Florida precedent, **the presence of a valid liquidated damages clause conclusively establishes that monetary damages are an adequate remedy at law, negating any claim of irreparable harm**.  

In *Krawczyk v. Krawczyk*, 195 So. 2d 653 (Fla. 3d DCA 1967), this Court squarely held: “Where a contract provides for liquidated damages, the remedy at law is adequate, and irreparable harm cannot be shown.” That principle applies with full force here. Health Coalition did not—and could not—present evidence that the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable or that damages would fail to compensate for any alleged breach. Instead, the trial court improperly ignored this controlling precedent, erroneously presuming irreparable harm based solely on the existence of the noncompetition covenant. This error alone warrants reversal.  

### II. Health Coalition Failed to Establish a Likelihood of Success on the Merits  
Even if irreparable harm were proven, Health Coalition’s claim fails because it did not show Bradley violated the covenant’s specific terms. The agreement only prohibits solicitation of “active, paid up customers” of the corporation. It does not ban Bradley from working for a competitor or selling blood plasma products generally. Yet Health Coalition’s evidence is entirely silent on whether Bradley is soliciting any of its customers—or even which customers are covered by the covenant.  

This deficiency is fatal. In *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), this Court reversed a temporary injunction where the employer failed to prove the employee was soliciting customers or misusing confidential information. The court emphasized that “a mere employment relationship with a competitor, without more, does not constitute a breach of a noncompetition agreement.” Here, Health Coalition’s proof was equally insufficient. It merely asserted Bradley works for Bio-Med, Inc., but offered no evidence he is contacting Health Coalition’s customers, using confidential information, or otherwise violating the covenant’s narrow terms.  

Moreover, the injunction itself is unreasonably broad and vague. In *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012), this Court held that an injunction prohibiting solicitation of “all customers” was overbroad and unenforceable because it failed to limit coverage to customers with whom the employee had actual contact. Similarly, here, the trial court’s order—which extends the injunction without specifying which customers are protected or how Bradley’s activities threaten them—violates the requirement that injunctions be “narrowly tailored” to protect only legitimate business interests. *See South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.*, 556 So. 2d 775, 778 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990) (“An injunction must be precise, specific, and limited to the harm alleged.”).  

### III. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Modifying and Extending the Injunction  
The trial court’s extension of the injunction compounded these errors. Florida law requires courts to conduct a rigorous, fact-specific analysis before modifying or extending injunctive relief. Yet the record shows no hearing on new evidence, no findings on whether the original injunction was justified, and no consideration of whether Bradley’s activities actually threatened Health Coalition’s interests. Instead, the court rubber-stamped the extension based on conclusory assertions—contrary to the fundamental principle that “injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation.” *See City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, 357 So. 2d 732, 734 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978).  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honor, the trial court’s order cannot stand. Health Coalition failed to prove irreparable harm—despite the liquidated damages clause—and failed to show Bradley breached the covenant’s specific terms. The injunction was unreasonably broad, unsupported by evidence, and improperly extended. This Court must reverse and remand for dismissal of Health Coalition’s claims.

**RESPONSE OF APPELLEE HEALTH COALITION, INC. TO APPELLANT'S OPENING ARGUMENT**

Your Honor,/Appellate Court,

Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this response in opposition to Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III's opening brief and in defense of the trial court's order granting a temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete. Contrary to Appellant's assertions, the trial court did not abuse its discretion. The evidence presented at the injunction hearing established all requisite elements for injunctive relief, and Appellant's arguments misstate Florida law and ignore the factual record.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The factual record establishes that Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III voluntarily entered into an employment agreement with Health Coalition, Inc. in March 1993, which included a valid covenant not to compete. The covenant expressly prohibited solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation" for a period of two years following termination of employment. The agreement also contained provisions protecting confidential corporate information and included a liquidated damages clause.

Bradley's employment terminated in April 1994. Health Coalition maintains that Bradley voluntarily resigned, while Bradley alternatively claims he was "forced to resign." Regardless of the characterization, the material fact is that Bradley's employment ended, triggering the covenant's two-year restriction period.

In February 1996, Health Coalition filed its action alleging that Bradley had violated the covenant by working for competing company Bio-Med, Inc. and selling blood plasma products to Health Coalition's customers. At the injunction hearing, Bradley raised defenses that the employer had materially breached the employment agreement and that he was entitled to disregard his contractual obligations.

The trial court, after considering the evidence and arguments presented, entered a temporary injunction. This Court should affirm that ruling.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm

Appellant's first argument—that the presence of a liquidated damages clause precludes a finding of irreparable harm—misstates Florida law and ignores the circumstances under which such clauses operate.

The case cited by Appellant, *Krawczyk v. Krawczyk*, 195 So. 2d 653 (Fla. 3d DCA 1967), is inapposite. That case addressed a situation where the sole remedy at issue was monetary damages for a breach of contract. Here, the covenant not to compete is designed to protect Health Coalition's customer relationships and confidential business information—assets that are inherently difficult to value and for which monetary damages often prove inadequate.

Florida courts consistently recognize that noncompetition agreements merit injunctive protection because the harm from a former employee's solicitation of customers is often unquantifiable and ongoing. In *Davis v. McCready*, 162 So. 2d 335 (Fla. 2d DCA 1964), the court held that where a covenant not to compete is at issue, irreparable harm may be presumed because the injury to the employer's business relationships cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages.

Moreover, the liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement does not negate the employer's right to injunctive relief. The clause provides an additional remedy but does not constitute the exclusive remedy. Florida courts have repeatedly held that an employer may pursue both injunctive relief and monetary damages under noncompetition agreements. See *Wheaton v. McCarthy*, 24 So. 2d 492 (Fla. 1945) (holding that liquidated damages provision does not preclude injunctive relief).

The trial court properly found that Health Coalition would suffer irreparable harm absent injunctive relief, as Bradley's continued solicitation of customers would cause ongoing damage to the employer's business relationships that monetary compensation cannot repair.

### B. The Trial Court Properly Found Likelihood of Success on the Merits

Appellant's argument that Health Coalition failed to establish a likelihood of success on the merits misreads the record and ignores the evidence presented.

The covenant not to compete prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." At the injunction hearing, Health Coalition presented evidence that Bradley had gone to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and was selling blood plasma products—the identical products he sold while employed by Health Coalition. This evidence, taken together with the reasonable inference that Bradley would necessarily contact customers in his new position, was sufficient to establish a likelihood of success.

The case cited by Appellant, *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), is distinguishable. In *Giurintano*, the employer failed to present any evidence that the employee was actually soliciting customers or using confidential information. Here, by contrast, Health Coalition presented evidence that Bradley was working for a direct competitor selling identical products, which distinguishes this case from one involving mere employment with a competitor.

Additionally, *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012), which Appellant cites, is distinguishable on its facts. In *Pinnell*, the injunction covered "all customers" without limitation. Here, the covenant specifically limits protection to "active, paid up customers"—a defined class that provides adequate specificity. The trial court's injunction tracks this contractual limitation.

The evidence presented was sufficient to establish a likelihood that Bradley was violating, or would violate, the covenant's terms by soliciting Health Coalition's customers.

### C. Appellant's Defenses Do Not Bar Enforcement of the Covenant

#### 1. The Alleged Material Breach Defense

At the injunction hearing, Appellant argued that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by refusing to pay certain commissions he had earned, thereby releasing him from his obligations under the covenant.

This defense fails for multiple reasons.

First, Appellant's characterization of the alleged non-payment as a "material breach" is disputed. The employment agreement's provisions regarding commission payments are contested, and the trial court was not required to resolve this factual dispute at the preliminary injunction stage. The appropriate remedy for disputed commission claims is an action for breach of contract—not repudiation of the covenant not to compete.

Second, even assuming arguendo that a breach occurred, Florida law holds that a party's right to enforce a contract is not automatically forfeited by the other party's alleged breach. The non-breaching party may elect to continue performance and seek damages. Health Coalition has pursued this course by seeking to enforce the covenant.

Third, the doctrine of unclean hands does not assist Appellant. If Bradley indeed refused to perform lawful instructions from his employer—such as selling products in the ordinary course of business—this does not constitute a defense to his contractual obligations. To the extent Bradley claims he was instructed to engage in improper conduct, he was obligated to seek legal recourse or report such instructions to authorities, not to unilaterally abandon his contractual obligations.

#### 2. The "Forced Resignation" Defense

Appellant contends that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to resell certain plasma products he considered unsafe and refusing to alter invoices.

These allegations, even accepted as true for purposes of the injunction hearing, do not constitute a defense to enforcement of the covenant.

Regarding the plasma products: Appellant's subjective belief that products were unsafe does not establish that they were actually unsafe. The employer took the position that the products were safe for consumption. A factual dispute existed, and Appellant was not entitled to unilaterally determine that the products were unsafe and refuse to perform his job duties. His refusal to sell products that his employer authorized for sale does not excuse his breach of the covenant.

Regarding the invoice allegations: Appellant claims he was instructed to alter invoices to charge higher prices. The employer denies giving any such instruction. Even accepting Bradley's version, his remedy was to report such instructions to appropriate authorities or to the company's compliance department—not to resign and then violate his noncompetition covenant. An employee who resigns rather than follow instructions cannot thereafter claim that his resignation was wrongful and thereby escape his contractual obligations.

The trial court properly rejected these defenses.

### D. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Modifying and Extending the Injunction

Appellant argues that the trial court improperly modified and extended the injunction without adequate consideration. This argument ignores the procedural posture of the case.

The record reflects that the trial court conducted a hearing on the motion to extend the injunction. The court was entitled to consider the existing record, including evidence previously presented, in determining whether to extend injunctive relief. Florida law does not require a full retrial of the underlying merits at every extension hearing.

The standard for modifying or extending a temporary injunction is not as rigorous as Appellant suggests. The court need only find that the circumstances warrant continued protection of the employer's legitimate interests. The trial court made such findings here.

---

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete.

The trial court properly found that Health Coalition demonstrated irreparable harm, that a likelihood of success on the merits existed, and that the balance of hardships favored injunctive relief. Appellant's defenses—regarding alleged material breach and forced resignation—were properly rejected as insufficient to bar enforcement of the covenant.

The temporary injunction should be affirmed, and this matter remanded for further proceedings on the merits.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Based upon the review of the arguments presented by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc., and the facts provided in the record, the trial court’s order granting a temporary injunction is reversed. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction because Health Coalition failed to establish the requisite elements of irreparable harm and a likelihood of success on the merits.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s ruling on a motion for a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate remedy at law; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent.

**II. Analysis**

**A. The Trial Court Erred in Finding Irreparable Harm**

The trial court’s finding of irreparable harm is contrary to established Florida precedent regarding liquidated damages clauses. The employment agreement at issue contains a valid liquidated damages clause. As argued by Appellant, the presence of such a clause dictates that monetary damages are an adequate remedy at law.

In *Krawczyk v. Krawczyk*, 195 So. 2d 653 (Fla. 3d DCA 1967), the court held that “where a contract provides for liquidated damages, the remedy at law is adequate, and irreparable harm cannot be shown.” This principle is directly applicable here. The liquidated damages clause serves as the agreed-upon remedy for a breach of the noncompetition agreement. By including this provision, the parties acknowledged that monetary damages would suffice to compensate the employer for any loss.

Appellee attempts to distinguish *Krawczyk* by citing *Wheaton v. McCarthy*, 24 So. 2d 492 (Fla. 1945), and *Davis v. McCready*, 162 So. 2d 335 (Fla. 2d DCA 1964), for the proposition that irreparable harm may be presumed in noncompetition cases. However, these cases do not override the specific contractual language providing for liquidated damages. Where, as here, the contract explicitly defines the monetary remedy for a breach, the presumption of irreparable harm is rebutted as a matter of law. The trial court erred in ignoring the liquidated damages clause and presuming irreparable harm based solely on the nature of the employer's business.

**B. The Trial Court Erred in Finding a Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also abused its discretion in finding that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation.” It does not prohibit Bradley from working for a competitor or selling blood plasma products generally.

Appellee argues that Bradley’s employment with a direct competitor selling identical products creates a “reasonable inference” of solicitation. While this inference may be plausible, it is insufficient to meet the burden of proof required for a temporary injunction. As stated in *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), “a mere employment relationship with a competitor, without more, does not constitute a breach of a noncompetition agreement.” The record is devoid of evidence that Bradley actually solicited any of Health Coalition’s “active, paid up customers” or misused confidential information. The trial court’s finding was based on speculation rather than the actual proof required by *Giurintano*.

Furthermore, the injunction is overbroad. In *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012), the court held that an injunction prohibiting solicitation of “all customers” was overbroad because it failed to limit coverage to customers with whom the employee had actual contact. Here, the trial court’s order similarly fails to identify the specific customers protected or how Bradley’s activities threaten them. The injunction must be “precise, specific, and limited to the harm alleged,” *South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.*, 556 So. 2d 775, 778 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990), and the current order fails this standard.

**C. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Extending the Injunction**

Finally, the trial court’s modification and extension of the injunction without a hearing on new evidence or specific findings of fact constituted an abuse of discretion. As noted in *City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, 357 So. 2d 732, 734 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), “injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation.” The extension of the injunction was rubber-stamped without the rigorous, fact-specific analysis required by Florida law.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s order granting and extending the temporary injunction is reversed. Health Coalition failed to prove that monetary damages were inadequate due to the liquidated damages clause, and it failed to provide sufficient evidence that Bradley breached the specific terms of the noncompetition agreement. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

As established by controlling Florida precedent, the Appellee failed to satisfy the threshold requirements for injunctive relief—irreparable harm, likelihood of success on the merits, and procedural rigor. ### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING IRREPARABLE HARM BECAUSE THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE PRECLUDED IT  
The Appellee’s attempt to distinguish *Krawczyk v. The Appellee cites *Davis v. McCready*, 162 So. 2d 335 (Fla. 2d DCA 1964), and *Wheaton v. The Appellee’s reliance on these cases ignores the precise contractual language at issue. Florida law does not permit courts to disregard valid liquidated damages provisions to create an artificial "presumption" of irreparable harm. *Krawczyk* controls, and the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm.  

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS  
The Appellee claims Bradley’s mere employment with Bio-Med, Inc.—a competitor selling "identical products"—constitutes a "reasonable inference" of solicitation. This is legally insufficient. In *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), this Court held that **"a mere employment relationship with a competitor, without more, does not constitute a breach of a noncompetition agreement."** 821 So. 2d at 319. The record contains **zero evidence** that Bradley solicited any "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition or misused confidential information. The Appellee’s reliance on "inference" violates *Giurintano*’s clear requirement for concrete proof of actual solicitation.  

Further, the injunction is overbroad and violates fundamental due process. The covenant prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers," yet the trial court’s order fails to identify specific customers Bradley allegedly threatened or how his conduct harmed them. As this Court explained in *South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.*, 556 So. 2d 775 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990), an injunction must be **"precise, specific, and limited to the harm alleged."** 556 So. 2d at 778. In *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012), a similar injunction was reversed because it covered "all customers" without limitation to those with whom the employee had actual contact. Here, the trial court’s order suffers the same defect: it lacks specificity and fails to tie Bradley’s activities to any particular customer or harm. The Appellee’s assertion that "active, paid up customers" provides "adequate specificity" ignores *Pinnell* and *Microcosm*—both require the injunction to define the protected customers with particularity, which it does not.  

### III. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN MODIFYING AND EXTENDING THE INJUNCTION  
The Appellee claims the extension was proper because the court "considered the existing record." This misstates the law. In *City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, 357 So. 2d 732 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), this Court held that **"injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation."** 357 So. 2d at 734. The trial court’s modification and extension of the injunction occurred without a hearing on new evidence, without identifying specific facts justifying continued relief, and without addressing the record’s deficiencies. Florida law requires "rigorous, fact-specific analysis" for such extensions, not a "rubber-stamp" approval. The Appellee’s argument that "no full retrial" is needed ignores *City of Miami Beach*’s mandate that every extension must rest on concrete, current proof of ongoing harm. Here, the record is devoid of such evidence—only speculation of future harm exists.  

### CONCLUSION  
The trial court’s order must be reversed. The Appellee failed to establish irreparable harm due to the liquidated damages clause (*Krawczyk*), failed to prove likelihood of success on the merits because mere employment with a competitor is insufficient without evidence of actual solicitation (*Giurintano*) and the injunction is overbroad (*Pinnell*, *Microcosm*), and improperly extended the injunction without new evidence (*City of Miami Beach*). This Court should vacate the temporary injunction and remand for proceedings consistent with Florida law.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL BRIEF

## IN THE COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA

---

**RESPECTFULLY SUBMITTED BY APPELLEE HEALTH COALITION, INC.**

---

Your Honor, the Appellant's response fails to overcome the well-established standards for temporary injunctive relief and misapplies the precedent cited in the Preliminary Ruling. While the Appellant correctly articulates the general standards, he ignores critical distinctions in the facts and law that compel affirmance of the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction. This Court should reaffirm its Preliminary Ruling and reject the Appellant's attempt to escape his binding contractual obligations through self-serving assertions unsupported by the record.

---

## I. THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DOES NOT NEGATE IRREPARABLE HARM IN THIS CASE

The Appellant correctly notes that *Krawczyk v. Krawczyk*, 195 So. 2d 653 (Fla. 3d DCA 1967), stands for the proposition that a liquidated damages clause may, in some circumstances, demonstrate the adequacy of legal remedies. However, the Appellant misapplies this precedent by ignoring the specific factual context in which this case arises.

The employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause, but this clause was designed to provide a contractual remedy in the event of a *breach by the employee*. The clause does not address the employer's damages in the event of a breach, nor does it account for the unique nature of customer relationships and goodwill that cannot be reduced to a predetermined monetary formula. As the Florida Supreme Court recognized in *Wheaton v. McCarthy*, 24 So. 2d 492 (Fla. 1945), the nature of business relationships and customer goodwill often defies precise monetary valuation, thereby justifying the presumption of irreparable harm in noncompetition cases.

The Preliminary Ruling incorrectly characterized *Wheaton* and *Davis v. McCready*, 162 So. 2d 335 (Fla. 2d DCA 1964), as inapposite. These cases represent the general rule in Florida that irreparable harm is presumed in noncompetition cases because the injury to the employer's business—particularly the loss of customers and goodwill—cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages. The presence of a liquidated damages clause does not override this presumption when the clause was not intended to cover the specific harm alleged or when the harm is of a character that liquidated damages cannot reasonably foresee.

Furthermore, the Appellant's reliance on *Krawczyk* is misplaced because that case involved a contract dispute where the liquidated damages provision was specifically negotiated to cover the precise injury at issue. Here, the employment agreement's liquidated damages clause is a general provision that does not specifically address the harm caused by employee solicitation of customers. The clause does not mention customers, goodwill, or the specific type of competitive injury alleged in this case.

---

## THE APPELLANT FAILED TO ESTABLISH A VALID DEFENSE TO THE NONCOMPETITION AGREEMENT

The Appellant contends that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in improper business practices, thereby releasing him from his obligations under the noncompetition agreement. This defense fails for multiple reasons.

### A. The Appellant's Version of Events Is Unsupported by the Record

The Appellant's assertion that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to resell plasma products and alter invoices is self-serving and unsupported by any corroborating evidence in the record. The employer expressly denies that any such instructions were given. The Appellant has presented no affidavits, deposition testimony, or other documentary evidence supporting his version of events.

Under Florida law, a party asserting a defense based on the other party's alleged misconduct must prove that misconduct by substantial evidence. *See Edenfield v. Fane*, 507 So. 2d 730 (Fla. 3d DCA 1987). The Appellant has failed to meet this burden. The mere assertion in his brief, without supporting evidence in the record, is insufficient to defeat the employer's motion for temporary injunction.

### B. The Appellant's Alleged Refusals Do Not Constitute a Material Breach

Even assuming arguendo that the Appellant was instructed to resell returned plasma products and to alter invoices, his refusal to comply would not constitute a material breach justifying his resignation. The Appellant was a salesperson, not a compliance officer. His obligation was to follow lawful instructions from his employer, subject to his rights under applicable law. The Appellant has not cited any authority establishing that an employee's refusal to follow an employer's direction—without more—constitutes a material breach of the employment agreement.

Moreover, the Appellant's alleged concerns about product safety and customer billing, while perhaps admirable from an ethical standpoint, do not automatically void his contractual obligations. An employee who believes his employer is engaging in wrongdoing has remedies, including reporting to regulatory authorities. He does not have the right to simply walk away from his contractual commitments and then compete with his former employer.

### C. The Appellant's Defense Is Barred by the Parol Evidence Rule

The Appellant attempts to introduce evidence of alleged oral instructions and his subjective beliefs about product safety to vary the terms of the written employment agreement. This is precisely what the parol evidence rule prohibits. The written employment agreement, including the noncompetition covenant, represents the complete and final expression of the parties' agreement. *See Taylor v. Taylor*, 174 So. 2d 50 (Fla. 2d DCA 1965). The Appellant cannot use self-serving allegations about oral communications to escape his written obligations.

---

## THE EVIDENCE ESTABLISHES A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS

The Preliminary Ruling erred in finding that the employer failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits. The record, when properly construed, establishes that the Appellant's employment with Bio-Med, Inc. constitutes a breach of his noncompetition agreement.

### A. The Noncompetition Agreement Prohibits Working for a Competitor

The noncompetition agreement prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." However, the agreement must be read in conjunction with the broader employment relationship. The Appellant was hired as a salesperson specifically to sell the employer's blood plasma products. His skills, relationships, and knowledge were developed at the employer's expense.

When an employee with specialized knowledge of a employer's products and customers goes to work for a direct competitor selling identical products, the inference of solicitation is not merely "reasonable"—it is virtually inevitable. The Appellant's argument that "mere employment" is insufficient under *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), ignores the specific factual context of that case. In *Giurintano*, the employee went to work for a competitor but there was no evidence that he had any contact with the employer's customers or used any confidential information. Here, by contrast, the Appellant was a salesperson whose entire job involved contact with the employer's customers.

### B. The Inference of Solicitation Is Overwhelming

The Appellant was employed by Health Coalition as a salesperson for blood plasma products. He then went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., selling blood plasma products. This is not a case where an employee with general skills moves to a competitor in a different market. This is a direct transfer of specialized skills and customer relationships to a direct competitor.

The noncompetition agreement specifically protects "active, paid up customers"—precisely the type of customers the Appellant was paid to service while employed by Health Coalition. The inference that the Appellant would leverage his existing relationships with these customers for the benefit of his new employer is not speculative; it is the natural and expected result of his employment decision. The trial court properly drew this inference.

### C. The Injunction Is Not Overbroad

The Preliminary Ruling's reliance on *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012), and *South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.*, 556 So. 2d 775 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990), is misplaced. In those cases, the injunctions were found overbroad because they covered customers with whom the employees had no contact. Here, the Appellant had extensive contact with Health Coalition's customers during his employment. The covenant specifically protects "active, paid up customers"—the very customers the Appellant serviced.

The Preliminary Ruling's requirement that the injunction "identify specific customers" is unrealistic and contrary to the nature of noncompetition enforcement. The employer cannot be required to predict which specific customers the Appellant will solicit. The purpose of the injunction is to prevent solicitation *before* it occurs. By prohibiting the Appellant from working for a direct competitor selling identical products, the injunction properly protects the employer's legitimate business interests.

---

## THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY EXTENDED THE INJUNCTION

The Appellant argues that the trial court abused its discretion in extending the injunction without a hearing on new evidence. However, the record demonstrates that the trial court had already conducted an evidentiary hearing on the employer's motion for temporary injunction. The extension was based on the existing record, which established the employer's entitlement to relief.

*City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, 357 So. 2d 732 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), stands for the proposition that injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation. Here, the injunction was based on actual proof—the Appellant's admitted employment with a direct competitor and his prior position as a salesperson for the employer. The trial court did not "rubber-stamp" the extension; it properly applied the law to the facts established at the evidentiary hearing.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Preliminary Ruling's analysis and reject the Appellant's attempt to reverse the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction. The Appellant has failed to establish a valid defense to the noncompetition agreement, and the employer has demonstrated its entitlement to injunctive relief under established Florida law.

**FINAL RULING**

Based upon the review of the rebuttals presented by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc., and the facts provided in the record, the trial court’s order granting a temporary injunction is reversed. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction because Health Coalition failed to establish the requisite elements of irreparable harm and a likelihood of success on the merits. The Appellee’s arguments in its rebuttal do not overcome the deficiencies identified in the preliminary ruling.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s ruling on a motion for a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate remedy at law; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent.

**II. Analysis**

**A. The Trial Court Erred in Finding Irreparable Harm**

The Appellee argues that the liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement is a "general provision" that does not specifically address the harm of solicitation or loss of goodwill, and therefore, the presumption of irreparable harm found in *Wheaton v. McCarthy*, 24 So. 2d 492 (Fla. 1945), and *Davis v. McCready*, 162 So. 2d 335 (Fla. 2d DCA 1964), should apply. This argument is unpersuasive.

The controlling precedent remains *Krawczyk v. Krawczyk*, 195 So. 2d 653 (Fla. 3d DCA 1967), which held that "where a contract provides for liquidated damages, the remedy at law is adequate, and irreparable harm cannot be shown." 195 So. 2d at 655. The Appellee has not provided specific facts regarding the content of the liquidated damages clause to demonstrate that it excludes the type of harm alleged here. The mere assertion that the clause is "general" is insufficient to override the clear contractual provision indicating that the parties agreed monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for a breach. Where a contract contains such a clause, the presumption of irreparable harm is rebutted as a matter of law. The trial court erred in relying on the general presumption of harm in noncompetition cases rather than the specific contractual language at issue.

**B. The Trial Court Erred in Finding a Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The Appellee contends that *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), is distinguishable because Bradley was a salesperson with direct contact with customers, making the inference of solicitation "inevitable." While the factual context of Bradley’s employment differs from the medical professional in *Giurintano*, the legal principle remains intact: "a mere employment relationship with a competitor, without more, does not constitute a breach of a noncompetition agreement." 821 So. 2d at 319.

The Appellee asks this Court to accept an inference of solicitation based solely on Bradley’s position as a salesperson and his subsequent employment with a competitor. However, the record is devoid of evidence that Bradley actually solicited any of Health Coalition’s "active, paid up customers" or misused confidential information. Speculation as to what a salesperson *might* do does not satisfy the burden of proving a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The trial court’s finding was based on speculation rather than the actual proof required by *Giurintano*.

Furthermore, the Appellee’s argument that the injunction is not overbroad because "active, paid up customers" is a sufficiently specific category fails to account for the requirements of *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012). In *Pinnell*, the court held that an injunction prohibiting solicitation of "all customers" was overbroad because it failed to limit coverage to customers with whom the employee had actual contact. Here, the trial court’s order similarly fails to identify the specific customers protected or how Bradley’s activities threaten them. As stated in *South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.*, 556 So. 2d 775, 778 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990), an injunction must be "precise, specific, and limited to the harm alleged." The current order fails this standard.

**C. The Appellee’s Arguments Regarding Bradley’s Defenses Do Not Save the Injunction**

The Appellee correctly notes that Bradley’s defense of "forced resignation" relies on self-serving assertions and that the parol evidence rule may limit the introduction of oral instructions to vary the written agreement, citing *Edenfield v. Fane*, 507 So. 2d 730 (Fla. 3d DCA 1987), and *Taylor v. Taylor*, 174 So. 2d 50 (Fla. 2d DCA 1965). However, even assuming Bradley’s defenses are legally insufficient to defeat the contract’s validity, this does not cure the Appellee’s failure to meet its own burden of proof for injunctive relief. The Appellee must still prove irreparable harm and a likelihood of success on the merits. The weakness of Bradley’s defenses does not substitute for the Appellee’s lack of evidence regarding actual solicitation or the adequacy of the liquidated damages clause.

**D. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Extending the Injunction**

Finally, the trial court’s modification and extension of the injunction without a hearing on new evidence or specific findings of fact constituted an abuse of discretion. As noted in *City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, 357 So. 2d 732, 734 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), "injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation." The extension of the injunction was based on the same insufficient record that failed to support the initial injunction.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s order granting and extending the temporary injunction is reversed. Health Coalition failed to prove that monetary damages were inadequate due to the liquidated damages clause, and it failed to provide sufficient evidence that Bradley breached the specific terms of the noncompetition agreement. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

The mere assertion that the clause is "general" is insufficient to override this settled principle.  

Health Coalition’s reliance on *Wheaton v. McCarthy* and *Davis v. McCready* is misplaced. Those cases establish a *general presumption* of irreparable harm in noncompetition disputes, but they do not apply when a contract explicitly provides for liquidated damages. *Krawczyk* controls because it directly addresses the interplay between liquidated damages clauses and irreparable harm. As this Court recognized in its Preliminary Ruling, the presumption of irreparable harm is rebutted as a matter of law when liquidated damages are agreed upon. Health Coalition’s attempt to distinguish *Krawczyk* by claiming the clause is "not intended to cover the specific harm" is pure speculation—the clause exists, and the parties bound themselves to it. The trial court abused its discretion by ignoring this binding precedent and substituting judicial discretion for contractual certainty.  

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS  
Health Coalition contends that Bradley’s employment with Bio-Med, Inc. "necessarily implies" solicitation of customers because he was a salesperson. This argument rests on conjecture, not evidence. In *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, the Fourth District held that "a mere employment relationship with a competitor, without more, does not constitute a breach of a noncompetition agreement." 821 So. 2d 316, 319 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). Here, Health Coalition presented *zero evidence* that Bradley actually solicited any customers, misused confidential information, or contacted "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition. The trial court’s finding that solicitation is "inevitable" is precisely the kind of speculation condemned by *Giurintano*—it conflates potential risk with actual breach.  

The injunction is also fatally overbroad. In *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, the Fifth District reversed an injunction prohibiting solicitation of "all customers" because it failed to limit coverage to customers with whom the employee had "actual contact." 84 So. 3d 376, 380 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012). Similarly, *South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.* requires injunctions to be "precise, specific, and limited to the harm alleged." 556 So. 2d 775, 778 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990). Yet the trial court’s order prohibits Bradley from working for a competitor broadly, without identifying any specific customers he allegedly threatened or explaining how his role at Bio-Med would harm Health Coalition. "Active, paid up customers" is a vague category that cannot stand without concrete identification of those customers or evidence of Bradley’s contact with them. The trial court’s failure to tailor the injunction to the precise harm alleged violates fundamental due process.  

### III. HEALTH COALITION’S FAILURE TO PROVE ITS BURDEN CANNOT BE CURED BY WEAK DEFENSES  
Health Coalition argues that Bradley’s defenses are "unsupported" and "barred by the parol evidence rule," but this misses the point entirely. The burden to prove injunctive relief rests *solely* on Health Coalition. Even if Bradley’s defenses were legally insufficient—which they are not—Health Coalition still must prove irreparable harm and a likelihood of success on the merits. As this Court correctly noted in its Preliminary Ruling, "the weakness of Bradley’s defenses does not substitute for the Appellee’s lack of evidence." Health Coalition’s rebuttal focuses on Bradley’s defenses not because they are relevant to the injunction’s validity, but because it cannot meet its own burden of proof. The law does not permit shifting this burden.  

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN EXTENDING THE INJUNCTION  
Finally, the extension of the injunction without new evidence or specific findings was an abuse of discretion. In *City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, the Third District held that "injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation." 357 So. 2d 732, 734 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978). The extension here relied on the same insufficient record that failed to support the initial injunction. There was no hearing on new facts, no findings of changed circumstances, and no analysis of whether the original order was justified. The trial court effectively rubber-stamped the injunction without the evidentiary foundation required by law.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honor, Health Coalition’s request for an injunction was denied by the trial court’s own failure to meet the law’s demands. It could not prove irreparable harm due to the liquidated damages clause, it could not show a likelihood of success without evidence of actual solicitation, and its overbroad injunction violated the precision required by precedent. The extension compounded this error. This Court must reverse the injunction and remand for dismissal. The law does not permit courts to enforce contracts through speculative inferences or vague prohibitions. Justice requires that Bradley be freed from an injunction that has no basis in the record or the law.

**FINAL RULING**

Based upon the review of the closing arguments presented by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, and the record provided, the trial court’s order granting a temporary injunction is reversed. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction because Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. failed to establish the requisite elements of irreparable harm and a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. Furthermore, the Appellant has raised significant defenses regarding material breach and constructive discharge that further undermine the Appellee’s position.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s ruling on a motion for a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) the unavailability of an adequate remedy at law; (3) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; and (4) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent.

**II. Analysis**

**A. The Trial Court Erred in Finding Irreparable Harm**

The Appellant correctly asserts that the liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement precludes a finding of irreparable harm. The controlling precedent remains *Krawczyk v. Krawczyk*, 195 So. 2d 653 (Fla. 3d DCA 1967), which held that "where a contract provides for liquidated damages, the remedy at law is adequate, and irreparable harm cannot be shown." 195 So. 2d at 655. The Appellee has not demonstrated that the liquidated damages clause is insufficient to cover the harm alleged. The existence of this contractual remedy rebuts the general presumption of irreparable harm often found in noncompetition cases. The trial court erred in ignoring this specific contractual provision in favor of general presumptions.

**B. The Trial Court Erred in Finding a Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The Appellee failed to establish a substantial likelihood of success on the merits for two independent reasons.

First, the record is devoid of evidence that the Appellant actually solicited any of Health Coalition’s "active, paid up customers." As held in *American Medical International, Inc. v. Giurintano*, 821 So. 2d 316 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), "a mere employment relationship with a competitor, without more, does not constitute a breach of a noncompetition agreement." 821 So. 2d at 319. The Appellee’s inference that solicitation was inevitable based solely on the Appellant’s position as a salesperson is insufficient to meet this burden.

Second, the Appellant has raised a substantive defense of material breach and constructive discharge. The Appellant contends that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in improper business practices, including the resale of potentially unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. Additionally, the Appellant alleges the employer wrongfully withheld earned commissions. If proven, these actions constitute a material breach of the employment agreement by the employer. A material breach by the employer can release the employee from his contractual obligations, including the covenant not to compete. The existence of this factual dispute further precludes a finding that the Appellee has a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

**C. The Injunction Is Overbroad**

The trial court’s order prohibits the solicitation of "active, paid up customers" without identifying specific customers or limiting the restriction to those with whom the Appellant actually had contact. In *Pinnell v. Invader Systems, Inc.*, 84 So. 3d 376 (Fla. 5th DCA 2012), the court held that an injunction prohibiting solicitation of "all customers" was overbroad because it failed to limit coverage to customers with whom the employee had actual contact. Similarly, *South Florida Water Management District v. Microcosm, Ltd.*, 556 So. 2d 775, 778 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990), requires that injunctions be "precise, specific, and limited to the harm alleged." The current order fails this standard by relying on a vague category of customers rather than a specific list tied to the Appellant’s prior activities.

**D. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Extending the Injunction**

Finally, the trial court’s modification and extension of the injunction without a hearing on new evidence or specific findings of fact constituted an abuse of discretion. As noted in *City of Miami Beach v. Forte Towers, Inc.*, 357 So. 2d 732, 734 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), "injunctions must be based on actual proof, not speculation." The extension of the injunction was based on the same insufficient record that failed to support the initial injunction.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s order granting and extending the temporary injunction is reversed. Health Coalition failed to prove that monetary damages were inadequate due to the liquidated damages clause, failed to provide sufficient evidence that Bradley breached the noncompetition agreement, and failed to address the Appellant's defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
