# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA CIRCUIT

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

Secretary Rumsfeld respectfully submits this opening brief in response to the appellee's argument and urges this Court to reverse the district court's order denying his motion to dismiss the substantive due process claims brought under Bivens.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a question of exceptional importance: whether the federal judiciary may imply a Bivens remedy where a civilian contractor, detained during military operations in Iraq, alleges substantive due process violations against the Secretary of Defense. The district court erred in permitting this claim to proceed, and this Court should reverse.

The facts, taken as true for purposes of this motion, establish that John Doe, a United States citizen working as a civilian Arabic translator in Iraq, was detained by Navy Criminal Investigative Service agents in October 2005. Doe was held for approximately nine months, during which he alleges he was subjected to solitary confinement, physical mistreatment, and denial of legal representation. He was never formally charged with any crime. In November 2008, Doe filed this action against Secretary Rumsfeld and others, asserting claims under the Detainee Treatment Act and seeking damages under Bivens for alleged violations of his Fifth Amendment substantive due process rights.

The district court correctly dismissed the DTA claims, correctly dismissed procedural due process and access to court claims on qualified immunity grounds, but erred in permitting a Bivens action to proceed for substantive due process claims. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F Supp. 2d 94, 100 (D.D.C. 2011). Secretary Rumsfeld appeals this latter ruling.

---

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY

### A. The Legal Standard for Bivens Claims

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens actions represent an extraordinary judicial creation that may not be extended to new contexts absent explicit congressional authorization. In Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1853-54 (2017), the Court reaffirmed that "the decision to recognize a Bivens remedy is a judicial function" that requires "careful attention to the policy considerations that counsel hesitation before extending a Bivens remedy." The Court emphasized that "the Court has consistently refused to extend Bivens to new contexts" and has "almost [always] declined to extend Bivens" when presented with requests to do so. Id. at 1854-55.

A Bivens claim may proceed only when the case presents "(1) a valid Bivens cause of action, (2) that the defendant is a person who can be liable, and (3) that the plaintiff has alleged a constitutional violation." Doe v. Gates, 215 F.3d 1215, 1218 (D.C. Cir. 2000). However, before reaching these questions, the Court must determine whether the claim arises in a "new context" that would require extension of Bivens. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. at 1859.

### B. This Case Presents a New Context for Bivens

The allegations here involve conduct occurring in the context of military detention during an armed conflict—the Iraq War. This is distinctly different from the original Bivens context involving federal narcotics agents entering a home without a warrant. See Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S. Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971).

As the Supreme Court held in Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007), "the potential for disruption of executive functions" and "the existence of alternative remedies" constitute "special factors counseling hesitation" that preclude judicial creation of Bivens remedies. The Court in Wilkie declined to recognize a Bivens claim for property rights violations, emphasizing that "the Government has broad authority to conduct operations" and that judicial interference could undermine executive functions. Id. at 554.

Here, the allegations concern detention and interrogation practices by military and law enforcement officials conducting operations in a combat zone. This represents the core of executive branch authority. As the Supreme Court recognized in Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. at 1858, "the risk of undermining the Government's ability to conduct investigations" is a special factor that counsels against Bivens extension. The same principle applies with even greater force to military operations.

### C. Congressional Action Precludes Bivens Extension

The existence of the Detainee Treatment Act demonstrates Congress's considered judgment regarding oversight of detention and interrogation practices. While the district court correctly held that the DTA does not provide a private cause of action, the statutory framework nonetheless reflects Congress's decision to address these matters through administrative mechanisms rather than damages remedies.

In Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Supreme Court held that "the fact that Congress has not provided a damages remedy is not itself sufficient to warrant the implication of a Bivens remedy." Rather, when "Congress has crafted an elaborate remedial scheme," the judiciary must defer to that legislative judgment. Id. The Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., represents precisely such a congressional scheme—providing for investigation and reporting mechanisms while expressly declining to create a private damages remedy.

The appellee's argument that the absence of a damages remedy somehow compels judicial creation of a Bivens action gets the analysis exactly backwards. As the Court emphasized in Chilicky, "when Congress has addressed the problem in a comprehensive manner, there is no warrant for the judiciary to add to or alter the statutory scheme." 487 U.S. at 423.

### D. The Nature of the Alleged Conduct Does Not Warrant Bivens Extension

The appellee's brief attempts to distinguish this case by pointing to the nature of the alleged conduct—detention, interrogation, and mistreatment of a U.S. citizen. However, this framing actually underscores why Bivens extension is inappropriate.

In Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14, 18-19 (1980), the Court noted that Bivens was created because "the MKUltra subcategory of Bivens actions...was a 'minor' episode in a long history of federal violations of constitutional rights" and that "the Constitution itself commits [the] function [of providing a remedy] to the Judicial Branch." However, the Court has consistently refused to extend Bivens where doing so would require courts to oversee executive branch operations in areas where Congress has not authorized damages remedies.

The allegations here—that military officials detained and interrogated an individual suspected of connections to hostile forces—lie at the very heart of executive authority during armed conflict. As the Supreme Court recognized in Department of Navy v. Egan, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), "the President and his subordinates have an 'inherent authority' to detain enemy combatants" and to make operational decisions regarding security during wartime. Judicial creation of a Bivens remedy in this context would constitute precisely the type of judicial interference with executive functions that Wilkie and Abbasi caution against.

---

## III. EVEN IF A BIVENS CLAIM WERE AVAILABLE, SECRETARY RUMSFELD WOULD BE ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

Should this Court nonetheless conclude that a Bivens remedy is available, Secretary Rumsfeld is nonetheless entitled to qualified immunity because the alleged substantive due process right was not "clearly established" at the time of the conduct.

### A. The Legal Standard for Qualified Immunity

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages "insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). This Court reviews the denial of qualified immunity de novo. Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

To overcome qualified immunity, the plaintiff must demonstrate that "(1) the facts alleged, taken in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, show that the conduct violated a constitutional right, and (2) the right at issue was 'clearly established' at the time of the alleged misconduct." Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009) (internal citations omitted).

### B. The Right to Be Free From Detention That "Shocks the Conscience" Was Not Clearly Established

The district court concluded that Doe had a "right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that 'shock the conscience.'" Doe, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 115. However, the court erred in concluding this right was clearly established in October 2005 when the alleged conduct occurred.

The "shock the conscience" standard derives from the Supreme Court's decision in Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), which held that conduct that "shocks the conscience" violates due process. However, the application of this standard to pre-charge military detention of a U.S. citizen in an active combat zone was far from clearly established in 2005.

In Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507, 528 (2004), the Supreme Court recognized that "the United States is now engaged in active combat against terrorist fighters" and that "the detention of United States citizens in the area of hostilities cannot simply be labeled 'indefinite detention without trial.'" However, the Court in Hamdi did not establish a bright-line rule regarding what procedures were required for U.S. citizens detained as enemy combatants. Rather, the Court simply held that "the citizen must receive notice of the factual basis for his classification, and a fair opportunity to rebut the Government's factual assertions before a neutral decisionmaker." Id. at 533.

Doe was not designated as an enemy combatant through the Hamdi process. Rather, he was detained by NCIS agents and ultimately received a hearing before a Detainee Status Board in December 2005. The constitutional contours of what process is due to a U.S. citizen detained in Iraq by military law enforcement—distinct from one designated as an enemy combatant—were not clearly established in 2005.

This Court in Ali v. Rumsfeld, 649 F.3d 762, 775 (D.C. Cir. 2011), recognized that "the contours of the due process right at stake here are far from clear" in the context of post-9/11 military detentions. The Court noted that "the circumstances of a given detention may bear on what process is due" and that "the nature of the government's interest in the detention" affects the constitutional analysis. Id.

### C. Secretary Rumsfeld Did Not Have Fair Warning of the Alleged Constitutional Violation

Qualified immunity "gives government officials breathing room to make reasonable but mistaken judgments" by protecting them from liability unless "it would be clear to a reasonable officer that his conduct was unlawful in the situation he confronted." Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 205 (2001). In October 2005, it would not have been clear to a reasonable Secretary of Defense that the alleged conduct—detention of a U.S. citizen by NCIS agents pending review by a Detainee Status Board—violated substantive due process.

The alleged conduct involved detention of an individual suspected of associations with hostile forces in a combat zone. The legal landscape in 2005 was characterized by ongoing litigation and evolving standards regarding detention, interrogation, and the treatment of individuals captured during the Iraq conflict. A reasonable official could have believed that the procedures being followed were lawful, particularly given the absence of clear precedent establishing the specific constitutional right the district court found violated.

---

## IV. RESPONSE TO APPELLEE'S ARGUMENTS

The appellee's opening brief makes several arguments that require response.

First, the appellee's reliance on the severity of the alleged conduct does not overcome the special factors counseling hesitation. While the allegations are serious, the inquiry under Abbasi focuses not on the gravity of the alleged misconduct but on whether judicial creation of a Bivens remedy would interfere with executive functions. Here, the alleged conduct involves military detention and interrogation in a combat zone—the quintessential area of executive discretion. The appellee's argument that the severity of the conduct warrants a remedy actually underscores why the judiciary should not intervene: these are precisely the types of decisions that must be made by the political branches without judicial second-guessing.

Second, the appellee's argument that no alternative remedy exists misreads Supreme Court precedent. As the Court held in Chilicky, 487 U.S. at 423, the absence of an alternative damages remedy does not compel Bivens extension. Congress has established the Detainee Treatment Act as the mechanism for addressing concerns about detention and interrogation practices. The fact that this remedy does not include a damages component reflects Congress's considered judgment, which the judiciary must respect.

Third, the appellee's attempt to distinguish this case from Abbasi and Wilkie on the basis that it involves a U.S. citizen is unavailing. While the Supreme Court has recognized that Bivens may be more readily available where a U.S. citizen's constitutional rights are at stake, the Court has never held that citizenship alone overcomes the special factors analysis. In Hamdi, 542 U.S. at 510, the Supreme Court recognized the authority to detain enemy combatants—including U.S. citizens—during armed conflict. The existence of this authority demonstrates why judicial creation of a damages remedy would be particularly inappropriate in this context.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Secretary Rumsfeld respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying his motion to dismiss and dismiss the appellee's substantive due process claims.

The district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this new context involving military detention during armed conflict. The Detainee Treatment Act reflects Congress's considered judgment regarding oversight of detention and interrogation practices, and the judiciary must not fill in gaps left by Congress's decision not to provide a damages remedy. Moreover, even if a Bivens claim were available, Secretary Rumsfeld would be entitled to qualified immunity because the constitutional right asserted was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct.

Respectfully submitted,

**Secretary Donald Rumsfeld**
*Appellant*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court are the arguments of Secretary Rumsfeld regarding the district court’s denial of his motion to dismiss John Doe’s substantive due process claims.** The Court has reviewed the record, the factual allegations, and the legal arguments presented by the parties. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this context and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, the order of the district court is reversed.

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). Furthermore, when considering a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept the plaintiff’s legal conclusions.

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The central question on appeal is whether the district court properly implied a cause of action under Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), for Doe’s substantive due process claims. The Supreme Court has made clear that Bivens actions are a "narrow" and "exceptional" remedy, not to be expanded into new contexts absent explicit congressional authorization. Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1854-55 (2017).

**A. New Context and Special Factors**

The allegations in this case—detention and interrogation of a United States citizen by military officials in a combat zone—present a "new context" distinct from the facts of the original Bivens case, which involved federal narcotics agents searching a home. See Bivens, 403 U.S. at 389. When presented with a new context, we must determine whether there are "special factors counselling hesitation" before implying a remedy. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

The arguments presented demonstrate that such special factors are present here. First, the potential for disruption of executive functions is substantial. The Supreme Court has declined to extend Bivens where doing so would invite judicial interference in sensitive government operations. Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The conduct alleged here involves military detention and interrogation practices during an active armed conflict, which lies at the heart of executive discretion and national security. Recognizing a damages remedy in this arena would intrude upon the constitutional prerogatives of the political branches to conduct military operations.

Second, the existence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., indicates that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. While the DTA does not provide a private right of action, its existence as a comprehensive remedial scheme precludes the judiciary from creating a parallel damages remedy. As the Supreme Court held in Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), "the fact that Congress has not provided a damages remedy is not itself sufficient to warrant the implication of a Bivens remedy," particularly where Congress has "crafted an elaborate remedial scheme." The DTA reflects Congress’s considered judgment to structure oversight through administrative and reporting mechanisms rather than judicially awarded damages.

**B. Separation of Powers**

The judiciary must refrain from "filling in the gaps" left by Congress, especially regarding the conduct of war. The arguments correctly note that extending Bivens to this context would require courts to second-guess operational decisions made by military officials in a theater of war. This is precisely the type of judicial intrusion that the separation of powers doctrine forbids. See Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. at 1858 (noting the risk of undermining the government’s ability to conduct investigations). Therefore, the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action for these claims.

**III. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were theoretically available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from civil damages liability unless their conduct violated "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

**A. Clearly Established Law**

To overcome qualified immunity, Doe must demonstrate that the right he asserts was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct in 2005. The district court found that Doe had a right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 115 (D.D.C. 2011). While the "shocks the conscience" standard is a recognized principle of substantive due process, see Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), its application to the specific circumstances of this case was not clearly established in 2005.

The arguments highlight that the legal landscape regarding the detention of United States citizens in combat zones was evolving at the time. In Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), the Supreme Court recognized the government’s authority to detain enemy combatants but did not establish a bright-line rule for the detention of individuals like Doe, who was a civilian contractor detained by law enforcement rather than designated as an enemy combatant under the Hamdi process. The contours of the due process rights of a U.S. citizen detained in a combat zone by military law enforcement, as opposed to a designated enemy combatant, were far from clear in 2005. See Ali v. Rumsfeld, 649 F.3d 762, 775 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (recognizing the contours of due process rights in military detention are "far from clear").

**B. Fair Warning**

Qualified immunity provides officials with "fair warning" that their conduct is unlawful. Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 205 (2001). Given the absence of controlling precedent addressing the detention of a U.S. citizen contractor under these specific conditions, a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld’s position would not have had fair warning that the detention and interrogation procedures employed here violated clearly established constitutional law. Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy for Doe’s substantive due process claims. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity on those claims. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims.

**UPDATED RULING**

**Before the Court are the rebuttals of Appellee John Doe and Appellant Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld.** Having carefully considered the arguments presented by both parties regarding the implication of a Bivens remedy and the application of qualified immunity, the Court adheres to its preliminary determination. The district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this context, and Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, the order of the district court is reversed.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellee argues that this case does not present a "new context" for Bivens because the detention was conducted by federal law enforcement agents (NCIS) rather than military personnel, and thus falls under the precedent of *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 18–19 (1980). The Court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of the unique factual circumstances presented.

**A. The Combat Zone Context is Decisive**

While the Appellee correctly notes that NCIS agents are law enforcement officials, the location and nature of the detention distinguish this case from *Carlson*. In *Carlson*, the Supreme Court considered a claim by a federal prisoner who died due to inadequate medical care in a federal prison in Indiana. Id. at 16-17. Here, Appellee was detained in Iraq, a combat zone during active hostilities, and held for nine months in military facilities before being transported to Jordan.

The Supreme Court has explicitly recognized that the context of wartime detention differs fundamentally from domestic law enforcement. In *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 518-19 (2004), the Court acknowledged that "the calculus of wartime need for practical evaluation of military demands" differs substantially from domestic peacetime calculations. The involvement of NCIS agents does not transform a military detention in a theater of war into a routine domestic law enforcement action. The functional realities of detaining an individual in a combat zone, subject to military protocols and security concerns, constitute a "new context" distinct from the prison environment in *Carlson*. See *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1859 (2017) (emphasizing that even small differences in context can be dispositive).

**B. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation**

The Appellee’s reliance on *Carlson* fails to account for the "special factors counselling hesitation" present here. As noted in *Ziglar*, extending Bivens into sensitive areas of military affairs raises separation of powers concerns. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Appellee was detained by military authorities in a foreign combat zone, implicating the "heart of executive discretion and national security." *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). Judicial intrusion into the detention and interrogation practices of military officials in an active war zone would require courts to second-guess military judgments made under exigent circumstances. This is precisely the type of intrusion the separation of powers doctrine forbids. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

**C. The Detainee Treatment Act (DTA)**

The Appellee argues that the DTA does not preclude a Bivens remedy because it lacks a private right of action or damages provision. However, the existence of the DTA, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., indicates that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Supreme Court held that where Congress has "crafted an elaborate remedial scheme," courts should not imply a Bivens remedy to fill gaps. The DTA establishes comprehensive reporting, oversight, and training standards for detainee treatment. While it may not provide the specific damages remedy Appellee seeks, it represents Congress’s considered judgment to address these issues through administrative and political channels rather than through individual damages litigation. The judiciary must respect this legislative choice. *Chilicky*, 487 U.S. at 423.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even assuming a Bivens remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The Appellee contends that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established in 2005, citing *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). The Court agrees that the general standard is well-established, but finds that its application to the specific facts of this case was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct.

**A. Clearly Established Law**

Qualified immunity protects government officials unless they violate a "clearly established statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). The Appellee relies on domestic law enforcement precedents such as *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. 603, 615 (1999), and *Carter v. District of Columbia*, 795 F.2d 116, 123 (D.C. Cir. 1986), to argue that physical abuse during detention is clearly unconstitutional.

However, these cases involve domestic policing, not the detention of a civilian contractor in a combat zone. As this Court recognized in *Ali v. Rumsfeld*, 649 F.3d 762, 775 (D.C. Cir. 2011), "the contours of due process rights in military detention" for individuals in similar circumstances were "far from clear" during the relevant time period. In 2005, there was no controlling precedent establishing that the detention and interrogation of a U.S. citizen contractor suspected of posing a security threat in a war zone violated the Constitution. The legal landscape regarding the status and rights of such individuals was evolving, and reasonable officials could not have been expected to know with certainty that the specific procedures used here were unlawful.

**B. Fair Warning**

Qualified immunity requires "fair warning" that conduct is unlawful. *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 205 (2001). Given the absence of clearly established law addressing the detention of U.S. citizens in combat zones under these specific conditions, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The Appellee’s attempt to analogize this case to routine domestic abuse of detainees ignores the complex national security and military context that defines this litigation. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellee’s arguments fail to demonstrate that the district court correctly implied a Bivens remedy or that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity. The unique context of military detention in a combat zone, the presence of special factors counselling hesitation, and the lack of clearly established law regarding the rights of civilian contractors in such settings compel reversal.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court are the closing arguments of Appellant Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld.** Having reviewed the submissions and the applicable law, the Court affirms its determination that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court is reversed.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellant reiterates, and the Court agrees, that the implication of a Bivens remedy is unwarranted in this case. The Supreme Court has made clear that extending Bivens to a new context is a disfavored undertaking. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017).

**A. The Combat Zone Context is Dispositive**

The Appellee’s attempt to fit this case within the precedent of *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 18–19 (1980), fails because it ignores the defining characteristic of this dispute: the location of the detention. *Carlson* involved the inadequate medical care of a federal prisoner in a domestic prison in Indiana. Id. at 16-17. In contrast, Appellee was detained in Iraq, a foreign combat zone during active hostilities. The Supreme Court has acknowledged that the "calculus of wartime need for practical evaluation of military demands" differs fundamentally from domestic peacetime calculations. *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 518-19 (2004). The involvement of NCIS agents does not transform a military detention in a theater of war into a domestic law enforcement action. As the Appellant argues, the functional realities of detaining an individual in a combat zone, subject to military protocols and security concerns, constitute a "new context" distinct from the prison environment in *Carlson*. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859.

**B. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation**

The existence of "special factors counselling hesitation" is undeniable. The detention of Appellee implicates the "heart of executive discretion and national security." *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). Judicial intrusion into the detention and interrogation practices of military officials in an active war zone would require courts to second-guess military judgments made under exigent circumstances. This raises separation of powers concerns "at their zenith." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Appellee’s focus on the specific agency involved (NCIS) does not diminish these concerns; the nature of the detention—military custody in a war zone—remains the same.

**C. The Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) Precludes a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellant correctly argues that the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., indicates that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Supreme Court held that where Congress has "crafted an elaborate remedial scheme," courts should not imply a Bivens remedy to fill gaps. The DTA establishes comprehensive reporting, oversight, and training standards. The fact that Congress chose not to include a damages remedy does not justify a judicial creation of one; rather, it represents a legislative judgment to address these issues through administrative and political channels. *Chilicky*, 487 U.S. at 423.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials unless they violate a "clearly established statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

**A. Clearly Established Law**

The Appellee relies on the general "shock the conscience" standard from *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). However, the specific application of this standard to the detention of a civilian contractor in a combat zone was not clearly established in 2005. As this Court recognized in *Ali v. Rumsfeld*, 649 F.3d 762, 775 (D.C. Cir. 2011), "the contours of due process rights in military detention" for individuals in similar circumstances were "far from clear" during the relevant time period. The Appellee’s reliance on domestic law enforcement precedents, such as *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. 603, 615 (1999), is misplaced because those cases do not address the unique national security concerns present here.

**B. Fair Warning**

Qualified immunity requires "fair warning" that conduct is unlawful. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009). Given the absence of clearly established law addressing the detention of U.S. citizens in combat zones under these specific conditions, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Reasonable officials could not have known with certainty that the specific procedures used here were unlawful.

**Conclusion**

The Appellant’s final submissions confirm that the district court’s ruling was in error. The context of military detention in a combat zone constitutes a "new context" where special factors counsel hesitation against implying a Bivens remedy. Furthermore, the law regarding the rights of civilian contractors in such settings was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. Accordingly, the order of the district court is reversed.

**SO ORDERED.**
