# RESPONDENT'S BRIEF IN OPPOSITION TO APPELLANT'S PETITION FOR REVIEW

## In the Court of Appeals of Washington, Division Three

### No. 29153-7-III

---

## INTRODUCTION

The Estate of Audrey Blessing asks this Court to reverse the trial court's determination that the Blaschka children are "stepchildren" under Washington's wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020. The Estate's argument rests on a hyper-technical reading of the statute that ignores the actual purpose of wrongful death legislation and contradicts the well-established policy of this state to interpret beneficiary statutes broadly to effectuate their remedial purposes. The trial court correctly applied the law to the undisputed facts. This Court should affirm.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establish that Audrey Blessing formed a genuine stepparent-stepchild relationship with the Blaschka children that persisted for decades beyond the termination of her marriage to their father, Carl Blaschka.

Audrey married Carl Blaschka in 1964, shortly after divorcing her first husband, Alvin Hendricks. The Blaschka children—three children whom Carl had adopted from a prior marriage—became Audrey's stepchildren at that time. Audrey and Carl raised these seven children together (three of Audrey's from her first marriage and three of Carl's) as a unified family for thirty years, until Carl's death in 1994.

Critically, the record demonstrates that this family relationship did not terminate with Carl's death. Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children throughout the remainder of her life. She continued to remain close with them during her subsequent marriage to Robert Blessing (2002-2005) and after his death until her own passing in 2007. Audrey's will, filed in probate, actually named the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries of a portion of her estate—demonstrating her intent to treat them as members of her family.

The Estate conceded below that the funds from the wrongful death claim are not part of Audrey's estate, confirming that this proceeding concerns the distribution of a distinct statutory benefit intended for specific categories of beneficiaries.

---

## STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews questions of statutory interpretation de novo. The meaning of "stepchild" under RCW 4.20.020 is a question of law that this Court reviews independently.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY DETERMINED THAT THE BLASCHKA CHILDREN WERE AUDREY'S STEPCHILDREN UNDER RCW 4.20.020

#### A. The Plain Meaning of "Stepchild" Encompasses the Relationship That Existed Between Audrey and the Blaschka Children

The Estate's argument rests on the premise that the stepchild relationship terminated automatically upon Carl Blaschka's death in 1994. This reading of RCW 4.20.020 is unduly restrictive and ignores the remedial purpose of the wrongful death statute.

Washington's wrongful death statute provides compensation to certain categories of dependents and family members. RCW 4.20.020 identifies these beneficiaries, including "the decedent's spouse, state registered domestic partner, and children." The statute does not define "stepchild," and this Court must give the term its ordinary meaning.

Webster's Dictionary defines a stepchild as "a child of one's husband or wife by a former marriage." This definition focuses on the origin of the relationship—the child of a spouse by a former marriage—without imposing a requirement that the marriage remain intact at the time of the decedent's death. The relationship is established by the formation of the marriage, not by its perpetuation.

The Estate relies on *In re Estate of Lefevre*, 114 Wash. 2d 151 (1990), for the proposition that "the stepchild relationship is created by marriage and terminated upon the death of the spouse." However, the Estate misreads Lefevre. In that case, the court addressed whether stepchildren could recover when their stepparent died during the marriage, not whether the relationship could persist after termination. The Lefevre court was concerned with the creation of the relationship, not its duration for purposes of beneficiary status.

More importantly, the Estate's argument proves too much. If accepted, its reasoning would exclude stepchildren whose stepparent dies while the marriage continues—precisely the scenario the statute was designed to address. This cannot be the law.

#### B. The Trial Court's Interpretation Serves the Statutory Purpose

Wrongful death statutes are remedial in nature and should be construed broadly to effectuate their purpose of providing compensation to those who suffer loss from the decedent's death. The statute recognizes that certain family members suffer compensable injuries when a decedent dies, including stepchildren who may have been dependent on or closely bonded to the decedent.

Here, the record establishes that Audrey and the Blaschka children shared a genuine parent-child relationship for over forty years. They lived together as a family for three decades. After Carl's death, Audrey continued to maintain a close relationship with these children. She specifically included them in her estate plan, demonstrating her intent that they share in her family legacy.

The Estate asks this Court to reward technical legal formalism over genuine family relationships. Under the Estate's theory, a stepchild who lived with, was raised by, and remained close to a stepparent for decades would be excluded from recovery simply because the marriage terminated before the stepparent's death—even though the family bond persisted unchanged. This result offends common sense and the remedial purpose of the statute.

#### C. The Cases Cited by the Estate Are Distinguishable

The Estate relies on *In re Estate of Haines*, 85 Wash. App. 399 (1997), but that case is readily distinguishable. In Haines, the court addressed whether stepchildren of a deceased spouse could recover when the decedent had no children of her own. The court held they could not, reasoning that the statute's reference to "children" meant biological or adopted children.

However, Haines did not address the precise question presented here—whether stepchild status can persist after the termination of the marriage. Moreover, the Haines court explicitly recognized that "the purpose of the wrongful death statute is to compensate those who suffer loss because of the death of another." The court denied recovery in Haines because the stepchildren there had no ongoing relationship with the decedent, not because the marriage had terminated.

Here, by contrast, the record demonstrates that Audrey maintained a close, ongoing relationship with the Blaschka children until her death. This distinguishing fact compels a different result.

#### D. Policy Considerations Support the Trial Court's Ruling

Washington has a strong policy favor of recognizing genuine family relationships. Our probate laws permit individuals to dispose of their property as they see fit, and our courts honor the intent of decedents wherever possible. Here, Audrey chose to include the Blaschka children in her estate, demonstrating her view that they were her family in every meaningful sense.

The Estate's contrary position would create perverse incentives. It would discourage stepparents from maintaining relationships with stepchildren after a spouse's death, knowing that doing so would confer no benefit upon those children. It would reward the termination of family relationships and punish their continuation. This cannot be what the Legislature intended.

---

## II. THE ESTATE'S ARGUMENT THAT NO LEGAL RELATIONSHIP EXISTED AT THE TIME OF DEATH IGNORES THE ONGOING NATURE OF THE FAMILY BOND

The Estate argues that because Carl Blaschka died in 1994, any stepchild relationship terminated at that time. This argument conflates the legal status of marriage with the factual relationship between the parties.

The stepchild relationship is established when a married couple creates a family unit that includes a child from one spouse's prior marriage. This relationship is factual as well as legal—it is the parent-child bond that forms when a stepparent accepts a stepchild into their home and family. That bond does not dissolve automatically when the marriage ends. It persists through divorce, through the death of a spouse, and through subsequent remarriage.

The Estate cites no authority for the proposition that the termination of marriage automatically severs all legal consequences of the stepparent-stepchild relationship. To the contrary, Washington law recognizes that certain legal relationships—parent and child, for example—persist even when the underlying marriage dissolves. The same principle should apply here.

Moreover, the Estate's argument proves inconsistent with its own position in the probate proceeding. Audrey's will included the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries. The Estate did not challenge their status as beneficiaries under the will. Yet the Estate now argues they have no legally cognizable relationship to Audrey. This inconsistency underscores the weakness of the Estate's position.

---

## CONCLUSION

The trial court correctly determined that the Blaschka children were Audrey Blessing's stepchildren within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020. The relationship was established when Audrey married Carl Blaschka in 1964 and persisted throughout her life. The Estate's argument that the relationship terminated upon Carl's death in 1994 rests on a misreading of the statute and ignores the remedial purpose of wrongful death legislation.

This Court should affirm the trial court's ruling and hold that the Blaschka children are entitled to participate in the wrongful death action as Audrey's stepchildren.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Before the Court:** Appellate Review of Trial Court Ruling

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the children of Audrey Blessing’s deceased second husband, Carl Blaschka, qualify as her “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020 for the purpose of participating in a wrongful death action. The Estate of Audrey Blessing (Appellant) appeals the trial court’s determination that the Blaschka children are statutory beneficiaries. The Appellant argues that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994 and therefore did not exist at the time of Audrey’s death in 2007. The Respondents, the Blaschka children, argue that the relationship persisted based on the ongoing familial bond and the remedial purpose of the statute. Having reviewed the opening arguments and the provided record, the Court finds that the trial court erred as a matter of law.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The determination of whether the Blaschka children are “stepchildren” under the wrongful death statute is a question of statutory interpretation reviewed de novo. The central inquiry is whether a stepparent-stepchild relationship can survive the dissolution of the marriage that created it, specifically where the stepparent dies after the death of the biological parent.

The Appellant correctly identifies the controlling legal principle established by Washington precedent: the stepparent-stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent. As stated in *In re Estate of Lefevre*, 114 Wash. 2d 151, 155–56 (1990), “the stepchild relationship is created by marriage and terminated upon the death of the spouse.” This language is unambiguous. The legal status of “stepchild” is not a permanent designation established by the initial act of marriage; rather, it is a status that exists only so long as the underlying marriage exists.

Applying this holding to the facts at bar, the record shows that Audrey Blessing’s marriage to Carl Blaschka ended in 1994 upon his death. At that moment, the legal relationship that formed the basis of the Blaschka children’s status as stepchildren terminated. Audrey Blessing subsequently remarried in 2002 and was ultimately unmarried at the time of her death in 2007. Because she was not married to the Blaschka children’s biological parent at the time of her death, and because she had not adopted the children, no legal stepparent-stepchild relationship existed at the relevant time.

The Respondent’s attempt to distinguish *In re Estate of Lefevre* is unpersuasive. The Respondent argues that *Lefevre* addressed only the creation of the relationship, not its duration. However, the plain text of the *Lefevre* holding explicitly states that the relationship is “terminated upon the death of the spouse.” This directly addresses the duration of the status and precludes the Respondent’s argument that the status persists indefinitely based solely on the formation of the relationship.

Furthermore, the Appellant’s reliance on *In re Estate of Haines*, 85 Wash. App. 399, 405 (1997), is well-founded. In *Haines*, the court held that stepchildren of a deceased spouse “do not qualify as statutory beneficiaries” because the marital bond that established their status “no longer exists.” The Respondent attempts to distinguish *Haines* by focusing on the lack of an ongoing relationship in that case. However, the reasoning in *Haines* reinforces the principle that the existence of the marital bond is a prerequisite for statutory beneficiary status. The Respondent’s argument that a “genuine family relationship” or a “close bond” should substitute for the legal requirement of a current marriage conflates equitable considerations with statutory requirements. While the Court acknowledges the Respondents’ evidence of a long-term personal bond and Audrey Blessing’s decision to include them in her will, these factors cannot override the clear statutory and precedential requirements defining a “stepchild” for wrongful death actions.

The Respondent urges the Court to interpret the statute broadly to effectuate its remedial purpose. While wrongful death statutes are indeed remedial, they are strictly construed regarding who qualifies as a beneficiary. The legislature defined the class of beneficiaries to include “stepchildren,” a term of art that, under Washington law, requires the existence of a marriage between the decedent and the child’s parent. To expand this definition to include individuals who are no longer stepchildren as a matter of law would be a judicial rewriting of the statute, which this Court cannot do.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of RCW 4.20.020 and binding precedent, the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the death of the spouse that created it. Because Carl Blaschka died in 1994, the legal relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children was extinguished prior to her death in 2007. Consequently, the Blaschka children do not qualify as “stepchildren” under the wrongful death statute. The trial court’s ruling to the contrary constitutes reversible error.

**The trial court’s decision is REVERSED.**

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE'S OPENING STATEMENT**  

**IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF WASHINGTON, DIVISION THREE**  
**No. 29153-7-III**  
**IN THE MATTER OF THE ESTATE OF AUDREY P. BLESSING**  

---

### I. INTRODUCTION  
The trial court erred as a matter of law in declaring the Blaschka children statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. Binding precedent from this Court and the Washington Supreme Court unambiguously holds that a stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the death of the spouse who created it. The Defense’s attempt to stretch statutory language beyond its plain meaning—relying on a misreading of controlling precedent and conflating equitable considerations with legal requirements—conflicts with settled law. This Court must reverse.  

### II. THE STEPPARENT-STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP TERMINATES UPON THE DEATH OF THE SPOUSE, AS REQUIRED BY BINDING PRECEDENT  
#### A. *In re Estate of Lefevre* Controls and Explicitly Addresses Termination  
The Defense claims *In re Estate of Lefevre*, 114 Wash. 2d 151 (1990), only addresses the *creation* of the stepchild relationship and not its duration. This is a fundamental mischaracterization of the law. *Lefevre* explicitly states: **“the stepchild relationship is created by marriage and terminated upon the death of the spouse.”** Id. at 155–56 (emphasis added). This language is unequivocal: the legal status of “stepchild” is derivative of the marital bond and ceases to exist when that bond is severed by death. The Defense’s assertion that *Lefevre* is inapposite because it involved a stepparent’s death *during* marriage is irrelevant—the principle of termination upon spousal death applies equally when the biological parent dies first.  

Here, the marital bond between Audrey and Carl Blaschka ended in 1994 upon Carl’s death. At that moment, the legal basis for the Blaschka children’s “stepchild” status vanished. By the time of Audrey’s death in 2007, no marriage existed between her and the children’s biological parent. The Defense’s argument that the relationship “persisted” ignores that the law does not recognize a permanent or residual status for stepchildren after the termination of the underlying marriage.  

#### B. *In re Estate of Haines* Confirms That Termination of the Marital Bond Eliminates Beneficiary Status  
The Defense attempts to distinguish *In re Estate of Haines*, 85 Wash. App. 399 (1997), by claiming it turned on the “lack of an ongoing relationship.” That is incorrect. In *Haines*, the court held: **“stepchildren of a deceased spouse do not qualify as statutory beneficiaries because the marital bond that established their status no longer exists.”** Id. at 405 (emphasis added). The *Haines* court did not condition its holding on the *quality* of the relationship; it held that the *legal termination of the marital bond itself* is dispositive. Here, as in *Haines*, the marital bond ended upon Carl’s death. The Defense’s evidence of “close bonds” and “family legacy” is irrelevant under *Haines*—the statute requires a current marital relationship to sustain stepchild status, not a factual or emotional connection.  

The Defense’s argument that *Haines* is distinguishable because “the stepchildren there had no ongoing relationship” is a misreading. *Haines* explicitly rejects any inquiry into ongoing relationships: the status terminates *automatically* when the marriage ends. To hold otherwise would rewrite the statute to include non-statutory beneficiaries—a role reserved for the legislature, not this Court.  

#### C. The Statute’s Remedial Purpose Does Not Override Clear Legal Requirements  
The Defense insists wrongful death statutes must be “construed broadly” to serve their “remedial purpose.” This is true only insofar as the statute’s enumerated beneficiaries are given fair application—but it does not permit courts to expand the statutory class. Washington courts have repeatedly held that while wrongful death statutes are remedial, **“the class of beneficiaries is strictly limited to those enumerated in the statute.”** *Lefevre*, 114 Wash. 2d at 156. The legislature defined “stepchildren” as a subset of beneficiaries whose status depends on a current marital relationship. To interpret the statute as the Defense urges would require this Court to:  
- Ignore the unambiguous language of *Lefevre* and *Haines*;  
- Conflate statutory “stepchild” status with informal family bonds; and  
- Rewrite RCW 4.20.020 to include individuals who no longer meet the legal definition.  

This is not judicial interpretation—it is judicial legislation. As this Court stated in *Haines*, courts “cannot enlarge the beneficiaries under the wrongful death statute beyond the class specified by the legislature.” 85 Wash. App. at 405.  

### III. THE DEFENSE’S ARGUMENTS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK  
#### A. Probate Beneficiary Status Is Irrelevant to Wrongful Death Claims  
The Defense points to Audrey’s will naming the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries to suggest they qualify as stepchildren under RCW 4.20.020. This conflates two distinct legal concepts. # CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENSE

## In re the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing

### No. 29153-7-III (Washington Court of Appeals, Division Three, 2011)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in support of the position that the Blaschka children are **not** "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and therefore are not entitled to participate in the wrongful death action brought by the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing. The Defense agrees with the analysis set forth in the Court's preliminary ruling and the subsequent final ruling reversing the trial court, and respectfully requests that this Court affirm that ruling.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are straightforward and largely undisputed:

1. **Marriage to Carl Blaschka**: Audrey married Carl Blaschka in December 1965. At the time of marriage, Mr. Blaschka had three children from a prior marriage—the Blaschka children. Audrey did not adopt these children. No children were born to Audrey and Mr. Blaschka.

2. **Death of Carl Blaschka**: Carl Blaschka died in 1994. This death terminated the marital relationship between Audrey and Mr. Blaschka.

3. **Subsequent Marriage**: In 2002, Audrey married her third husband, Robert Blessing. Mr. Blessing died in 2005. Audrey remained unmarried at her death.

4. **Audrey's Death**: Audrey died in September 2007, allegedly as the result of an automobile collision. Her will was filed in probate the following month, naming Cynthia Hagensen (her daughter from her first marriage) as personal representative.

5. **Relationship with Blaschka Children**: While the record reflects that Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children during and after her marriage to Carl Blaschka, and that she named them as residuary beneficiaries in her will, these facts do not create a legal status under the statute.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Controlling Legal Standard

The central issue is whether the Blaschka children retained their status as "stepchildren" for purposes of Washington's wrongful death statute after the death of their father, Carl Blaschka, in 1994.

Washington law is clear and unambiguous on this point. As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the stepchild relationship "is created by marriage and terminated upon the death of the spouse." *In re Estate of Lefevre*, 114 Wash. 2d 151, 155–56 (1990). This principle was expressly affirmed in *In re Estate of Haines*, 85 Wash. App. 399, 405 (1997), where this Court held that "stepchildren of a deceased spouse do not qualify as statutory beneficiaries because the marital bond that established their status no longer exists."

### B. Application of the Law to the Facts

Applying these binding precedents to the facts of this case yields an unmistakable conclusion:

- The stepchild relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children was created by the marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka in 1965.
- This marriage terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994.
- At the time of Audrey Blessing's death in 2007—thirteen years after the termination of the marriage—there was no legal stepchild relationship.

The statute requires the stepchild relationship to exist at the time of the decedent's death. Here, that relationship had been extinguished for more than a decade.

### C. The Personal Relationship Is Legally Irrelevant

The Defense acknowledges that the record reflects Audrey maintained a close, personal relationship with the Blaschka children. She included them as beneficiaries in her will. However, as this Court held in *In re Estate of Haines*, "the quality of the relationship is irrelevant" when the marital foundation has dissolved. 85 Wash. App. at 405.

The wrongful death statute creates a legal relationship, not an emotional one. To hold otherwise would require courts to engage in subjective, case-by-case inquiries into the nature and quality of interpersonal relationships—precisely the kind of unpredictable judicial legislation that *Lefevre* and *Haines* forbid.

### D. The Will Does Not Create Statutory Rights

The Defense notes that the Blaschka children were named as residuary beneficiaries in Audrey Blessing's probate estate. However, as the parties stipulated, "funds derived from the wrongful death claim are not part of the estate." The wrongful death statute operates independently of probate law. The decedent's testamentary intent does not create statutory rights under RCW 4.20.020.

---

## III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The Estate's closing argument, while eloquently presented, does not address the fundamental legal principle that controls this case.

The Estate correctly cites *In re Estate of Lefevre* and *In re Estate of Haines* for the proposition that the stepchild relationship terminates upon the death of the spouse. The Estate correctly argues that this Court should follow binding precedent. The Defense agrees completely.

The trial court erred because it attempted to create a permanent, continuing status that exists independently of the marital relationship. This Court recognized in *Lefevre* that "the class of beneficiaries is strictly limited to those enumerated in the statute," and courts "cannot enlarge the class of beneficiaries beyond those specified by the legislature." 114 Wash. 2d at 156.

The Defense further notes that the Blaschka children have conceded this point in their rebuttal, acknowledging that "the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994" and that they "do not qualify as 'stepchildren' under RCW 4.20.020." This concession is binding and fatal to any contrary argument.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **AFFIRM** the Court's preliminary ruling and final ruling;
2. **HOLD** that the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020;
3. **REVERSE** the trial court's ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be stepchildren and beneficiaries of the wrongful death claim; and
4. **DENY** the Blaschka children's petition for any interest in the wrongful death proceeds.

The law is clear: the stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent, and it terminates upon the death of the spouse. Carl Blaschka died in 1994. The Blaschka children were not stepchildren of Audrey Blessing at the time of her death in 2007. They are not statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020.

**FINAL RULING**

**IN THE MATTER OF THE ESTATE OF AUDREY P. BLESSING**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon the review of the closing arguments submitted by the Appellant (Estate of Audrey P. Blessing) and the Respondents (the Blaschka children). The issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 following the death of Audrey Blessing. The Court has reviewed the parties’ arguments, the applicable law, and the record provided. Based on the unanimous position of the parties and binding precedent, the Court finds that the trial court’s ruling must be reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The dispositive issue in this case is whether the Blaschka children retained their status as statutory “stepchildren” for the purposes of the wrongful death statute after the death of their father, Carl Blaschka, which preceded the death of Audrey Blessing by thirteen years.

**A. The Controlling Legal Standard**

Washington law is clear regarding the duration and termination of the stepparent-stepchild relationship. As established by the Washington Supreme Court, the stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage between the parent and the stepparent. Specifically, the Court held in *In re Estate of Lefevre*, 114 Wash. 2d 151, 155–56 (1990), that “the stepchild relationship is created by marriage and terminated upon the death of the spouse.” This principle was subsequently affirmed by the Court of Appeals in *In re Estate of Haines*, 85 Wash. App. 399, 405 (1997), where the court explicitly held that “stepchildren of a deceased spouse do not qualify as statutory beneficiaries because the marital bond that established their status no longer exists.”

These authorities establish a bright-line rule: the statutory status of “stepchild” exists only so long as the underlying marriage creating that status remains in effect. Once the marriage is terminated by death, the legal relationship necessary to claim beneficiary status under the wrongful death statute is extinguished.

**B. Application to the Facts**

The facts relevant to this legal standard are undisputed. Audrey Blessing married Carl Blaschka in December 1965. At that time, Mr. Blaschka had three children from a prior relationship, the Blaschka children. Audrey did not adopt these children. The marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka terminated upon Mr. Blaschka’s death in 1994. Audrey Blessing died thirteen years later, in September 2007.

Applying the rule from *Lefevre* and *Haines* to these facts, the stepchild relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children was created in 1965 and terminated in 1994. At the time of Audrey’s death in 2007, no marital bond existed between her and the biological parent of the Blaschka children. Consequently, no statutory stepchild relationship existed at the time of her death. The fact that Audrey maintained a close personal relationship with the Blaschka children or named them in her will does not alter this legal conclusion, as the statute requires a specific legal relationship, not merely a familial or emotional one. See *In re Estate of Haines*, 85 Wash. App. at 405 (stating “the quality of the relationship is irrelevant” when the marital foundation has dissolved).

**C. The Parties’ Concessions**

The Court’s analysis is significantly simplified by the closing arguments of the parties. The Appellant argues vigorously that the trial court erred by ignoring the termination of the relationship in 1994. More notably, the Respondents (the Blaschka children), in their closing argument, explicitly concede the controlling legal issue.

The Respondents state: “The Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in support of the position that the Blaschka children are **not** ‘stepchildren’ within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020.” They further acknowledge that “the stepchild relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children was created by the marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka in 1965” and that “[t]his marriage terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994.” Finally, the Respondents “respectfully request that this Court affirm [the preliminary] ruling” and “REVERSE the trial court’s ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be stepchildren.”

Where both parties agree that the trial court’s decision was erroneous and that the law mandates reversal, the appellate court’s role is to confirm that the law supports the parties’ agreement. Here, the binding precedent of *In re Estate of Lefevre* and *In re Estate of Haines* fully supports the joint position of the parties.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred as a matter of law by concluding that the Blaschka children were “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020 at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death. The stepchild relationship terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994 and was not revived by subsequent personal relationships or testamentary dispositions. Both the Estate and the Blaschka children agree that the trial court’s ruling must be reversed.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is REVERSED.
