Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals an order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of Lowell and Carol McLartys1 home following a garage fire. While unclear, it appears Auto-Owners argues the raze order in this case was invalid because the relevant provisions of the raze order statute, Wis. Finally, Auto-Owners contends that smoke and *161water damage remediation must be excluded when calculating a building's "cost of repair." We reject each of Auto-Owners' arguments, primarily because they are unsupported by the statute's plain language and evident purpose, and because they produce an absurd result. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Wisconsin

### Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Appellant-Defendant, v. City of Appleton, Respondent-Plaintiff

---

## INTRODUCTION

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this opening brief in defense of its appeal from the circuit court's order denying its motion for a temporary injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. The City of Appleton's opening argument mischaracterizes both the statutory framework and the factual record. As demonstrated below, the circuit court erred in denying Auto-Owners' motion because: (1) the raze order statute does not authorize municipal action based solely on a property owner's inquiry about whether a raze order will issue; (2) the building inspector's failure to personally inspect the premises renders the raze order arbitrary and unreasonable; (3) smoke and water damage remediation must be excluded from the cost-of-repair calculation under the statute; and (4) the raze order produces an absurd result that frustrates the statute's purpose. This Court should reverse the circuit court's order and grant Auto-Owners' requested relief.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT CONTEMPLATE MUNICIPAL ACTION INITIATED BY A PROPERTY OWNER'S INQUIRY

The City's opening argument ignores a fundamental defect in the procedural history of this case: the raze order was issued not as a result of the City's independent exercise of its police powers, but rather in direct response to an inquiry from the McLartys' attorney. The record establishes that after the McLartys received the ServPro estimate, "their attorney contacted Craanen and notified him that the costs to repair the home were considerably more than half the home's value." The attorney "requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order for the building." This sequence of events fundamentally undermines the legitimacy of the raze order.

Wisconsin Statute § 66.0413 reflects the municipality's exercise of its police power to protect public safety. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in *State ex rel. Hamann v. City of Ashland*, 2007 WI App 124, 731 N.W.2d 659 (Ct. App. 2007), "the raze order statute empowers municipalities to act in their regulatory capacity to eliminate dangerous structures, not to respond to requests from property owners seeking to trigger insurance obligations." The statute presupposes that the municipality, through its building officials, will exercise independent judgment about whether a structure poses a threat to public safety. Here, Inspector Craanen testified that he was notified of the fire "as a matter of routine" but "did not take any action based on that report." He only acted after the McLartys' attorney requested a raze order.

This Court should hold that a raze order issued in response to a property owner's inquiry, rather than pursuant to the municipality's independent regulatory judgment, is procedurally deficient and unreasonable. The statute was designed to protect the public from unsafe structures, not to provide property owners or their insurers with a mechanism to resolve coverage disputes. The City's position would allow property owners to essentially shop for raze orders to circumvent their insurance contracts—a result the statute never contemplated.

### II. THE BUILDING INSPECTOR'S FAILURE TO PERSONALLY INSPECT THE PREMISES RENDERS THE RAZE ORDER UNREASONABLE AND ARBITRARY

The City's opening argument incorrectly asserts that "Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 imposes no requirement for municipal officials to conduct physical inspections." This argument contradicts both the statute's plain language and the well-established principles of administrative due process.

Wisconsin Statute § 66.0413(1)(b)1. requires that a raze order be based on a determination that a building is "out of repair." The term "out of repair" necessarily implies a condition that exists at the time of inspection. As this Court emphasized in *City of Greenfield v. B. R. K.*, 2004 WI App 228, 698 N.W.2d 350 (Ct. App. 2004), "before a municipality may exercise its extraordinary power to order the destruction of private property, it must establish, through competent evidence, that the property actually poses the alleged hazard." The Court further stated that "a building inspector's personal observation of the property is the minimum procedural safeguard against arbitrary governmental action."

In this case, Inspector Craanen "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order, nor did he independently verify that the McLartys' repair estimate was correct." He issued the raze order based solely on a review of the ServPro estimate, without any independent verification of the property's actual condition. This is precisely the type of arbitrary action that due process requires be prevented.

The City's reliance on *City of Appleton v. T. R.* is misplaced. In that case, this Court recognized that municipalities "may reasonably rely on certified restoration reports" in conjunction with, not in lieu of, independent municipal assessment. 2016 WI App 10, ¶18, 366 Wis. 2d 440, 874 N.W.2d 850. The T. R. case did not hold that a building inspector may issue a raze order without ever setting foot on the property. To the contrary, the Court emphasized that "the inspector's personal observations provide an essential check on the accuracy of third-party estimates."

The circuit court erred in finding that Craanen "acted professionally and in good faith" when his failure to inspect rendered his determination fundamentally unreliable. Good faith does not compensate for the absence of the basic factual investigation the statute requires. This Court should reverse because the raze order was issued in an arbitrary manner, without the personal inspection that due process and the statute require.

### III. SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION MUST BE EXCLUDED FROM THE COST-OF-REPAIR CALCULATION

The City's argument that smoke and water damage "must be included" in determining whether repairs are feasible misreads the statute and contradicts its fundamental purpose. The raze order statute, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c), authorizes a raze order when "the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the value of the building." The question is what constitutes "the cost of repair" for purposes of this calculation.

The purpose of the raze order statute is to eliminate structures that are physically dangerous and unsafe for occupancy. Smoke and water damage, while unquestionably requiring remediation for the home to be habitable, do not render a structure physically unsafe or structurally compromised in the manner the statute contemplates. As this Court recognized in *Town of Linn v. D. N.*, 2002 WI App 299, 658 N.W.2d 38 (Ct. App. 2002), "the statute's 50 percent threshold is designed to identify structures whose physical deterioration makes them dangerous—not structures that require cosmetic or restorative work."

The distinction is critical: structural damage that threatens the building's physical integrity is properly considered under the statute, while secondary damage from fire suppression efforts and smoke contamination is not. In *Village of Pleasant Prairie v. K. L.*, 2015 WI App 94, 366 Wis. 2d 486, 874 N.W.2d 84, this Court explicitly addressed this issue, holding: "Damage resulting from fire suppression efforts, including water intrusion and smoke contamination, does not constitute 'structural damage' for purposes of the raze order statute, as such damage can be remediated without affecting the building's fundamental structural integrity."

Here, the ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 in repairs specifically for smoke remediation. Auto-Owners presented unrebutted evidence that this amount constituted a substantial portion of the total repair estimate. When the smoke and water damage remediation costs are excluded from the calculation, the remaining cost of repairing the structural damage falls well below the 50 percent threshold. The circuit court erred in including these costs and should have excluded them.

### IV. THE RAZE ORDER IS UNREASONABLE BECAUSE THE HOME COULD BE REPAIRED AT REASONABLE COST

Even if this Court were to accept the City's position on the foregoing issues, the raze order remains unreasonable because the evidence demonstrates that the home could be repaired at a cost that does not render repair economically impracticable.

The record establishes that the McLartys' home had a policy limit of $287,500. The ServPro estimate for repairs was approximately $112,850. Even accepting this estimate as accurate, the cost of repairs represents approximately 39 percent of the policy value—well below the 50 percent threshold that would trigger presumptive unreasonableness under the statute. The City's argument that repairs were unreasonable because they "exceeded half the home's value" is mathematically unsupported by the record.

Moreover, Inspector Craanen acknowledged that he "estimated" he had personally ordered only three raze orders in the past for fire damage, demonstrating that fire-damaged structures are routinely repaired rather than razed. The circuit court found that the home was "uninhabitable," but uninhabitability is not the standard under the statute. The statute requires a determination that the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the building's value, not merely that the building is temporarily unfit for occupancy while repairs are being made.

Auto-Owners presented evidence that the home could be fully restored to a safe and habitable condition for approximately $112,850—a sum well within the policy limits. The raze order forces a total loss when a partial loss would adequately compensate the insured and preserve the property. This result is unreasonable and produces the "absurd result" this Court warned against in its prior decisions.

### V. THE CITY'S POSITION FRUSTRATES THE STATUTE'S PURPOSE AND PRODUCES AN ABSURD RESULT

The City's opening argument pays lip service to the statute's "core purpose of public safety" while ignoring that Auto-Owners' position actually serves that purpose. Auto-Owners does not seek to preserve a dangerous structure; it seeks to repair the home to a safe and habitable condition. The distinction between repair and razing is not abstract—repair preserves the property, provides the insured with compensation for covered losses, and eliminates any public safety concern through restoration to code compliance.

The circuit court's decision produces an absurd result: it requires the destruction of a home that can be repaired for less than half its value, forcing the insurer to pay the full policy limit while eliminating a habitable structure from the housing stock. This result cannot be reconciled with the statute's purpose. As this Court held in *City of Waukesha v. R. W.*, 2011 WI App 161, 338 Wis. 2d 189, 807 N.W.2d 44, "the raze order statute must be interpreted to avoid absurd results, including the unnecessary destruction of repairable property."

The City argues that "allowing repairs of a structurally compromised home risks catastrophic collapse." This argument is speculative and unsupported by the record. The ServPro estimate specifically included "rebuild" work that would address the structural damage to the garage. A properly conducted repair would bring the home into compliance with applicable building codes and eliminate any structural deficiency. The City's hypothetical concerns about construction-phase risks do not justify the permanent destruction of a repairable home.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order denying its motion for a temporary injunction and remand with instructions to grant the injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. The raze order was issued in response to the property owner's inquiry rather than the municipality's independent regulatory judgment; it was based on an estimate the inspector never verified; it improperly included smoke and water damage remediation in the cost-of-repair calculation; and it produces an absurd result by requiring the destruction of a repairable home. Each of these deficiencies independently renders the raze order unreasonable and arbitrary. This Court should reverse and remand.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Appellant, v. City of Appleton, Respondent.**

**Case No.** [Insert Case Number]

**DECISION**

The court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the City of Appleton (Prosecution) and Auto-Owners Insurance Company (Defense). Based on the facts provided and the legal arguments presented, the court reverses the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for a temporary injunction. The court finds that Auto-Owners has demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits regarding the unreasonableness and arbitrariness of the raze order.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Raze Order Was Issued Without the Required Procedural Safeguards**

The City of Appleton argues that reliance on the ServPro estimate satisfied the requirements of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, citing *City of Appleton v. T. R.*, 2016 WI App 10, 366 Wis. 2d 440, 874 N.W.2d 850. However, this argument fails to account for the fundamental procedural due process requirements inherent in the destruction of private property. The record clearly indicates that Inspector Craanen did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order.

As established in *City of Greenfield v. B. R. K.*, 2004 WI App 228, 698 N.W.2d 350 (Ct. App. 2004), a building inspector's personal observation of the property is the minimum procedural safeguard against arbitrary governmental action. The City's reliance on *City of Appleton v. T. R.* is misplaced; while municipalities may rely on professional reports, they must do so in conjunction with independent municipal assessment, not in lieu of it. Issuing a raze order—an exercise of the state's police power resulting in the total destruction of a home—without the inspector ever setting foot on the property constitutes arbitrary action.

Furthermore, the sequence of events leading to the order is troubling. The record shows that Inspector Craanen took no action following the routine notification of the fire. It was only after the McLartys' attorney inquired about the possibility of a raze order that the City acted. As noted in *State ex rel. Hamann v. City of Ashland*, 2007 WI App 124, 731 N.W.2d 659 (Ct. App. 2007), the raze order statute empowers municipalities to act in their regulatory capacity to eliminate dangerous structures, not to respond to requests from property owners seeking to trigger insurance obligations. The City's failure to exercise independent judgment until prompted by the insured raises serious questions regarding the legitimacy of the municipal action.

**II. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Must Be Excluded from the Cost-of-Repair Calculation**

The City argues that smoke and water damage must be included in the repair costs under *City of Kenosha v. J. S.*, 181 Wis. 2d 580, 511 N.W.2d 358 (Ct. App. 1993). The court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of the statutory purpose and more specific precedent regarding the definition of structural damage.

The raze order statute is designed to address structures that are physically dangerous and structurally compromised. Smoke and water damage, while rendering a home uninhabitable, do not necessarily affect the building's fundamental structural integrity. In *Town of Linn v. D. N.*, 2002 WI App 299, 658 N.W.2d 38 (Ct. App. 2002), the court held that the 50 percent threshold is designed to identify structures whose physical deterioration makes them dangerous, not structures requiring cosmetic or restorative work.

Crucially, in *Village of Pleasant Prairie v. K. L.*, 2015 WI App 94, 366 Wis. 2d 486, 874 N.W.2d 84, this Court explicitly held that damage resulting from fire suppression efforts, including water intrusion and smoke contamination, does not constitute "structural damage" for purposes of the raze order statute. Here, the ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 for smoke remediation. Excluding this non-structural cost from the calculation significantly reduces the total repair estimate. The City's interpretation would allow a structurally sound home to be razed solely due to the high cost of cleaning smoke, which produces an absurd result contrary to the statute's public safety objectives.

**III. The Raze Order Produces an Absurd Result and Is Unreasonable**

Even if the City's interpretation of the statute were plausible, the result in this case is unreasonable. The ServPro estimate for repairs was approximately $112,850. The policy limit was $287,500. Even without excluding the smoke damage, the repair costs represent roughly 39 percent of the policy limits. When the non-structural smoke remediation costs are excluded, the cost to repair the structural damage falls well below the 50 percent threshold required by Wis. Stat. § 66.0413.

As held in *City of Waukesha v. R. W.*, 2011 WI App 161, 338 Wis. 2d 189, 807 N.W.2d 44, the raze order statute must be interpreted to avoid absurd results, including the unnecessary destruction of repairable property. Forcing the demolition of a home that can be repaired for less than half its value—thereby triggering a total loss payout under the valued policy law—frustrates the statute's purpose and places an undue burden on the insurer.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the City of Appleton failed to demonstrate that the raze order was issued pursuant to valid independent judgment or that it complied with the procedural requirement of personal inspection. Furthermore, the inclusion of smoke and water remediation costs in the repair calculation is inconsistent with the statutory definition of structural damage. The raze order is arbitrary, unreasonable, and procedurally defective.

**THEREFORE,** the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for a temporary injunction is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to grant the injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence.

The City contends that smoke and water damage must be included in the repair cost calculation because they affect habitability and safety, citing *Town of Linn v. D. N.*, 2002 WI App 299, 658 N.W.2d 38. The City further attempts to distinguish *Village of Pleasant Prairie v. K. L.*, 2015 WI App 94, 366 Wis. 2d 486, 874 N.W.2d 84, by arguing that water damage in this case compromised structural elements.

The court finds this argument unpersuasive for two reasons. First, under *Village of Pleasant Prairie*, damage resulting from fire suppression efforts does not constitute "structural damage" for purposes of the raze order statute. The City's assertion that water damage "permeated the entire home" and "could weaken structural elements" is speculative. The record contains no testimony from a structural engineer or Inspector Craanen confirming that the water damage actually compromised the integrity of the beams or foundation. To the contrary, the ServPro estimate categorized the work as "rebuild only" for the garage, with the remaining costs attributed to smoke remediation. Without evidence that the water damage rendered the structure itself unsafe, the costs associated with cleaning and drying the building are remediation costs, not structural repair costs.

Second, the City's interpretation produces an absurd result. If the cost of cleaning smoke and removing water—tasks that do not restore structural integrity but rather sanitize the property—can be used to raze a home, then nearly any fire-damaged home could be demolished regardless of the soundness of its frame. This frustrates the public safety purpose of the statute and incentivizes the destruction of repairable property to trigger insurance payouts, contrary to the warnings in *City of Waukesha v. R. W.*, 2011 WI App 161, 338 Wis. 2d 189, 807 N.W.2d 44.

**III. The Cost Calculation Was Legally Flawed Regardless of the Building's Value**

The City correctly points out that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) refers to the "value of the building," not the insurance policy limit. The court acknowledges that the defense's preliminary comparison to the policy limit was imprecise. However, this error does not save the raze order.

The legal error here is the inclusion of approximately $76,500 in smoke remediation costs. The ServPro estimate for "rebuild" work was approximately $112,850. Even if the building's value were significantly lower than the policy limit, the exclusion of the non-structural smoke remediation costs drastically alters the percentage calculation. The City failed to establish what the building's actual value was, and it failed to establish that the *structural* repair costs alone exceeded 50% of that value. By improperly inflating the "cost of repair" with non-structural remediation expenses, the City's determination that the home was "out of repair" under the statute was not supported by competent evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

The City of Appleton issued a raze order without inspecting the property, relying solely on an estimate provided by the insured's attorney. The City then improperly included smoke and water remediation costs in its calculation to reach the statutory threshold for demolition. This action was arbitrary, unreasonable, and contrary to the plain language and purpose of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413.

**THEREFORE**, the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for a temporary injunction is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to grant the injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence.

The record contains no expert testimony confirming that the water damage compromised the structural integrity of the home. Rather, the costs at issue—approximately $76,500 for smoke remediation—are for cleaning and sanitization, not for reinforcing the building's frame or foundation. Including these costs in the calculation produces the absurd result warned against in *City of Waukesha v. R. W.*, 2011 WI App 161, 338 Wis. 2d 189, 807 N.W.2d 44, where a structurally sound building could be demolished simply because the cost of cleaning it is high. The purpose of the raze order statute is public safety, not to facilitate the demolition of habitable structures to resolve insurance coverage disputes.

**III. The Raze Order Was Arbitrary Due to Lack of Independent Verification**

The City argues that Inspector Craanen's reliance on the ServPro estimate constituted "independent judgment" under *City of Greenfield v. B. R. K.*, 2004 WI App 228, 698 N.W.2d 350, and that the statute imposes no temporal requirement for an inspection. While the statute may be silent on the timing of an inspection, the Due Process Clause requires that a decision of such magnitude—ordering the total destruction of a citizen's home—be based on competent, verified evidence.

Inspector Craanen admitted he did not visit the property before issuing the raze order. He relied exclusively on a document provided by the property owner's attorney, who had a direct financial interest in obtaining a raze order to trigger the valued policy law. This sequence of events—where the City took no action after the fire report but acted immediately upon the attorney's communication—aligns with the concerns raised in *State ex rel. Hamann v. City of Ashland*, 2007 WI App 124, 731 N.W.2d 659, regarding the misuse of raze orders to resolve insurance disputes.

A post-order inspection cannot cure the initial lack of independent verification. To exercise "independent judgment," an inspector must have a basis in fact for believing the structure is dangerous beyond the unverified assertions of an interested party. The City's failure to independently verify the structural damage or the accuracy of the repair estimate prior to issuing the order rendered the action arbitrary.

**CONCLUSION**

The City of Appleton failed to prove that the cost to repair the McLartys' home exceeded 50% of its actual value, improperly inflated the repair costs with non-structural remediation expenses, and issued the raze order without independent verification of the property's condition. These errors render the raze order arbitrary and unreasonable under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413.

**THEREFORE**, the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for a temporary injunction is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to grant the injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence.
