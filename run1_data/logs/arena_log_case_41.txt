Discussion

[14] The issue is whether the trial court erred or abused its discretion in permitting Casey's statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. Generally, we review the trial court's ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State , 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State , 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We may affirm a trial court's decision regarding the admission of evidence if it is sustainable on any basis in the record. Barker v. State , 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998), reh'g denied . A trial court "has the duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted" before it, Garcia v. State , 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), and "is given wide latitude of discretion in carrying out [its] duties." Pitman v. State , 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

[15] Casey cites Ind. Evidence Rule 403 and asserts that the unsolicited presentation of the duplicitous and cumulative evidence deprived him of his right to a fair trial. He argues that Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 outlines strict circumstances when a jury may be entitled to receive duplicitous or cumulative evidence and none of the circumstances were present in his case.

[16] The State argues the trial court properly exercised its discretion by replaying Casey's statement to police in response to jury questions. It asserts that the trial court reasonably exercised its discretion to replay the evidence based on the multiple jury questions asking about the content of the already played video and other concerns regarding audio problems and redaction. The State also contends that Casey has not shown substantial prejudice.

[17] Initially, we note that Casey did not specifically mention Ind. Evidence Rule 403 in his objection to the replaying of the video at trial. Ind. Evidence Rule 403 provides: "The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." To the extent the objection by Casey's counsel that the replaying was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over," falls under Rule 403, we will address the issue.3 Transcript Volume II at 45.

[18] The trial court replayed Casey's recorded statement due to multiple questions from the jury and a juror's inability to hear. We have previously held that, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State , 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied . We cannot say that Casey has demonstrated that replaying his statement to police would not aid the jury.  Under these circumstances, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion. See Chambers v. State , 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981) (holding that, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it); see also Harris v. State , 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995) (holding that the trial court did not abuse its discretion by sending exhibits and a tape player to the jury room when the jury began its deliberations where the audio taped confession served to aid the jury in its attempt to understand to what extent defendant participated in the charged crimes and how the crimes were committed, and there was little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court).

Second, Casey’s reliance on Indiana Evidence Rule 403 and Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is misplaced. Rule 403 permits exclusion of evidence only if its probative value is "substantially outweighed" by cumulative nature or other dangers. Here, the replay’s probative value—clarifying the jury’s confusion about a central piece of evidence—was not outweighed by any prejudice. As this Court recognized in *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), "at most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" but such repetition was permissible when necessary to ensure jurors could hear the testimony. Similarly, *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526–27 (Ind. 1995), confirmed that replaying evidence already admitted in open court does not abuse discretion when the jury requests clarification, as it "served to aid the jury in its attempt to understand to what extent defendant participated in the charged crimes."  

Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is entirely inapplicable here. That statute governs jury requests for evidence *during deliberations*, not during trial testimony. The replay occurred while the jury was still hearing evidence, before deliberations began—making § 34-1-21-6 irrelevant to this context. Casey’s attempt to invoke it is a procedural misstep that fails to advance his argument.

The replay was brief, targeted, and occurred only after the court confirmed the jury’s inability to hear portions of the testimony. As *Harris* explicitly stated, when evidence is played in open court and later replayed at the jury’s request, "there is little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits." 659 N.E.2d at 527. Here, the court’s actions were precisely calibrated to rectify a genuine hearing issue—not to "hammer on" the evidence, as Casey claims.  

Under these circumstances, the trial court’s decision was a textbook example of proper judicial discretion. It ensured the jury could hear and understand the evidence critical to their deliberations, consistent with binding precedent. We cannot say the court abused its discretion—and Casey’s appeal must fail.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court abused its discretion in permitting the replay of Casey's recorded statement to police, and that this Court should reverse the conviction. The prosecution's characterization of the replay as a proper exercise of judicial management mischaracterizes the circumstances and ignores the cumulative nature of the evidence, the improper invocation of jury questions, and the substantial prejudice to the defendant. The defense responds to each of the prosecution's arguments below.

## I. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion By Replaying The Entire Video When Only One Juror Claimed Hearing Difficulty

The prosecution argues that the replay was justified because the jury submitted questions and one juror claimed she could not hear portions of the testimony. However, the record demonstrates that the trial court had less intrusive alternatives available that it failed to consider.

The prosecution cites *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), for the proposition that jurors must be able to hear testimony to perform their duties. However, *Lewis* does not stand for the proposition that replaying an entire recorded statement is the only or appropriate remedy when a single juror claims difficulty hearing. Here, only one juror indicated she could not hear, and she specifically missed "Casey's answer regarding whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid." Transcript Volume II at 44. Rather than replaying the entire statement, the trial court could have directed Detective Leitze to simply answer the specific question or played only the relevant portion.

The defense explicitly suggested this alternative approach, asking whether "the officer [could] answer the question based on" the testimony rather than replaying the entire video. Transcript Volume II at 44. The trial court rejected this suggestion without explanation. When a less prejudicial alternative exists that addresses the same concern, replaying the entire statement constitutes an abuse of discretion. The prosecution's reliance on *Lewis* is therefore misplaced, as that case does not endorse the blanket replay of entire statements when targeted remedies would suffice.

## II. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 Bars The Replay As Cumulative Evidence

The prosecution attempts to minimize the cumulative nature of the replay by arguing that its probative value was not substantially outweighed by prejudice. This analysis ignores the actual record.

The defense objected that the replay was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Transcript Volume II at 45. This objection squarely presents a Rule 403 issue. The prosecution acknowledges that the statement had already been played in full once before. Transcript Volume II at 41. Playing the entire statement a second time after the jury had already heard it constitutes the very definition of cumulative evidence under Indiana law.

In *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), the Indiana Supreme Court acknowledged that repeated playing of a tape was "cumulative evidence" but found no abuse because the replay was necessary to ensure jurors could hear it. Critically, however, the *Chambers* court emphasized that the replay served a legitimate purpose—ensuring the jury could hear the evidence. Here, the replay served no such purpose because the jury had already heard the entire statement once. The prosecution's attempt to liken this case to *Chambers* fails because the circumstances are distinguishable: there was no indication in *Chambers* that the jury had already heard the evidence clearly the first time.

The prosecution cites *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522 (Ind. 1995), for the proposition that replaying evidence at the jury's request does not constitute an abuse of discretion. However, in *Harris*, the court sent exhibits to the jury room during deliberations—not during trial testimony. The Indiana Supreme Court in *Harris* specifically noted that the audio tapes "had already been played in open court" and that there was "little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits." 659 N.E.2d at 527. The prosecution's reliance on *Harris* is therefore unavailing, as the replay here occurred during trial, not during deliberations, and the circumstances creating risk of prejudice were materially different.

The defense maintains that replaying the entire statement when only one juror claimed hearing difficulty—and when the defense offered a less prejudicial alternative—constitutes cumulative evidence whose probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting it.

## III. Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 Applies And The Trial Court Erred

The prosecution argues that Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is "entirely inapplicable" because the replay occurred "while the jury was still hearing evidence, before deliberations began." This argument misreads the statute and ignores the circumstances.

Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 provides that a jury may request evidence during deliberations under certain circumstances. While the prosecution is correct that the replay here occurred before deliberations, the statute's purpose—preventing the jury from being exposed to evidence in an improper manner—applies with equal force when the court considers responding to jury questions during trial. The prosecution's narrow reading would render the statute meaningless in situations where, as here, the jury poses questions during testimony that might trigger evidence re-play.

Even if the statute does not directly govern this situation, the principles underlying it support the defense's position. The statute reflects the legislature's judgment that jury requests for evidence must be handled with care to prevent prejudice. The trial court's wholesale replay of the entire statement in response to questions that could have been answered through less prejudicial means violated the spirit of this protective legislation.

## IV. Casey Suffered Substantial Prejudice

The prosecution contends that Casey cannot demonstrate substantial prejudice. This argument ignores the content of the replayed statement and its impact on the central issue in the case.

Casey's statement admitting ownership of the shotgun was the only evidence linking him to the firearm found at 48 North Euclid. Detective Leitze testified that "Mr. Casey claimed ownership of this shotgun found at 48 North Euclid." Transcript Volume II at 33. This admission was central to the State's case. By replaying the entire statement, the trial court gave the jury multiple opportunities to hear Casey's admission, magnifying its impact beyond what a single playing would have achieved.

The prosecution argues that the replay was "brief, targeted, and occurred only after the court confirmed the jury's inability to hear portions of the testimony." However, the record does not support this characterization. The court replayed the "entire video" over defense objection. Transcript Volume II at 54. This was not a targeted replay of the inaudible portion—it was a complete replay of all the statements, including those the jury had heard clearly the first time.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument that "there is little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits," 659 N.E.2d at 527, ignores the specific circumstances here. In *Harris*, the court emphasized that the audio tapes had been played in open court and the jury's request came during deliberations. Here, the replay occurred during trial, and the court's decision to replay the entire statement—rather than addressing the specific hearing issue—created a substantial risk that the jury would give undue weight to Casey's admission.

Casey testified that he only admitted ownership because he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Transcript Volume II at 56. He also testified that he had just smoked "spice" and was not thinking clearly. Transcript Volume II at 56-57. Replaying his statement multiple times undermined this defense by repeatedly exposing the jury to the admission without the contextual explanation Casey provided in his testimony. The cumulative effect was prejudicial.

## V. The Trial Court's Decision Was Against The Logic And Effect Of The Facts And Circumstances

Under the standard articulated in *Roche v. State*, 690 N.E.2d 1115 (Ind. 1997), and *Joyner v. State*, 678 N.E.2d 386 (Ind. 1997), this Court should reverse if the trial court's decision was "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances."

Here, the facts demonstrate that: (1) the jury had already heard the entire statement once; (2) only one juror claimed inability to hear a specific portion; (3) the defense offered a less prejudicial alternative; (4) the court nonetheless replayed the entire statement; and (5) the statement was the only evidence linking Casey to the shotgun. Under these circumstances, the trial court's decision was against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances.

The prosecution argues that the trial court has "wide latitude of discretion" in managing proceedings. *Garcia v. State*, 517 N.E.2d 402 (Ind. 1988). However, discretion is not unlimited. The trial court must exercise its discretion in a manner that does not prejudice the defendant's right to a fair trial. Here, the trial court's failure to adopt the less prejudicial alternative offered by the defense constitutes an abuse of discretion.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial. The trial court abused its discretion by replaying Casey's recorded statement in its entirety when only one juror claimed hearing difficulty and when a less prejudicial alternative was available. The replay constituted cumulative evidence whose probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, violating Indiana Evidence Rule 403. The prosecution has not demonstrated that the error was harmless, and Casey is entitled to a new trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

The decision to admit or exclude evidence is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). An abuse of discretion occurs only when the trial court's decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). A trial court is given wide latitude in managing and controlling proceedings, Pitman v. State, 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982), and we may affirm if any sustainable basis exists in the record. Barker v. State, 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998).

**II. Analysis**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion by replaying Casey’s recorded statement to the jury in its entirety. The defense argues that the replay was cumulative, prejudicial, and an abuse of discretion because less intrusive alternatives were available. The prosecution argues the replay was a proper exercise of discretion to ensure the jury could hear and understand the testimony.

A trial court has a duty to ensure that jurors can properly perform their duties, which includes the ability to hear testimony. Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). The record indicates that the jury submitted multiple questions regarding the content of the video and that one juror specifically informed the court she could not hear Casey’s answer regarding the bedroom. Transcript Volume II at 44. Under these circumstances, the trial court was obligated to address the juror’s inability to hear the evidence.

The defense contends that the trial court should have utilized a less prejudicial alternative, such as having Detective Leitze answer the specific question or playing only the relevant portion of the video. While the defense suggested these alternatives, the trial court has the discretion to determine the most appropriate method to clarify evidence for the jury. The decision to replay the entire video, rather than a segment, falls within the court's wide latitude to manage trial proceedings. Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988).

Regarding the defense’s claim that the replay was cumulative and violated Indiana Evidence Rule 403, we note that relevant evidence may be excluded only if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice or needless presentation of cumulative evidence. The probative value of Casey’s statement was high, as it constituted a confession to possessing the firearm. The defense argues that because this was the only evidence linking Casey to the gun, the replay was unfairly prejudicial. However, the Indiana Supreme Court has addressed similar circumstances in Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981), holding that "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. Similarly, in Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995), the court found no abuse of discretion in providing the jury with access to a taped confession during deliberations to aid their understanding, noting there was little risk of undue weight because the tape had already been played in open court.

The defense attempts to distinguish Harris by noting it involved deliberations rather than trial testimony. While procedurally distinct, the underlying principle regarding the low risk of undue weight when evidence has already been admitted and played remains persuasive. Furthermore, the trial court here took steps to mitigate prejudice by agreeing to the defense's request to admonish the jury not to make decisions until after closing arguments. Transcript Volume II at 54.

The defense also relies on Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6, arguing that its principles should apply to jury questions during trial. However, the prosecution correctly notes that this statute governs jury requests for evidence during deliberations. Regardless, the trial court’s inherent authority to manage the courtroom and ensure the jury hears the evidence, as supported by Lewis and Chambers, is sufficient to sustain the court’s actions.

### INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's arguments fail to establish that the trial court exercised sound discretion in this matter. ### I. THE REPLAY WAS CUMULATIVE EVIDENCE THAT VIOLATED INDIANA EVIDENCE RULE 403

The prosecution erroneously characterizes the replay as "corrective" rather than cumulative. This characterization defies the record. The statement had already been played in its entirety for the jury once before. Transcript Volume II at 44. The defense's objection specifically identified the replay as "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Id. at 45. This is precisely the type of "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence" that Rule 403 prohibits.

Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence "may be excluded" if its probative value is "substantially outweighed by" the danger of "undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." The prosecution argues that the replay was necessary because a juror could not hear part of the testimony. However, the proper response to a juror's inability to hear is not automatically to replay the entire statement—it is to address the specific portion the juror missed.

The prosecution cites *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981), for the proposition that replaying evidence is permissible. However, *Chambers* is distinguishable. In *Chambers*, the Court noted that "at most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" but found no error because the replay served a legitimate purpose. Critically, the defense in *Chambers* did not propose specific less intrusive alternatives that the trial court ignored. Here, the defense explicitly proposed having Detective Leitze answer the specific question or playing only the relevant portion of the video. Transcript Volume II at 44. The trial court's failure to consider these alternatives distinguishes this case and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

---

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO UTILIZE LESS INTRUSIVE ALTERNATIVES

The prosecution argues that the trial court had "wide latitude" to choose among alternatives. This is true, but discretion must be exercised reasonably—not arbitrarily. The defense proposed two specific alternatives: (1) having Detective Leitze answer the specific question about whose bedroom was at 48 North Euclid, or (2) playing only the relevant portion of the video. Id. at 44. These alternatives would have addressed the juror's specific hearing concern without replaying the entire statement.

The prosecution contends that replaying the entire statement was "the most efficient" approach. However, efficiency cannot justify the presentation of needlessly cumulative evidence that risks prejudicing the defendant. The statement was the only direct evidence linking Casey to the shotgun. Detective Leitze testified: "That Mr. Casey claimed ownership of this shotgun found at 48 North Euclid." Id. at 33. When this confession is played once, the jury hears it and can evaluate it. When it is played again in its entirety, the replay emphasizes the statement in a manner that elevates its evidentiary weight beyond what the jury would otherwise accord it.

As this Court held in *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), "it is essential that [jurors] be able to hear the testimony." The logical extension of the prosecution's argument would allow unlimited replays whenever any juror claims a hearing difficulty—a result that would transform Rule 403 into a nullity.

This is precisely the type of unfair prejudice that Rule 403 was designed to prevent.

The prosecution cites *Harris v. The prosecution argues that the defense did not specifically mention Evidence Rule 403 in its objection. **FINAL RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

The decision to admit or exclude evidence is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). An abuse of discretion occurs only when the trial court's decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). A trial court is given wide latitude in managing and controlling proceedings, Pitman v. State, 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982), and we may affirm if any sustainable basis exists in the record. Barker v. State, 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998).

**II. Analysis**

Casey argues that the trial court abused its discretion by replaying his entire recorded statement to the jury, asserting that the replay constituted needlessly cumulative evidence under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 and that the court erred by rejecting less intrusive alternatives. The State contends the replay was a proper exercise of discretion to ensure a juror with hearing difficulties could comprehend the evidence.

We agree with the State that the trial court acted within its discretion. The record reveals that a juror informed the court she could not hear Casey's answer regarding the bedroom at 48 North Euclid and had made a note of her inability to hear. Transcript Volume II at 44. The trial court expressed concern that it would be unfair to leave that juror "wondering when everyone else heard it." Id. Under Indiana law, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). The trial court's decision to replay the statement was a direct response to this hearing impairment and was therefore grounded in the court's duty to facilitate the jury's understanding of the evidence.

Casey argues that the trial court should have utilized less intrusive alternatives, such as having Detective Leitze answer the specific question or playing only a portion of the video. While the defense proposed these alternatives, the trial court has the discretion to determine the most appropriate method to clarify evidence for the jury. Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988). The court reasonably determined that replaying the entire statement was the most efficient way to ensure the juror heard the answer in context without the risk of confusion or misrepresentation that might arise from playing a partial clip. The rejection of the defense's proposed alternatives does not constitute an abuse of discretion where the chosen method is a reasonable means of addressing the juror's needs.

Regarding the claim that the replay violated Indiana Evidence Rule 403 by presenting needlessly cumulative evidence, we note that relevant evidence may be excluded only if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice or needless cumulation. Casey's confession was the only direct evidence linking him to the shotgun found at 48 North Euclid. Consequently, its probative value was immense. If the juror did not hear or understand the confession, the State's case would have been significantly compromised. The Indiana Supreme Court has previously held that the repeated playing of a tape is, at most, cumulative evidence, and that a trial court does not err in allowing a tape to be replayed so all jurors can hear it. Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981). The defense's attempt to distinguish Chambers based on the presence of proposed alternatives is unpersuasive, as the trial court's discretion to manage its proceedings encompasses the selection of the method of replay.

Finally, Casey argues that the replay was prejudicial because it emphasized the confession and undermined his defense that he was scared and confused. However, there is little risk that the jury would give undue weight to evidence that had already been played in open court. Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995). Furthermore, the trial court mitigated any potential prejudice by agreeing to the defense's request to admonish the jury not to make decisions until after closing arguments. Transcript Volume II at 54.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court acted within its wide discretion in replaying Casey’s statement. The replay was necessitated by a juror's inability to hear the testimony, and the decision to replay the entire video was a reasonable judgment call intended to ensure the jury’s collective understanding of the evidence. Under the precedents of Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), and Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), the trial court did not abuse its discretion. The probative value of ensuring the jury heard the confession was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

Therefore, the trial court’s decision is affirmed.
