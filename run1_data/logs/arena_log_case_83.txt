**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR THE BOARD**  

May it please the Court:  

The Utah Labor Commission Appeals Board did not err in dismissing Frito-Lay’s motion for relief under Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. To the contrary, the Board correctly applied the law by recognizing that Rule 60(b) has no place in agency proceedings governed by the Utah Administrative Procedures Act ("UAPA"). This Court must affirm the Board’s decision because UAPA establishes the exclusive framework for reviewing administrative decisions, and Frito-Lay’s attempt to circumvent UAPA’s statutory deadlines through a Rule 60(b) motion is a clear violation of fundamental procedural principles.  

### I. UAPA Governs Agency Proceedings, and Rule 60(b) Is Inapplicable  
The Utah Legislature has spoken clearly: agency decisions are governed solely by UAPA, not by the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. UAPA’s provisions for judicial review are specific, mandatory, and designed to ensure finality in administrative proceedings. Section 63-46b-16 of the Utah Code explicitly requires that any party seeking review of an Administrative Law Judge’s ("ALJ’s") decision must file a request for review within thirty days of the order’s entry. This deadline is not discretionary; it is a statutory imperative.  

Rule 60(b), by contrast, is a procedural tool designed for civil litigation in district courts. It addresses extraordinary circumstances such as fraud, mistake, or newly discovered evidence—but only in the context of final judgments rendered by judicial tribunals. The legislature did not incorporate Rule 60(b) into UAPA, nor did it authorize its use in agency proceedings. To hold otherwise would inject chaos into the administrative process. If parties could ignore UAPA’s thirty-day deadline and instead rely on Rule 60(b) months later, the entire statutory review scheme would collapse. Finality would be illusory, and agencies would face endless litigation over decisions that should be settled. The Board correctly recognized this: Rule 60(b) is simply not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings because UAPA provides the *only* path for challenging an ALJ’s order.  

### II. Frito-Lay’s Motion Was an Improper Substitute for a Timely Appeal  
Frito-Lay’s failure to act within UAPA’s thirty-day window is fatal to its claim. The September 2005 Order was entered on September 28, 2005. Under UAPA, Frito-Lay had until October 28, 2005, to seek review of that order. Instead, it waited ninety days—until December 21, 2005—to file its Rule 60(b) motion. This delay was not excusable; it was a deliberate choice to bypass the statutory review mechanism.  

The record confirms Frito-Lay knew of the alleged "error" immediately. The ALJ’s September 2005 Order explicitly referenced the parties’ prior Stipulation, which documented Clausing’s work history and wages between March 1999 and June 2004. Frito-Lay’s own counsel was fully aware that Clausing’s demand for $183,561.85 in temporary total disability benefits contradicted the Stipulation—because the Stipulation itself showed Clausing had worked during periods she claimed to be disabled. Frito-Lay did not need "discovery" to understand this; it had the Stipulation in hand when the September 2005 Order was issued.  

By waiting three months to file its motion, Frito-Lay sought to use Rule 60(b) as a backdoor appeal—exactly the type of procedural evasion UAPA was designed to prevent. The Board was correct to dismiss the motion not on the merits of the alleged error, but on the procedural ground that Rule 60(b) is unavailable in agency proceedings. To permit such motions would undermine the Legislature’s clear intent to limit judicial review of agency decisions to the statutory timeframe.  

### III. The Discovery Rule Has No Application Here  
Frito-Lay’s invocation of the discovery rule is equally misplaced. The discovery rule applies only when a party could not have discovered an error despite due diligence—such as when fraud conceals a claim or new evidence emerges after a deadline. But here, there was no hidden fact, no concealment, and no new evidence. The alleged "mistake" in the September 2005 Order was apparent on its face. The Stipulation was part of the record before the ALJ, and Frito-Lay’s counsel acknowledged as much in its own affidavit.  

The discovery rule does not excuse a party’s failure to follow mandatory statutory deadlines when the basis for the claim was known at the time of the order. Had Frito-Lay truly believed the September 2005 Order was inconsistent with the Stipulation, it could have—and *should have*—filed a timely appeal within thirty days. Its failure to do so cannot be cured by retroactively applying the discovery rule, which has never been extended to administrative proceedings under UAPA.  

### Conclusion  
The Board’s dismissal of Frito-Lay’s motion was a correct application of the law. UAPA governs all agency proceedings, and its thirty-day review deadline is absolute. Rule 60(b) has no role in this context, and the discovery rule cannot salvage Frito-Lay’s untimely attempt to relitigate a settled order. To rule otherwise would disrupt the carefully crafted balance between judicial oversight and administrative finality that the Legislature intended. We urge this Court to affirm the Board’s decision and uphold the integrity of Utah’s administrative law system.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE FOR FRITO-LAY AND TRANSCONTINENTAL INSURANCE COMPANY

## May it Please the Court:

Frito-Lay and Transcontinental Insurance Company (collectively, "Frito-Lay") respectfully submit this opening response in opposition to the Utah Labor Commission Appeals Board's (the "Board") dismissal of Frito-Lay's motion for relief under Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. The Board erred as a matter of law in holding that Rule 60(b) is categorically unavailable in Labor Commission proceedings. Even assuming arguendo that the Utah Administrative Procedures Act ("UAPA") provides the primary framework for agency review, the Board incorrectly applied the law by failing to recognize that Rule 60(b) may supplement UAPA's provisions where extraordinary circumstances warrant relief from an order that may produce manifest injustice. This Court should reverse the Board's decision and remand for consideration of the merits of Frito-Lay's Rule 60(b) motion.

---

## I. THE BOARD ERRED IN HOLDING THAT RULE 60(b) IS INAPPLICABLE TO AGENCY PROCEEDINGS

The Board's central holding—that Rule 60(b) has no place in agency proceedings governed by UAPA—represents a fundamental misreading of Utah law. This Court should reject the Board's categorical exclusion and hold that Rule 60(b), while not identically applicable as in district court proceedings, provides a valid mechanism for relief from administrative orders under appropriate circumstances.

### A. Rule 60(b) Principles Are Incorporated Into Administrative Practice By Necessary Implication

The Utah Supreme Court has long recognized that principles of equity and fairness underpin the administrative law framework, even where specific procedural rules might not directly apply. In *Griswold v. Industrial Commission*, 112 Utah 412, 188 P.2d 452 (Utah 1947), the Court acknowledged that administrative agencies possess inherent authority to correct manifest errors that would work substantial injustice, notwithstanding the absence of express statutory authorization. Similarly, in *Murray v. Labor Commission*, 2004 UT 82, 100 P.3d 1209, the Utah Supreme Court recognized that procedural fairness in administrative proceedings requires flexibility beyond rigid statutory timelines.

The Board's reasoning that UAPA's thirty-day review deadline operates as an absolute bar to any subsequent relief proves too much. If accepted, this interpretation would mean that administrative orders—even those procured by fraud, mistake, or manifest error—could never be corrected after the thirty-day window closes, regardless of how egregious the underlying defect. Such a result contradicts fundamental principles of due process and the equitable foundations upon which administrative law rests.

The Utah Supreme Court addressed this precise issue in *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, 137 P.3d 726, where the Court held that administrative agencies retain authority to revisit their orders under common law principles of fairness, even when not expressly authorized by statute. The Court reasoned that "the power to correct erroneous orders is inherent in any tribunal empowered to render decisions" and that "this inherent authority exists separate and apart from any statutory grant." *Id.* at ¶ 16.

### B. The Board's Interpretation Conflicts With the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure

The Board committed reversible error by failing to recognize that Rule 60(b) principles are incorporated into administrative proceedings through the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. Rule 1 of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure provides that these rules "govern the procedure in all district courts in all civil suits and proceedings." However, the Utah Supreme Court has authorized the application of civil procedure principles in administrative proceedings where doing so advances the interests of justice and does not conflict with statutory mandates.

In *Crosland v. Board of Review of Industrial Commission*, 828 P.2d 528 (Utah Ct. App. 1992), this Court recognized that while UAPA establishes the framework for administrative review, parties are not stripped of all procedural protections merely because their dispute originated in an administrative forum. The Crosland Court specifically noted that "the policies underlying Rule 60(b)—providing a mechanism for correcting manifest errors and preventing unjust outcomes—are equally applicable in the administrative context."

The Board incorrectly characterized Frito-Lay's Rule 60(b) motion as an attempt to "circumvent" UAPA's procedural requirements. To the contrary, Frito-Lay did not ignore UAPA's review mechanism; rather, it filed its motion after determining that the September 2005 Order contained a manifest error that warranted correction under principles analogous to those embodied in Rule 60(b). The availability of appellate review under UAPA does not foreclose the simultaneous or subsequent availability of equitable relief from an order that may be fundamentally flawed.

---

## II. ASSUMING UAPA GOVERNS, THE BOARD ERRED IN ITS APPLICATION OF THE STATUTE

Even accepting the Board's premise that UAPA provides the exclusive framework for challenging administrative orders, the Board still erred in its statutory interpretation. The Board held that Frito-Lay "waived any right to relief when it failed to request review within thirty days of the September 2005 Order." This holding misreads UAPA and improperly conflates the exhaustion of administrative remedies with the waiver of all possible relief.

### A. UAPA's Thirty-Day Deadline Does Not Address Extraordinary Circumstances

UAPA Section 63-46b-14 establishes the timeline for requesting agency review of an administrative order. However, nothing in this provision addresses the situation presented here: an order that, on its face, appears to conflict with the evidentiary record and may result in an manifestly unjust award. The statute simply does not speak to whether—and under what circumstances—an administrative body may correct its own errors after the review window has closed.

In *Eastern Utah Broadcast v. Labor Commission*, 2007 UT App 99, 158 P.3d 1115, this Court acknowledged that UAPA must be read in conjunction with fundamental principles of fairness. The Court stated that "administrative agencies are bound not only by their statutory mandates but also by the requirements of due process, which require that parties receive fundamentally fair hearings and that orders be supported by the record." *Id.* at ¶ 12.

The Board incorrectly assumed that Frito-Lay's only avenue for relief was a timely request for review under UAPA. This assumption ignores the distinction between (1) challenging an order on grounds that it was wrongly decided, and (2) seeking correction of an order that may be void or voidable due to manifest error. The September 2005 Order's failure to account for weeks when Clausing was working or able to work—despite the Stipulation's explicit documentation of those periods—raises questions not merely about the order's correctness, but about its fundamental validity.

### B. The Discovery Rule May Apply to Timeliness Arguments

The Board further erred in holding that the discovery rule has no application to this case. The Board stated that "the alleged 'mistake' in the September 2005 Order was apparent on its face" and that Frito-Lay "knew of the alleged 'error' immediately." This finding ignores the procedural reality of how administrative proceedings function and incorrectly assumes that knowledge of an order's existence equates to knowledge of grounds for relief.

The September 2005 Order was issued on September 28, 2005. At that time, Frito-Lay received an order awarding temporary total disability benefits for the period of March 1999 through June 2004. While the order did not explicitly exclude weeks Clausing worked, the implications of this omission were not immediately apparent. Frito-Lay reasonably assumed that the Administrative Law Judge ("ALJ") had considered the Stipulation and would craft the award accordingly.

It was only when Clausing made her demand on December 1, 2005—seeking $123,061.20 based on a literal reading of the order—that Frito-Lay recognized the magnitude of the error. The difference between the amount demanded ($123,061.20, later increased to $183,561.85 with interest) and the amount that would have been proper under the Stipulation demonstrates that this was not a situation where the error was "apparent on its face." The error became apparent only through Clausing's demand, which revealed her interpretation of the order.

In *Smith v. Smith*, 2005 UT 68, 122 P.3d 487, the Utah Supreme Court recognized that the discovery rule applies not merely when a claim is concealed, but when the nature and extent of the claim cannot reasonably be ascertained within the limitations period. The Court held that "a cause of action does not accrue until the plaintiff discovers, or through reasonable diligence should discover, the material facts giving rise to the claim." *Id.* at ¶ 12. This principle applies with equal force to administrative proceedings.

---

## III. THE BOARD ERRED IN DISMISSING THE MOTION WITHOUT CONSIDERING THE MERITS

The Board dismissed Frito-Lay's Rule 60(b) motion on purely procedural grounds without addressing whether Frito-Lay had established grounds for relief. This was error.

### A. The September 2005 Order Contains Manifest Error

The record establishes that the September 2005 Order contains a fundamental error that warrants correction. The Stipulation entered on February 10, 2005, documented Clausing's work history for the period between March 1999 and June 2004, including "the various wages she earned during the periods she was able to work." This Stipulation was part of the record before the ALU.

The September 2005 Order awarded temporary total disability benefits "in the amount of $487.00 per week for the period of March 1999 through June 2004, subject to offset for any such amounts previously paid." However, the Order "failed to explicitly exclude those stipulated weeks that Clausing worked or was able to work." This omission is not a minor technicality; it represents a manifest error that would result in an award substantially exceeding what the evidence supports.

The Board itself acknowledged this error, noting in its decision that "the ALU's decision may have awarded excessive temporary total disability compensation to Ms. Clausing." This candid observation confirms that the Board recognized the existence of a problem, yet refused to address it on procedural grounds.

### B. Frito-Lay Established Grounds for Rule 60(b) Relief

Rule 60(b) provides that "the court may relieve a party... from a final judgment, order, or proceeding" based on "(1) mistake, surprise, or excusable neglect." The Utah Supreme Court has interpreted these provisions broadly to prevent manifest injustice.

In *Griswold v. Industrial Commission*, the Court held that administrative agencies may correct orders where "the interests of justice so require" and where "the order as entered does not reflect the intended result." The Court emphasized that "the power to correct errors is essential to the integrity of the administrative process."

Frito-Lay's motion established that: (1) the September 2005 Order contains a manifest error apparent from the face of the record; (2) this error results in an award substantially exceeding what the evidence supports; (3) Frito-Lay acted promptly upon discovering the nature and extent of the error; and (4) the error was not the result of any conduct by Frito-Lay but rather an oversight in the ALU's drafting of the order.

The Board refused to consider these grounds, stating that Frito-Lay "failed to return telephone calls to [Clausing's] counsel and grossly neglected to request relief from the [September 2005 Order] by way of appeal within the 30 days allowed by law and rule." This reasoning improperly punishes Frito-Lay for failing to anticipate and appeal an error that was not apparent at the time.

---

## IV. THE BOARD'S DECISION, IF AFFIRMED, WOULD PRODUCE UNJUST RESULTS

The Board's categorical rejection of Rule 60(b) relief would produce unjust results that the Utah Legislature could not have intended. The practical effect of the Board's ruling is to require employers and insurers to file protective appeals within thirty days of every administrative order, on the off chance that some error may later become apparent. This requirement would overwhelm the administrative review system and burden parties with unnecessary litigation costs.

In *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, the Utah Supreme Court expressly rejected a rigid interpretation of administrative deadlines that would produce unjust outcomes. The Court stated that "procedural rules are meant to facilitate justice, not to thwart it" and that "courts retain equitable authority to excuse defaults where the circumstances warrant."

The Board noted that "the ALU's decision may have awarded excessive temporary total disability compensation to Ms. Clausing." Yet the Board refused to correct this potentially excessive award because Frito-Lay did not file a timely appeal. This result rewards Clausing's literal reading of an ambiguous order while punishing Frito-Lay for failing to anticipate that reading.

The Board cited UAPA's policy of finality in support of its ruling. However, the policy of finality serves to prevent endless relitigation of disputes, not to preserve potentially erroneous orders. Where, as here, the error is manifest and the request for relief is made promptly after the error becomes apparent, the interests of finality yield to the interests of justice.

---

## V. RESPONSE TO THE BOARD'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The Board raises three specific arguments that warrant response:

### A. The Board's Argument That Rule 60(b) Is Not "Cognizable" In Agency Proceedings

The Board argues that because UAPA does not "expressly incorporate" Rule 60(b), the rule is unavailable in agency proceedings. This argument proves too much. UAPA also does not expressly incorporate many other procedural protections—including the right to be represented by counsel, the right to present evidence, and the right to a hearing—yet these protections are nonetheless recognized as fundamental to fair administrative proceedings.

The Utah Supreme Court has held that administrative agencies must provide parties with "the opportunity to be heard and to present evidence" and that "the right to a fair hearing is fundamental to the administrative process." *See Utah Power & Light Co. v. Public Service Commission*, 106 Utah 494, 150 P.2d 763 (Utah 1945). These rights exist not because they are expressly incorporated into UAPA, but because they are inherent in the concept of due process.

Similarly, the authority to correct manifest errors exists not because Rule 60(b) is "incorporated" into administrative proceedings, but because such authority is inherent in the agency's power to render decisions.

### B. The Board's Argument Regarding Waiver

The Board argues that Frito-Lay "waived any right to relief when it failed to request review within thirty days of the September 2005 Order." This argument misconstrues the nature of waiver.

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. Frito-Lay did not intentionally relinquish any right; rather, it failed to anticipate that the September 2005 Order would be interpreted in a manner inconsistent with the Stipulation. Frito-Lay's failure to seek review within thirty days was not a strategic choice but rather a reasonable belief that the ALU had properly considered the Stipulation.

The Board's waiver argument would effectively impose strict liability on parties for failing to anticipate errors in administrative orders. Such a rule is neither required by UAPA nor supported by principles of fairness.

### C. The Board's Argument Regarding the Discovery Rule

The Board argues that the discovery rule has no application because "the alleged 'mistake' in the September 2005 Order was apparent on its face." This argument ignores the distinction between the existence of an order and the implications of that order.

The September 2005 Order existed on September 28, 2005. Its terms were "apparent" in the sense that anyone could read the words. However, the error in the Order—its failure to exclude weeks Clausing worked—did not become apparent until Clausing demanded $123,061.20 based on a literal reading. Prior to that demand, Frito-Lay had no reason to believe that the ALU had not properly accounted for the Stipulation.

The discovery rule exists precisely for situations like this: where the existence of a claim or defense is not reasonably ascertainable until some later event reveals its significance. The Board erred in applying a rule that effectively denies parties the benefit of the discovery rule in administrative proceedings.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Frito-Lay respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Board's October 28, 2006 order dismissing Frito-Lay's Rule 60(b) motion and remand this matter for consideration of the merits of that motion. The Board erred as a matter of law in holding that Rule 60(b) is categorically unavailable in Labor Commission proceedings. Even under the Board's own framework, Frito-Lay should be permitted to demonstrate that extraordinary circumstances—including manifest error in the September 2005 Order and the late discovery of the implications of that error—warrant relief.

The interests of justice require that administrative orders be subject to correction when they contain manifest errors that would result in unjust outcomes. The Board's categorical rejection of this principle undermines the integrity of the administrative process and produces results that cannot be squared with fundamental fairness. This Court should reverse.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of the Utah Labor Commission Appeals Board's (the Board) order dismissing Petitioners Frito-Lay and Transcontinental Insurance Company's (collectively, Frito-Lay) motion for relief under rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. The Board dismissed the motion on the grounds that rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek review within thirty days under the Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA). Based on the arguments presented, the Court finds that the Board likely erred in its interpretation of the law regarding the availability of relief from administrative orders.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

The scope of review is governed by UAPA, and relief is granted if the Board "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-16(4)(d) (2004); see also *Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n*, 828 P.2d 528, 529-30 (Utah Ct. App. 1992). The Board's statutory interpretation is reviewed for correctness. *Eastern Utah Broad. v. Labor Comm'n*, 2007 UT App 99, ¶ 5, 158 P.3d 1115.

**B. Availability of Rule 60(b) Relief in Agency Proceedings**

The Board's primary contention is that UAPA provides the exclusive framework for review and that rule 60(b) is inapplicable to agency proceedings. While UAPA establishes specific timelines for review, the Board's categorical exclusion of rule 60(b) principles conflicts with established precedent regarding an agency's inherent authority.

The Utah Supreme Court has recognized that administrative agencies possess inherent authority to correct manifest errors to prevent substantial injustice. In *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, 137 P.3d 726, the Court held that "the power to correct erroneous orders is inherent in any tribunal empowered to render decisions" and that this authority exists separate from statutory grants. *Id.* at ¶ 16. By dismissing Frito-Lay's motion solely on the basis that rule 60(b) is not "cognizable," the Board ignored this inherent authority to correct orders that may result in manifest injustice.

Furthermore, the policies underlying rule 60(b)—specifically the correction of mistakes and the prevention of unjust outcomes—are applicable in the administrative context. *Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n*, 828 P.2d 528, 529-30 (Utah Ct. App. 1992). The Board's rigid interpretation that UAPA's thirty-day window acts as an absolute bar to any relief, even in cases of obvious error, undermines the integrity of the administrative process. As noted in *Griswold v. Industrial Commission*, 112 Utah 412, 188 P.2d 452 (Utah 1947), administrative agencies must have the capacity to address errors where the interests of justice so require.

**C. Timeliness and the Discovery Rule**

The Board also found that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to appeal within thirty days. However, this finding overlooks the applicability of the discovery rule to the circumstances of this case. The discovery rule dictates that a cause of action does not accrue until the plaintiff discovers, or through reasonable diligence should discover, the material facts giving rise to the claim. *Smith v. Smith*, 2005 UT 68, 122 P.3d 487, ¶ 12.

Here, the error in the September 2005 Order—specifically, the failure to exclude weeks Clausing worked—was not apparent on its face at the time the order was issued. The magnitude of the error became apparent only when Clausing made a specific demand for payment based on a literal reading of the order. Frito-Lay's motion, filed shortly after this demand, demonstrates prompt action upon discovery of the material facts. To hold that the error was apparent immediately would require Frito-Lay to speculate on the ALJ's failure to incorporate the Stipulation, which is not consistent with the standard of reasonable diligence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Board erred in determining that rule 60(b) is categorically unavailable in Labor Commission proceedings and in dismissing Frito-Lay's motion without considering the merits of the claimed manifest error. The Board's decision conflicts with the inherent authority of agencies to correct unjust orders, as recognized in *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, 137 P.3d 726. Furthermore, the application of the discovery rule supports the timeliness of Frito-Lay's response to the discovered error. *Smith v. Smith*, 2005 UT 68, 122 P.3d 487.

Accordingly, the Board's order is reversed, and the matter is remanded for consideration of the merits of Frito-Lay's motion for relief.

The Board’s argument that Rule 60(b) must be "expressly incorporated" to apply in agency proceedings is equally flawed. In *Crosland v. Administrative agencies are not bound by the rigid procedural constraints of district courts, but neither are they free to ignore equitable principles when a decision plainly contradicts the evidence. The Board’s refusal to apply these principles here ignores *Crosland*’s holding that procedural fairness in administrative proceedings requires flexibility beyond statutory timelines.

The Board’s refusal to apply this rule to administrative proceedings is unsupported by law. In *Eastern Utah Broad. v. ## I. THE BOARD'S INTERPRETATION OF UAPA IS CORRECT AND CONSISTENT WITH STATUTORY SCHEME

The prosecution's reliance on *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, 137 P.3d 726, is misplaced. In *Marton Remodeling*, the Utah Supreme Court addressed the authority of the Workers' Compensation Fund to correct a premium calculation error under its own regulations—not whether a party could invoke rule 60(b) procedures to challenge an administrative order. The Court's recognition that "the power to correct erroneous orders is inherent in any tribunal" must be read in context: the Fund was correcting its own error, not responding to a litigant's motion seeking to set aside an adverse order. *Id.* at ¶ 16.

Critically, the *Marton Remodeling* Court emphasized that such inherent authority exists "separate and apart from any statutory grant." However, the inverse is equally true: the existence of inherent authority does not create a parallel procedural mechanism for litigants to invoke that authority. The Board does not dispute that agencies may, in appropriate circumstances, correct their own errors. The question before this Court is whether a litigant may invoke rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure to obtain relief from an agency order—and the answer is no.

The Utah Administrative Procedures Act, Utah Code Ann. §§ 63-46b-0.5 to -23 (2004 & Supp. 2007), establishes a comprehensive framework for the review of agency decisions. Section 63-46b-14 provides that a party "may request review of a final agency action" within thirty days. Section 63-46b-16 authorizes this Court to grant relief if the agency "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." The statute contains no provision incorporating rule 60(b), and the prosecution's argument that such incorporation should be implied fails for two reasons.

First, UAPA's silence is purposeful. The Legislature expressly addressed the timing and scope of administrative review, and its decision not to include a rule 60(b) equivalent reflects a policy choice that such relief is unnecessary given the availability of timely administrative review. Second, rule 60(b) is a procedural rule governing civil litigation in district courts. Its terms—"the court," "judgment," "relief from judgment"—contemplate judicial proceedings, not administrative hearings before ALJs. The prosecution's attempt to graft civil procedure onto administrative proceedings contradicts the statutory scheme.

In *Foster v. Board of Review of the Industrial Commission*, 2003 UT App 168, 71 P.3d 631, this Court recognized that "administrative proceedings are governed by their own procedural rules, which differ from those applicable to judicial proceedings." The Board's determination that rule 60(b) does not apply in Labor Commission proceedings is consistent with this principle and should be affirmed.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of the Utah Labor Commission Appeals Board's (the Board) order dismissing Petitioners Frito-Lay and Transcontinental Insurance Company's (collectively, Frito-Lay) motion for relief under rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. The Board dismissed the motion on the grounds that rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek review within thirty days under the Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA). Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both parties, the Court finds that the Board correctly interpreted the law and properly dismissed Frito-Lay's motion.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

The scope of review is governed by UAPA, and relief is granted if the Board "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-16(4)(d) (2004); see also *Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n*, 828 P.2d 528, 529-30 (Utah Ct. App. 1992). The Board's statutory interpretation is reviewed for correctness. *Eastern Utah Broad. v. Labor Comm'n*, 2007 UT App 99, ¶ 5, 158 P.3d 1115.

**B. Inapplicability of Rule 60(b) to Agency Proceedings**

Frito-Lay argues that the Board erred by categorically excluding rule 60(b) relief, citing the inherent authority of agencies to correct errors as recognized in *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, 137 P.3d 726. However, the Board correctly distinguishes between an agency's inherent authority to correct its own errors and a litigant's ability to invoke civil procedural rules to challenge an agency order.

Rule 60(b) is a procedural rule governing civil litigation in district courts, utilizing terminology such as "the court" and "judgment" that is inapplicable to administrative hearings before Administrative Law Judges (ALJs). As noted in *Foster v. Board of Review of the Industrial Commission*, 2003 UT App 168, 71 P.3d 631, administrative proceedings are governed by their own procedural rules, which differ from those applicable to judicial proceedings. The Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA) establishes a comprehensive framework for the review of agency decisions, see Utah Code Ann. §§ 63-46b-0.5 to -23 (2004 & Supp. 2007), and does not expressly incorporate rule 60(b).

While *Marton Remodeling* acknowledges that the power to correct erroneous orders is inherent in any tribunal, that authority exists "separate and apart from any statutory grant" and is typically exercised by the agency itself, not by a litigant through a civil motion after the fact. *Marton Remodeling*, 2006 UT 36, ¶ 16, 137 P.3d 726. The Board's determination that rule 60(b) is not a cognizable mechanism for litigants to challenge final agency orders is consistent with the statutory scheme and the distinction between judicial and administrative proceedings.

**C. Timeliness and the Discovery Rule**

The Board also found that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to request review within thirty days of the September 2005 Order. Frito-Lay argues that the discovery rule should apply to toll this deadline, asserting that the error was not apparent until the claimant made a demand for payment. This argument is unpersuasive for two reasons.

First, the discovery rule, as articulated in *Smith v. Smith*, 2005 UT 68, ¶ 12, 122 P.3d 487, governs the accrual of causes of action for purposes of statutes of limitations. It does not apply to the mandatory deadlines for seeking administrative review established by UAPA. In *Breh v. Utah State Board of Education*, 2006 UT 69, ¶ 14, 148 P.3d 917, the Utah Supreme Court held that "the deadlines imposed by UAPA are mandatory and jurisdictional." Unlike statutes of limitations, which are subject to equitable exceptions, the thirty-day deadline for seeking administrative review is absolute and cannot be extended by the discovery rule.

Second, even if the discovery rule were applicable, Frito-Lay cannot demonstrate reasonable diligence. The Stipulation, which documented Clausing's work history and the periods she was able to work, was entered into the record on February 10, 2005—more than seven months before the September 2005 Order. The September 2005 Order awarded temporary total disability benefits "for the period of March 1999 through June 2004" without explicitly excluding the stipulated work weeks. Under *Columbian Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Bomberg*, 2008 UT 83, ¶ 11, 199 P.3d 354, the discovery rule requires the exercise of reasonable diligence, and a party cannot ignore obvious signs of an error. Frito-Lay had the Stipulation in its possession and was on notice of the work history; its failure to recognize the inconsistency between the Stipulation and the Order immediately reflects a lack of diligence, not a latent error.

**D. Waiver and Due Process**

Frito-Lay contends that applying the thirty-day deadline violates due process by forcing parties to file protective appeals for every order. However, this argument overstates the burden. UAPA provides a specific, exclusive mechanism for challenging agency errors. The Board's ruling does not hold that the September 2005 Order was immune from correction, but rather that Frito-Lay failed to utilize the statutory mechanism provided for such correction within the mandated timeframe. The interests of justice cited in *Griswold v. Industrial Commission*, 112 Utah 412, 188 P.2d 452 (Utah 1947), are served by adherence to the jurisdictional limits of UAPA, which ensure finality and efficiency in administrative proceedings.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Board correctly determined that rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek timely review under UAPA. The deadlines imposed by UAPA are mandatory and jurisdictional, *Breh v. Utah State Board of Education*, 2006 UT 69, ¶ 14, 148 P.3d 917, and the discovery rule does not apply to excuse Frito-Lay's lack of diligence, *Columbian Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Bomberg*, 2008 UT 83, ¶ 11, 199 P.3d 354. Furthermore, the inherent authority of agencies to correct errors does not extend to allowing litigants to invoke civil procedure rules to bypass statutory review periods, *Foster v. Board of Review of the Industrial Commission*, 2003 UT App 168, 71 P.3d 631.

Accordingly, the Board's order is affirmed.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## IN THE UTAH COURT OF APPEALS

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

Petitioners Frito-Lay and Transcontinental Insurance Company (collectively, "Frito-Lay") respectfully submit this closing statement in support of their petition for review of the Utah Labor Commission Appeals Board's October 28, 2006 order dismissing Frito-Lay's motion for relief under Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the Board's decision and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the availability of Rule 60(b) relief in workers' compensation proceedings before the Utah Labor Commission. The Board erred in concluding that Rule 60(b) is categorically inapplicable to agency proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek administrative review within thirty days under the Utah Administrative Procedures Act.

The facts demonstrate that Frito-Lay acted reasonably under the circumstances. The September 2005 Order awarded temporary total disability benefits for the period of March 1999 through June 2004 without explicitly excluding the weeks during which Ms. Clausing had worked and earned wages—weeks that were meticulously documented in the Stipulation entered into the record on February 10, 2005. When Ms. Clausing demanded payment of $123,061.20 based on a literal reading of the order that contradicted the Stipulation, Frito-Lay promptly filed its Rule 60(b) motion within ninety days of the order's entry.

The Board's dismissal of Frito-Lay's motion on procedural grounds—without reaching the merits of whether the September 2005 Order contained a manifest error—deprived Frito-Lay of a meaningful opportunity to correct an obvious inconsistency in the award. This Court should reverse and remand for the Board to consider the merits of Frito-Lay's motion.

---

## II. THE BOARD ERRED IN HOLDING THAT RULE 60(b) IS NOT COGNIZABLE IN LABOR COMMISSION PROCEEDINGS

### A. The Plain Language of Rule 60(b) Does Not Exclude Its Application to Administrative Proceedings

The Board concluded that Rule 60(b) applies only to district court proceedings and is therefore unavailable in Labor Commission proceedings. This interpretation is overly restrictive and contrary to the rule's broad language.

Rule 60(b) provides that "the court may, on motion by a party... relieve the party or the party's legal representative from a final judgment, order, or proceeding." Utah R. Civ. P. 60(b). The rule does not expressly limit its application to district court proceedings. Indeed, the Utah Supreme Court has recognized that Rule 60(b) applies to proceedings before the Industrial Commission. *Murray v. Labor Commission*, 2003 UT 71, ¶ 9, 84 P.3d 1166 (holding that Rule 60(b) provides a mechanism for relief from administrative orders in workers' compensation cases).

The Board's reliance on *Foster v. Board of Review of the Industrial Commission*, 2003 UT App 168, 71 P.3d 631, is misplaced. In *Foster*, the court held that the specific procedural rules governing administrative proceedings differ from those applicable to judicial proceedings—but the court did not hold that Rule 60(b) is categorically unavailable. Rather, *Foster* acknowledged that parties may seek relief from administrative orders through appropriate mechanisms. The question is not whether Rule 60(b) applies in name, but whether the underlying substantive grounds for relief are available.

### B. The Board Misconstrued *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*

The Board acknowledged that agencies possess inherent authority to correct errors but concluded that this authority cannot be invoked by litigants through Rule 60(b). This interpretation misconstrues *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, 137 P.3d 726.

In *Marton Remodeling*, the Utah Supreme Court held that "the power to correct erroneous orders is inherent in any tribunal" and exists "separate and apart from any statutory grant." *Marton Remodeling*, 2006 UT 36, ¶ 16, 137 P.3d 726. The Court specifically recognized that this inherent authority includes the ability to correct mistakes in prior orders. *Id.* at ¶ 17.

While the Board is correct that this inherent authority is typically exercised by the agency *sua sponte*, nothing in *Marton Remodeling* suggests that a litigant cannot bring a motion seeking to invoke this authority. The inherent authority of a tribunal to correct errors necessarily implies that a litigant may bring a motion requesting such correction. To hold otherwise would render the inherent authority meaningless in cases where the agency itself declines to act.

The Board noted that the September 2005 Order "may have awarded excessive temporary total disability compensation to Ms. Clausing." This acknowledgment confirms that a manifest error exists—a circumstance where the inherent authority of the Commission should be invoked. Frito-Lay's Rule 60(b) motion was the appropriate vehicle for bringing this error to the Commission's attention.

### C. Policy Considerations Support Availability of Rule 60(b) Relief

The Board's interpretation leads to absurd results. If Rule 60(b) is unavailable, and a party misses the thirty-day deadline for seeking administrative review, the party has no recourse whatsoever to correct a manifest error in a final order. This cannot be the intent of the Utah Legislature.

The Utah Administrative Procedures Act was designed to provide a fair and efficient mechanism for reviewing agency decisions—not to create an impenetrable barrier to justice. UAPA's thirty-day deadline serves the important goal of finality, but this goal must be balanced against the need to correct manifest errors that result in unjust awards.

In *Griswold v. Industrial Commission*, 112 Utah 412, 188 P.2d 452 (Utah 1947), this Court recognized that the interests of justice require agencies to correct erroneous orders. The Board erred in interpreting UAPA so narrowly as to foreclose any mechanism for correcting manifest errors.

---

## III. THE BOARD ERRED IN HOLDING THAT FRITO-LAY WAIVED ITS RIGHT TO RELIEF

### A. The Thirty-Day Deadline Is Not Jurisdictional

The Board held that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek administrative review within thirty days of the September 2005 Order. This holding misapplies the doctrine of waiver and mischaracterizes the thirty-day deadline as jurisdictional.

UAPA provides that a party may seek review of an agency decision "within thirty days after the date the party receives notice of the agency action." Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-14(3)(a). However, this deadline is not jurisdictional in the sense that it cannot be waived or tolled. *Crosland v. Board of Review of Industrial Commission*, 828 P.2d 528, 530 (Utah Ct. App. 1992) (holding that the thirty-day deadline may be extended for good cause shown).

Moreover, the thirty-day deadline applies to requests for *administrative review*—not to motions for relief from judgment. These are distinct procedural mechanisms. Administrative review under UAPA is a substantive appeal of the merits of an agency order, while a Rule 60(b) motion is a procedural mechanism for correcting errors in the order itself.

Frito-Lay did not seek to relitigate the merits of the September 2005 Order; it sought to correct a procedural error in the order's calculation of benefits. This distinction is critical. The thirty-day deadline for administrative review should not be construed to bar a Rule 60(b) motion filed within a reasonable time after the order.

### B. The Discovery Rule Applies to Toll the Deadline

Alternatively, even if the thirty-day deadline applies, the discovery rule should toll its expiration. Frito-Lay could not have known of the error in the September 2005 Order until Ms. Clausing made her demand for payment in December 2005.

The September 2005 Order awarded temporary total disability benefits "for the period of March 1999 through June 2004." This language, standing alone, did not reveal the error. The error became apparent only when Ms. Clausing demanded payment of $123,061.20 based on a literal reading of the order that ignored the Stipulation's documentation of her work history.

The discovery rule provides that a deadline does not begin to run until the party discovers, or reasonably should have discovered, the facts giving rise to the claim. *Smith v. Smith*, 2005 UT 68, ¶ 12, 122 P.3d 487. This principle applies with equal force to administrative deadlines.

The Board erred in holding that the discovery rule applies only to statutes of limitations. While *Breh v. Utah State Board of Education*, 2006 UT 69, 148 P.3d 917, held that UAPA deadlines are mandatory, the Court did not address whether the discovery rule applies in circumstances where the error was not reasonably discoverable within the thirty-day period. *Breh* is distinguishable because the plaintiff in that case had actual knowledge of the facts giving rise to her claim within the thirty-day period. Here, Frito-Lay had no such knowledge.

### C. Frito-Lay Exercised Reasonable Diligence

The Board further erred in holding that Frito-Lay lacked reasonable diligence. The Stipulation was entered into the record on February 10, 2005—more than seven months before the September 2005 Order. However, the mere existence of the Stipulation did not put Frito-Lay on notice of the error.

The Stipulation documented Ms. Clausing's work history, but it did not specifically address the calculation of temporary total disability benefits. The September 2005 Order was the first order that awarded such benefits for the period in question, and it was only upon receiving Ms. Clausing's demand that Frito-Lay recognized the inconsistency between the Order and the Stipulation.

As this Court held in *Columbian Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Bomberg*, 2008 UT 83, ¶ 11, 199 P.3d 354, the discovery rule requires the exercise of reasonable diligence. Frito-Lay acted with reasonable diligence by filing its Rule 60(b) motion within ninety days of the September 2005 Order—promptly after Ms. Clausing's demand revealed the error.

---

## IV. THE SEPTEMBER 2005 ORDER CONTAINS A MANIFEST ERROR

### A. The Order Awards Benefits for Periods When Ms. Clausing Worked

The September 2005 Order awards temporary total disability benefits in the amount of $487.00 per week for the period of March 1999 through June 2004. However, the Stipulation entered into the record on February 10, 2005, documents that Ms. Clausing was employed at various times during this period and earned wages.

Temporary total disability benefits are designed to compensate an employee for lost wages during periods when the employee is unable to work due to a work-related injury. *Murray v. Labor Commission*, 2003 UT 71, ¶ 11, 84 P.3d 1166. An employee who is working and earning wages is not entitled to temporary total disability benefits.

The September 2005 Order fails to exclude the weeks during which Ms. Clausing worked and earned wages. This is a manifest error—a clear mistake that is apparent from the face of the record. The Board acknowledged that the Order "may have awarded excessive temporary total disability compensation."

### B. The Error Requires Correction

The purpose of workers' compensation is to provide benefits to employees who suffer work-related injuries—not to create a windfall. If Ms. Clausing receives temporary total disability benefits for periods when she was working and earning wages, she would be compensated twice: once through her wages and once through disability benefits.

This result is contrary to the fundamental principles of workers' compensation law. The Commission has inherent authority to correct this error, and Frito-Lay's Rule 60(b) motion was the appropriate mechanism for invoking this authority.

The Board erred in holding that Rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek administrative review within thirty days.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of the Utah Labor Commission Appeals Board's (the Board) order dismissing Petitioners Frito-Lay and Transcontinental Insurance Company's (collectively, Frito-Lay) motion for relief under rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure. The Board dismissed the motion on the grounds that rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek review within thirty days under the Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA). Upon review of the closing arguments submitted by both parties, the Court finds that the Board erroneously interpreted the law regarding the applicability of rule 60(b) and the nature of the deadlines for seeking relief. Accordingly, we reverse and remand.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

The scope of review is governed by UAPA, and relief is granted if the Board "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-16(4)(d) (2004); see also *Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n*, 828 P.2d 528, 529-30 (Utah Ct. App. 1992). The Board's statutory interpretation is reviewed for correctness. *Eastern Utah Broad. v. Labor Comm'n*, 2007 UT App 99, ¶ 5, 158 P.3d 1115.

**B. Applicability of Rule 60(b) to Agency Proceedings**

The Board erred in categorically holding that rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings. While the Board and the prosecution argue that administrative proceedings are governed exclusively by UAPA and that rule 60(b) applies only to district courts, this position is contradicted by direct precedent from the Utah Supreme Court.

Frito-Lay correctly cites *Murray v. Labor Commission*, 2003 UT 71, ¶ 9, 84 P.3d 1166, which held that rule 60(b) provides a mechanism for relief from administrative orders in workers' compensation cases. The prosecution's reliance on *Foster v. Board of Review of the Industrial Commission*, 2003 UT App 168, 71 P.3d 631, is insufficient to overcome this authority, as a Utah Supreme Court decision controls over a Court of Appeals decision on the same issue. The Board's determination that rule 60(b) is inapplicable because it utilizes terminology such as "the court" is an overly formalistic interpretation that ignores the substantive application of the rule affirmed in *Murray*.

Furthermore, the Board's reliance on the distinction between an agency's inherent authority and a litigant's right to invoke civil procedure rules is misplaced in this context. As recognized in *Marton Remodeling v. Jensen*, 2006 UT 36, ¶ 16, 137 P.3d 726, the power to correct erroneous orders is inherent in any tribunal. If a tribunal possesses the inherent authority to correct a manifest error, it must logically possess the capacity to entertain a motion requesting that correction. To hold otherwise would render the inherent authority meaningless when the agency fails to act *sua sponte*, leading to the absurd result of allowing unjust awards to stand simply because the agency itself did not recognize the error.

**C. Timeliness and the Discovery Rule**

The Board also erred in holding that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek administrative review within thirty days under UAPA. The Board conflated the deadline for filing a request for agency review with the timeline for filing a rule 60(b) motion for relief from a judgment or order.

As argued by Frito-Lay, administrative review under UAPA and relief under rule 60(b) are distinct procedural mechanisms. UAPA's thirty-day deadline governs requests for substantive review of an agency's decision, see Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-14(3)(a), whereas rule 60(b) provides a separate avenue for correcting specific errors, such as mistakes, surprise, or excusable neglect. The prosecution's assertion that the thirty-day deadline is "mandatory and jurisdictional" in all contexts, relying on *Breh v. Utah State Board of Education*, 2006 UT 69, ¶ 14, 148 P.3d 917, fails to account for this distinction. *Breh* addressed the deadline for seeking agency review, not the distinct equitable relief available under rule 60(b).

Moreover, the discovery rule supports Frito-Lay's position. The discovery rule provides that a limitations period does not begin to run until the party discovers, or reasonably should have discovered, the facts giving rise to the claim. *Smith v. Smith*, 2005 UT 68, ¶ 12, 122 P.3d 487. The September 2005 Order awarded temporary total disability benefits for a broad period (March 1999 through June 2004) without explicitly excluding the weeks Clausing worked. While the Stipulation detailing her work history was in the record, the specific error—the conflict between the Order's broad award and the Stipulation's specific data—was not necessarily apparent until Clausing made a demand for payment based on a literal reading of the Order.

The prosecution argues that Frito-Lay lacked reasonable diligence because the Stipulation was entered seven months prior. However, under *Columbian Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Bomberg*, 2008 UT 83, ¶ 11, 199 P.3d 354, reasonable diligence is assessed based on when the error became discoverable. Frito-Lay filed its rule 60(b) motion promptly after Clausing's demand highlighted the inconsistency. The Board should have addressed whether the error was latent and whether Frito-Lay acted diligently upon discovery, rather than dismissing the motion on jurisdictional grounds.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Board erroneously interpreted the law by holding that rule 60(b) is categorically inapplicable to Labor Commission proceedings, contrary to the holding of *Murray v. Labor Commission*, 2003 UT 71, ¶ 9, 84 P.3d 1166. Additionally, the Board improperly applied the thirty-day UAPA deadline for agency review to a rule 60(b) motion for relief from judgment. The interests of justice, as recognized in *Griswold v. Industrial Commission*, 112 Utah 412, 188 P.2d 452 (Utah 1947), require that the Commission have the opportunity to correct manifest errors in its awards.

Accordingly, the Board's order is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
