The Supreme Court has long held that disclosure of a confidential informant’s identity is not required when the defendant fails to demonstrate that the informant’s testimony is necessary for a fair determination of the case. In *Roviaro v. Here, the appellant’s request for disclosure was predicated on issues of guilt or innocence and entrapment—matters entirely disconnected from the suppression motion’s sole focus on the legality of the search. The Supreme Court further clarified in *United States v. The appellant made no such showing; instead, he sought disclosure for trial-specific defenses that were mooted by the dispositive motion. The trial court properly found the informant’s reliability established through the officer’s testimony about past tips, which the court deemed sufficient to support probable cause for the search.

In the context of suppression hearings, courts uniformly require a specific showing that disclosure is necessary to determine the validity of the search itself. As the Ninth Circuit held in *United States v. Smith*, 282 F.3d 758 (9th Cir. 2002), "the defendant must make a specific showing that disclosure is necessary for the court to determine the legality of the search." The appellant’s assertions—that the confidential informant was present during the encounter and that her belongings were found in his car—do not establish that the informant’s identity was critical to assessing the legality of the search. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's argument mischaracterizes the nature of the defense motion and the scope of the "dispositive" agreement. The defense did not seek disclosure of the confidential informant's identity solely for purposes of guilt or innocence or for trial-specific defenses such as entrapment. Rather, the defense sought disclosure because the informant's identity was essential to determining the reliability of the tip that formed the basis for probable cause—the very issue before the court on the motion to suppress. The prosecution's attempt to segregate the reliability inquiry from the suppression analysis fails because probable cause determinations are fundamentally about the trustworthiness of the informant's information. The trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose without properly considering the new evidence regarding the confidential informant's identity and potential bias, and this Court should reverse.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Confidential Informant's Identity Was Relevant to the Suppression Motion, Not Just Guilt or Innocence

The prosecution erroneously contends that the informant's identity related only to "guilt or innocence" matters that were not before the court due to the dispositive nature of the motion to suppress. This characterization ignores the defense's explicit argument that disclosure was needed to challenge the reliability of the informant—a critical component of the probable cause analysis.

The defense counsel expressly stated during the hearing that "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress, even on a Motion to Suppress." The defense argued that if the informant "set him up, it goes to her reliability." This argument directly ties the informant's identity to the suppression issue, not to guilt or innocence. The prosecution's own cited authority in *United States v. Smith*, 282 F.3d 758 (9th Cir. 2002), acknowledges that "the defendant must make a specific showing that disclosure is necessary for the court to determine the legality of the search." The defense made exactly this showing by arguing that the informant's identity was necessary to assess whether the tip was reliable enough to support probable cause.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573 (1971), for the proposition that identity need not be disclosed. However, *Harris* involved a situation where the magistrate had already made a probable cause determination based on the informant's tip. Here, the defense was seeking to challenge that very determination by attacking the informant's reliability through disclosure of her identity. The defense was not seeking to relitigate a settled probable cause finding; it was seeking the evidence necessary to challenge that finding in the first instance.

### B. The Defense Did Not Waive the Right to Challenge the Informant's Reliability by Agreeing to a Dispositive Motion

The prosecution argues that by agreeing to a dispositive motion, the defendant waived any right to disclosure. This argument proves too much. The very purpose of a suppression motion is to test the legality of the search. If the defendant cannot obtain information necessary to challenge the reliability of the informant who provided the basis for the search, the suppression motion becomes a hollow exercise.

The agreement that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive" addressed the consequences of the ruling—whether the defendant would plead guilty or the state would dismiss. It did not, and could not, extinguish the defendant's right to have the motion adjudicated on a complete record. The defense explicitly argued that it had received "new information regarding the CI" that warranted disclosure. The agreement to make the motion dispositive did not include a waiver of the right to present new evidence relevant to the suppression issue.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Mezzanatto*, 513 U.S. 196 (1995), is misplaced. In *Mezzanatto*, the Court held that defendants may waive certain rights in plea negotiations. However, the defendant here did not waive his right to challenge the search; he merely agreed that the resolution of the suppression motion would determine whether he would plead guilty. The waiver the prosecution invokes was not explicitly stated in the agreement, and the defense never knowingly waived the right to challenge the informant's reliability.

### C. The Defense Made the Required Showing Under Roviaro

The prosecution contends that the defendant failed to meet his burden under *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), and *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858 (1982). This argument ignores the specific facts presented by the defense.

Defense counsel represented that "the confidential informant was with appellant that day" and that "her belongings were found in his car when it was released from evidence." These facts, if true, would establish that the informant was not a mere tipster but was present at the scene and had personal knowledge of the circumstances surrounding the alleged possession. This directly impacts the reliability of the informant's tip and, consequently, the probable cause determination.

In *Bodin v. State*, the case cited by defense counsel, the court recognized that disclosure may be required when the informant's testimony may be necessary to a fair determination of guilt or innocence. However, the defense argument in this case went further—the informant's presence and potential setup of the defendant goes to the very reliability of the tip that established probable cause. The defense argued that "the CI set him up, that is critical to his defense" and that "she is a material witness." While the prosecution characterizes this as a guilt-or-innocence argument, it is equally a reliability argument—if the informant set up the defendant, her tip was inherently unreliable and could not support probable cause.

The trial court's statement that it "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them" does not satisfy the requirement to evaluate reliability under the totality of the circumstances. The defense was entitled to test this reliability determination by learning the informant's identity and presenting evidence of the informant's bias, presence at the scene, and potential involvement in the alleged crime.

### D. The Trial Court Failed to Properly Consider the New Evidence

The defense represented to the court that it had received documents "yesterday" containing new information regarding the confidential informant. This new information was the basis for re-urging the motion to disclose. The trial court failed to address this new evidence or provide the defense an opportunity to present it.

The prosecution argues that the defendant should have raised these issues before the dispositive motion was heard. However, the defense represented that the information was newly discovered. A trial court commits reversible error when it denies a motion without considering newly discovered evidence that is material to the issue before the court. The defense was not seeking to relitigate the suppression motion; it was seeking disclosure of evidence necessary to properly adjudicate that motion.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. The trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity without properly considering the defense's argument that disclosure was necessary to determine the reliability of the informant's tip—the very foundation of the probable cause analysis. The prosecution's characterization of the disclosure request as irrelevant to the suppression motion ignores the fundamental relationship between informant reliability and probable cause. The defendant did not waive his right to challenge the informant's reliability by agreeing to a dispositive motion, and the trial court erred in refusing to consider new evidence relevant to that issue.

Respectfully submitted,

Defense Counsel

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The court finds that the trial court erred in denying the appellant’s motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. While the prosecution correctly notes that the parties entered into an agreement making the motion to suppress "dispositive," this agreement governed the procedural consequences of the ruling—specifically, that a denial would result in a guilty plea rather than a trial. It did not, however, constitute a waiver of the appellant’s right to challenge the factual basis of probable cause, nor did it transform the suppression hearing into a proceeding where the State’s evidence could not be contested with newly discovered evidence.

The defense’s request for disclosure was not limited to "trial-specific" defenses such as entrapment, as characterized by the prosecution, but was fundamentally grounded in the challenge to the informant’s reliability. The foundational standard for disclosing an informant’s identity was established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), where the Supreme Court held that the government’s privilege to withhold an informant’s identity must yield when the disclosure is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." Here, the defense presented specific allegations that the confidential informant was present during the alleged offense and that her belongings were found in the appellant’s car. These allegations, if proven, suggest the informant was an active participant rather than a passive tipster. Such participation is directly relevant to the informant’s credibility and the reliability of the tip provided to law enforcement.

The reliability of an informant is a cornerstone of the probable cause analysis. See *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). By refusing to consider the new evidence regarding the informant’s potential involvement and bias, the trial court failed to conduct a proper "totality of the circumstances" review of the probable cause determination. The trial court’s reliance on the informant’s "past tips" did not render the specific allegations of fabrication or entrapment in this instance irrelevant to the suppression hearing. As the defense argued, if the informant "set him up," the tip is inherently unreliable, and probable cause fails. Therefore, the informant’s identity was necessary to determine the legality of the search, satisfying the standard for disclosure in a suppression context outlined in *United States v. Smith*, 282 F.3d 758 (9th Cir. 2002).

Furthermore, the defense’s receipt of documents "yesterday" containing new information regarding the informant warranted the court’s consideration. The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Mezzanatto*, 513 U.S. 196 (1995), regarding waivers is misplaced because the record does not demonstrate a "knowing and voluntary" waiver of the right to challenge the *basis* of probable cause. The defense counsel’s acknowledgment that the motion was "dispositive" referred to the plea bargain structure, not an agreement to stipulate to the informant’s reliability or to ignore exculpatory evidence regarding the search's legality.

Accordingly, the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose without a hearing on the new evidence. The defense made a specific showing that the informant’s identity was necessary to determine the legality of the search. The judgment of the court of appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The defense's reliance on *Roviaro v. ## II. THE DISPOSITIVE AGREEMENT DID NOT WAIVE THE RIGHT TO CHALLENGE PROBABLE CAUSE THROUGH DISCLOSURE OF THE INFORMANT'S IDENTITY

The prosecution contends that defense counsel's statements during the hearing constitute a knowing and voluntary waiver of the right to disclose the informant under *United States v. Mezzanatto*, 513 U.S. 196 (1995). This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: the waiver of the right to a trial and the waiver of the right to challenge the legal basis for a search. Second, the defense consistently argued that the informant's identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the tip that established probable cause. To hold otherwise would allow the state to insulate an informant's identity from scrutiny simply by characterizing a suppression hearing as "dispositive."

The prosecution argues that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), does not apply to suppression hearings and that the more stringent standard from *United States v. Smith*, 282 F.3d 758 (9th Cir. 2002), governs. This argument misreads the applicable law.

*Roviaro* established the principle that the government must disclose an informant's identity when that informant's testimony is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." 353 U.S. at 60-61. While the prosecution is correct that *Smith* applied a more restrictive standard in the suppression context, *Smith* is distinguishable because it involved circumstances where the informant's testimony was not necessary to challenge the probable cause basis for the search. In *Smith*, the court noted that the defendant had "not shown that the informant's identity would have been relevant to determining the legality of the search." 282 F.3d at 766.

Here, the defense has made exactly that showing. The informant's tip formed the sole basis for probable cause. The defense sought to demonstrate that the informant was not reliable — not because of past tips, but because she was present at the scene and "set up" the defendant. If the informant "set up" the defendant, there was no reliable tip — only a manufactured crime. This is not a "trial defense"; it goes to the heart of whether probable cause ever existed.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727 (1980), for the proposition that "a defendant cannot use a suppression motion as a vehicle to litigate guilt or innocence." This argument is a red herring. The defense is not seeking to litigate guilt or innocence; it is seeking to litigate whether the search was supported by probable cause. But the defense's challenge was not to the accuracy of the tip; it was to the informant's credibility. The defense sought to prove that the informant fabricated the entire scenario — that she "set up" the defendant. This is not a challenge to the tip's accuracy; it is a challenge to the informant's integrity.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on *Gates* is premature. *Gates* establishes the standard for evaluating probable cause, but it does not address whether a defendant must be permitted to challenge the informant's credibility. The defense is entitled to test the prosecution's probable cause case, and that includes the right to examine the informant's background and potential bias. The trial court's finding of reliability was based on testimony about past tips — testimony that the defense was unable to test because the informant's identity was concealed.

The defense's allegations — that the informant was present at the scene, that she "set up" the defendant, and that her belongings were found in the car — are not merely "speculative" as the prosecution contends. They are specific factual allegations that, if proven, would demonstrate that the informant was not a reliable source but rather an agent provocateur. The prosecution's argument that the defense should have raised the issue "before agreeing to the dispositive motion" ignores the sequential nature of the proceedings. ## VI. THE DISCLOSURE WAS "MATERIAL AND FAVORABLE" UNDER VALENZUELA-BERNAL

The prosecution argues that the defense failed to meet the standard from *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858 (1982), which requires a showing that the informant's testimony is "material and favorable to his defense, and not merely cumulative or speculative." This argument fails.

The prosecution's argument that the disclosure would not have "altered the probable cause determination" is circular. The prosecution cannot simultaneously conceal the informant's identity and argue that the disclosure would not have mattered.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, the court affirms the judgment of the trial court. The defense’s arguments fail to overcome the binding nature of the dispositive plea agreement or the established legal standards governing informant disclosure during a suppression hearing.

**I. The Dispositive Agreement and Waiver**

The defense contends that the "dispositive" agreement did not waive the right to challenge the informant’s reliability. However, the record clearly demonstrates that defense counsel knowingly and voluntarily agreed to limit the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search. When the prosecutor explicitly defined the dispositive motion as one where "issues relating to guilt or innocence did not matter," defense counsel affirmed, "You are clear in your understanding of the Motion to Suppress." This exchange constitutes a valid waiver of the right to litigate guilt-based defenses, such as entrapment or the claim that the informant "set him up," during the suppression hearing. As established in *United States v. Mezzanatto*, 513 U.S. 196 (1995), waivers of procedural rights in plea agreements are presumptively valid and enforceable. The defense cannot now retroactively expand the scope of the hearing to include trial defenses that were expressly excluded by the agreement.

**II. Standard for Disclosure in a Suppression Hearing**

The defense relies heavily on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), arguing that the informant’s testimony was essential to a fair determination of the cause. However, *Roviaro* primarily addresses disclosure at trial where the informant is a material witness to the crime. In the context of a suppression hearing, the standard is more stringent. As recognized in *United States v. Smith*, 282 F.3d 758 (9th Cir. 2002), disclosure is required only when the informant’s identity is necessary for the court to determine the legality of the search.

Here, the defense sought disclosure to prove that the informant "set him up" and that her belongings were in the car. These allegations pertain to the defendant’s culpability and potential entrapment, not the legality of the search. The Supreme Court has held that "a defendant cannot use a suppression motion as a vehicle to litigate guilt or innocence." *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727 (1980). Because the defense’s theory challenges the defendant’s guilt rather than the officers' probable cause, the request for disclosure was properly denied under the *Smith* standard.

**III. Probable Cause and Informant Reliability**

The trial court found that the informant was reliable based on testimony that her past tips were "effective and accurate." Under the totality-of-the-circumstances test articulated in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), this track record was sufficient to establish probable cause. The defense’s attempt to distinguish "tip accuracy" from "informant credibility" is unavailing in this context. The officers' reliance on the tip was based on the informant’s history of providing accurate information, not on her moral character or potential role in the alleged crime. The defense’s speculation that the informant "set him up" does not negate the reliability of the information she provided regarding the presence of contraband, nor does it undermine the probable cause determination made by the officers at the time of the search.

**IV. Materiality of the Evidence**

Finally, the defense failed to meet its burden under *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858 (1982), to show that the informant’s testimony was "material and favorable" to the defense. The defense’s claim that the informant "set him up" is speculative and, even if true, would not negate the probable cause supported by her accurate past tips. Because the defense did not demonstrate that disclosure was necessary to challenge the legality of the search, the evidence was not material to the suppression issue.

**Conclusion**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The defense’s attempt to litigate issues of guilt and innocence was barred by the dispositive agreement and is inapplicable to the limited scope of a suppression hearing. The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
