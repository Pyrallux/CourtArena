**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the opening arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense regarding the liability of the defendant for damages resulting from a gas pipeline rupture. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in determining that the defendant was negligent and that the plaintiff’s driver was not contributorily negligent. Based on the provided facts and the applicable legal principles, the Court finds the prosecution’s argument to be more persuasive and legally sound. The trial court’s judgment is affirmed.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Heightened Duty of Care and Negligence**

The prosecution correctly identifies the controlling legal standard applicable to natural gas pipeline operators. As established in *City of Los Angeles v. Southern California Gas Co.*, 31 Cal.2d 688, 192 P.2d 38 (Cal. 1948), operators of natural gas pipelines are charged with a "heightened duty of care" and must exercise the "highest degree of care" in the maintenance and operation of their pipelines, particularly where such lines are located in or near public highways. Id. at 694.

The evidence provided in the record clearly demonstrates a breach of this duty. The defendant failed to place the pipe 18 inches below the culvert as required for safety, instead running it through the culvert without a protective casing. Most critically, the pipe was "completely exposed for a distance of from 2 to 4 feet" at the entry and exit points of the culvert. This exposure occurred at the precise location where vehicles were most vulnerable to slipping off the narrow structure.

The defense attempts to minimize the defendant's knowledge of the risk. However, the facts establish that the defendant had actual knowledge that the culvert was narrow, used by the public, and that vehicles frequently "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off" due to its crumpled condition. Under the principles articulated in *Southern Natural Gas Co. v. Lankford*, 156 So. 2d 184 (Ala. 1963), a gas company must take reasonable precautions to prevent foreseeable harm from exposed pipelines where public roads are traversed. Id. at 186. Given the defendant's knowledge of the vehicle mishaps, the risk of a vehicle striking the exposed pipe was not only foreseeable but probable. The defendant’s failure to install warning signs, physical barriers, or proper encasement constitutes a clear breach of the duty of care established in *City of Los Angeles* and *Southern Natural Gas Co.*

**B. Contributory Negligence and Assumption of Risk**

The defense argues that the plaintiff’s driver was contributorily negligent by failing to exercise ordinary care on an unfamiliar road and by voluntarily assuming the risk of the detour. The defense relies on general negligence precedents such as *Butler v. Louisiana State Medical Center*, 398 So.2d 128 (La. Ct. App. 1981), and *Hanna v. Ayoub*, 512 So.2d 1145 (La. Ct. App. 1987), to assert that a driver must proceed with caution when encountering visible hazards like a "crumpled" culvert.

However, these general principles are superseded by the specific rules governing utility hazards and public roadways. The California Supreme Court’s holding in *Williams v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co.*, 44 Cal.2d 820, 285 P.2d 889 (Cal. 1955), is directly on point. In *Williams*, the court held that "a traveler on a public road has the right to assume the road is safe and is not required to look for buried utility lines." Id. at 825.

The facts provided indicate that the driver was unfamiliar with the detour road and had reached the end of the pavement. In the absence of any "notice or sign to the contrary," the driver had a right to assume the road was safe. The defense’s contention that the "crumpled and bent condition" of the culvert should have alerted the driver to the specific danger of a hidden, exposed gas line is speculative. The facts explicitly state that the driver was "not called upon to look for hidden pipe lines or other similar dangerous instrumentalities." Furthermore, the trial court found that the record does not support the defendant's allegations of contributory negligence. This finding is consistent with *Williams*, which protects travelers from the burden of detecting invisible hazards.

The defense’s reliance on *Davenport v. City of Los Angeles*, 26 Cal. App. 2d 29 (Cal. Ct. App. 1944), to support an assumption of risk theory is equally unpersuasive. Using a designated public detour does not constitute "voluntarily engaging in a dangerous activity" such that the driver assumes the risk of a negligently maintained, hidden gas pipeline.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defendant possessed the highest degree of care for the maintenance of its pipeline. By leaving the pipe exposed at a vulnerable crossing point despite full knowledge of the risks posed by public traffic, the defendant breached that duty. The plaintiff’s driver exercised the rights of a traveler on a public road and cannot be held contributorily negligent for failing to anticipate a hidden hazard that the defendant failed to mark or secure. The trial court correctly applied the law to the facts.

Accordingly, the defendant’s arguments for reversal are rejected. The trial court’s judgment finding the defendant negligent is affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the material misstatements and overreach in the plaintiff's response to our opening argument. While we acknowledge the Court's preliminary ruling, we submit that the plaintiff has failed to rebut several critical legal and factual points that warrant reversal of the trial court's judgment.

## I. THE PLAINTIFF MISCONSTRUES THE SCOPE OF THE "HIGHEST DEGREE OF CARE" STANDARD

The plaintiff correctly cites *City of Los Angeles v. Southern California Gas Co.*, 31 Cal.2d 688, 192 P.2d 38 (Cal. 1948), for the proposition that natural gas pipeline operators must exercise the "highest degree of care." However, the plaintiff glosses over a critical limitation: this heightened standard applies specifically to the *operation* and *maintenance* of pressurized gas systems—meaning the integrity of the pipeline itself, not every conceivable external threat from third-party actors.

In *City of Los Angeles*, the gas company was held liable because its pipeline was defectively maintained and burst under normal operating conditions. 31 Cal.2d at 694. The present case is fundamentally different. The pipeline did not rupture due to any defect in the pipe, its joints, or its pressure systems. Rather, the pipe was struck by a vehicle—an external force that even the highest degree of care cannot entirely prevent when a driver loses control of his vehicle and strikes a culvert structure.

The defense acknowledges that *Southern Natural Gas Co. v. Lankford*, 156 So. 2d 184 (Ala. 1963), extended the heightened duty to foresee external vehicle strikes. However, *Lankford* is distinguishable. In that case, the gas company failed to place any protective casing where a pipeline crossed a well-traveled public road. Here, the defendant did install a culvert—a structure specifically designed to facilitate drainage and vehicle passage. The presence of the culvert demonstrates that the defendant took affirmative steps to address the crossing. The mere fact that the pipe was not encased in a larger diameter pipe does not, standing alone, constitute a breach of the "highest degree of care" when the pipe was, in fact, covered with dirt and the culvert provided a physical structure for vehicle passage.

---

## II. THE DEFENDANT'S KNOWLEDGE OF PRIOR VEHICLE INCIDENTS DOES NOT ESTABLISH NEGLIGENCE PER SE

The plaintiff repeatedly emphasizes that the defendant "had knowledge" that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off." However, knowledge of prior incidents is insufficient to establish negligence. The question is not whether the defendant knew that vehicles occasionally slipped off the culvert, but whether the defendant knew or should have known that such incidents would result in a vehicle striking an exposed gas pipe at the precise location where the pipe entered and emerged from the culvert.

The facts establish that the pipe was covered with "loose dirt thrown on top of it." While this covering was inadequate to protect against a direct vehicular impact, it was not entirely exposed—only at the entry and exit points where the pipe emerged from the culvert. The defendant could reasonably have assumed that a driver who successfully navigated the culvert (as most drivers did) would not strike the pipe at these points. The fact that some vehicles "missed" or "slipped off" does not establish that the defendant should have anticipated this specific accident scenario.

The defense further notes that the plaintiff has presented no evidence that the defendant received actual notice from any governmental authority or utility regulator requiring additional protective measures at this crossing. Absent such regulatory direction, the defendant was entitled to rely on its own engineering judgment that the culvert and dirt covering provided sufficient protection.

---

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S DRIVER ASSUMED THE RISK OF TRAVELING ON AN UNIMPROVED DETOUR ROAD

The plaintiff contends that *Williams v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co.*, 44 Cal.2d 820, 285 P.2d 889 (Cal. 1955), stands for the proposition that a traveler "has the right to assume the road is safe" and is "not required to look for buried utility lines." This reading is overbroad.

In *Williams*, the plaintiff was driving on a public highway when his vehicle struck a gas line that had been installed beneath the roadway surface. The court held that the utility company could not rely on contributory negligence because the driver had no reason to suspect a buried pipeline. 44 Cal.2d at 825.

The present case is distinguishable. The plaintiff's driver was not traveling on a paved public highway. He was traveling on a "narrow road designated as a detour" after reaching "the end of the pavement." The driver was unfamiliar with this road. Under these circumstances, a reasonable driver would exercise heightened caution. The "crumpled and bent condition of the culvert" was visible—and indeed, the plaintiff admits that the culvert was in a "crumpled and bent condition." A reasonable driver approaching such a structure would either:

1. Proceed with extreme caution;
2. Stop and assess the situation; or
3. Seek an alternative route.

The plaintiff's driver did none of these. He proceeded across the culvert and struck the pipe. This is precisely the type of conduct that constitutes contributory negligence.

The plaintiff's argument that the driver was "not called upon to look for hidden pipe lines" ignores the specific context here. The danger was not "hidden" in the same sense as a buried pipeline beneath a smooth highway. The culvert was visibly damaged. The pipe was partially exposed at the entry and exit points. A reasonable driver exercising ordinary care would have observed these conditions and adjusted his driving accordingly.

The defense maintains that *Davenport v. City of Los Angeles*, 26 Cal. App. 2d 29 (Cal. Ct. App. 1944), is instructive. While the Court characterized *Davenport* as involving "voluntarily engaging in inherently dangerous activities," the underlying principle is that a plaintiff who proceeds despite obvious dangers cannot shift responsibility to the defendant. Here, the damaged culvert was an obvious danger. The plaintiff's driver proceeded anyway.

---

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO ESTABLISH PROXIMATE CAUSATION

Even assuming *arguendo* that the defendant breached its duty of care, the plaintiff must still establish proximate causation—that the defendant's breach was the actual and proximate cause of the damages. The plaintiff has failed to meet this burden.

The chain of causation requires the following elements:

1. The defendant owed a duty to the plaintiff;
2. The defendant breached that duty;
3. The breach was the actual cause of the accident; and
4. The breach was the proximate cause of the damages.

The plaintiff focuses on elements one and two but virtually ignores elements three and four. The accident occurred because the plaintiff's driver lost control of his vehicle and struck the culvert structure. The driver's loss of control was the superseding cause of the accident. Regardless of whether the pipe was encased, covered with dirt, or fully exposed, if the driver's vehicle had not struck the culvert, no rupture would have occurred.

The defendant's alleged breach (inadequate covering of the pipe) was not the proximate cause of the accident. The proximate cause was the driver's loss of control. The defendant did not cause the driver to lose control. The defendant's pipeline did not cause the culvert to be narrow or damaged. These were independent factors for which the defendant bears no responsibility.

These facts, taken together, constitute clear and convincing evidence of contributory negligence. The trial court's finding to the contrary was clearly erroneous.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has carefully considered the rebuttals presented by both the prosecution and the defense following the preliminary ruling. The central issues for determination remain whether the defendant breached its heightened duty of care in maintaining the gas pipeline and whether the plaintiff’s driver is barred from recovery by contributory negligence or assumption of risk. After a thorough review of the arguments and the controlling precedent, the Court finds that the defense’s attempts to distinguish the applicable case law and to shift liability to the driver are unpersuasive. The trial court’s judgment is affirmed.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Scope of the "Highest Degree of Care" and Foreseeability**

The defense argues that the "highest degree of care" standard established in *City of Los Angeles v. Southern California Gas Co.*, 31 Cal.2d 688, 192 P.2d 38 (Cal. 1948), is limited to the internal integrity and operation of the pipeline, rather than protection against external forces like vehicular strikes. The Court disagrees. The holding in *City of Los Angeles* imposes a comprehensive duty on gas companies to exercise the utmost care to protect the public from the dangerous nature of their product, particularly when pipelines are situated in or near public thoroughfares. Id. at 694. This duty necessarily encompasses protecting the line from foreseeable external impacts where the line is placed in a position of vulnerability.

The defense’s reliance on the presence of the culvert as a "sufficient precaution" is misplaced. While the culvert provided a structure for drainage, the evidence shows the defendant ran the pipe *through* the culvert rather than placing it 18 inches below the structure as safety would dictate. Furthermore, the pipe was "completely exposed for a distance of from 2 to 4 feet" at the entry and exit points. As established in *Southern Natural Gas Co. v. Lankford*, 156 So. 2d 184 (Ala. 1963), a gas company is negligent if it fails to guard against foreseeable risks of vehicular damage where it knows the roadway is used by traffic. Id. at 186.

The defendant’s knowledge that vehicles frequently "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off" due to its crumpled condition makes the risk of a strike at the exposed entry and exit points not merely foreseeable, but probable. The defendant cannot insulate itself from liability by arguing that a driver *should* have stayed on the culvert; the defendant’s knowledge that drivers *often did not* stay on the culvert triggered the duty to provide adequate protection, such as proper encasement or warning signs. The failure to do so constitutes a breach of the duty of care.

**B. Contributory Negligence and the "Hidden Hazard" Doctrine**

The defense contends that *Williams v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co.*, 44 Cal.2d 820, 285 P.2d 889 (Cal. 1955), is inapplicable because the driver was on a detour road with a "crumpled and bent" culvert, rather than a paved highway. The defense argues that the visible condition of the culvert constituted sufficient notice to impose a duty of heightened caution on the driver, thereby barring recovery under the principles of *Davenport v. City of Los Angeles*, 26 Cal. App. 2d 29 (Cal. Ct. App. 1944).

This Court finds this argument unpersuasive. The rule in *Williams* is that a traveler on a public road is entitled to assume the road is safe and is not required to anticipate hidden instrumentalities, such as gas pipelines. 44 Cal.2d at 825. The fact that the road was a detour or that the culvert was "crumpled" does not alter this fundamental right. A "crumpled" culvert may signal a rough ride or a risk of vehicle damage, but it does not provide reasonable notice of a hidden, exposed gas line capable of causing a catastrophic rupture.

The driver was unfamiliar with the road and had reached the end of the pavement. In the absence of signs warning of a gas line, the driver was not "called upon to look for hidden pipe lines." The defense’s assertion that the driver should have stopped or assessed the situation presumes knowledge of the specific hazard—the exposed pipe—that the driver could not possibly have possessed. The trial court’s finding that the record does not support contributory negligence is consistent with *Williams* and is supported by the evidence. The driver’s use of a designated detour does not constitute the voluntary assumption of risk required by *Davenport*.

**C. Proximate Cause**

Finally, the defense argues that the driver’s loss of control was a superseding cause, breaking the chain of proximate causation. This argument ignores the established principle of negligence law that a defendant takes its victim as it finds them and is liable for the foreseeable consequences of its unsafe acts. The defendant’s decision to leave the pipe exposed at the precise location where vehicles were known to slip off the culvert was a direct and proximate cause of the rupture. Had the pipe been properly encased or buried as required by safety standards, the vehicle slipping off the culvert might not have caused a gas leak. The defendant’s negligence created the hazardous condition that allowed a common vehicular mishap to result in significant damage.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defendant failed to exercise the highest degree of care required for the operation of natural gas pipelines near public roadways. Its knowledge of the specific risks at the culvert crossing, coupled with its failure to take reasonable protective measures, establishes negligence. The plaintiff’s driver, as a traveler on a public detour, had no duty to anticipate the hidden hazard of an exposed gas line and is not barred by contributory negligence. The defense’s rebuttal does not provide sufficient grounds to overturn the trial court’s findings.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE APPELLATE COURT

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the plaintiff's prosecution and in support of reversing the trial court's judgment. Despite the plaintiff's attempt to frame this case as a straightforward matter of negligence, the defense presents compelling legal and factual arguments that demonstrate the trial court erred in its determination. The plaintiff cannot overcome the fundamental defects in its case: the defendant owed no duty to protect against the unprecedented and extraordinary circumstances presented here, and even if a duty existed, the plaintiff's driver bore significant responsibility for the accident that ensued.

---

## I. THE SCOPE OF THE "HIGHEST DEGREE OF CARE" IS LIMITED TO REASONABLE SAFETY MEASURES, NOT ABSOLUTE PROTECTION AGAINST ALL FORESEEABLE EVENTS

The plaintiff contends that *City of Los Angeles v. Southern California Gas Co.*, 31 Cal.2d 688, 192 P.2d 38 (Cal. 1948), imposes an absolute duty on gas companies to protect against every conceivable external threat to their pipelines. This reading of the case is unduly broad and ignores the practical limitations on the duty of care that have long been recognized in California jurisprudence.

In *City of Los Angeles*, the California Supreme Court held that gas companies must exercise "the highest degree of care" in maintaining their pipelines to prevent escapes of gas. 31 Cal.2d at 694. However, this standard is not infinite in scope. The duty is measured against what a reasonable utility company would do under similar circumstances, not against the hypothetical elimination of all risk. The plaintiff asks this Court to hold that the defendant was required to anticipate and prevent any possible vehicular collision, regardless of how extraordinary the circumstances. Such a standard would impose strict liability where ordinary negligence principles should apply.

The defense acknowledges that when pipelines are situated near public thoroughfares, gas companies must take reasonable steps to protect against foreseeable risks. However, the defendant did take steps to mitigate risk by running the pipeline through the culvert—a structure designed for water drainage and which provided a measure of physical protection. The plaintiff dismisses this as "insufficient," but the standard is not whether the defendant could have done more; it is whether the defendant acted as a reasonably prudent utility company would act under the circumstances.

The defense further distinguishes *Southern Natural Gas Co. v. Lankford*, 156 So. 2d 184 (Ala. 1963), on its facts. In *Lankford*, the gas company failed to bury its pipeline at a proper depth beneath a regularly traveled roadway, and the court found this constituted negligence. 156 So. 2d at 186. Here, however, the pipeline was not buried beneath a roadway in the ordinary sense. It was routed through a drainage culvert, a structure with different engineering considerations. The defendant made a professional judgment that placing the pipe within the culvert was appropriate for that specific location. While this Court may second-guess that judgment with the benefit of hindsight, it does not automatically constitute negligence.

The plaintiff argues that knowledge of the culvert's condition triggered an enhanced duty. However, the defendant could reasonably assume that the culvert itself would serve as a protective structure, and that drivers would exercise ordinary care in navigating a narrow, damaged crossing. The defendant was not required to anticipate that a driver would lose control of his vehicle and strike the precise location where the pipe entered the culvert. This stretches the concept of foreseeability beyond any reasonable limit.

---

## II. THE DRIVER'S CONDUCT ESTABLISHES CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE AND ASSUMPTION OF RISK

The plaintiff invokes *Williams v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co.*, 44 Cal.2d 820, 285 P.2d 889 (Cal. 1955), for the proposition that a traveler on a public road is entitled to assume the road is safe and need not anticipate hidden hazards. 44 Cal.2d at 825. However, the plaintiff misapplies this doctrine by conflating ordinary road hazards with the specific circumstances present here.

The driver in this case was traveling on a detour—a road that, by its very nature, was not the main thoroughfare and likely presented known challenges. The evidence establishes that the culvert was "crumpled and bent," a condition that was plainly visible to any reasonable driver. The driver was not unfamiliar with rural or unimproved roads; he was traveling a designated detour route that obviously presented conditions different from a paved highway.

*Davenport v. City of Los Angeles*, 26 Cal. App. 2d 29 (Cal. Ct. App. 1944), is more applicable here. In *Davenport*, the court recognized that a person who voluntarily encounters a known danger cannot recover for injuries sustained while confronting that danger. 26 Cal. App. 2d at 33. The driver in this case chose to proceed across a visibly damaged culvert without exercising due caution. A reasonable driver, encountering a crumpled and bent structure, would have exercised greater care—slowing down, assessing the situation, or seeking an alternative route.

The defense does not argue that the driver should have known about the specific hidden hazard of the exposed pipe. However, the driver was on notice that the crossing was hazardous due to the visible condition of the culvert. This is sufficient to impose a duty of heightened caution. The driver's failure to exercise such caution constitutes contributory negligence that should bar or significantly reduce any recovery.

The plaintiff's argument that the driver "had no duty to look for hidden pipe lines" mischaracterizes the defense's position. The defense does not claim the driver should have discovered the specific pipeline hazard. Rather, the driver should have recognized that the damaged culvert presented a general hazard and adjusted his driving accordingly. This is not a case of a hidden danger unknown to the driver; it is a case of a visible hazard that the driver chose to confront without adequate precaution.

---

## III. THE DRIVER'S LOSS OF CONTROL WAS A SUPERSEDING CAUSE THAT BREAKS THE CHAIN OF PROXIMATE CAUSATION

Even assuming arguendo that the defendant was negligent in some respect, the driver's loss of control and subsequent collision with the pipeline constitutes a superseding cause that breaks the chain of proximate causation. The defendant cannot be held liable for damages that resulted from the driver's failure to maintain control of his vehicle.

The evidence shows that the driver reached the end of the pavement and was traveling on a narrow detour road. The culvert was visibly damaged. A reasonable driver would have approached this crossing with caution, yet the driver apparently failed to do so and lost control of his truck. This loss of control was not a foreseeable consequence of the pipeline's placement; it was an independent act for which the driver bears responsibility.

The plaintiff's reliance on the "eggshell skull" doctrine is misplaced. That doctrine applies when a defendant's negligence makes a plaintiff more susceptible to injury from an event that would otherwise cause minor harm. It does not apply when the plaintiff's own negligence is the proximate cause of the injury. Here, the driver's loss of control was the immediate cause of the collision. Had the driver maintained control of his vehicle, no collision would have occurred regardless of the pipeline's condition.

The defendant created no "hazardous condition" in the sense meant by negligence law. The pipeline was situated in a culvert, and while it may not have been buried at the depth the plaintiff now claims was required, it was not lying exposed on a traveled portion of the roadway. The driver chose to traverse a damaged culvert and lost control. This is the type of independent, unforeseeable act that severs the causal chain.

---

## IV. THE DEFENDANT TOOK REASONABLE PRECAUTIONS UNDER THE CIRCUMSTANCES

The defendant did not ignore its duty of care. To the contrary, it routed the pipeline through an existing drainage culvert, a decision that provided physical protection from most vehicular traffic. The culvert was not a new structure imposed on the landscape; it was an established drainage feature that the defendant reasonably relied upon for pipeline protection.

The plaintiff's claim that the defendant "had knowledge" of prior incidents involving vehicles missing or slipping off the culvert does not establish negligence per se. Knowledge of occasional incidents does not impose a duty to redesign every crossing. The defendant could reasonably conclude that most drivers would navigate the culvert safely, particularly if they exercised due care. The defendant was not required to anticipate that drivers would consistently lose control at this location.

Furthermore, the plaintiff has not established that the installation method used by the defendant violated any applicable safety regulations or industry standards at the time of installation. The claim that the pipe should have been placed "18 inches below the culvert" reflects a standard the plaintiff now advocates with hindsight, not one that was required at the time of installation. The defendant relied on its engineering judgment, and absent evidence of a specific regulatory violation, this judgment should not be second-guessed by this Court.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and enter judgment in favor of the defendant. The plaintiff has failed to establish that the defendant breached any duty of care owed to the traveling public. The pipeline was situated in an existing culvert, and while the defendant could have taken additional precautions, its conduct fell within the bounds of reasonable care.

More fundamentally, the plaintiff's driver contributed to his own injury by failing to exercise due caution when approaching a visibly damaged culvert crossing. A driver who encounters a crumpled and bent structure on a detour road must exercise heightened care. The driver's failure to do so constitutes contributory negligence that bars recovery.

Finally, the driver's loss of control was an independent act that constitutes a superseding cause. Regardless of any deficiency in the pipeline's installation, the defendant cannot be held liable for injuries that resulted from the driver's failure to maintain control of his vehicle.

The defense submits that the trial court's judgment was erroneous and should be reversed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The case requires this Court to determine whether the defendant gas company breached its duty of care by failing to adequately protect a pipeline situated at a hazardous culvert crossing, and whether the plaintiff’s driver is barred from recovery due to contributory negligence or assumption of risk. After careful consideration of the arguments and the evidence provided, the Court finds that the defendant’s negligence was the proximate cause of the damage and that the defense’s theories regarding contributory negligence and superseding cause are insufficient to overturn the trial court’s judgment. The judgment is affirmed.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The "Highest Degree of Care" Encompasses Protection Against Foreseeable External Impacts**

The defense argues that the "highest degree of care" standard articulated in *City of Los Angeles v. Southern California Gas Co.*, 31 Cal.2d 688, 192 P.2d 38 (Cal. 1948), is not absolute and should be limited to reasonable safety measures rather than protection against all conceivable external threats. The defense contends that routing the pipeline through the existing drainage culvert constituted a reasonable engineering judgment and sufficient precaution.

This Court rejects the notion that the defendant’s actions met the requisite standard of care. The evidence establishes that the pipe was "completely exposed for a distance of from 2 to 4 feet" at the entry and exit points of the culvert. While the defendant argues the culvert provided protection, this protection was illusory at the precise points where the pipe was most vulnerable. The defendant’s knowledge that vehicles frequently "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off" due to its crumpled condition made the risk of a strike at these exposed entry and exit points not only foreseeable but probable.

As held in *Southern Natural Gas Co. v. Lankford*, 156 So. 2d 184 (Ala. 1963), a gas company is negligent when it fails to guard against foreseeable risks of vehicular damage where it knows the roadway is used by traffic. 156 So. 2d at 186. The defendant’s decision to run the pipe through the culvert, rather than placing it 18 inches below the structure or encasing it in a larger diameter pipe, was a failure to protect against a known hazard. The standard of care requires a gas company to act with foresight regarding the dangerous nature of its product; relying on a damaged culvert to shield an exposed line from vehicles known to deviate from the path falls short of the "highest degree of care" mandated by *City of Los Angeles*.

**B. The "Hidden Hazard" Doctrine Precludes a Finding of Assumption of Risk**

The defense asserts that the driver is barred from recovery under the doctrine of assumption of risk, citing *Davenport v. City of Los Angeles*, 26 Cal. App. 2d 29 (Cal. Ct. App. 1944). The defense argues that the "crumpled and bent" condition of the culvert was a visible danger that the driver voluntarily encountered, thereby imposing a duty of heightened caution on him.

This argument misapprehends the distinction between a visible road defect and a hidden utility line. The controlling precedent is *Williams v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co.*, 44 Cal.2d 820, 285 P.2d 889 (Cal. 1955), which holds that a traveler on a public road is entitled to assume the road is safe and is not required to anticipate hidden instrumentalities, such as gas pipelines. 44 Cal.2d at 825.

While the driver may have been able to observe the damaged culvert, that observation did not provide notice of the hidden gas line lying exposed at the entry and exit points. A "crumpled" culvert suggests a risk of a rough ride or vehicle damage, but it does not inherently signal the presence of a high-pressure gas pipeline capable of catastrophic rupture. The driver was unfamiliar with the detour and had no reason to look for hidden utilities. The trial court’s finding that the driver was not contributorily negligent is supported by the record; the driver was not called upon to look for hidden pipe lines, nor did the visible condition of the culvert provide adequate warning of the specific danger that caused the injury. Therefore, *Williams* governs, and the defense’s reliance on *Davenport* is misplaced.

**C. The Driver’s Actions Were Not a Superseding Cause**

Finally, the defense argues that the driver’s loss of control was a superseding cause that broke the chain of proximate causation. The defense contends that the collision was an independent, unforeseeable act for which the driver bears sole responsibility.

Proximate cause is not broken by a foreseeable intervening act. The defendant was fully aware that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off" due to its condition. The defendant is chargeable with the knowledge of the probable consequence if this exposed pipe were struck by vehicles. Because the defendant knew or should have known that vehicles were likely to strike the exact area where the pipe was exposed, the driver’s deviation from the culvert was a foreseeable intervening event, not a superseding cause.

Had the pipe been properly buried or encased as required by the highest degree of care, the vehicle slipping off the culvert would not have resulted in a gas leak. The defendant’s negligence in leaving the pipe exposed was the direct and proximate cause of the damage. The defense cannot escape liability by blaming the driver for a type of accident that the defendant itself recognized as probable.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defendant failed to exercise the highest degree of care required for the maintenance of gas pipelines near public thoroughfares. The evidence demonstrates that the defendant left the pipeline exposed at known points of vulnerability, despite knowledge that vehicles frequently struck those areas. The plaintiff’s driver, entitled to the protections of the *Williams* doctrine, had no duty to anticipate this hidden hazard and is not barred by contributory negligence or assumption of risk. The chain of proximate causation remains intact. The trial court’s judgment is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
