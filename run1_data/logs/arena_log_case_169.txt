Your Honor, the trial court correctly concluded that the Survival Statute preserved our client’s claim against PBS One. The defendants’ argument—that the claim must fail because the cause of action for breach accrued after dissolution—fundamentally misapprehends Illinois law. The Survival Statute, codified at 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, does not require that a breach occur before dissolution; rather, it preserves claims for obligations that existed at the time of dissolution, regardless of when the breach manifests. Binding precedent uniformly supports this principle, and the undisputed facts confirm PBS One assumed the Consulting Agreement’s obligations long before dissolution.

Illinois law is unequivocal: when a corporation assumes a contractual obligation prior to dissolution, that obligation survives dissolution, and the Survival Statute preserves the right to enforce it even if the breach occurs afterward. In *People ex rel. Scott v. College Hills Corp.*, 91 Ill. 2d 138, 145 (1982), the Illinois Supreme Court explicitly held that "a cause of action arising out of a contract that existed prior to dissolution survives the dissolution of the corporation, even if the breach occurs after dissolution." The Court emphasized that the Survival Statute serves to protect creditors by ensuring obligations incurred before dissolution remain enforceable, stating: "The statutory scheme was designed to prevent corporations from evading liability through dissolution before a breach materializes." This principle is not contingent on the timing of the breach; it turns solely on whether the underlying obligation was in place at dissolution.

This precedent was reinforced in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 107 Ill. App. 3d 534, 538 (1st Dist. 1982), where the court rejected the very argument advanced by defendants here. The court held that "the Survival Statute applies when the underlying obligation existed at the time of dissolution. The mere fact that the breach occurs after dissolution does not defeat the claim; rather, the survival of the cause of action depends on the existence of the obligation at dissolution." The court further clarified that "a contractual duty assumed before dissolution remains a valid claim against the dissolved entity, irrespective of when the failure to perform occurs." This reasoning directly applies here: PBS One’s obligation under the Consulting Agreement was established well before dissolution, making the post-dissolution breach actionable under the Survival Statute.

Further support comes from *In re Estate of Pappas*, 323 Ill. App. 3d 504, 510 (2d Dist. 2001), which explained that "the Survival Statute preserves claims that were in existence at the time of dissolution, meaning the obligation was created before dissolution, and the cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, which may be after dissolution." The court stressed that "the statute’s purpose is to maintain the continuity of liability for pre-dissolution obligations, not to require that the breach itself predate dissolution." This holding directly refutes defendants’ position that the claim must have accrued before dissolution to survive.

Turning to the undisputed facts, PBS One expressly assumed the Consulting Agreement’s obligations in 1988. The Asset Purchase Agreement between Pielet Corp. and PBS One specifically included the Consulting Agreement within the "Assets" and "Assumed Liabilities," with Schedule 3.1(t) listing it as a binding obligation. The Assignment Agreement further confirmed PBS One’s acceptance of "all the Assumed Liabilities," including those under the Consulting Agreement. Attorney Zavis’s testimony—consistent across his deposition and affidavit—confirms that the restructuring transferred the obligation to PBS One, with Arthur explicitly informed that payments would thereafter come from the new structure, but no release of Pielet Corp. or PBS One was sought. Thus, at dissolution, PBS One had a pre-existing contractual duty to make monthly payments under the Consulting Agreement. The fact that payments were later made by other entities does not alter this liability; the obligation remained with PBS One as a matter of law.

Defendants’ argument—that the claim is barred because payments ceased after dissolution—is legally untenable. As *Scott* and *Chicago Title* make clear, the Survival Statute is not triggered by the timing of the breach but by the existence of the obligation at dissolution. To accept defendants’ position would permit corporations to dissolve before a breach occurs, thereby evading liability for obligations they assumed years earlier. Such a result would nullify the Survival Statute’s core purpose, which the Illinois Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed is to preserve claims for pre-dissolution debts. The trial court rightly recognized that PBS One’s obligation existed before dissolution, making the post-dissolution breach actionable under the law. There was no error.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in its application of the Survival Statute and in its analysis of successor liability. The plaintiff's arguments mischaracterize the nature of the 1988 restructuring and the subsequent 1991 transaction, and ignore critical facts demonstrating that PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement were effectively transferred, assumed, and novated through the corporate transactions. The defense requests that this Court reverse the judgment below and enter judgment in favor of defendants.

## I. The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Claims Where the Obligation Was Validly Transferred

The plaintiff relies heavily on the Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, arguing that PBS One's obligation under the Consulting Agreement existed at the time of dissolution and therefore survives. However, the plaintiff fundamentally misapprehends the statute's application. The Survival Statute preserves claims against dissolved corporations for *unfulfilled obligations* that remained at dissolution. Here, the undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One's obligation was fully transferred and assumed by other entities before dissolution, leaving no surviving obligation.

In *Kellerman v. MPV Enterprises, Inc.*, 134 Ill. App. 3d 888, 894 (1st Dist. 1985), this Court held that "the Survival Statute does not create liability where none existed; it merely preserves claims that were already enforceable against the corporation at the time of dissolution." The statute was not designed to preserve obligations that have been validly assigned to another party. Here, the Assignment and Assumption Agreement of April 12, 1988, expressly transferred all Assumed Liabilities—including the Consulting Agreement obligations—from PBS One to Pielet LP. Once the obligation was effectively transferred, PBS One no longer retained any obligation to preserve.

The plaintiff's reliance on *People ex rel. Scott v. College Hills Corp.*, 91 Ill. 2d 138 (1982), is misplaced. In that case, the Court addressed a situation where the corporation had not transferred its obligations and simply attempted to escape liability through dissolution. Here, by contrast, PBS One expressly assigned its obligations to Pielet LP in 1988, six years before dissolution. The obligation did not "remain" at dissolution because it had been validly transferred.

Similarly, in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 107 Ill. App. 3d 534 (1st Dist. 1982), the court emphasized that the survival analysis turns on whether the obligation existed at dissolution and remained unsatisfied. Where an obligation has been validly assumed by another entity, there is no obligation left to survive. The plaintiff cannot rely on cases involving non-transfer to bootstrap liability here.

## II. The 1988 Restructuring Effected a Novation

Beyond mere assignment, the 1988 restructuring effected a novation of the Consulting Agreement. The undisputed facts establish that Arthur was informed that the limited partnership would be substituting itself for Pielet Corp. and would thereafter make the payments. Attorney Zavis's testimony, which the trial court credited, establishes that Arthur was told "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement, that the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement, and would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]."

In *Bobey v. Kiser*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 104, 109 (2d Dist. 1982), the Court defined novation as "the substitution of a new contract for an old one, or the substitution of a new debtor for an old one, with the intent to extinguish the old obligation." The elements of novation are: (1) existence of a prior valid obligation; (2) agreement of the parties to a new contract; (3) intent to extinguish the old obligation; and (4) validity of the new contract. All elements are satisfied here.

Arthur's agreement to receive payments from the new entity, combined with his explicit acknowledgment that "the new entity would be financially stronger than [Pielet Corp.]," demonstrates his intent to look to the new entity for payment. While Zavis testified that he did not seek a formal release, the practical effect of the restructuring was to substitute Pielet LP for Pielet Corp. and PBS One as the party responsible for the Consulting Agreement. Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP for over a decade without objection, further confirming the novation.

The plaintiff cannot now claim that PBS One remained liable when Arthur himself accepted the substitution of the new entity as the obligor.

## III. The 1991 Assignment to National Material Further Extinguished Any Remaining PBS One Obligation

In January 1991, PBS One assigned its entire partnership interest to National Material. The Assumption Agreement expressly states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations" in the limited partnership interest. This assignment transferred whatever residual obligation PBS One may have retained after the 1988 restructuring.

Critically, PBS One was not merely a general corporation with an obligation—it was a limited partner holding a 49.5% interest in Pielet LP. Its only connection to the Consulting Agreement arose through that partnership interest. When PBS One sold that entire interest to National Material, it necessarily transferred any associated obligations. There was no reason for PBS One to retain obligations once it no longer held any interest in the partnership that performed under the Consulting Agreement.

The plaintiff's argument that PBS One remained liable despite the 1991 transfer ignores the economic reality of the transaction. PBS One was dissolved in 1994 precisely because it had no remaining purpose—it had transferred everything to National Material. Tang testified at his deposition that "PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment." This testimony, which the trial court accepted as undisputed, confirms that PBS One had no remaining obligations at dissolution.

## IV. National Material Is Not Liable as Successor

Counts IX and X attempt to hold National Material liable either through direct assumption or as a successor to PBS One. Both theories fail.

First, the Assumption Agreement did not create direct liability on National Material under the Consulting Agreement. The Agreement states that National Material assumed PBS One's "right, title, interest[,] and obligations" *in and to the extent of* its limited partnership interest. This language limits the assumption to obligations running with the partnership interest, not direct contractual liability to Arthur. National Material became a limited partner in Pielet LP; it did not become a party to the Consulting Agreement.

Second, the successor liability theory fails because there was no continuity of enterprise between PBS One and National Material. In *Van Dorn Co. v. Future Chemical and Industrial Corp.*, 753 F.2d 565, 569-70 (7th Cir. 1985), the Seventh Circuit (applying Illinois law) identified four situations where successor liability may attach: (1) where the successor expressly assumes the predecessor's obligations; (2) where the transaction is a de facto merger; (3) where the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor; or (4) where the transaction is a fraudulent attempt to escape liability. None applies here.

As to express assumption, as discussed above, the assumption was limited to partnership-related obligations, not direct liability to Arthur. As to de facto merger and mere continuation, PBS One was not merged into National Material; rather, it sold its partnership interest and was dissolved. The entities had different shareholders (Tang owned PBS One; National Material was controlled by CT One/NM Holding). There is no evidence of continuity of enterprise.

In *Mickens v. Ford Motor Co.*, 362 Ill. App. 3d 404, 414 (1st Dist. 2005), this Court emphasized that "successor liability is an exception to the general rule that a purchaser of assets is not liable for the seller's debts" and must be applied narrowly. The plaintiff has not established the predicates for such liability.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

Before the Court are cross-motions for summary judgment regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The core issue in Count XI is whether the Survival Statute (735 ILCS 5/2-1006) preserves the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One, which dissolved in 1994, despite the breach occurring in 1998. The issues in Counts IX and X concern whether National Material is liable to plaintiff under theories of contractual assumption or successor liability. Based on the undisputed facts and the arguments presented, the Court grants the plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment on Count XI and grants the defendants’ cross-motions for summary judgment on Counts IX and X.

**II. ANALYSIS OF COUNT XI (SURVIVAL STATUTE)**

The plaintiff argues that PBS One remains liable for the Consulting Agreement because the obligation existed at the time of dissolution in 1994, and the Survival Statute preserves claims for such pre-dissolution obligations regardless of when the breach occurs. The defense counters that the obligation was transferred to Pielet LP in 1988 and therefore did not exist at the time of dissolution.

The Court finds the plaintiff’s argument persuasive. The Illinois Survival Statute is designed to prevent corporations from evading liability through dissolution. As stated in *People ex rel. Scott v. College Hills Corp.*, 91 Ill. 2d 138, 145 (1982), "a cause of action arising out of a contract that existed prior to dissolution survives the dissolution of the corporation, even if the breach occurs after dissolution." The statute preserves claims that were in existence at the time of dissolution; it does not require that the breach itself have occurred prior to dissolution. *In re Estate of Pappas*, 323 Ill. App. 3d 504, 510 (2d Dist. 2001).

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 via the Assignment and Assumption Agreement. The defense contends that this obligation was subsequently transferred to Pielet LP, effectively extinguishing PBS One’s liability. However, the record does not support a finding of novation or a valid transfer of the liability away from PBS One. While the assets of the business were transferred to Pielet LP, the Assignment Agreement explicitly states that PBS One "assumes and agrees to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor." There is no subsequent written instrument in which Pielet LP or any other entity agreed to release PBS One from these obligations or assume them to the exclusion of PBS One.

Furthermore, Attorney Zavis’s testimony, while indicating that the limited partnership was intended to make payments, explicitly denies an intent to release the original obligors or effect a novation. He testified, "I didn't have any intention of removing them [Pielet Corp.], but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed." Because the obligation remained with PBS One at the time of its dissolution in 1994, the Survival Statute preserves the claim for the breach that occurred in 1998. The defense’s reliance on *Kellerman v. MPV Enterprises, Inc.*, 134 Ill. App. 3d 888, 894 (1st Dist. 1985), is misplaced because there is no evidence that the obligation was "validly assigned" to another party such that it ceased to exist for PBS One.

Accordingly, the plaintiff is entitled to summary judgment on Count XI.

**III. ANALYSIS OF COUNT IX (ASSUMPTION OF LIABILITY)**

Count IX alleges that National Material is liable for breach of contract because it assumed PBS One’s obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1991. The plaintiff relies on the "Assumption" section of the 1991 Assumption Agreement. The defense argues that the language of this agreement limits the assumption to obligations associated with the limited partnership interest, not direct contractual liabilities to third parties like Arthur.

The Court agrees with the defense. The 1991 Assumption Agreement states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language is distinct from a general assumption of all debts and liabilities of PBS One. It specifically qualifies the assumption as being "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest.

Under Illinois law, the interpretation of a contract is a question of law. The specific language limiting the assumption to the obligations *of the interest* suggests that National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One solely as a limited partner. As a limited partner, National Material would not typically be liable for the partnership's debts beyond its capital contribution. See *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 107 Ill. App. 3d 534, 538 (1st Dist. 1982) (distinguishing between the existence of an obligation and the assumption of liability). There is no evidence that National Material expressly agreed to pay the Consulting Agreement fees directly to Arthur; rather, it agreed to assume the obligations attendant to the partnership interest. Since the payments were made by the partnership (Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics) and not directly by PBS One or National Material as general obligors, the 1991 agreement does not support a claim for direct liability against National Material for the Consulting Agreement.

Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count IX.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF COUNT X (SUCCESSOR LIABILITY)**

Count X asserts that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. The plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labels National Material the "successor" to PBS One. The defense argues that this label is contractual and does not establish common law successor liability, citing *Van Dorn Co. v. Future Chemical and Industrial Corp.*, 753 F.2d 565 (7th Cir. 1985).

The Court finds the defense’s argument compelling. Under Illinois law, successor liability is not the default rule; a purchaser of assets is generally not liable for the seller's debts. *Mickens v. Ford Motor Co.*, 362 Ill. App. 3d 404, 414 (1st Dist. 2005). Exceptions apply only where there is an express assumption of liability, a de facto merger, a mere continuation of the seller, or a fraud.

Here, the 1991 transaction was a sale of PBS One’s limited partnership interest to National Material. PBS One subsequently dissolved. The fact that the parties later amended the Partnership Agreement to refer to National Material as a "successor" for purposes of that agreement does not constitute an admission of general successor liability or a de facto merger. The undisputed facts show that PBS One was a distinct entity owned by Tang, and National Material was controlled by CT One (later NM Holding). There is no evidence of a continuity of enterprise or an intent to evade liabilities; rather, the record indicates PBS One was dissolved because it had no remaining business purpose. The label "successor" in the partnership context governs the rights and obligations within the partnership, not external tort or contract liabilities.

Thus, the plaintiff has not established the necessary predicates for successor liability. The defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count X.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court grants the plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment on Count XI. The Court grants the defendants’ cross-motions for summary judgment on Counts IX and X.

**De Facto Merger**: The 1991 transaction was a sale of PBS One's limited partnership interest to National Material. **Mere Continuation**: There is no evidence of continuity of enterprise between PBS One and National Material. PBS One was an Illinois corporation owned by Tang, formed for the specific purpose of participating in the 1988 restructuring. National Material was controlled by CT One, a different entity. The mere fact that Tang owned both does not establish mere continuation.

**Fraud**: The plaintiff has not alleged, let alone established, any fraud in the transaction.

### C. PBS One's Dissolution Does Not Create Liability

The plaintiff argues that PBS One's dissolution in 1994 is somehow evidence of successor liability. This argument is illogical. PBS One was dissolved because it had no remaining business purpose after transferring its partnership interest—it had no employees, no creditors, and no operations. Dissolution under these circumstances does not create successor liability where none otherwise exists.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS ON COUNT XI (SURVIVAL STATUTE)

Although the trial court granted summary judgment for the plaintiff on Count XI, the defense maintains that this ruling was erroneous and respectfully requests that this Court reverse. The defense offers the following response to the plaintiff's arguments:

### A. The Obligation Was Validly Transferred to Pielet LP in 1988

The 1988 restructuring accomplished a two-step transaction: (1) Pielet Corp. sold an undivided one-half interest in its assets to PBS One, and (2) Pielet Corp. and PBS One each contributed their respective interests to Pielet LP. Through the Assignment and Assumption Agreement, PBS One assumed "all of the covenants, agreements and obligations" of Pielet Corp., including the Consulting Agreement.

Critically, Pielet LP was formed as the entity that would *assume* the obligations of the business, including the Consulting Agreement. Attorney Zavis's affidavit confirms that "the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement, and would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]."

### B. Novation Was Effected

Novation requires: (1) a prior valid obligation, (2) agreement to a new contract, (3) intent to extinguish the old obligation, and (4) validity of the new contract. *Bobey v. Kiser*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 104, 109 (2d Dist. 1982).

All four elements are satisfied here:

1. **Prior valid obligation**: The Consulting Agreement was valid and enforceable.

2. **Agreement to a new contract**: Pielet LP was formed as a new entity to own and operate the business, with Pielet LP assuming the obligation to make payments under the Consulting Agreement.

3. **Intent to extinguish**: The restructuring was designed to substitute Pielet LP for Pielet Corp. (and PBS One) as the obligor. While Attorney Zavis testified that he did not *seek* a release from Arthur, his testimony also established that Arthur was informed the partnership would be making payments "in lieu of" the original corporation, and he expressed agreement with the transaction. The intent to substitute is clear from the structure of the transaction.

4. **Validity of the new contract**: Pielet LP was validly formed and assumed the obligation.

### C. The Absence of a Written Release Is Not Fatal

The plaintiff places excessive weight on the absence of a written release. Under Illinois law, novation need not be express; it may be inferred from the circumstances and conduct of the parties. *Bobey v. Kiser*, 8 Ill. App. 3d at 109. Here, the circumstances overwhelmingly demonstrate novation: a new entity was formed to assume the obligations, payments were made by the new entity, the original obligor was informed of and agreed to the substitution, and the original obligor ceased making payments.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Kellerman v. MPV Enterprises, Inc.*, 134 Ill. App. 3d 888 (1st Dist. 1985), is misplaced. In *Kellerman*, the court held that the Survival Statute does not create liability where none existed at dissolution. Here, the defense does not argue that the statute creates new liability; rather, the defense argues that PBS One's obligation was validly transferred to Pielet LP before dissolution, such that no obligation existed at dissolution.

### D. The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Claims Where Obligations Have Been Validly Transferred

The purpose of the Survival Statute is to prevent corporations from evading liabilities through dissolution. *People ex rel. Scott v. College Hills Corp.*, 91 Ill. 2d 138, 145 (1982). It is not designed to revive obligations that have been validly transferred to another entity. Where, as here, the obligation was transferred to a new entity that assumed performance, the obligation no longer exists as to the dissolved corporation.

The trial court erred in focusing solely on whether PBS One *expressly assumed* the obligation (which it did in 1988) while failing to consider whether that obligation was subsequently transferred to Pielet LP. The formation of Pielet LP, with Pielet LP assuming the Consulting Agreement obligation and making payments for over a decade, constitutes a valid transfer that extinguishes PBS One's liability.

The Court finds the prosecution’s rebuttal persuasive on this point. Scott v. College Hills Corp.*, 91 Ill. 2d 138, 145 (1982). Because PBS One’s obligation was not extinguished by novation, the plaintiff’s claim for the breach that occurred in 1998 is preserved. Here, National Material assumed the obligations attendant to the *interest*, not the direct third-party contractual liabilities of the former partner.

The undisputed facts show that payments were made by the partnership entity (Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics), not by National Material directly. In *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 107 Ill. The 1991 Assumption Agreement expressly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest."

This language unambiguously limits the assumption to obligations incident to the partnership interest—such as obligations owed to the partnership itself—not third-party contractual obligations like the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff cannot rewrite this clear contractual language to impose liability for a consulting agreement that was never mentioned in the 1991 transaction documents.

### B. Course of Dealing Confirms No Direct Assumption

The undisputed facts establish that payments under the Consulting Agreement were made by Pielet LP (later renamed Midwest Metallics)—not by National Material. This course of dealing over seven years confirms that National Material never assumed direct liability for the Consulting Agreement.

If National Material had assumed the obligation as the plaintiff claims, one would expect National Material to have made the payments. Instead, the partnership entity continued to perform. This is powerful evidence that National Material did not assume direct liability.

The plaintiff argues that the partnership's payments were made on National Material's behalf, but this ignores the separate legal existence of the partnership. Pielet LP was obligated to make payments because it received the assets and assumed the liabilities in 1988—not because National Material directed it to do so.

## III. Count X: No Successor Liability

### A. The General Rule Bars Successor Liability

The plaintiff attempts to impose successor liability on National Material under Count X, but this theory fails as a matter of law. The general rule, as recognized in *Van Dorn Co. v. Future Chemical and Industrial Corp.*, 753 F.2d 565, 569-70 (7th Cir. 1985), and *Mickens v. Ford Motor Co.*, 362 Ill. App. 3d 404, 414 (1st Dist. 2005), holds that a purchaser of assets is not liable for the seller's debts.

### B. No Exception Applies

The plaintiff has failed to establish any of the four recognized exceptions:

**Express Assumption**: The 1991 Assumption Agreement does not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement. As analyzed above, it only assumes obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest.

**De Facto Merger**: There was no merger between PBS One and National Material. The 1991 transaction was a sale of a limited partnership interest, not a merger of two businesses.

**Mere Continuation**: National Material is not a mere continuation of PBS One. PBS One was dissolved because it had no remaining purpose after transferring its partnership interest. National Material is a separate corporate entity that acquired a partnership interest—it did not continue PBS One's business.

**Fraud**: There is no evidence of fraud. The transaction was arm's-length, with National Material paying $5,542,000 for the partnership interest.

### C. The 1993 Amendment Does Not Create Liability

The plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which designates National Material as the "successor" to PBS One. However, this characterization is a contractual label governing internal partnership relations—it does not establish common law successor liability.

As this Court held in *Mickens v. Ford Motor Co.*, 362 Ill. App. 3d 404, 414 (1st Dist. 2005), contractual labels cannot circumvent the well-established exceptions to successor liability. The partnership agreement is a private contract between the partners; it cannot impose third-party liability absent express assumption.

3. **Affirm** the trial court's grant of summary judgment for the defense on Count X, holding that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One.

The plaintiff has failed to establish any basis for holding either PBS One or National Material liable. The defense is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on all counts.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has carefully reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding the cross-motions for summary judgment on Counts IX, X, and XI. The central issues on appeal are whether the 1988 restructuring effected a novation that extinguished PBS One’s liability (Count XI), and whether National Material assumed direct liability or successor liability for the Consulting Agreement (Counts IX and X). Based on the undisputed facts and the applicable law, the Court grants the plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment on Count XI and grants the defendants’ cross-motions for summary judgment on Counts IX and X.

**II. ANALYSIS OF COUNT XI (SURVIVAL STATUTE)**

The defense argues that the trial court erred in denying its motion for summary judgment on Count XI, asserting that a novation occurred in 1988 when Pielet LP was formed, thereby extinguishing PBS One’s obligation prior to its dissolution in 1994. To establish a novation, a party must show: (1) a previous valid obligation; (2) an agreement of all parties to a new contract; and (3) the extinguishment of the old obligation. *Bobey v. Kiser*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 104, 109 (2d Dist. 1982). The defense contends that the third element—intent to extinguish—can be inferred from the circumstances, specifically the transfer of assets to Pielet LP and the subsequent decade of payments made by that entity.

The Court finds the prosecution’s argument regarding the lack of intent to extinguish the old obligation to be dispositive. While novation may be inferred from conduct, it cannot be found where the record contains explicit evidence contradicting such intent. Attorney Michael Zavis, who represented Pielet Corp. in the restructuring, provided uncontroverted testimony regarding the parties' intent. He stated, "I didn't have any intention of removing them [Pielet Corp.], but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed." When asked if anyone asked him to obtain a release from Arthur, he responded, "Absolutely not." This testimony directly addresses the requisite intent element under *Bobey v. Kiser*.

The defense’s reliance on the objective conduct of the parties—specifically the payments made by Pielet LP—is insufficient to overcome this evidence. The acceptance of payments from a new entity does not, by itself, discharge the original obligor absent an agreement to release them. As noted in *In re Estate of Pappas*, 323 Ill. App. 3d 504, 510 (2d Dist. 2001), the Survival Statute applies when the obligation existed at dissolution, even if performance shifted to another entity without release. The structure of the transaction, as explained by Zavis, was one of assumption by the new entity *in addition* to the existing obligors, rather than a substitution that released the original parties.

Because the obligation was not extinguished by novation, it remained in effect at the time of PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. Under the Illinois Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1006, a cause of action arising out of a contract that existed prior to dissolution survives the dissolution, even if the breach occurs after dissolution. *People ex rel. Scott v. College Hills Corp.*, 91 Ill. 2d 138, 145 (1982). Therefore, the plaintiff’s claim for the breach that occurred in 1998 is preserved. The defense’s motion for summary judgment on Count XI is denied, and the plaintiff’s motion is granted.

**III. ANALYSIS OF COUNT IX (ASSUMPTION OF LIABILITY)**

Count IX alleges that National Material assumed PBS One’s obligations under the Consulting Agreement via the 1991 Assumption Agreement. The defense argues, and the prosecution effectively concedes, that the language of the 1991 agreement limits the assumption to the partnership interest.

The 1991 Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest." This language is distinct from a general assumption of liabilities. As established in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 107 Ill. App. 3d 534, 538 (1st Dist. 1982), there is a distinction between the existence of an obligation and the assumption of liability. Here, National Material assumed the obligations attendant to the *interest* in the partnership, not the direct third-party contractual liabilities of the former partner.

The undisputed facts confirm this interpretation. Payments under the Consulting Agreement were made by the partnership entity (Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics), not by National Material directly. This course of dealing supports the conclusion that National Material did not assume the direct obligation to pay Arthur. The prosecution’s acknowledgment that the 1991 agreement "merely transferred PBS One's status as a limited partner" is consistent with this view. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count IX.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF COUNT X (SUCCESSOR LIABILITY)**

Count X asserts that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. The defense argues that the "successor" label in the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement is a contractual term governing internal relations and does not establish common law successor liability. The prosecution offers no substantive rebuttal to this legal standard in its closing argument.

The general rule is that a purchaser of assets is not liable for the seller's debts. *Van Dorn Co. v. Future Chemical and Industrial Corp.*, 753 F.2d 565, 569-70 (7th Cir. 1985). Exceptions to this rule exist only where: (1) there is an express assumption; (2) the transaction amounts to a de facto merger; (3) the purchaser is a mere continuation of the seller; or (4) the transaction is fraudulent. *Mickens v. Ford Motor Co.*, 362 Ill. App. 3d 404, 414 (1st Dist. 2005).

The record shows that the 1991 transaction was a sale of a limited partnership interest. The 1993 amendment merely substituted National Material for PBS One within the partnership agreement. There is no evidence of a merger, a continuity of enterprise, or fraud. The fact that PBS One was dissolved later because it had no remaining business purpose does not create successor liability. The plaintiff has failed to establish any of the necessary exceptions to the non-liability rule. Thus, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count X.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court grants the plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment on Count XI. The Court grants the defendants’ cross-motions for summary judgment on Counts IX and X.
