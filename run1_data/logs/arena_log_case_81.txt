# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS
### FOURTH DISTRICT OF TEXAS

---

**APPELLANT'S OPENING BRIEF ADDRESSES** the denial of his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the trial court's judgment.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The trial court properly denied the appellee's motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial. The appellee's claim rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of both the facts and the law. The twenty-month delay between his arrest in December 2006 and his trial in August 2008 does not constitute a constitutional violation. Critically, the evidence demonstrates that the State exercised due diligence once it could lawfully take the appelee into custody, and that the appellee's own conduct—specifically his repeated dismissal of court-appointed counsel—constituted the primary cause of any delay. The appellee's reliance on the death of Marco Toledo does not establish the requisite prejudice under established constitutional standards.

---

## II. THE DELAY IN THIS CASE DOES NOT TRIGGER A CONSTITUTIONAL ANALYSIS

The threshold question in any speedy trial analysis is whether the length of delay is sufficient to trigger a constitutional inquiry. In **Barker v. Wingo**, 407 U.S. 514, 530-31 (1972), the Supreme Court held that the length of delay that "would be considered presumptively prejudicial" varies with the complexity of the case. While no bright-line rule exists, courts generally find delays of one year or more sufficient to trigger analysis in more complex cases. Here, the approximately twenty-month delay between arrest and trial marginally satisfies this threshold.

However, this Court should reject the appellee's attempt to measure the delay from the date of the offense in January 2003. The State correctly asserts that the speedy trial clock begins when the defendant is formally accused—either by arrest or indictment. See **United States v. MacDonald**, 456 U.S. 1, 7 (1982) (holding that the Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial is "attached" to the formal accusation, not the underlying criminal conduct). Here, the formal accusation occurred when Bexar County officials executed the arrest warrant on December 5, 2006, and the State obtained a formal indictment on March 1, 2007.

The appellee's argument that the State should have pursued him while he was incarcerated in Ohio ignores the practical and legal realities of interstate custody. The State could not physically arrest the appelee while he was serving a sentence in Ohio on unrelated charges. The appellee's alleged waiver of extradition, signed in February 2003, did not obligate the State to initiate costly and complicated extradition proceedings while the appelee was serving time for unrelated offenses. The State acted with reasonable diligence by executing the arrest warrant promptly upon the appellee's release from Ohio custody in December 2006.

---

## III. EVEN IF THE DELAY TRIGGERS ANALYSIS, THE BARKER FACTORS WEIGH AGAINST THE APPELLEE

The Supreme Court in **Barker v. Wingo**, 407 U.S. 514 (1972), established a balancing test that considers four factors: (1) the length of delay, (2) the reason for the delay, (3) the defendant's assertion of the right, and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. No single factor is determinative; rather, the court must engage in a "sensitive balancing process." Id. at 533.

### A. The Reason for the Delay

The most critical factor here is the reason for the delay. The record demonstrates that the State bears minimal responsibility for the twenty-month interval between arrest and trial. Rather, the evidence shows that the appelee himself created the circumstances that prolonged the proceedings.

Within ten days of his arrest, the trial court appointed counsel to represent the appelee. On February 16, 2007—just over two months after his arrest—the appelee filed his first motion to dismiss appointed counsel, claiming he had "already received ineffective assistance of counsel." Less than six weeks later, on March 30, 2007, the appelee filed a second motion to dismiss counsel, alleging "a great conflict of interest." On May 22, 2007, defense counsel filed a motion to withdraw, which the trial court granted. The trial court then appointed the appelee a second attorney on July 10, 2007.

Less than eight months later, on February 26, 2008, the appelee's second attorney filed a motion to withdraw. The trial court granted this motion and appointed a third attorney—James Tocci—on the same day. Then, at a June 16, 2008, pretrial hearing, the appelee argued his previously-filed motions to dismiss for lack of speedy trial.

The trial court directly addressed the appelee's conduct, stating: "It might have something to do with the fact that you have filed grievances or made accusations against your lawyers." The court further explained:

> "[I]n all fairness to the lawyers who have been appointed, they are entitled to have time to get ready for trial. They cannot be expected to go to trial even though you may be familiar with the facts of the case, they have an obligation and a duty to review the State's file, especially in light of the charges...."

This Court should accord great weight to the trial court's assessment of the causes of delay. The record supports the trial court's conclusion that the appelee's repeated dismissal of counsel—three separate attorneys in less than a year—was the primary cause of the delays. Each change of counsel necessitated additional time for the new attorney to review the case file, investigate the facts, and prepare for trial. The appellee's own actions, not any inaction by the State, prolonged the proceedings.

The Supreme Court has recognized that docket congestion and administrative delays do not weigh heavily against the State. **Barker**, 407 U.S. at 531. Similarly, here, any minimal delay attributable to the State's docket should be weighed only slightly against the State, while the delay caused by the appellee's own conduct should be weighed heavily against him.

### B. The Appellee's Assertion of the Right

The appellee's assertion of his speedy trial right is entitled to little weight. While he raised the issue at a June 2008 pretrial hearing, the record shows that his primary concern throughout the proceedings was not speedy resolution but rather the dismissal of his court-appointed attorneys. The appellee's multiple motions to dismiss counsel demonstrate that he was actively seeking to delay the proceedings—or at minimum, was willing to sacrifice speedy resolution in favor of obtaining counsel of his choosing.

Moreover, the appellee's conduct throughout the proceedings was inconsistent with a genuine desire for a speedy trial. He cannot simultaneously demand new attorneys—which necessarily requires time for new counsel to prepare—and claim that the resulting delay violated his right to a speedy trial. See **United States v. Taylor**, 487 U.S. 326, 333-34 (1988) (holding that defendant's conduct is relevant to the speedy trial analysis).

### C. Prejudice to the Appellee

The appellee claims he was prejudiced by the death of Marco Toledo, a key witness, before trial. He further claims the trial court admitted statements made by Toledo to other witnesses, depriving him of the opportunity to cross-examine Toledo. These claims fail for several reasons.

First, the prejudice inquiry under **Barker** focuses on whether the delay impaired the defendant's ability to present his defense. 407 U.S. at 532. The mere death of a witness, without more, does not establish the requisite prejudice. The Supreme Court has held that "the possibility that the defense might be prejudiced is not enough" to demonstrate a speedy trial violation. **United States v. Loud Hawk**, 474 U.S. 302, 315 (1986).

Second, the appellee's claim that he was denied the opportunity to cross-examine Toledo is misleading. The trial court admitted Toledo's statements through other witnesses under exceptions to the hearsay rule. The appellee had the opportunity to cross-examine those witnesses regarding what they heard Toledo say. The hearsay exceptions—including statements against interest and dying declarations, if applicable—were properly invoked, and the appelee's Sixth Amendment rights were not violated by the admission of these statements.

Third, the appellee's claim regarding the "unavailable neighbor's testimony" is unsupported by the record. The appellee has failed to identify what testimony this neighbor would have provided or how it would have materially affected the outcome of the trial. Speculation about potential testimony does not establish prejudice.

Fourth, and most importantly, the appellee's claim of prejudice must be viewed in light of his own contribution to the delay. Even assuming some prejudice existed, the balance of the **Barker** factors does not tip in the appellee's favor when he bears significant responsibility for the delay. See **Loud Hawk**, 474 U.S. at 315 (holding that prejudice must be weighed against the reasons for the delay).

---

## IV. THE STATE DID NOT ACT IN BAD FAITH

The appellee claims the State exhibited bad faith by "waiting for his release from an Ohio prison" before arresting and indicting him. This claim is without merit.

The State cannot be faulted for failing to initiate extradition proceedings while the appelee was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges. Extradition is a costly and time-consuming process, and the State reasonably chose to wait until the appelee completed his Ohio sentence before executing the Texas arrest warrant. This is not bad faith; it is the exercise of reasonable prosecutorial discretion. See **United States v. Lovasco**, 431 U.S. 783, 795 (1977) (holding that the government may delay prosecution for investigative reasons without violating due process, provided the delay is not undertaken to gain a tactical advantage).

Moreover, the appellee's own conduct contributed to any delay. He was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges from February 2003 until December 2006. During this period, he did not contact Texas authorities to offer to return. He did not file a motion to expedite extradition. Instead, he served his Ohio sentence and awaited release. The State cannot be blamed for failing to pursue a defendant who was unavailable due to his own criminal conduct in another state.

---

## V. THE TWENTY-MONTH DELAY IS NOT PRESUMPTIVELY UNREASONABLE

Even considering the full twenty-month period from arrest to trial, this delay is not so lengthy as to presume prejudice. In **Doggett v. United States**, 505 U.S. 647, 652 (1992), the Supreme Court held that delay approaching one year may be presumptively prejudicial in some cases, but the Court emphasized that this presumption is "triggered by delay of such length that the defendant can reasonably allege that the government has held him in limbo for more than a year." Here, the twenty-month delay, while not insignificant, cannot be characterized as the government "holding the defendant in limbo." Rather, the defendant himself, through his repeated dismissal of counsel, contributed significantly to this delay.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the trial court's denial of the appellee's motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial. The State exercised due diligence in bringing the appelee to trial once it could lawfully take custody of him. The appellee's own conduct—specifically his repeated dismissal of court-appointed counsel—constituted the primary cause of any delay. The appellee's claim of prejudice based on the death of Marco Toledo does not establish a constitutional violation, particularly in light of his own contribution to the delay. The trial court's judgment should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Appellant appeals the trial court’s denial of his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial. The Appellant argues that the State violated his rights under the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 10 of the Texas Constitution by failing to bring him to trial promptly. Specifically, the Appellant contends that more than three years elapsed before his indictment and six years before trial, resulting in the loss of a key witness, Marco Toledo. The State contends that the delay was caused primarily by the Appellant’s own conduct in dismissing multiple court-appointed attorneys and that the speedy trial clock did not begin until his arrest in Texas.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The right to a speedy trial is fundamental to the concept of justice. The analysis of whether this right has been violated is governed by the four-factor balancing test set forth in *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 530 (1972). These factors include: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant’s assertion of his right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. No single factor is dispositive, and the court must engage in a sensitive balancing process. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 533.

**A. Length of the Delay**

The threshold inquiry is whether the delay is long enough to be "presumptively prejudicial." *Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647, 652 (1992). The Appellant argues the delay should be measured from the date of the offense in January 2003. However, the Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial "attaches" only upon the initiation of formal judicial proceedings, such as an arrest or indictment. *United States v. MacDonald*, 456 U.S. 1, 7 (1982). The State correctly asserts that the Appellant was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges from February 2003 until December 2006. The State could not have brought the Appellant to trial in Texas during this period. Therefore, the relevant delay period begins on December 5, 2006, when Bexar County officials executed the arrest warrant and took the Appellant into custody, and ends with the trial in August 2008. This results in a delay of approximately twenty months. While this delay exceeds one year and is sufficient to trigger a *Barker* analysis, it is not presumptively prejudicial given the circumstances. *Doggett*, 505 U.S. at 652 n.1.

**B. Reason for the Delay**

The most significant factor in this case is the reason for the delay. The record reveals that the Appellant was the primary cause of the delay between his arrest and trial. The Appellant filed his first motion to dismiss appointed counsel on February 16, 2007, less than three months after his arrest. He filed a second motion to dismiss counsel on March 30, 2007. On May 22, 2007, counsel filed a motion to withdraw, which was granted. A second attorney was appointed on July 10, 2007. On February 26, 2008, the second attorney filed a motion to withdraw, and a third attorney was appointed the same day.

The trial court explicitly found that the Appellant’s repeated grievances and dismissal of counsel were the primary causes of the delay. The court noted, "it might have something to do with the fact that you have filed grievances or made accusations against your lawyers." Delays caused by the defendant, particularly those involving the dismissal of multiple attorneys, weigh heavily against the defendant in the *Barker* analysis. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531. The State demonstrated due diligence in obtaining an indictment within three months of the Appellant’s return to Texas. The subsequent delay was largely attributable to the Appellant’s inability to maintain a consistent attorney-client relationship.

**C. Assertion of the Right**

The Appellant asserted his right to a speedy trial at a pretrial hearing on June 16, 2008. While this assertion was timely once the delay became apparent, it is given less weight because the Appellant’s actions were inconsistent with a genuine desire for a speedy trial. A defendant cannot simultaneously demand new counsel—which necessarily requires time for preparation—and claim that the resulting delay violates his right to a speedy trial. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531-32. The Appellant’s focus on replacing his attorneys suggests he prioritized that objective over the expeditious resolution of his case.

**D. Prejudice to the Defendant**

The Appellant claims he was prejudiced by the death of Marco Toledo, a key witness, and the unavailability of a neighbor. To establish a violation, the Appellant must show that the delay caused actual prejudice, not merely speculative harm. *United States v. Loud Hawk*, 474 U.S. 302, 315 (1986). The record indicates that Toledo died of unrelated causes. The State did not act in bad faith to cause the death, nor did the State delay the prosecution to gain a tactical advantage. *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 795 (1977). Furthermore, the admission of Toledo’s statements through other witnesses, subject to cross-examination, does not rise to the level of constitutional prejudice required to reverse a conviction, especially where the delay was largely caused by the Appellant himself. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531.

**III. CONCLUSION**

After balancing the *Barker* factors, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying the motion to dismiss. The twenty-month delay was primarily caused by the Appellant’s own conduct in dismissing multiple attorneys. The Appellant’s assertion of the right was weakened by his actions, and he has failed to demonstrate the actual prejudice necessary to overcome the weight of the other factors. The State acted with due diligence, and there is no evidence of bad faith.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s judgment is affirmed.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Appellant appeals the trial court’s denial of his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial. The Appellant contends that the State violated his rights under the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 10 of the Texas Constitution. Specifically, the Appellant argues that the delay from the offense date in 2003 to the trial in 2008 resulted in the loss of a key witness, Marco Toledo. The State argues that the speedy trial clock did not begin until the Appellant’s arrest in Texas in 2006 and that the subsequent delay was caused by the Appellant’s own conduct in dismissing multiple court-appointed attorneys. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both parties, the Court affirms the trial court’s judgment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The right to a speedy trial is fundamental, and its violation is determined by the four-factor balancing test established in *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 530 (1972). These factors are: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant’s assertion of his right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 533.

**A. Length of the Delay**

The threshold inquiry is whether the delay is presumptively prejudicial. *Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647, 652 (1992). The Appellant argues the delay should be measured from the date of the offense in January 2003, or at the very least, from February 2003 when he waived extradition. The defense contends that the State’s failure to act on the extradition waiver should be included in the delay calculation.

However, the Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial "attaches" only upon the initiation of formal judicial proceedings, such as an arrest or indictment. *United States v. MacDonald*, 456 U.S. 1, 7 (1982). The Appellant was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges from February 2003 until December 2006. The State could not have brought the Appellant to trial in Texas during this period. While the Appellant’s waiver of extradition in February 2003 may have facilitated an earlier transfer, it did not trigger the constitutional right to a speedy trial under *MacDonald*. Therefore, the relevant delay period begins on December 5, 2006, when Bexar County officials executed the arrest warrant and took the Appellant into custody, and ends with the trial in August 2008. This results in a delay of approximately twenty months. While this delay is sufficient to trigger a *Barker* analysis, it is not presumptively prejudicial given the circumstances. *Doggett*, 505 U.S. at 652 n.1.

**B. Reason for the Delay**

The most significant factor is the reason for the delay. The Appellant argues that his motions to dismiss counsel were based on "valid reasons," such as ineffective assistance and conflicts of interest, and therefore should not be weighed against him. The defense further asserts that the State’s failure to pursue extradition in 2003 constitutes a failure of due diligence.

The record reveals that the Appellant was the primary cause of the delay between his arrest and trial. The Appellant filed his first motion to dismiss appointed counsel on February 16, 2007, less than three months after his arrest. He filed a second motion to dismiss counsel on March 30, 2007. On May 22, 2007, counsel filed a motion to withdraw, which was granted. A second attorney was appointed on July 10, 2007. On February 26, 2008, the second attorney filed a motion to withdraw, and a third attorney was appointed the same day.

While the Appellant has a right to effective counsel, the repeated dismissal of attorneys necessitates time for new counsel to prepare. The trial court explicitly found that the Appellant’s repeated grievances and dismissal of counsel were the primary causes of the delay. The court noted, "it might have something to do with the fact that you have filed grievances or made accusations against your lawyers." Delays caused by the defendant, even those ostensibly made to secure competent representation, weigh against the defendant in the *Barker* analysis. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531. The State demonstrated due diligence in obtaining an indictment within three months of the Appellant’s return to Texas. The subsequent delay was largely attributable to the Appellant’s inability to maintain a consistent attorney-client relationship. The State’s inaction regarding extradition prior to the attachment of the right in 2006 does not negate the Appellant's responsibility for the delay that occurred after the right attached.

**C. Assertion of the Right**

The Appellant asserts that he timely asserted his right to a speedy trial at a pretrial hearing on June 16, 2008. The defense argues this assertion was entitled to "strong evidentiary weight" because it was made at the first opportunity after the delays became apparent.

However, the Appellant’s assertion was made approximately 18 months after his arrest and only after the appointment of his third attorney. While the assertion was timely once the delay became apparent, it is given less weight because the Appellant’s actions were inconsistent with a genuine desire for a speedy trial. A defendant cannot simultaneously demand new counsel—which necessarily requires time for preparation—and claim that the resulting delay violates his right to a speedy trial. *United States v. Taylor*, 487 U.S. 326, 333–34 (1988). The Appellant’s focus on replacing his attorneys suggests he prioritized that objective over the expeditious resolution of his case.

**D. Prejudice to the Defendant**

The Appellant claims he was "severely" prejudiced by the death of Marco Toledo, a key witness, and the unavailability of a neighbor. The defense distinguishes *United States v. Loud Hawk*, 474 U.S. 302, 315 (1986), arguing that the loss of a specific, identified witness constitutes actual prejudice rather than speculative harm.

The Court acknowledges that the death of a key witness constitutes actual prejudice. *Doggett*, 505 U.S. at 654. However, to establish a violation, the Appellant must show that the delay caused the prejudice. *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 795 (1977). The record indicates that Toledo died of unrelated causes. The State did not act in bad faith to cause the death, nor did the State delay the prosecution to gain a tactical advantage. *Lovasco*, 431 U.S. at 795.

Crucially, the prejudice factor must be weighed against the reason for the delay. Because the Appellant was the primary cause of the delay during the relevant period (post-arrest), he cannot now claim that the resulting prejudice warrants a reversal. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531. The Appellant’s decision to dismiss multiple attorneys directly contributed to the passage of time during which the witness died. Therefore, the prejudice does not outweigh the Appellant’s responsibility for the delay.

**III. CONCLUSION**

After balancing the *Barker* factors, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying the motion to dismiss. The twenty-month delay was primarily caused by the Appellant’s own conduct in dismissing multiple attorneys. The Appellant’s assertion of the right was weakened by his actions, and while he suffered actual prejudice due to the loss of a witness, that prejudice is attributable to his own delay tactics. The State acted with due diligence, and there is no evidence of bad faith.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s judgment is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Appellant appeals the trial court’s denial of his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial. The Appellant contends that the delay from the offense date in 2003 to the trial in 2008 violated his rights under the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 10 of the Texas Constitution. Specifically, the Appellant argues that the State’s failure to extradite him in 2003, combined with the subsequent loss of a key witness, Marco Toledo, requires reversal. The State argues that the speedy trial clock did not begin until the Appellant’s arrest in Texas in 2006 and that the subsequent delay was caused by the Appellant’s own conduct in dismissing multiple court-appointed attorneys. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, the Court affirms the trial court’s judgment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The right to a speedy trial is fundamental, and its violation is determined by the four-factor balancing test established in *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 530 (1972). These factors are: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant’s assertion of his right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 533.

**A. Length of the Delay and Attachment of the Right**

The threshold inquiry is whether the delay is presumptively prejudicial. *Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647, 652 (1992). The Appellant argues the delay should be measured from the date of the warrant in February 2003, or at the very least, from February 2003 when he waived extradition. The defense contends that the State’s failure to act on the extradition waiver constitutes a violation that should be included in the delay calculation.

However, the Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial "attaches" only upon the initiation of formal judicial proceedings, such as an arrest or indictment. *United States v. MacDonald*, 456 U.S. 1, 7 (1982). The Appellant was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges from February 2003 until December 2006. The State could not have brought the Appellant to trial in Texas during this period. While the defense argues that the issuance of the warrant and the lodging of a detainer constituted the initiation of proceedings, the Supreme Court in *MacDonald* rejected the argument that the government's obligation to provide a speedy trial arises before the accused is arrested or indicted. The Appellant’s waiver of extradition in February 2003 may have facilitated an earlier transfer, but it did not trigger the constitutional right to a speedy trial under *MacDonald*. Therefore, the relevant delay period begins on December 5, 2006, when Bexar County officials executed the arrest warrant and took the Appellant into custody, and ends with the trial in August 2008. This results in a delay of approximately twenty months. While this delay is sufficient to trigger a *Barker* analysis, it is not presumptively prejudicial given the circumstances. *Doggett*, 505 U.S. at 652 n.1.

**B. Reason for the Delay**

The most significant factor is the reason for the delay. The Appellant argues that his motions to dismiss counsel were based on "valid reasons," such as ineffective assistance and conflicts of interest, and therefore should not be weighed against him. The defense further asserts that the State’s failure to pursue extradition in 2003 constitutes bad faith and a lack of due diligence.

The record reveals that the Appellant was the primary cause of the delay between his arrest and trial. The Appellant filed his first motion to dismiss appointed counsel on February 16, 2007, less than three months after his arrest. He filed a second motion to dismiss counsel on March 30, 2007. On May 22, 2007, counsel filed a motion to withdraw, which was granted. A second attorney was appointed on July 10, 2007. On February 26, 2008, the second attorney filed a motion to withdraw, and a third attorney was appointed the same day.

While the Appellant has a right to effective counsel, and the record reflects that his motions to dismiss cited specific concerns such as ineffective assistance and conflict of interest, the repeated dismissal of attorneys necessitates time for new counsel to prepare. The trial court explicitly found that the Appellant’s repeated grievances and dismissal of counsel were the primary causes of the delay. The court noted, "it might have something to do with the fact that you have filed grievances or made accusations against your lawyers." Delays caused by the defendant, even those ostensibly made to secure competent representation, weigh against the defendant in the *Barker* analysis. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531. The State demonstrated due diligence in obtaining an indictment within three months of the Appellant’s return to Texas. The subsequent delay was largely attributable to the Appellant’s inability to maintain a consistent attorney-client relationship.

Regarding the State’s inaction between 2003 and 2006, while the defense characterizes the failure to extradite as "sitting on the case" or bad faith, the right to a speedy trial had not yet attached under *MacDonald*. Therefore, the State’s lack of diligence during this period does not weigh heavily in the *Barker* analysis. The State’s inaction regarding extradition prior to the attachment of the right in 2006 does not negate the Appellant's responsibility for the delay that occurred after the right attached.

**C. Assertion of the Right**

The Appellant asserts that he timely asserted his right to a speedy trial at a pretrial hearing on June 16, 2008. The defense argues this assertion was entitled to "strong evidentiary weight" because it was made at the first opportunity after the delays became apparent.

However, the Appellant’s assertion was made approximately 18 months after his arrest and only after the appointment of his third attorney. While the assertion was timely once the delay became apparent, it is given less weight because the Appellant’s actions were inconsistent with a genuine desire for a speedy trial. A defendant cannot simultaneously demand new counsel—which necessarily requires time for preparation—and claim that the resulting delay violates his right to a speedy trial. *United States v. Taylor*, 487 U.S. 326, 333–34 (1988). The Appellant’s focus on replacing his attorneys suggests he prioritized that objective over the expeditious resolution of his case.

**D. Prejudice to the Defendant**

The Appellant claims he was "severely" prejudiced by the death of Marco Toledo, a key witness, and the unavailability of a neighbor. The defense argues that the death of Toledo constitutes actual prejudice and that the State should not be permitted to benefit from its own inaction.

The Court acknowledges that the death of a key witness constitutes actual prejudice. *Doggett*, 505 U.S. at 654. However, to establish a violation, the Appellant must show that the delay caused the prejudice. *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 795 (1977). The record indicates that Toledo died of unrelated causes. The State did not act in bad faith to cause the death, nor did the State delay the prosecution to gain a tactical advantage. *Lovasco*, 431 U.S. at 795.

Crucially, the prejudice factor must be weighed against the reason for the delay. Because the Appellant was the primary cause of the delay during the relevant period (post-arrest), he cannot now claim that the resulting prejudice warrants a reversal. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531. The Appellant’s decision to dismiss multiple attorneys directly contributed to the passage of time during which the witness died. The defense argues that the State’s initial failure to extradite created the window for this prejudice, but because the speedy trial right had not attached during that period, the State’s negligence in 2003 does not shift the constitutional burden for the post-2006 delay to the State. Therefore, the prejudice does not outweigh the Appellant’s responsibility for the delay.

**III. CONCLUSION**

After balancing the *Barker* factors, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying the motion to dismiss. The twenty-month delay was primarily caused by the Appellant’s own conduct in dismissing multiple attorneys. The Appellant’s assertion of the right was weakened by his actions, and while he suffered actual prejudice due to the loss of a witness, that prejudice is attributable to his own delay tactics. The State acted with due diligence, and there is no evidence of bad faith.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s judgment is affirmed.
