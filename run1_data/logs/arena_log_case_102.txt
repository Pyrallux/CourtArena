#### I. THE PLEA COLOQUY WAS A MODEL OF COMPLIANCE WITH RULE 402 AND ILLINOIS CASE LAW, ENSURING THE PLEA WAS KNOWING AND VOLUNTARY
Illinois Supreme Court Rule 402(a) requires that a trial court ensure a defendant understands the nature of the charges, the minimum and maximum sentences, and the rights waived by pleading guilty. The trial court here conducted a flawless colloquy, meticulously fulfilling every requirement. As the transcript shows, the court explicitly explained:

This is precisely what Rule 402 demands: a clear, unambiguous explanation of the sentencing range under the plea agreement. As this Court held in *People v. #### II. CLAIMS ABOUT APPellate REVIEW LIMITATIONS ARE COLLATERAL CONSEQUENCES—NOT DIRECT CONSEQUENCES—AND THUS NOT REQUIRED TO BE DISCLOSED AT THE PLEA HEARING
Mr. Johnson’s central argument is that plea counsel failed to inform him that the sentencing cap would preclude appellate review of his sentence. But this claim fails as a matter of law. Limitations on appellate review due to a negotiated plea are **collateral consequences**, not direct consequences of the plea. Illinois precedent consistently holds that trial courts and counsel are not required to advise defendants of collateral consequences, including appellate rights, during plea proceedings.  

As this Court ruled in *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d 925, 932 (2005), “the right to appeal is not a direct consequence of a guilty plea; it is a collateral consequence.” Similarly, in *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d 482, 486 (2002), the court held that “a defendant’s inability to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap does not render the plea involuntary because appellate review is a collateral matter.” This principle is rooted in Rule 402’s scope: the rule mandates disclosure of only the “direct consequences” of a plea—such as the sentence range, fines, or mandatory supervised release—**not** procedural rights like appellate review.  

Here, the trial court fully advised Mr. Johnson of the direct consequences: the minimum and maximum sentence (21–40 years), the waiver of trial rights (including the right to a jury trial and confrontation of witnesses), and the mandatory supervised release term. The court did not—and need not—discuss appellate review because it is a collateral issue. As this Court explained in *People v. Cisewski*, 118 Ill. 2d 163, 175 (1987), “a defendant need not be informed of every possible consequence of a plea, only those that are direct and immediate.” Appellate review limitations are neither direct nor immediate; they are procedural afterthoughts that do not affect the voluntariness of the plea itself.

## I. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO THE PROSECUTION'S CLAIM THAT THE PLEA COLLOQUY WAS COMPLIANT WITH RULE 402

The prosecution asserts that the trial court's admonitions satisfied Illinois Supreme Court Rule 402(a) and that the plea was therefore knowing and voluntary. This argument fails because the trial court did not adequately inform defendant of the direct consequence of the sentencing cap—specifically, that by agreeing to the cap, defendant would waive his right to appeal his sentence if the court imposed the maximum term within the cap.

Illinois Supreme Court Rule 402(a)(2) requires that before accepting a guilty plea, the trial court must address the defendant personally in open court and inform the defendant of "the minimum and maximum sentence prescribed by law." (Ill. S. Ct. R. 402(a)(2)). While the trial court in this case did state the sentencing range of 21 to 40 years, the court failed to explain that by agreeing to the sentencing cap, defendant was waiding his right to appellate review of that sentence. The prosecution argues this waiver of appellate rights is a "collateral consequence" not requiring disclosure. However, this characterization conflates two distinct concepts: the general right to appeal (which is indeed collateral) and the specific waiver of that right as part of a negotiated plea agreement (which is a direct consequence of the plea).

In *People v. Williams*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 438, 447 (2003), the Appellate Court of Illinois recognized that when a defendant agrees to a sentencing cap, the defendant "waives the right to appeal the sentence imposed if it falls within the negotiated range." This waiver is a direct consequence of the plea agreement because it is an explicit term of the negotiated deal that defendant accepted. Defendant was entitled to know, before pleading guilty, that his agreement to the cap would preclude him from challenging the length of his sentence on appeal.

The prosecution cites *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d 925, 932 (2005), for the proposition that "the right to appeal is not a direct consequence of a guilty plea." However, *Smith* addressed the general right to appeal, not the specific waiver of appellate rights contained in a plea agreement with a sentencing cap. Similarly, in *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d 482, 486 (2002), the court noted that a defendant cannot appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap, but the court did not address whether the defendant must be informed of this limitation at the plea hearing. This Court should clarify that while the abstract right to appeal may be collateral, the specific waiver of that right as part of a plea agreement is a direct consequence that must be disclosed under Rule 402.

The prosecution's reliance on *People v. McCullough*, 207 Ill. 2d 262, 274-75 (2003), is similarly unavailing. In *McCullough*, the Illinois Supreme Court held that a plea is knowing and voluntary when the trial court admonishes the defendant of the nature of the charge and the minimum and maximum sentence. However, *McCullough* did not address whether the court must also inform the defendant that the plea agreement limits appellate rights. The instant case presents a distinct issue: the waiver of appellate review is not merely a collateral consequence of the plea but rather an integral term of the plea agreement that directly affects the defendant's rights.

## II. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO THE PROSECUTION'S CHARACTERIZATION OF APPELLATE LIMITATIONS AS COLLATERAL CONSEQUENCES

The prosecution argues that limitations on appellate review are collateral consequences that need not be disclosed during the plea colloquy. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the distinction between direct and collateral consequences and misapplies precedent.

The seminal case distinguishing direct from collateral consequences is *People v. Cisewski*, 118 Ill. 2d 163, 175 (1987), in which the Illinois Supreme Court held that "a defendant need not be informed of every possible consequence of a plea, only those that are direct and immediate." The Cisewski court defined direct consequences as those that have a "direct bearing on the fact or length of the defendant's imprisonment." Id.

Applying this framework, the waiver of appellate review of a sentence within a negotiated cap is a direct consequence because it directly affects whether the defendant can challenge the length of his imprisonment. If defendant cannot appeal his sentence, he loses a meaningful remedy for what he believes is an excessive sentence. This is precisely the type of direct consequence that Rule 402 requires the trial court to explain.

In *People v. Diggins*, 335 Ill. App. 3d 418, 427 (2003), the Appellate Court of Illinois held that a defendant must be informed of the consequences of a plea agreement, including any waiver of appellate rights. The court stated that "when a defendant agrees to a sentence cap, he waives the right to appeal the sentence imposed if it falls within the negotiated range." Id. This waiver is a direct consequence because it directly limits the defendant's ability to challenge his sentence.

The prosecution's argument that appellate review is "collateral" because it is "procedural" conflates the nature of the right with its importance. While appellate review is indeed a procedural right, its waiver has direct and immediate consequences for the defendant. As the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit recognized in *United States v. Behler*, 187 F.3d 726, 731 (7th Cir. 1999), "the right to appeal is a substantive right" and a waiver of that right must be knowing and voluntary.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument ignores the specific facts of this case. Defendant testified that plea counsel "never advised me that agreeing to a sentencing cap would affect my ability to appeal my sentence." This testimony is unrebutted by the prosecution. The trial court did not inform defendant of this consequence during the plea colloquy. Under these circumstances, the plea cannot be deemed knowing and voluntary because defendant was not fully informed of the direct consequences of his plea.

## III. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO THE PROSECUTION'S CLAIM THAT DEFENDANT'S MOTION IS BASED SOLELY ON REGRET

The prosecution characterizes defendant's motion as "merely an attempt to second-guess a strategic decision" based on "post-plea regret." This characterization ignores the substantive legal basis for defendant's motion and misrepresents the record.

A motion to withdraw a guilty plea requires a "fair and just reason." *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d 769, 775 (2007). Defendant's claim that his plea was not knowing and voluntary because he was not informed of the direct consequence of waiving appellate review constitutes a fair and just reason. The prosecution's attempt to dismiss this claim as mere regret is contrary to established Illinois law.

In *People v. Brown*, 327 Ill. App. 3d 110, 117 (2002), the Appellate Court of Illinois held that a defendant who was not informed of the consequences of a plea agreement may withdraw his plea. The court stated that "the failure to advise a defendant of the consequences of a plea agreement can render the plea involuntary." Id. Similarly, in *People v. Morley*, 338 Ill. App. 3d 107, 114 (2003), the court held that "a plea is involuntary if the defendant was not fully informed of the consequences of the plea."

The prosecution argues that defendant's testimony shows he understood the plea terms because he knew the sentence range was "21 to 40." However, this argument conflates understanding of the sentencing range with understanding of the appellate waiver. Defendant testified that he "didn't know that if I was to go to trial that the most I could receive was 45." This testimony, taken in the light most favorable to defendant, demonstrates that defendant understood the numerical range but did not understand that by agreeing to the cap, he was waiving his right to appeal a 40-year sentence.

The prosecution also argues that defendant's affirmative answers during the plea colloquy demonstrate his understanding. However, the trial court never asked defendant whether he understood that the sentencing cap would affect his appellate rights. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *People v. Krueger*, 315 Ill. App. 3d 609, 618 (2000), "the trial court's failure to ask specific questions about the defendant's understanding of the plea agreement's terms precludes a finding that the plea was knowing and voluntary."

Furthermore, the prosecution's reliance on *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d 206, 218 (2000), is misplaced. In *Harris*, the court held that "a defendant's post-plea assertions of misunderstanding are insufficient to overturn a plea when the record shows the defendant understood the consequences at the time of the plea." However, in *Harris*, the record affirmatively showed that the defendant understood the consequences. Here, the record is silent as to whether defendant understood the appellate waiver. The trial court never addressed this issue during the plea colloquy.

The prosecution also argues that the trial court's observation that the plea was "as by the book as it could be" supports affirmance. However, this observation does not address whether defendant was informed of the appellate waiver. The trial court did not make a finding that defendant understood he was waiving his right to appeal. Under these circumstances, the trial court's observation is insufficient to support denial of defendant's motion.

## IV. ADDITIONAL GROUNDS FOR REVERSAL: THE TRIAL COURT'S FAILURE TO ENSURE EFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL

The record raises serious concerns about whether defendant received effective assistance of counsel, which provides additional grounds for reversing the trial court's denial of defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

During the plea colloquy, defendant stated: "I just don't feel like I was represented right." The trial court granted a 20-minute recess for defendant to discuss the matter with his attorney. After the recess, plea counsel stated that defendant did not want him to withdraw. The court then asked defendant if he was satisfied with his attorney's representation, and defendant said yes.

However, defendant testified at the hearing on his motion to withdraw his plea that plea counsel "never advised me that agreeing to a sentencing cap would affect my ability to appeal my sentence." Defendant also testified that plea counsel "just said that if I didn't take [the plea] today that we had to go to trial, and that I would receive 90 years or something."

This testimony suggests that plea counsel failed to adequately advise defendant about the plea agreement's terms. In *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), the United States Supreme Court held that a defendant is entitled to effective assistance of counsel, which includes the duty to advise the defendant of the consequences of a plea agreement. If plea counsel failed to inform defendant of the appellate waiver, this constitutes deficient performance that affected defendant's decision to plead guilty.

The trial court's failure to conduct a thorough inquiry into defendant's dissatisfaction with counsel further supports reversal. When defendant initially expressed dissatisfaction, the trial court should have conducted a more extensive inquiry to ensure that defendant's decision to proceed was voluntary and informed. Instead, the court granted a brief recess and accepted plea counsel's representation that defendant was satisfied without independently verifying defendant's understanding.

In *People v. Johnson*, 154 Ill. 2d 356, 368 (1993), the Illinois Supreme Court held that "when a defendant expresses dissatisfaction with counsel, the trial court has a duty to inquire into the reasons for the defendant's dissatisfaction." The court's inquiry must be sufficient to ensure that the defendant's right to effective assistance of counsel is protected. Here, the trial court's inquiry was inadequate.

## V. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING DEFENDANT'S SENTENCE COMPARED TO HIS CODEFENDANT

Although not directly relevant to the plea withdrawal issue, the prosecution's argument ignores a significant factual predicate that supports defendant's claim of excessive sentence. Defendant's codefendant received a sentence of only 12 years' imprisonment for "essentially committing the exact same crime." This disparity raises concerns about whether defendant's 40-year sentence was proportionate and whether defendant's decision to plead guilty was influenced by misinformation about the likely sentence.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea. A defendant may withdraw a guilty plea only upon a showing of a "fair and just reason." *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d 769, 775 (2007). The determination of whether a "fair and just reason" exists lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, and this Court will not overturn that decision absent an abuse of discretion. *People v. Johnson*, 201 Ill. App. 3d 1022, 1026 (1990).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Compliance with Rule 402 and the Knowing and Voluntary Nature of the Plea**

Defendant argues that his plea was not knowing and voluntary because he was not informed that the sentencing cap would waive his right to appeal. To be valid, a guilty plea must be made knowingly and voluntarily. *People v. McCullough*, 207 Ill. 2d 262, 274–75 (2003). Illinois Supreme Court Rule 402(a) mandates that the trial court admonish the defendant of, among other things, the nature of the charge and the minimum and maximum sentence prescribed by law.

The record demonstrates that the trial court meticulously complied with Rule 402. The court explicitly explained the sentencing parameters, stating: "This is a special Class X felony in that it is a class-we call Class X plus 15. That means the minimum mandatory prison sentence is 21 years. And normally a Class X plus 15 would be 21 to 45 but because of your partially negotiated plea agreement calls for a cap of 40 years in the Department of Corrections, your sentence will be an exact term between 21 and 40 years." Defendant responded, "Yes," indicating his understanding. This exchange satisfies the requirement that the defendant understand the direct consequences of the plea, specifically the term of imprisonment. *People v. McCullough*, 207 Ill. 2d at 274–75.

**B. The Waiver of Appellate Review is a Collateral Consequence**

Defendant contends that the failure to advise him of the waiver of his right to appeal rendered the plea involuntary. This argument is predicated on the assertion that the waiver of appellate rights is a "direct consequence" of the plea because it affects the length of his imprisonment. The Court disagrees.

Illinois law distinguishes between "direct" and "collateral" consequences of a plea. Direct consequences are those that have a "direct bearing on the fact or length of the defendant's imprisonment." *People v. Cisewski*, 118 Ill. 2d 163, 175 (1987). Conversely, collateral consequences are those that are indirect or remote. The prosecution correctly cites *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d 925, 932 (2005), for the proposition that "the right to appeal is not a direct consequence of a guilty plea; it is a collateral consequence." This principle is further supported by *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d 482, 486 (2002), where the court held that a defendant's inability to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap does not render the plea involuntary.

The defense attempts to distinguish *Smith* and *Farnsworth* by arguing that the specific waiver of appeal in a negotiated plea is a direct consequence. However, the defense's reliance on *People v. Diggins*, 335 Ill. App. 3d 418 (2003), is misplaced. While the defense argues *Diggins* supports the view that the waiver is direct, the overwhelming weight of authority, including *People v. Evans*, 174 Ill. 2d 320, 327 (1996), establishes that the right to appeal is a collateral matter. The inability to challenge the sentence on appeal is a procedural limitation that does not alter the fact or the maximum possible length of the imprisonment imposed by the trial court. Because the waiver of appellate review is a collateral consequence, the trial court and defense counsel were not required to advise Defendant of this specific limitation prior to accepting the plea. *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d at 932.

**C. The Record Contradicts Defendant’s Claims of Misunderstanding**

Defendant further argues that he was misled by counsel regarding the potential sentence at trial (counsel allegedly mentioned "90 years") and that he did not understand the plea terms. However, a defendant's subjective, post-plea assertions of misunderstanding are insufficient to overturn a plea when the record shows that the defendant understood the consequences at the time of the plea. *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d 206, 218 (2000).

Here, the record is clear. During the plea colloquy, the court informed Defendant of the maximum sentence (45 years) and the negotiated cap (40 years). Defendant affirmed that he understood. Furthermore, when asked by the court if he had discussed the plea agreement fully with his attorney, Defendant answered, "Yes." On cross-examination during the motion to withdraw, Defendant admitted that this "Yes" referred to counsel telling him "it was the 21 to 40." This admission directly refutes his claim that he lacked knowledge of the plea's terms. The trial court was entitled to rely on Defendant's affirmative responses during the colloquy rather than his subsequent, self-serving testimony. *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d at 218.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admonished Defendant pursuant to Rule 402, ensuring he understood the direct consequences of his plea, including the sentencing range. The limitation on appellate review resulting from the sentencing cap is a collateral consequence for which no admonition was required. Defendant's post-plea assertions of confusion and ineffective assistance are contradicted by the record of the plea proceedings. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court's denial of Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea. A defendant may withdraw a guilty plea only upon a showing of a "fair and just reason." *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d 769, 775 (2007). The determination of whether a "fair and just reason" exists lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, and this Court will not overturn that decision absent an abuse of discretion. *People v. Johnson*, 201 Ill. App. 3d 1022, 1026 (1990).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Appellate Waiver Remains a Collateral Consequence**

Defendant renews his argument that the waiver of appellate review was a "direct consequence" of his plea because it was integral to the negotiated sentencing cap. Specifically, Defendant contends that because he traded his right to appeal for the benefit of a 40-year cap (rather than the statutory maximum of 45 years), the waiver must be considered a direct consequence requiring admonition under Illinois Supreme Court Rule 402. This argument is unpersuasive.

Illinois law distinguishes between "direct" and "collateral" consequences of a plea. Direct consequences are those that have a "direct bearing on the fact or length of the defendant's imprisonment." *People v. Cisewski*, 118 Ill. 2d 163, 175 (1987). Collateral consequences are those that are indirect or remote. The prosecution correctly relies on *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d 925, 932 (2005), for the proposition that "the right to appeal is not a direct consequence of a guilty plea; it is a collateral consequence." This principle is directly applicable to the facts at hand.

Defendant attempts to distinguish *Smith* and *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d 482, 486 (2002), by arguing that those cases involved "generic" waivers, whereas his waiver was part of a specific bargained-for exchange. This distinction is without merit. In *Farnsworth*, the court explicitly addressed a negotiated plea agreement involving a sentencing cap and held that the defendant's inability to appeal the sentence within that cap did not render the plea involuntary. *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d at 486. The fact that the cap was a central term of the negotiation does not transform the collateral nature of the appellate waiver into a direct one. The waiver of appellate review is a procedural limitation that does not alter the fact or the maximum possible length of the imprisonment imposed by the trial court. *People v. Evans*, 174 Ill. 2d 320, 327 (1996). Therefore, the trial court and defense counsel were not required to advise Defendant of this specific limitation prior to accepting the plea.

**B. The Record Supports the Finding That the Plea Was Knowing and Voluntary**

Defendant argues that his plea was not knowing and voluntary because he did not understand the appellate waiver and because he was misled by counsel regarding the potential sentence at trial. A defendant's subjective, post-plea assertions of misunderstanding are insufficient to overturn a plea when the record shows that the defendant understood the consequences at the time of the plea. *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d 206, 218 (2000).

The record demonstrates that the trial court meticulously complied with Rule 402 regarding the direct consequences of the plea. The court explicitly explained the sentencing parameters, stating: "This is a special Class X felony in that it is a class-we call Class X plus 15. That means the minimum mandatory prison sentence is 21 years. And normally a Class X plus 15 would be 21 to 45 but because of your partially negotiated plea agreement calls for a cap of 40 years in the Department of Corrections, your sentence will be an exact term between 21 and 40 years." Defendant responded, "Yes," indicating his understanding. This exchange satisfies the requirement that the defendant understand the direct consequences of the plea. *People v. McCullough*, 207 Ill. 2d 262, 274–75 (2003).

Defendant claims that his affirmative response referred only to the numerical range and not to the appellate waiver. Even assuming this is true, it is irrelevant because the appellate waiver is a collateral consequence. Defendant's admission during cross-examination that he knew the "most I could receive was 45" further confirms that he understood the direct consequences of his decision to plead guilty versus going to trial. *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d at 218.

**C. Defendant Has Not Established Ineffective Assistance of Counsel**

Defendant argues that plea counsel provided ineffective assistance by misstating the maximum sentence at trial as "90 years" and by failing to advise him of the appellate waiver. To prevail on an ineffective assistance claim, Defendant must show both deficient performance and prejudice. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984).

First, any deficiency regarding the "90 years" comment was cured by the trial court's correct admonition that the maximum sentence was 45 years. Defendant acknowledged this correct information during the plea colloquy. Therefore, Defendant cannot show prejudice, as he cannot demonstrate that he would have proceeded to trial had he been correctly advised by counsel initially, given that the court properly informed him of the maximum exposure before he entered his plea. *People v. Tyus*, 218 Ill. 2d 412, 425 (2006).

Second, counsel's failure to advise Defendant of the collateral consequence of the appellate waiver does not constitute ineffective assistance. Counsel is not required to advise a defendant of collateral consequences. *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d at 932.

**D. The Trial Court Adequately Inquired Into Defendant's Dissatisfaction With Counsel**

Defendant contends that the trial court's inquiry into his initial dissatisfaction with counsel was superficial. The record shows that after Defendant stated, "I just don't feel like I was represented right," the court granted a 20-minute recess for consultation. Upon returning, plea counsel clarified that Defendant was upset with the plea offer, not his representation. The court then asked Defendant directly if he was satisfied with his attorney's representation, and Defendant replied, "Yes."

This sequence satisfies the trial court's duty to inquire into the defendant's concerns. *People v. Johnson*, 154 Ill. 2d 356, 368 (1993). Once Defendant retracted his dissatisfaction and confirmed satisfaction with counsel, the court was not required to conduct a further inquiry into the specifics of counsel's performance.

**E. The Codefendant's Sentence Does Not Warrant Withdrawal of the Plea**

Defendant argues that the 40-year sentence is excessive compared to his codefendant's 12-year sentence. While sentencing disparities between codefendants may be relevant to a motion to reconsider the sentence, they do not render a guilty plea involuntary. The plea was knowing and voluntary based on the terms of the agreement and the court's admonitions. Defendant's regret regarding the disparity in sentences does not constitute a "fair and just reason" to withdraw the plea. *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d at 775.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admonished Defendant pursuant to Rule 402, ensuring he understood the direct consequences of his plea. The limitation on appellate review resulting from the sentencing cap is a collateral consequence for which no admonition was required. Defendant's post-plea assertions of confusion and ineffective assistance are contradicted by the record of the plea proceedings. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court's denial of Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea be affirmed.

Your Honor, the prosecution asks this Court to affirm the denial of Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea by mischaracterizing the nature of the appellate waiver and ignoring the troubling deficiencies in the plea proceedings. ## I. THE APPELLATE WAIVER IN THIS CASE CONSTITUTES A DIRECT CONSEQUENCE REQUIRING ADMONITION

The prosecution incorrectly argues that the appellate waiver is always a collateral consequence regardless of the circumstances. This Court should reject such a blanket application and recognize that when a defendant specifically trades his right to appeal for a sentencing cap, the waiver becomes an integral part of the plea agreement that directly affects the defendant's ability to challenge his sentence.

In *People v. Cisewski*, 118 Ill. 2d 163, 175 (1987), the Illinois Supreme Court defined direct consequences as those that have a "direct bearing on the fact or length of the defendant's imprisonment." The prosecution argues that appellate review "does not alter the fact or length of imprisonment." However, this analysis overlooks the practical reality faced by Defendant here. By agreeing to a 40-year cap instead of the full 45-year maximum, Defendant specifically bargained for a limited sentencing range. When he waived his right to appeal that sentence, he gave up the very mechanism by which he could have challenged an excessive sentence within that cap. The waiver of appellate review, when tied to a negotiated sentencing cap, directly affects whether the defendant can later contest the length of his imprisonment—a quintessential direct consequence.

The prosecution relies on *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d 925, 932 (2005), and *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d 482, 486 (2002), for the proposition that appellate waivers are always collateral. However, those cases involved generic appellate waivers that were not specifically tied to the terms of the plea negotiation. Here, Defendant's waiver was not an incidental procedural formality—it was the specific consideration exchanged for the State's agreement to cap the sentence at 40 years instead of exposing Defendant to the full 45-year maximum. This bargained-for exchange distinguishes this case from the authorities cited by the prosecution.

As this Court recognized in *People v. Evans*, 174 Ill. 2d 320, 327 (1996), the distinction between direct and collateral consequences turns on whether the consequence affects the fact or length of imprisonment. When a defendant specifically negotiates a sentencing cap and waives appeal in exchange, the waiver directly determines whether the defendant can later challenge the length of imprisonment imposed within that cap. This is precisely the type of consequence that Rule 402 requires be explained to the defendant.

As *People v. Tyus*, 218 Ill. 2d 412, 425 (2006), held, a defendant cannot show prejudice from prior misadvice "if the court correctly advises the defendant of the maximum sentence before the plea is entered." However, Tyus is distinguishable because there, the defendant received correct information before pleading. Here, Defendant was told he faced "90 years" if he went to trial—a figure nearly double the actual maximum. This extreme misadvice could have coerced Defendant into accepting the plea out of fear, regardless of what the court subsequently said.

The prosecution argues that Defendant "cannot prove he would have gone to trial." But this misapplies the prejudice standard. The question is whether the deficient advice affected Defendant's decision-making process—not whether he can prove a different outcome. When counsel misrepresents the maximum sentence by a factor of two, the defendant is deprived of the ability to make an informed choice about whether to accept the plea or proceed to trial.

Additionally, while the prosecution correctly notes that counsel is not required to advise on all collateral consequences, counsel has a duty to explain the material terms of the plea agreement. *People v. The problem here is that after the recess, plea counsel—not the court—explained Defendant's concerns. Plea counsel stated that Defendant was "upset with the State's plea offer" and not with representation. The court then asked if Defendant was satisfied, and Defendant said yes. But this exchange never addressed why Defendant initially expressed dissatisfaction or whether the underlying concerns had been resolved. The court accepted counsel's characterization of Defendant's concerns without independently probing whether Defendant had been properly advised about the plea agreement.

A defendant who initially expresses dissatisfaction with counsel, then "confirms" satisfaction after a brief recess, has not received the meaningful inquiry that due process requires. The court should have asked follow-up questions about what "not being represented right" meant and whether Defendant had received adequate advice about the appellate waiver.

The prosecution cites *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d 769, 775 (2007), for the proposition that disparities do not constitute a "fair and just reason" to withdraw a plea. However, Tellez is distinguishable because there, the defendant did not argue that the disparity rendered his plea involuntary—he merely argued it made his sentence excessive. Here, Defendant argues that the disparity demonstrates the unfairness of the plea agreement itself—that he agreed to a cap expecting a reasonable sentence, but received a term more than three times that of his codefendant who committed "essentially the same crime."

This disparity is particularly troubling given the facts of this case. The prosecution argues that Defendant was the "terrorist" who held guns to the victims' heads. But the factual basis showed that at least four individuals were involved in the home invasion, and Defendant was not identified as the primary aggressor. The codefendant, who was tried and convicted, received 12 years—yet Defendant, who accepted responsibility through a guilty plea, received 40 years. This outcome contradicts the fundamental premise of plea bargaining—that acceptance of responsibility warrants a more favorable sentence.

Your Honor, the prosecution asks this Court to apply a rigid rule that appellate waivers are always collateral consequences, regardless of the plea agreement's specific terms. When a defendant specifically trades his right to appeal for a sentencing cap, the waiver is integral to that bargain and must be understood by the defendant.

The record shows that Defendant did not understand he was waiving his right to appeal when he accepted the 40-year cap. Counsel misadvised him about the maximum sentence at trial. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea. A defendant may withdraw a guilty plea only upon a showing of a "fair and just reason." *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d 769, 775 (2007). The determination of whether a "fair and just reason" exists lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, and this Court will not overturn that decision absent an abuse of discretion. *People v. Johnson*, 201 Ill. App. 3d 1022, 1026 (1990).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Appellate Waiver Remains a Collateral Consequence**

Defendant renews his argument that the waiver of appellate review was a "direct consequence" of his plea because it was integral to the negotiated sentencing cap. Specifically, Defendant contends that because he traded his right to appeal for the benefit of a 40-year cap (rather than the statutory maximum of 45 years), the waiver must be considered a direct consequence requiring admonition under Illinois Supreme Court Rule 402. This argument is unpersuasive.

Illinois law distinguishes between "direct" and "collateral" consequences of a plea. Direct consequences are those that have a "direct bearing on the fact or length of the defendant's imprisonment." *People v. Cisewski*, 118 Ill. 2d 163, 175 (1987). Collateral consequences are those that are indirect or remote. The prosecution correctly relies on *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d 925, 932 (2005), for the proposition that "the right to appeal is not a direct consequence of a guilty plea; it is a collateral consequence." This principle is directly applicable to the facts at hand.

Defendant attempts to distinguish *Smith* and *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d 482, 486 (2002), by arguing that those cases involved "generic" waivers, whereas his waiver was part of a specific bargained-for exchange. This distinction is without merit. In *Farnsworth*, the court explicitly addressed a negotiated plea agreement involving a sentencing cap and held that the defendant's inability to appeal the sentence within that cap did not render the plea involuntary. *People v. Farnsworth*, 328 Ill. App. 3d at 486. The fact that the cap was a central term of the negotiation does not transform the collateral nature of the appellate waiver into a direct one. The waiver of appellate review is a procedural limitation that does not alter the fact or the maximum possible length of the imprisonment imposed by the trial court. *People v. Evans*, 174 Ill. 2d 320, 327 (1996). Therefore, the trial court and defense counsel were not required to advise Defendant of this specific limitation prior to accepting the plea.

**B. The Record Supports the Finding That the Plea Was Knowing and Voluntary**

Defendant argues that his plea was not knowing and voluntary because he did not understand the appellate waiver and because he was misled by counsel regarding the potential sentence at trial. A defendant's subjective, post-plea assertions of misunderstanding are insufficient to overturn a plea when the record shows that the defendant understood the consequences at the time of the plea. *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d 206, 218 (2000).

The record demonstrates that the trial court meticulously complied with Rule 402 regarding the direct consequences of the plea. The court explicitly explained the sentencing parameters, stating: "This is a special Class X felony in that it is a class-we call Class X plus 15. That means the minimum mandatory prison sentence is 21 years. And normally a Class X plus 15 would be 21 to 45 but because of your partially negotiated plea agreement calls for a cap of 40 years in the Department of Corrections, your sentence will be an exact term between 21 and 40 years." Defendant responded, "Yes," indicating his understanding. This exchange satisfies the requirement that the defendant understand the direct consequences of the plea. *People v. McCullough*, 207 Ill. 2d 262, 274–75 (2003).

Defendant claims that his affirmative response referred only to the numerical range and not to the appellate waiver. Even assuming this is true, it is irrelevant because the appellate waiver is a collateral consequence. Defendant's admission during cross-examination that he knew the "most I could receive was 45" further confirms that he understood the direct consequences of his decision to plead guilty versus going to trial. *People v. Harris*, 192 Ill. 2d at 218.

**C. Defendant Has Not Established Ineffective Assistance of Counsel**

Defendant argues that plea counsel provided ineffective assistance by misstating the maximum sentence at trial as "90 years" and by failing to advise him of the appellate waiver. To prevail on an ineffective assistance claim, Defendant must show both deficient performance and prejudice. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984).

First, any deficiency regarding the "90 years" comment was cured by the trial court's correct admonition that the maximum sentence was 45 years. Defendant acknowledged this correct information during the plea colloquy. Therefore, Defendant cannot show prejudice, as he cannot demonstrate that he would have proceeded to trial had he been correctly advised by counsel initially, given that the court properly informed him of the maximum exposure before he entered his plea. *People v. Tyus*, 218 Ill. 2d 412, 425 (2006). The defense's argument that the "90 years" comment coerced the plea regardless of the court's correction is contrary to the holding in *Tyus*, which establishes that a court's correct admonition negates prejudice from prior misadvice.

Second, counsel's failure to advise Defendant of the collateral consequence of the appellate waiver does not constitute ineffective assistance. Counsel is not required to advise a defendant of collateral consequences. *People v. Smith*, 354 Ill. App. 3d at 932.

**D. The Trial Court Adequately Inquired Into Defendant's Dissatisfaction With Counsel**

Defendant contends that the trial court's inquiry into his initial dissatisfaction with counsel was superficial. The record shows that after Defendant stated, "I just don't feel like I was represented right," the court granted a 20-minute recess for consultation. Upon returning, plea counsel clarified that Defendant was upset with the plea offer, not his representation. The court then asked Defendant directly if he was satisfied with his attorney's representation, and Defendant replied, "Yes."

This sequence satisfies the trial court's duty to inquire into the defendant's concerns. *People v. Johnson*, 154 Ill. 2d 356, 368 (1993). Once Defendant retracted his dissatisfaction and confirmed satisfaction with counsel, the court was not required to conduct a further inquiry into the specifics of counsel's performance. The defense's assertion that the court should have ignored counsel's representation of the situation and interrogated Defendant further is unsupported by precedent.

**E. The Codefendant's Sentence Does Not Warrant Withdrawal of the Plea**

Defendant argues that the 40-year sentence is excessive compared to his codefendant's 12-year sentence. While sentencing disparities between codefendants may be relevant to a motion to reconsider the sentence, they do not render a guilty plea involuntary. The plea was knowing and voluntary based on the terms of the agreement and the court's admonitions. Defendant's regret regarding the disparity in sentences does not constitute a "fair and just reason" to withdraw the plea. *People v. Tellez*, 371 Ill. App. 3d at 775.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admonished Defendant pursuant to Rule 402, ensuring he understood the direct consequences of his plea. The limitation on appellate review resulting from the sentencing cap is a collateral consequence for which no admonition was required. Defendant's post-plea assertions of confusion and ineffective assistance are contradicted by the record of the plea proceedings. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court's denial of Defendant's motion to withdraw his guilty plea be affirmed.
