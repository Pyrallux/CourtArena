### I. INTRODUCTION

The defendant's convictions must be vacated because no rational trier of fact could have found, based on the evidence presented, that the defendant acted with malice when he drove into an intersection while in a state of extreme intoxication and possible unconsciousness resulting from a head injury sustained moments earlier.

---

### II. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY IDENTIFIED THE ABSENCE OF MALICE

The defense agrees with the State that appellate courts generally should not raise issues sua sponte that were not preserved by the parties. However, the Court of Appeals' identification of the complete absence of malice evidence constitutes a recognition of a fundamental constitutional deficiency that permeates the entire proceeding. When the evidence is insufficient to support a conviction, the conviction cannot stand regardless of whether the issue was preserved. This Court has long held that insufficiency of the evidence constitutes a question of law that may be raised at any time, including by the appellate court on its own motion, because "no conviction can stand if it is based on conjecture and speculation rather than evidence." *State v. Williams*, 301 N.C. 193, 203, 270 S.E.2d 456, 463 (1980).

The Court of Appeals did not commit error in recognizing that the evidence was wholly insufficient to establish malice. The State bears the burden of proving every element of second-degree murder, including malice, beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Brown*, 315 N.C. 40, 47, 337 S.E.2d 808, 814 (1985). Where, as here, the State failed to present any evidence from which malice could be inferred, the convictions cannot stand.

---

### III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

**A. The Legal Standard for Second-Degree Murder**

Second-degree murder requires proof of malice, which may be either express or implied. Express malice exists when the defendant unlawfully kills another with the intent to cause death or serious bodily harm. Implied malice exists when the killing results from "an intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. 559, 568, 247 S.E.2d 905, 911 (1978). Implied malice requires "a reckless disregard for the safety of others and a conscious indifference to the probable consequences of one's actions." *State v. Hammonds*, 290 N.C. 1, 9, 224 S.E.2d 595, 601 (1976).

Critically, the defense submits that implied malice requires a showing that the defendant acted *voluntarily* and with the requisite *conscious* mental state. As this Court explained in *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. at 568, 247 S.E.2d at 911, implied malice arises from conduct that demonstrates "a disregard for human life." This inherently requires that the defendant was capable of disregarding human life—that is, that the defendant was conscious and capable of understanding the nature and consequences of his actions.

**B. The Defendant's Intoxication and Possible Unconsciousness Negate Malice**

The evidence presented at trial established that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 percent and .32 percent—approximately three to four times the legal limit for driving in most jurisdictions. More significantly, the evidence showed that moments before the fatal crash, the defendant had been struck in the head during a fight with the bar owner, fell, and struck his head on the base of a door. The defendant offered evidence that he could not remember anything after being struck and that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident.

The trial court erred in refusing to allow the defendant to present evidence of unconsciousness and in refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense submits that this error was not only reversible in its own right but also directly contributed to the absence of malice evidence. If the jury had been permitted to consider the defendant's claim of unconsciousness, it could have found that the defendant was not conscious when he drove into the intersection and therefore could not have possessed the requisite malice.

**C. The State Mischaracterizes the Evidence of "Malice"**

The State argues that the defendant's conduct—driving 60-70 mph through a red light while swerving to avoid traffic—demonstrates wanton and reckless disregard for human life. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the State conflates mere negligence or recklessness with the specific mental state required for implied malice. While dangerous driving may establish involuntary manslaughter or reckless driving, second-degree murder requires more. As this Court held in *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. at 568, 247 S.E.2d at 911, implied malice requires "a reckless disregard for the safety of others and a conscious indifference to the probable consequences." The emphasis on "conscious" is dispositive. The defendant, if unconscious or in a state of extreme intoxication rendering him incapable of conscious thought, cannot possess the requisite mental state.

Second, the State's reliance on *State v. Brown*, 315 N.C. 40, 337 S.E.2d 808 (1985), is misplaced. In *Brown*, the defendant was driving at high speed through a red light when he caused a fatal collision. However, in *Brown*, there was no evidence that the defendant was unconscious or had suffered a head injury immediately before the crash. The defendant in *Brown* was fully conscious and deliberately chose to drive at high speed through a red light. Here, by contrast, the defendant had just suffered a head injury and was operating a vehicle with a blood alcohol level approximately four times the legal limit.

Third, the circumstances here more closely resemble cases involving involuntary manslaughter where the defendant, while intoxicated, caused a death through criminal negligence but without the malice required for murder. In *State v. Ward*, 300 N.C. 515, 268 S.E.2d 446 (1980), this Court reversed a second-degree murder conviction where the evidence showed the defendant was intoxicated but there was insufficient evidence of malice. The Court held that "mere intoxication, even to the point of intoxication itself, does not constitute the malice necessary to support a conviction of murder in the second degree." *Id.* at 519, 268 S.E.2d at 449.

**D. The Defendant's Actions Must Be Viewed in Context**

The State asks this Court to view the defendant's conduct in isolation—approaching an intersection at 60-70 mph through a red light—and conclude that this conduct alone establishes malice. However, the evidence must be viewed in the context of the entire transaction. The defendant had been drinking heavily throughout the afternoon. He was involved in an altercation in which he was struck twice in the head, fell, and struck his head on a door base. He then went to his car and drove away. The critical question is whether, at the moment he entered the intersection, he possessed the conscious mental state required for malice.

The defendant testified that he could not remember anything after being struck. While the State argues that this testimony was self-serving and properly excluded, the question is not whether the jury believed the defendant, but whether there was sufficient evidence from which the jury could infer that the defendant was conscious and acting with malice. The defense submits that the combination of (1) a blood alcohol level of .24-.32 percent, (2) a head injury sustained moments before the crash, (3) the lack of any memory of the incident, and (4) the objective circumstances of the crash creates reasonable doubt as to whether the defendant possessed the requisite malice.

---

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING UNCONSCIOUSNESS EVIDENCE

The trial court committed reversible error in refusing to permit the defendant to present evidence of unconsciousness and in refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. Unconsciousness is a complete defense to a charge of murder because a person who is unconscious cannot form the requisite intent or malice. *State v. Cutter*, 261 N.C. 202, 208, 134 S.E.2d 197, 201 (1964).

The defense offered testimony that the defendant could not remember anything after being struck and that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. This evidence, if believed by the jury, could have established that the defendant was not conscious when he drove into the intersection and therefore could not have possessed malice. The trial court's refusal to allow this evidence effectively stripped the defendant of his defense and allowed the State to argue, without contradiction, that the defendant was fully conscious when he caused the fatal crash.

The State argues that the trial court properly excluded the unconsciousness evidence because "no credible testimony supported it." This characterization is incorrect. The defendant himself testified that he could not remember anything after being struck. The bar owner struck the defendant twice, causing him to fall and strike his head on a door base. The defendant's blood alcohol level was extremely high. This evidence, taken together, was sufficient to require the trial court to submit the issue to the jury.

---

### V. THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT SUPPORT THE INFERENCES THE STATE ASKS THIS COURT TO DRAW

The State asks this Court to infer, from the objective facts of the crash, that the defendant possessed malice. Specifically, the State asks this Court to infer that the defendant consciously chose to drive at high speed through a red light while swerving to avoid traffic, thereby demonstrating a wanton disregard for human life.

This inference, however, requires the assumption that the defendant was conscious and capable of making choices at the time of the crash. The evidence presented at trial directly undermines this assumption. The defendant had a blood alcohol level of .24-.32 percent. He had just suffered a head injury. He had no memory of the incident. Under these circumstances, the more reasonable inference is that the defendant was not fully conscious and was not making deliberate choices to endanger others.

The State's argument conflates the objective facts of the defendant's conduct with the subjective mental state required for malice. While the defendant may have engaged in dangerous driving, the evidence does not establish that he did so with the conscious indifference to human life required for second-degree murder. The evidence, at most, establishes criminal negligence or involuntary manslaughter.

---

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the defendant's three second-degree murder convictions. The evidence presented at trial was constitutionally insufficient to establish the element of malice beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant was in a state of extreme intoxication and had just suffered a head injury that may have rendered him unconscious at the time of the crash. Under these circumstances, no rational trier of fact could have found that the defendant acted with the malice required for second-degree murder.

Alternatively, the defense requests that this Court remand the case for a new trial at which the defendant may present evidence of unconsciousness and the jury may properly consider whether the defendant possessed the requisite mental state.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. This Court finds that the Court of Appeals erred by reversing the defendant's convictions *sua sponte* on the ground that there was no evidence of malice. The case is remanded to the Court of Appeals for consideration of the defendant's properly preserved appellate issues.

**OPINION**

The State seeks review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing three second-degree murder convictions. The Court of Appeals, acting on its own motion, held that the evidence was insufficient to establish malice. After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, we conclude that the Court of Appeals erred in finding no evidence of malice and in reversing the convictions on a ground not raised by the parties.

**I. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime requires an intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately causes death. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from conduct that demonstrates a reckless disregard for the safety of others and a conscious indifference to the probable consequences of one's actions. State v. Hammonds, 290 N.C. 1, 224 S.E.2d 595 (1976).

The evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, was sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with implied malice. Walden Ball testified that the defendant approached the intersection at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour while the traffic light was red. The defendant entered the intersection without slowing, swerved to avoid one vehicle, and crashed into another, killing three occupants. This conduct—operating a vehicle at an excessive speed through a red light and executing evasive maneuvers—constitutes "wanton and willful disregard of the probable consequences to human life." State v. Brown, 315 N.C. 40, 337 S.E.2d 808 (1985).

The defendant argues that his extreme intoxication and the head injury sustained prior to the accident negate the finding of "conscious indifference" required for implied malice. While the defendant presented evidence of a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent and a lack of memory following the altercation, these facts go to the weight of the evidence and the credibility of the defendant's claim of unconsciousness. They do not render the evidence of malice legally insufficient. The jury was entitled to infer consciousness and malice from the defendant's physical actions—driving, steering, and swerving to avoid traffic—despite his claimed memory loss. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that no rational trier of fact could have found malice.

**II. The Court of Appeals' Sua Sponte Reversal**

Appellate courts generally do not consider issues not raised by the parties. State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983). The "plain error" rule, which allows a court to notice unpreserved error, applies only in the rare instance where the error is "fundamental" and "seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." Id.

Because the evidence at trial was sufficient to support the second-degree murder convictions, there was no fundamental defect in the proceedings. The Court of Appeals' finding of "no evidence of malice" was an incorrect application of the law to the facts. By reversing the convictions on this ground, the Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury on an issue that was adequately supported by the record. The State correctly argues that the Court of Appeals violated the plain error doctrine by reversing on a ground not argued by the defendant where the evidence of guilt was substantial.

**III. Remand for Consideration of Preserved Issues**

The defendant raised several issues on appeal, including the trial court's refusal to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness and the exclusion of evidence regarding his mental state. The Court of Appeals did not address these preserved issues because it reversed the convictions on the sufficiency of the evidence. Since we hold that the evidence was sufficient and the reversal was improper, we must remand the case to the Court of Appeals to consider the defendant's properly preserved assignments of error. See State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. The record contains substantial evidence of malice to support the convictions for second-degree murder. The case is remanded to the Court of Appeals for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

The prosecution reliance on State v. Brown, 315 N.C. 40, 337 S.E.2d 808 (1985), is misplaced. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. This Court finds that the Court of Appeals erred by reversing the defendant's convictions *sua sponte* on the ground that there was no evidence of malice. The evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, was sufficient to support a jury finding of implied malice. The case is remanded to the Court of Appeals for consideration of the defendant's properly preserved assignments of error regarding the trial court's exclusion of evidence and refusal to instruct on the defense of unconsciousness.

**OPINION**

The State seeks review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing three second-degree murder convictions. The Court of Appeals, acting on its own motion, held that the evidence was insufficient to establish malice. After reviewing the record and the rebuttal arguments of counsel, we conclude that the Court of Appeals erred in finding no evidence of malice and in reversing the convictions on a ground not raised by the parties.

**I. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime requires an intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately causes death. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from conduct that demonstrates a reckless disregard for the safety of others and a conscious indifference to the probable consequences of one's actions. State v. Hammonds, 290 N.C. 1, 224 S.E.2d 595 (1976).

The evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, was sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with implied malice. Walden Ball testified that the defendant approached the intersection at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour while the traffic light was red. The defendant entered the intersection without slowing, swerved to avoid one vehicle, and crashed into another, killing three occupants. This conduct—operating a vehicle at an excessive speed through a red light and executing evasive maneuvers—constitutes "wanton and willful disregard of the probable consequences to human life." State v. Brown, 315 N.C. 40, 337 S.E.2d 808 (1985).

The defendant argues, relying on State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 515, 268 S.E.2d 446 (1980), that his extreme intoxication and head injury negate the finding of "conscious indifference" required for implied malice. In Ward, the defendant was found asleep at the wheel, and the court held that evidence of intoxication alone does not constitute malice. However, the instant case is factually distinct from Ward. Here, the defendant was not asleep or passive; he was actively driving, passing other vehicles, and swerving to avoid a collision. These actions support an inference of consciousness and control, distinguishing this case from those where a defendant is truly incapacitated.

The defense contends that the defendant's actions were the result of "involuntary reflexes" rather than conscious volition due to a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent and a head injury. While the defendant presented evidence of memory loss, these facts go to the weight of the evidence and the credibility of the defendant's claim of unconsciousness. They do not render the evidence of malice legally insufficient. The jury was entitled to infer consciousness and malice from the defendant's physical actions—driving, steering, and swerving to avoid traffic—despite his claimed memory loss. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that no rational trier of fact could have found malice.

**II. The Court of Appeals' Sua Sponte Reversal**

Appellate courts generally do not consider issues not raised by the parties. State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983). The "plain error" rule, which allows a court to notice unpreserved error, applies only in the rare instance where the error is "fundamental" and "seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." Id.

Because the evidence at trial was sufficient to support the second-degree murder convictions, there was no fundamental defect in the proceedings. The Court of Appeals' finding of "no evidence of malice" was an incorrect application of the law to the facts. By reversing the convictions on this ground, the Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury on an issue that was adequately supported by the record. The State correctly argues that the Court of Appeals violated the plain error doctrine by reversing on a ground not argued by the defendant where the evidence of guilt was substantial.

**III. Remand for Consideration of Preserved Issues**

The defendant raised several issues on appeal, including the trial court's refusal to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness and the exclusion of evidence regarding his mental state. The Court of Appeals did not address these preserved issues because it reversed the convictions on the sufficiency of the evidence. Since we hold that the evidence was sufficient and the reversal was improper, we must remand the case to the Court of Appeals to consider the defendant's properly preserved assignments of error. See State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983).

The defense argues that the trial court erred in excluding evidence of unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense contends that the combination of the head injury and extreme intoxication created a genuine issue of material fact regarding the defendant's consciousness. The prosecution argues that the evidence was properly excluded because the defendant's actions—swerving and steering—demonstrated consciousness, rendering the evidence of unconsciousness incredible. This presents a close question of law regarding the admissibility of evidence and the trial court's duty to instruct the jury on a defense supported by more than a scintilla of evidence. State v. Cutter, 261 N.C. 202, 134 S.E.2d 197 (1964). As the Court of Appeals did not reach these arguments, we remand for their consideration.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. The record contains substantial evidence of malice to support the convictions for second-degree murder. The case is remanded to the Court of Appeals for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed in part and the case is remanded to the Superior Court for a new trial. While we adhere to our prior determination that the evidence was sufficient to support a finding of malice, we hold that the trial court committed reversible error by excluding evidence regarding the defendant's unconsciousness and by refusing to instruct the jury on that defense.

**OPINION**

**I. Procedural History and Standard of Review**

This case comes before the Court on remand from our prior decision, State v. Defendant, [Citation], in which we reversed the Court of Appeals' determination that there was insufficient evidence of malice. We remanded the case to the Court of Appeals for consideration of the defendant's properly preserved assignments of error, specifically regarding the trial court's exclusion of evidence and refusal to instruct on the defense of unconsciousness. The defendant argues that the trial court's refusal to allow evidence of his unconsciousness and the corresponding jury instruction deprived him of a fair trial. The State argues that the defendant's actions—driving at high speeds, swerving, and avoiding one car before striking another—demonstrated consciousness and thus rendered the evidence of unconsciousness inadmissible.

**II. The Defense of Unconsciousness**

The defense of unconsciousness, if established, negates the mental state required for murder, including malice. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). "A defendant is entitled to an instruction on his theory of the case if there is more than a scintilla of evidence to support it." State v. Cutter, 261 N.C. 202, 134 S.E.2d 197 (1964). This Court has long held that the trial court's role is to determine the legal sufficiency of the evidence to support a defense, while the jury's role is to determine the weight and credibility of that evidence.

**III. Exclusion of Evidence and Jury Instruction**

The trial court refused to permit the defendant to offer evidence that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident and refused to instruct the jury on this defense. The evidence proffered by the defendant included testimony that he had suffered a head injury during an altercation at Smokey's Lounge, had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, and had no memory of the events following the altercation.

The State contends that the defendant's physical actions—operating a vehicle, passing another car, and swerving to avoid a collision—conclusively demonstrated consciousness and therefore justified the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence. The State relies on State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 515, 268 S.E.2d 446 (1980), for the proposition that evidence of intoxication alone does not constitute malice.

We find the State's argument unpersuasive in light of the specific facts presented. While evidence of intoxication alone, as in Ward, may not support a finding of unconsciousness, the combination of extreme intoxication and a significant head injury presents a different factual scenario. The defendant's evidence of a head blow resulting in memory loss, coupled with a blood alcohol level three to four times the legal limit, constitutes more than a scintilla of evidence to support a theory of unconsciousness or automatism. See State v. Cutter, 261 N.C. 202, 134 S.E.2d 197 (1964).

The fact that the defendant was able to perform physical actions, such as steering and swerving, does not automatically render the evidence of unconsciousness inadmissible. The human body is capable of reflexive and automatic actions without conscious awareness. Whether the defendant's driving was the result of conscious volition or involuntary reflex was a question of fact for the jury to resolve. By excluding the evidence and refusing the instruction, the trial court usurped the jury's function and decided the issue of consciousness as a matter of law. This was error.

**IV. Harmless Error Analysis**

The State argues that any error was harmless because the evidence of malice was overwhelming. However, errors involving the exclusion of a defense that negates the mental state of the crime are presumed prejudicial. The defense of unconsciousness, if believed, would have absolved the defendant of criminal liability entirely. Therefore, we cannot conclude that the trial court's error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court erred in excluding evidence of the defendant's unconsciousness and in refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defendant is entitled to a new trial where the jury may consider all the evidence regarding his mental state.

**SO ORDERED.**
