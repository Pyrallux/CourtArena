The Administrative Law Judge determined that Mr. McRae was injured while performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment and was entitled to benefits. ## III. THE COMMISSION'S INTERPRETATION IS CONTRARY TO WISCONSIN STATUTE § 102.03

Wisconsin Statute § 102.03(1) provides that compensation shall be payable for injuries sustained "while performing service growing out of and incidental to one's employment." The Commission's decision ignores the specific provision for traveling employees. Mr. McRae was employed to travel to various job sites. The Commission's characterization of his commute as "simply going to work" misconstrues the nature of his employment.

### B. The Traveling Employee Doctrine

In *Kaus v. Marshall & Ilsley Bank*, 2004 WI 83, 273 Wis. 2d 649, 683 N.W.2d 55, this Court addressed the traveling employee doctrine and held that when an employee's work requires travel, the course of employment includes travel to and from job sites. The Court recognized that employees whose work involves travel to varying locations are performing services growing out of and incidental to their employment during such travel.

The Commission's attempt to distinguish this case based on the lack of reimbursement is unavailing. The absence of mileage reimbursement does not negate the traveling nature of Mr. McRae's employment. This Court rejected such a narrow interpretation in *Keen v. The Court stated that "an employee whose work requires him to travel is performing services incidental to his employment during such travel."

Mr. McRae's employment fit this description. He was not a traditional commuting employee with a fixed workplace. He was a traveling employee whose job required him to report to different job sites daily. The Commission's requirement of a "business-related purpose" beyond the commute itself improperly restricts the statute's broad coverage.

### A. The Course of Employment Test

In *Harnischfeger Corp. v. Mr. McRae's commute was reasonably related to his employment. He was traveling to a job site to perform his duties as a painter. The travel was not a purely personal journey but an integral part of his work assignment. The Commission's decision contradicts the liberal construction mandate.

The critical inquiry is whether the travel is within the scope of employment, not whether the vehicle belongs to the employer.

Mr. McRae's use of his personal vehicle was necessitated by his employment. He was required to travel to varying job sites, and the employer provided no vehicle or transportation. This does not transform his travel into a personal journey outside the scope of employment.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a decision of the Circuit Court affirming the Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission's (LIRC) order denying worker's compensation benefits to Mr. McRae. The central issue on appeal is whether LIRC's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03, which found that Mr. McRae's injuries did not arise out of and incidental to his employment, is a reasonable interpretation entitled to great weight deference, or if it constitutes an error of law warranting reversal.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Under Wisconsin law, an agency's interpretation of a statute it administers is generally entitled to great weight deference. As stated in *West Bend Education Association v. Public Employment Relations Board*, 121 Wis. 2d 335, 359 N.W.2d 736 (1984), this deference applies when the agency's interpretation is reasonable and the agency possesses special expertise. The Commission correctly asserts its expertise in administering the Workers' Compensation Act. However, this deference is not absolute. As noted in *AT&T Corp. v. Department of Revenue*, 2005 WI 80, 282 Wis. 2d 120, 698 N.W.2d 733, great weight deference disappears when an agency's interpretation is contrary to the plain meaning of the statute or established precedent.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Commission determined that Mr. McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment because he was driving his own vehicle, received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area, and was "simply going to work." The Commission relied on the "going and coming" rule to exclude coverage.

The Defense argues, and the Court agrees, that the Commission's analysis fails to adequately account for the "traveling employee" doctrine. Under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f), an employee "who is employed to travel and does travel" is considered to be performing services incidental to employment while traveling.

The undisputed facts indicate that Mr. McRae was a union painter who "normally drove from his personal residence... directly to a particular job location he was assigned to for that day." This distinguishes him from a stationary employee with a fixed workplace. In *Kaus v. Marshall & Ilsley Bank*, 2004 WI 83, 273 Wis. 2d 649, 683 N.W.2d 55, this Court recognized that when an employee's work requires travel to varying locations, the course of employment includes travel to and from those job sites. The Commission's characterization of Mr. McRae's travel as a routine commute ignores the statutory mandate that traveling employees are covered while in transit.

Furthermore, the Commission's reliance on the lack of reimbursement is misplaced. In *Parker v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 48 Wis. 2d 212, 179 N.W.2d 907 (1970), the Court held that the use of a personal vehicle does not automatically negate the employment relationship when the employer's business requires such use. The absence of mileage reimbursement does not transform a required work-related journey into a purely personal errand.

The Commission also suggested that Mr. McRae needed to be on a "special errand" to qualify for benefits. This narrow view contradicts *Keen v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 83 Wis. 2d 680, 266 N.W.2d 262 (1978), where the Court held that the "going and coming" exception does not apply to employees whose work requires travel to various locations. The critical inquiry is whether the travel is a necessary component of the job duties, not whether a specific special mission was assigned at that moment.

Finally, the Workers’ Compensation Act must be liberally construed to achieve its beneficent purposes. *Miller v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 37 Wis. 2d 131, 154 N.W.2d 416 (1967). The Commission's decision, which effectively denies coverage to a traveling employee based on technicalities regarding reimbursement and vehicle ownership, conflicts with this liberal construction mandate as discussed in *Harnischfeger Corp. v. Labor and Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

While the Commission is generally entitled to deference, its interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 in this instance is unreasonable as applied to the undisputed facts. The Commission failed to apply the traveling employee doctrine as defined by *Kaus* and *Keen*, and improperly relied on the lack of reimbursement to exclude coverage where the employment contract required travel to varying job sites.

Accordingly, the Defense's arguments are persuasive. The Commission's decision is contrary to the plain language of the statute and binding precedent. The preliminary ruling is to reverse the decision of the Circuit Court and the Commission, and to reinstate the Administrative Law Judge's award of benefits.

Under Wisconsin law, great weight deference applies when (1) the agency administers the statute at issue, (2) the interpretation is reasonable, and (3) the agency possesses special expertise in the subject matter. *West Bend Education Association v. Public Employment Relations Board*, 121 Wis. 2d 335, 359 N.W.2d 736 (1984). LIRC unquestionably satisfies all three criteria. It is the statutorily designated agency responsible for interpreting and administering the Workers’ Compensation Act, and its expertise in resolving complex factual and legal questions under this remedial statute is well-documented. This Court has repeatedly recognized that LIRC’s interpretation of § 102.03 warrants deference precisely because it involves "the application of a statute to a particular factual context within the agency’s technical expertise." *Id.* at 349. The Defense’s argument that LIRC’s interpretation is "unreasonable" because it applied the "going and coming" rule to Mr. McRae’s daily commute is a mischaracterization of the standard. Reasonableness does not require perfection; it requires only that the interpretation fall within the bounds of plausible statutory construction. *AT&T Corp. v. The Defense’s reliance on *Kaus v. Marshall & Ilsley Bank*, 2004 WI 83, 273 Wis. 2d 649, 683 N.W.2d 55, is misplaced. In *Kaus*, the employee was required to travel between branches as part of her regular duties, making the travel integral to her job. By contrast, Mr. McRae’s situation involved daily commutes from home to a single assigned job site—no different from a plumber or electrician who works at multiple locations but commutes each day. LIRC reasonably distinguished *Kaus* because Mr. McRae was not "employed to travel" in the sense of being on the road as a core component of his work; rather, he was a stationary employee with a shifting workplace, which the Commission has consistently treated as a classic "going and coming" scenario. This interpretation is consistent with *Parker v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 48 Wis. 2d 212, 179 N.W.2d 907 (1970), which held that the use of a personal vehicle alone does not transform a commute into employment-related travel. The Defense’s assertion that LIRC "improperly restricted coverage" by applying the rule is circular; the rule is a well-established exception to the general coverage of § 102.03, and LIRC’s application of it to Mr. McRae’s undisputed facts is reasonable. # DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Defense submits this rebuttal in defense of the Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission's (LIRC) original decision denying workers' compensation benefits to Mr. McRae. The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes both the applicable standard of review and the relevant precedent, and this Court should reject its invitation to overturn LIRC's reasonable interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03.

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The prosecution correctly states that great weight deference applies to agency interpretations, but this Court in its preliminary ruling correctly identified the limits of that deference. As stated in *AT&T Corp. v. Department of Revenue*, 2005 WI 80, 282 Wis. 2d 120, 698 N.W.2d 733, great weight deference disappears when an agency's interpretation is contrary to the plain meaning of the statute or established precedent. The preliminary ruling correctly found that LIRC's interpretation here fails under this standard.

The prosecution's argument conflates "reasonableness" with "correctness." LIRC's interpretation may be plausible in isolation, but when examined against the plain language of § 102.03(1)(f) and binding precedent from this Court, it becomes unreasonable. The deference framework does not insulate agency decisions from judicial review when they conflict with statutory text.

## III. THE TRAVELING EMPLOYEE DOCTRINE

### A. The Statutory Text Controls

Wisconsin Statute § 102.03(1)(f) provides coverage for an employee "who is employed to travel and does travel." The critical phrase is "employed to travel"—not "travels as an incident to employment." The prosecution attempts to blur this distinction, but the statute draws a clear line.

Mr. McRae was not "employed to travel." He was employed as a painter. His union contract required him to report to assigned job sites, but travel was not the essence of his employment. He drove his personal vehicle without reimbursement, his paid time began only upon arrival at the job site, and he had no discretionary authority regarding travel. These undisputed facts demonstrate that Mr. McRae was a stationary employee whose workplace merely shifted—a category this Court has consistently distinguished from traveling employees.

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies *Kaus*

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Kaus v. Marshall & Ilsley Bank*, 2004 WI 83, 273 Wis. 2d 649, 683 N.W.2d 55, but in doing so, reveals the weakness of its position. In *Kaus*, the employee was a bank branch manager who was *required to travel between branches as part of her regular duties*. The travel was integral to her job function—she was managing multiple locations, not simply commuting to a single worksite.

Mr. McRae's situation is fundamentally different. He reported to one assigned job site per day. He was not managing multiple locations, not performing services at various sites simultaneously, and not exercising any travel-related discretion. He was a painter going to a painting job—a classic "going and coming" scenario regardless of where that job was located.

The preliminary ruling erred in treating every employee who works at varying locations as a "traveling employee." That interpretation renders the "employed to travel" language surplusage and ignores the distinction this Court recognized in *Kaus* between employees whose jobs inherently require travel and those who merely commute to different workplaces.

### C. *Keen* Is Inapplicable

The prosecution correctly notes that *Keen v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 83 Wis. 2d 680, 266 N.W.2d 262 (1978) involved a special errand. But the preliminary ruling cited *Keen* for the broader proposition that the "going and coming" exception does not apply to employees whose work requires travel to various locations. This broader proposition is incorrect as applied to Mr. McRae.

In *Keen*, the employee was specifically directed to travel during work hours to perform a task away from his normal workplace. The travel was directed by the employer as part of a specific work assignment. Mr. McRae, by contrast, was simply driving to his regularly assigned job site at the start of his workday. There was no special errand, no directed travel, and no employer-mandated mission. His travel was indistinguishable from any employee's commute, merely with a variable destination.

## IV. THE "GOING AND COMING" RULE

The prosecution correctly states that the "going and coming" rule is a well-established exception to workers' compensation coverage. This rule provides that injuries sustained during the commute to and from work are generally not covered because the employee is not then performing services for the employer.

LIRC reasonably applied this rule to Mr. McRae. The facts support this determination:

1. Mr. McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area—indicating the employer did not consider this travel compensable.

2. Mr. McRae's paid time began only upon arrival at the job site—confirming that no employer-employee relationship existed during travel.

3. There was no business-related purpose to the commute itself—Mr. McRae was simply traveling from his home to his assigned workplace.

The preliminary ruling dismissed these facts as "technicalities," but they represent the core factors this Court has historically considered in determining whether travel falls within the course of employment. In *Parker v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 48 Wis. 2d 212, 179 N.W.2d 907 (1970), while the Court held that use of a personal vehicle does not automatically negate coverage, it did not hold that such use creates coverage where other factors indicate the commute is personal in nature.

## V. LIBERAL CONSTRUCTION DOES NOT OVERRIDE STATUTORY LIMITS

The prosecution correctly notes that the Workers' Compensation Act must be liberally construed. However, as this Court stated in *Harnischfeger Corp. v. Labor and Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995), liberal construction does not erase the "going and coming" rule or create coverage where the statute does not authorize it.

The preliminary ruling invoked liberal construction to override the statutory text and established precedent. This approach improperly substitutes policy preferences for legislative intent. The legislature specifically included the "traveling employee" category in § 102.03(1)(f), thereby excluding employees who are not within that category. Mr. McRae does not fit within this category, and no amount of liberal construction can create coverage where none exists.

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense requests that this Court:

1. Reverse the preliminary ruling;

2. Affirm LIRC's decision denying workers' compensation benefits to Mr. McRae; and

3. Restore the Commission's order as affirmed by the circuit court.

The prosecution's arguments fail to demonstrate that LIRC's interpretation was unreasonable or contrary to law. The Commission's decision was based on a reasonable application of the "going and coming" rule to the undisputed facts, and this Court should uphold it.

**Respectfully submitted,**

*Defense*
