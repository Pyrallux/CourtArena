FACTS:
The State seeks review of a Court of Appeals decision which reversed three convictions for murder in the second degree upon a finding that there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case to that court for further consideration.

The evidence at trial tended to show that on September 4, 1982 the defendant spent the afternoon with his brother in Winston-Salem. The two drank until about 4:00 p.m. when they went to a local tavern called Smokey’s Lounge. The defendant was described as being loud, vulgar, rowdy and drunk. Worth Shelton, the proprietor of the bar, refused to serve the two men drinks and ordered them to leave. A fight ensued in which Shelton struck the defendant twice. The defendant fell and hit his head on the base of a door. Rising, the defendant staggered to his car in the parking lot and struck it with his fist. He got inside the car and drove off onto Highway 311.

Walden Ball was traveling on Highway 311 at that time. He testified that he heard squealing tires behind his car. In his rear-view mirror, Ball saw the defendant approaching from behind in a beige Cutlass.  Ball further testified that after passing him,As he approached the intersection, the traffic light facing him was red, as it had been for some time. The defendant entered the intersection at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour.

As the defendant entered the intersection, he swerved to avoid one car in his lane and crashed into another car occupied by three persons who were killed by the impact. After the accident, the defendant was taken to Forsyth Memorial Hospital. Records *393at the hospital indicated that the defendant had a blood alcohol level of .24 percent and perhaps as high as .32 percent.

The defendant’s evidence at trial tended to show that he could not remember anything that occurred after being struck by the tavern owner. He attempted to offer testimony showing that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident, but the trial court refused to permit him to produce evidence along those lines and refused to instruct the jury on a defense of unconsciousness. The jury found the defendant guilty of three counts of second-degree murder. In sentencing the defendant, the trial court found as an aggravating factor that the defendant was engaged in a pattern of violent conduct. The trial court found no mitigating factors and sentenced the defendant to twenty years imprisonment for each conviction to be served concurrently.

The defendant appealed to the Court of Appeals. The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions. Although the issue was not raised by the parties, the Court of Appeals held that the convictions constituted “plain error” as there was no evidence at trial of malice on the part of the defendant. Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element of murder in the second degree, that crime does not exist in the absence of some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Supreme Court of North Carolina

---

**Your Honors,** the defendant-respondent respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals correctly reversed the three convictions for second-degree murder, and this Court should affirm that decision. The State has failed to demonstrate that the Court of Appeals committed reversible error in finding an absence of evidence of malice. To the contrary, the evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the defendant, established that the defendant lacked the requisite malice for second-degree murder. The trial court's refusal to permit evidence of unconsciousness and its corresponding refusal to instruct the jury on that defense deprived the defendant of a complete defense and tainted the proceedings. The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Court of Appeals reversal and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

---

## I. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY FOUND NO EVIDENCE OF MALICE

The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of second-degree murder beyond a reasonable doubt, including malice. *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). Second-degree murder requires "the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation." *State v. Robbins*, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983). Malice, in the context of second-degree murder, encompasses either actual intent to kill or that "abandoned and malignant heart" standard—a reckless disregard for human life that is "equally as heinous as an actual intent to kill." *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. at 568, 247 S.E.2d at 911.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Wilkerson* is misplaced. In *Wilkerson*, this Court upheld a second-degree murder conviction where the defendant intentionally drove his vehicle at high speed through a red light while being pursued by police, resulting in the death of a pedestrian. Critically, the defendant in *Wilkerson* was fully aware of his actions and made a conscious choice to flee from law enforcement in a dangerous manner. The defendant there "intentionally" operated his vehicle in a manner that demonstrated a "wanton and reckless disregard for the lives of others." *Id.* at 569, 247 S.E.2d at 911.

The instant case is distinguishable on its essential facts. Here, the defendant was struck in the head during an altercation at Smokey's Lounge, fell to the ground, and struck his head on the base of a door. The defendant could not remember anything after being struck. He attempted to offer evidence that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident, but the trial court refused to permit such evidence and refused to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness.

This distinction is dispositive. The prosecution has not established—and cannot establish—that the defendant acted with the requisite malice because the defendant may have been unconscious or in a state of automatism at the time of the collision. As this Court recognized in *State v. Lang*, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), malice requires an "intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." *Id.* at 519, 308 S.E.2d at 322. An unconscious or involuntarily intoxicated person is not capable of forming the malicious intent required for second-degree murder.

---

## II. THE DEFENDANT'S INtoxication AND POSSIBLE UNCONSCIOUSNESS NEGATE THE ELEMENT OF MALICE

The evidence at trial established that the defendant had a blood alcohol level of .24 percent and "perhaps as high as .32 percent." This level of intoxication far exceeds the legal limit for driving and represents a state of severe impairment. More significantly, the defendant struck his head during the altercation at the bar and could not remember any events subsequent to being struck. The defendant sought to introduce evidence that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident—a defense that would have addressed whether he possessed the requisite mental state for murder.

The trial court's refusal to permit evidence of unconsciousness constitutes reversible error. Under North Carolina law, evidence of unconsciousness is relevant to whether a defendant possessed the requisite mental state for a crime. *See State v. Caddell*, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975) (holding that evidence of automatism or unconsciousness is relevant to criminal liability). When a defendant claims he was unconscious at the time of the offense, the jury must be instructed on that defense if there is evidence to support it. The trial court erred in refusing to allow the defendant to present this defense to the jury.

The prosecution's argument that malice can be inferred from the circumstances of reckless driving fails because it ignores the critical question of the defendant's capacity to form malice. The mere fact that a defendant engaged in dangerous driving does not, by itself, establish malice if the defendant was unconscious or otherwise incapable of forming the requisite intent. The prosecution cannot bypass the element of malice by arguing that the driving was dangerous without first establishing that the defendant was conscious and capable of forming the malicious intent.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON RECKLESS DRIVING IS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

The prosecution argues that the defendant's conduct—driving through a red light at sixty to seventy miles per hour—demonstrates malice. This argument conflates the standard for second-degree murder with the lesser offense of involuntary manslaughter. While reckless driving may establish criminal negligence sufficient for manslaughter, it does not automatically rise to the level of malice required for second-degree murder.

In *State v. Wilkerson*, this Court emphasized that the defendant's conduct there demonstrated "a wanton and reckless disregard for the lives of others" because the defendant was aware of the danger and chose to continue his flight from police. *Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. at 569, 247 S.E.2d at 911. The prosecution has not established that the defendant in this case was aware of anything at the time of the accident. The defendant had been struck in the head, was highly intoxicated, and had no memory of the events. There is no evidence from which the jury could infer that the defendant was consciously aware of his actions or that he acted with a "depraved heart."

The prosecution's argument that the defendant's prior conduct at the bar demonstrates malice is equally unavailing. The bar altercation occurred before the defendant was struck in the head and before he entered his vehicle. The prosecution cannot bootstrap the defendant's earlier behavior—however rowdy or intoxicated—onto the moment of the collision to establish malice. The question is whether the defendant possessed malice at the time he caused the deaths of the three victims, not whether he acted badly earlier in the day.

---

## IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS DID NOT COMMIT PLAIN ERROR

The prosecution contends that the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions because the issue of malice was not properly preserved for appeal. However, the Court of Appeals properly exercised its authority to address plain error under North Carolina Rule of Appellate Procedure 10(b)(4). The absence of evidence to support an essential element of a crime constitutes plain error that may be addressed by an appellate court regardless of whether the issue was preserved at trial. *See State v. Odom*, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E.2d 867 (1983) (discussing the plain error doctrine).

The Court of Appeals correctly identified that the prosecution failed to present any evidence from which a jury could find malice beyond a reasonable doubt. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the defendant, established that the defendant may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. Without evidence of the defendant's conscious mental state at the moment of the collision, the prosecution cannot establish the element of malice.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the three convictions for second-degree murder. The prosecution failed to present sufficient evidence of malice, and the trial court's refusal to permit the defendant to present evidence of unconsciousness deprived him of a complete defense. The convictions should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The State’s petition for review is granted. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded to that court for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**OPINION**

The central issue on appeal is whether the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the defendant’s convictions for second-degree murder upon a finding that there was no evidence of malice. After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, we hold that the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with malice.

Murder in the second degree is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a requisite element of the offense, the State must prove some intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately causes the death of the victim. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from conduct that demonstrates a reckless disregard for human life or a wanton injury to others. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905.

The State contends, and we agree, that the Court of Appeals misapplied the law by focusing on the absence of specific intent to kill rather than examining whether the defendant’s conduct evidenced a depraved heart or reckless disregard for safety. The evidence at trial showed that the defendant entered an intersection against a red light—which had been red for some time—at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour. This evidence is legally sufficient to support an inference of malice. As this Court held in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905, driving at a high rate of speed through a red light constitutes evidence of malice sufficient to sustain a conviction for second-degree murder. The defendant’s act of speeding through a red light and swerving to avoid one vehicle before crashing into another is precisely the type of wanton conduct that the law recognizes as malice.

The defendant argues that the convictions should be reversed because he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident, citing State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), and State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975). The defense posits that without proof of consciousness, there can be no "intentional act" and thus no malice. While the defense of unconsciousness, if supported by evidence, is a valid defense that negates the requisite mental state for murder, the existence of this potential defense does not render the State's evidence of malice nonexistent.

The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions based on a finding of "plain error" due to a lack of evidence of malice. This was error. The State presented substantial evidence of the defendant’s reckless driving. The fact that the trial court may have erred in excluding evidence of the defendant’s potential unconsciousness is a separate issue regarding the sufficiency of the defense presentation, not a lack of evidence of malice on the part of the State. The jury heard evidence that the defendant drove at an excessive speed through a red light, causing a fatal collision. This evidence supports a finding of malice. The defendant’s claim that he was struck in the head and intoxicated goes to the weight of the evidence and the credibility of the defendant's state of mind, but it does not legally vitiate the probative value of the defendant's actions regarding the element of malice.

Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that there was no evidence of malice. The State’s evidence was sufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss and to support the jury’s verdict. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for consideration of the remaining issues, including the propriety of the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence.

**SO ORDERED.**

**FINAL RULING**

The State’s petition for review is allowed. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded to that court for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**OPINION**

The fundamental issue presented for review is whether the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the defendant’s convictions for second-degree murder upon a finding that there was no evidence of malice. After careful consideration of the rebuttal arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, we hold that the evidence presented at trial was legally sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with malice. The Court of Appeals’ application of the "plain error" doctrine to reverse the convictions on the grounds of insufficient evidence was misplaced.

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a requisite element of the offense, the State must prove some intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately causes the death of the victim. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from conduct that demonstrates a reckless disregard for human life. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905.

The prosecution correctly argues that the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to establish malice. The evidence showed that the defendant entered an intersection against a red light—which had been red for some time—at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour. This conduct is legally sufficient to support an inference of malice. As this Court held in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905, driving at a high rate of speed through a red light constitutes evidence of malice sufficient to sustain a conviction for second-degree murder. The defendant’s act of speeding through a red light and swerving to avoid one vehicle before crashing into another is precisely the type of wanton conduct that the law recognizes as malice.

The defense contends that the convictions should be reversed because there was no evidence the defendant was conscious at the time of the collision, relying on State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), and State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975). The defense argues that without proof of consciousness, there can be no "intentional act" and thus no malice. While the defense of unconsciousness, if supported by evidence, is a valid defense that negates the requisite mental state for murder, the existence of this potential defense does not render the State's evidence of malice nonexistent.

The State is not required to disprove the defense of unconsciousness to make out a prima facie case of second-degree murder. The State presented substantial evidence of the defendant’s reckless driving. The fact that the trial court may have erred in excluding evidence of the defendant’s potential unconsciousness is a separate issue regarding the sufficiency of the defense presentation, not a lack of evidence of malice on the part of the State. The jury heard evidence that the defendant drove at an excessive speed through a red light, causing a fatal collision. This evidence supports a finding of malice. The defendant’s claim that he was struck in the head and intoxicated goes to the weight of the evidence and the credibility of the defendant's state of mind, but it does not legally vitiate the probative value of the defendant's actions regarding the element of malice.

The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions based on a finding of "plain error" due to a lack of evidence of malice. This was error. Under State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E. 2d 867 (1983), plain error must be fundamental and affect the fairness of the trial. The State’s evidence was sufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss and to support the jury’s verdict. The exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence, if erroneous, is a matter of trial error that does not equate to a total absence of evidence of malice.

Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that there was no evidence of malice. The State’s evidence was sufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss and to support the jury’s verdict. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for consideration of the remaining issues, including the propriety of the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence.

**FINAL RULING**

The decision of the Court of Appeals is affirmed in part and reversed in part. The Court of Appeals’ finding that there was no evidence of malice is reversed. However, the trial court’s exclusion of evidence regarding the defendant’s unconsciousness and its refusal to instruct the jury on that defense constituted reversible error. Consequently, the judgment of the Court of Appeals reversing the defendant’s convictions is affirmed, and the case is remanded to the Superior Court for a new trial.

**OPINION**

This case requires us to balance the State’s interest in prosecuting violent conduct with the defendant’s right to present a complete defense. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties, we hold that while the State presented sufficient evidence of malice to survive a motion to dismiss, the trial court committed prejudicial error by excluding the defendant’s evidence of unconsciousness. Therefore, while we disagree with the Court of Appeals’ rationale for reversal, we agree that the defendant is entitled to a new trial.

**I. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

We first address the State’s argument that the Court of Appeals erred in finding no evidence of malice. We agree with the State. The evidence presented at trial showed that the defendant, with a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, drove his vehicle into an intersection at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour despite a red traffic light that had been red for some time. To avoid one vehicle, the defendant swerved and crashed into another, killing three occupants.

This conduct is legally sufficient to support an inference of malice. As we held in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978), driving at a high rate of speed through a red light constitutes evidence of malice sufficient to sustain a conviction for second-degree murder. Malice may be implied from conduct that demonstrates a reckless disregard for human life. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905. The defendant’s actions were not merely accidental; they were the result of a conscious decision to drive at an excessive speed through a controlled intersection. Thus, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that there was "no evidence of malice" as a matter of law.

**II. The Trial Court’s Error in Excluding the Unconsciousness Defense**

However, the existence of sufficient evidence to support a conviction does not cure the trial court’s error in excluding the defendant’s primary defense. The defense argues, and we find, that the trial court improperly excluded evidence of the defendant’s unconsciousness and refused to instruct the jury on this defense.

Murder in the second degree requires "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Unconsciousness is a complete defense to murder because it negates the mental state required to form malice. State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975). If the defendant was unconscious at the time of the collision, he could not have committed the "intentional act" necessary for murder.

The record reveals evidence that would support a finding of unconsciousness. The defendant was struck in the head by the tavern owner, fell, and hit his head on the base of a door. His blood alcohol level was .24 percent, potentially as high as .32 percent. He testified that he could not remember anything after being struck. This evidence, if believed by the jury, could establish that the defendant was unconscious at the time of the accident and therefore lacked the capacity to act with malice.

The trial court’s refusal to permit the defendant to present evidence on this issue or to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness was error. The prosecution’s assertion that this was a "separate trial issue" does not mitigate the harm. By excluding this evidence, the trial court effectively directed a verdict against the defendant on the issue of his mental state. The jury was precluded from considering a defense that, if successful, would have negated the essential element of malice.

**III. The Error Was Not Harmless**

The prosecution contends that the error was harmless because the State’s evidence of malice was overwhelming. We disagree. Under State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E. 2d 867 (1983), plain error must be fundamental and affect the fairness of the trial. Denying a defendant the opportunity to present a defense that goes to the heart of the crime—intent and malice—is a fundamental error.

The evidence of the defendant’s head injury and extreme intoxication created a genuine factual dispute regarding his consciousness. The jury, not the trial court, should have weighed this evidence to determine whether the defendant possessed the requisite mental state for second-degree murder. Because the exclusion of this evidence deprived the defendant of a fair trial, the error cannot be deemed harmless.

**Conclusion**

The Court of Appeals correctly reversed the defendant’s convictions, albeit for the wrong reason. The State presented sufficient evidence of malice to withstand a motion to dismiss, but the trial court erred by excluding the defendant’s evidence of unconsciousness. This error violated the defendant's right to present a defense and necessitates a new trial. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals reversing the convictions and remand the case to the Superior Court for a new trial consistent with this opinion.

**SO ORDERED.**
