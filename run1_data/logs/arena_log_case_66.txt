Your Honor, the court of appeals correctly rejected the appellant’s claim that the trial court erred in denying disclosure of the confidential informant’s identity. The appellant’s motion for disclosure was premised on the assertion that the informant’s testimony was necessary for a fair determination of guilt. However, this case was before the trial court solely on a motion to suppress evidence—a procedural mechanism exclusively concerned with the legality of the search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment, not the ultimate question of guilt or innocence. As this Court has consistently held, suppression hearings do not serve as forums to litigate the merits of the underlying criminal charges; their sole purpose is to determine whether evidence was obtained in violation of constitutional protections. See *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 100 S. Ct. 2439, 65 L. Ed. 2d 468 (1980) (“The suppression remedy is designed to deter police misconduct, not to test the truth of the government’s case or to address guilt or innocence.”).  

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 591 (1957), establishes that the government’s privilege to withhold an informant’s identity must be balanced against a defendant’s need for disclosure. Yet this balancing applies only to the specific issues before the court at the time. In the context of a suppression hearing, disclosure of an informant’s identity is required only when the informant’s testimony is directly relevant to determining the legality of the search. See *United States v. Smith*, 126 F.3d 1231, 1238 (10th Cir. 1997) (“The government’s privilege to withhold the identity of an informant applies in suppression hearings, and disclosure is required only when the informant’s testimony is necessary to a determination of the suppression issue.”). Here, the appellant failed to demonstrate that the informant’s identity was material to the suppression issue—whether the officers had probable cause for the search. Instead, the appellant’s arguments centered entirely on entrapment and guilt, which are irrelevant to the suppression motion’s scope.  

Texas precedent confirms this principle. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals has explicitly held that “the determination of whether to disclose a confidential informant’s identity in a suppression hearing depends on whether the informant’s testimony is relevant to the suppression issue, not to guilt or innocence.” *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436, 439 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001). Similarly, the Texas Court of Appeals has ruled that “the reliability of a confidential informant may be established through the testimony of law enforcement officers without disclosing the informant’s identity” when addressing probable cause in a suppression context. *State v. The court of appeals did not err in affirming the trial court’s denial of disclosure; it correctly recognized that the suppression hearing’s narrow purpose precluded litigating guilt-related defenses. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant during a hearing on a motion to suppress evidence. The Appellant argues that the informant’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information provided to the police, thereby attacking the probable cause for the search. The Appellee counters that the hearing was a "dispositive" motion to suppress, limited solely to the legality of the search, and that the Appellant’s request for disclosure was improperly based on issues of guilt and innocence, specifically entrapment, which are irrelevant to the Fourth Amendment inquiry.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion to Suppress**

The record establishes that the parties agreed the hearing on the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." This agreement limited the scope of the proceeding to a single issue: whether the evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. As the prosecution correctly notes, the purpose of a suppression hearing is narrow; it is designed to deter police misconduct, not to test the truth of the government’s case or to address the ultimate question of guilt or innocence. See *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 100 S. Ct. 2439, 65 L. Ed. 2d 468 (1980).

The defense’s arguments at the hearing, however, frequently strayed into the realm of guilt and innocence. Defense counsel explicitly cited *Holmes v. South Carolina* and argued that the informant "set him up," which is a "critical defense." This line of reasoning relates to entrapment and the defendant's culpability, not the legality of the search or seizure. As held in *United States v. Smith*, 126 F.3d 1231, 1238 (10th Cir. 1997), "disclosure is required only when the informant's testimony is necessary to a determination of the suppression issue." Because the defense’s primary justification for disclosure appeared to be the preparation of a trial defense (entrapment) rather than a specific challenge to the legality of the search itself, the trial court was within its discretion to deny the motion on this basis.

**B. The Reliability of the Informant and Probable Cause**

The defense contends that even within the context of a suppression hearing, the informant’s identity was necessary to challenge probable cause because the informant’s alleged involvement ("setting him up") undermines her reliability. While the Supreme Court in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 591 (1957), established that the government’s privilege to withhold an informant’s identity must be balanced against the defendant’s need for disclosure, this balance is heavily weighted in favor of the government when the informant’s tip has been corroborated or when the informant’s reliability has been established through other means.

The trial court found that the reliability of the informant had been sufficiently established. The judge noted, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." Texas precedent supports the proposition that such testimony is sufficient to uphold a finding of probable cause without revealing the informant’s identity. See *State v. Brown*, 830 S.W.2d 575, 578 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1992, no pet.) (holding that the reliability of a confidential informant may be established through the testimony of law enforcement officers without disclosing the informant's identity).

The defense argues that "new information"—specifically that the informant was with the Appellant on the day of the arrest and that her belongings were found in his car—necessitated disclosure to impeach this reliability. However, the presence of the informant or the location of her belongings does not inherently contradict the accuracy of the tip provided to the officers or the validity of the past tips cited by the judge. The defense failed to demonstrate how this specific information proved the informant was untruthful in her communication with law enforcement, as opposed to merely being involved in the underlying criminal activity. See *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436, 439 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001) (stating that disclosure depends on whether the informant's testimony is relevant to the suppression issue, not to guilt or innocence).

**C. Conclusion on the Motion to Disclose**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The defense did not meet the high burden required to override the government’s informant privilege. The arguments presented by the defense largely concerned the informant’s role in the alleged crime (entrapment/guilt) rather than a specific defect in the probable cause affidavit that could not be addressed through the officers' testimony. The "dispositive" nature of the motion further underscores that issues of guilt were not properly before the court at that stage.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The court properly limited the hearing to the legality of the search and found that the reliability of the informant was sufficiently established by the officers' testimony regarding past tips. The defense’s arguments regarding the informant "setting up" the Appellant pertained to guilt and innocence, which are outside the scope of a dispositive motion to suppress.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling affirms the decision of the trial court.

The defense repeatedly invoked entrapment and "setting up" as grounds for disclosure, yet these are quintessential guilt-or-innocence defenses outside the scope of a suppression hearing. As *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 100 S. Ct. 2439, 65 L. Ed. 2d 468 (1980), confirms, suppression hearings "are designed to deter police misconduct, not to test the truth of the government’s case or to address guilt or innocence." The defense’s assertion that "if she set him up, it goes to her reliability" is a transparent attempt to inject entrapment—a trial defense—into the suppression hearing. This directly contravenes *United States v. Smith*, 126 F.3d 1231, 1238 (10th Cir. 1997), which holds that disclosure is required "only when the informant’s testimony is necessary to a determination of the suppression issue." Here, the defense did not challenge the *accuracy* of the informant’s tip or the *corroboration* of its details by police; it instead argued the informant’s involvement in the crime undermined her credibility for trial purposes. Such a claim is irrelevant to whether the officers had probable cause based on the tip itself.  

#### II. THE DEFENSE FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE HOW "NEW INFORMATION" AFFECTED PROBABLE CAUSE  
The defense’s reliance on "new information" — that the informant was present at the arrest and her belongings were found in the appellant’s car — is procedurally and substantively deficient. Texas precedent squarely rejects such claims when they do not challenge the reliability of the tip for probable cause. In *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436, 439 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001), the court held disclosure is warranted only "when the informant’s testimony is relevant to the suppression issue, not to guilt or innocence." The presence of the informant at the scene or the location of her belongings does not establish that the tip was untruthful or that officers lacked probable cause. As *State v. Brown*, 830 S.W.2d 575, 578 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1992, no pet.), explains, "the reliability of a confidential informant may be established through the testimony of law enforcement officers without disclosing the informant’s identity." The trial court properly credited officer testimony about the informant’s "past tips" and "accuracy," which sufficed to support probable cause. The defense never demonstrated how the "new information" contradicted the tip’s factual accuracy or the officers’ reasonable reliance on it.  

#### III. THE DISPOSITIVE NATURE OF THE MOTION BARRED DEFENSE ATTEMPTS TO LITIGATE TRIAL DEFENSES  
The parties’ agreement that the suppression hearing would be dispositive — meaning a ruling in the State’s favor would trigger a guilty plea without trial — definitively circumscribed the issues before the court. As the prosecutor correctly stated during the hearing, "if they wanted to have the confidential informant disclosed and then have that attacked, they should have had a trial." This procedural context is critical: in a dispositive suppression hearing, the court is limited to the Fourth Amendment legality of the search. *Roviaro* does not require disclosure when the defendant seeks to "test the truth of the government’s case" for trial purposes, as *Payner* makes clear. The defense’s invocation of *Holmes v. South Carolina* — which concerns trial rights to present a defense — is wholly inapposite to a suppression hearing. The trial court acted correctly in denying disclosure because the defense’s arguments were irrelevant to the narrow suppression issue.  

#### IV. THE DEFENSE MISAPPLIED *ROVIARO* BY IGNORING THE HIGH BURDEN FOR DISCLOSURE  
*Roviaro* requires a defendant to show a "reasonable probability" that the informant’s testimony would be "helpful in the defense of the accused" on the specific issue before the court. 353 U.S. at 64–65. Here, the defense failed to meet this burden. Its arguments focused on entrapment and guilt — not whether the informant’s tip was unreliable for probable cause. The "new information" cited does not establish that the informant’s testimony would have altered the probable cause analysis. Texas courts consistently hold that mere speculation about an informant’s involvement in the crime does not overcome the informant’s privilege. See *Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d at 439. The trial court’s finding that the informant’s reliability was established through officer testimony — without disclosure — was fully supported by the record.  

#### CONCLUSION  
The Court of Appeals correctly affirmed the trial court’s denial of disclosure. The defense’s arguments were barred by the dispositive nature of the suppression hearing and improperly conflated trial defenses with Fourth Amendment analysis. Under *Payner*, *Smith*, and Texas precedent, the informant’s identity was irrelevant to the suppression issue, and the defense failed to demonstrate how "new information" undermined the tip’s reliability for probable cause. The judgment should be upheld.

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the defense's arguments and ignores the fundamental principle that informant reliability is squarely within the scope of a probable cause analysis. The prosecution relies on *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 100 S. Ct. 2439, 65 L. Ed. 2d 468 (1980), for the proposition that suppression hearings are not designed to test the truth of the government's case. However, *Payner* addressed the distinct question of whether a defendant could challenge the government's case through suppression motions when the evidence was obtained through constitutional violations by third parties. The case does not stand for the proposition that a defendant cannot challenge the reliability of the informant who provided the information forming the basis of probable cause.

Critically, the defense did not argue that the informant's involvement meant the defendant was innocent. Rather, the defense argued that an informant who "sets up" a defendant has a motive to fabricate or exaggerate information provided to police—a classic credibility attack that goes to the heart of probable cause. As the Supreme Court held in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 591 (1957), disclosure may be required when the informant's testimony "may be helpful to the defense" on issues before the court. Probable cause is precisely such an issue.

## II. THE "NEW INFORMATION" DIRECTLY CHALLENGED PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution dismisses the "new information"—that the informant was with the defendant on the day of arrest and that her belongings were found in his car—as irrelevant to probable cause. This analysis is fundamentally flawed.

The reliability of a confidential informant under the *Aguilar-Spinelli* test (and its Texas counterpart) requires examination of both the informant's basis of knowledge and the informant's veracity or reliability. See *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 84 S. Ct. 1509, 12 L. Ed. 2d 723 (1964); *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410, 89 S. Ct. 584, 21 L. Ed. 2d 637 (1969). When an informant is discovered to have been personally present at the transaction giving rise to the charges, and particularly when the informant's belongings are found in the defendant's possession, this information directly impeaches the informant's reliability.

The prosecution argues that this information "does not establish that the tip was untruthful." However, this misstates the applicable standard. The question under *Roviaro* is not whether the defendant can prove the tip was actually untruthful, but whether the informant's testimony could be "helpful to the defense" in challenging the suppression issue. 353 U.S. at 64. An informant who was present at the transaction has personal knowledge that could corroborate or contradict the information provided to police. The defense is entitled to explore this inconsistency.

In *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001), the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals held that disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is "relevant to the suppression issue." The defense's "new information" is directly relevant to the suppression issue because it goes to whether the informant provided reliable information upon which officers could reasonably rely for probable cause. The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Brown*, 830 S.W.2d 575 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1992, no pet.), is misplaced because in *Brown*, there was no assertion that the informant had personal knowledge contradicting the tip or that the informant's reliability had been undermined by subsequent evidence.

The prosecution places significant weight on the parties' agreement that the suppression hearing would be "dispositional." This argument fails for two reasons.

The prosecution's argument that "issues relating to guilt or innocence did not matter" conflates the consequence of the ruling with the substance of the inquiry. The court was still required to determine whether the search was lawful—this required examination of probable cause, which requires examination of informant reliability.

Second, even assuming the "dispositive" nature of the hearing limits the issues properly before the court, the defense's arguments were directed at probable cause—the precise issue before the court. The prosecution argues that the defense sought disclosure to "have that attacked" at trial. This is not a trial defense—it is a Fourth Amendment defense.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Smith*, 126 F.3d 1231 (10th Cir. 1997), is unavailing. In *Smith*, the Tenth Circuit held that disclosure is required "only when the informant's testimony is necessary to a determination of the suppression issue." This standard supports the defense, not the prosecution. The informant's testimony was necessary to determine whether the informant's tip was reliable enough to support probable cause. The defense's "new information" suggested the informant had personal knowledge that could contradict her tip—precisely the kind of information that could affect the suppression determination.

The prosecution argues the defense failed to meet the *Roviaro* burden because it did not show a "reasonable probability" that the informant's testimony would be helpful. This argument ignores the defense's specific showing.

Defense counsel argued that the informant was with the defendant on the day of the arrest, that her belongings were found in his car, and that she "set him up." This information, if true, would establish that the informant had a personal stake in the outcome of the investigation—precisely the type of information that could render her tip unreliable. The defense did not need to prove these facts definitively; it needed only to show that the informant's testimony could be helpful to the defense on the suppression issue.

The trial court's refusal to consider this showing was error. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Roviaro*, the government's privilege against disclosure "has limited existence" and must give way "when the disclosure of an informer's identity ... is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." 353 U.S. at 60-61. The prosecution's arguments below focused on the "dispositive" nature of the hearing and attempted to confine the defense to arguments that did not touch on guilt or innocence. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Appellant’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in refusing to order disclosure because the informant’s identity and testimony were necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the probable cause affidavit. The Appellee argues that the hearing was limited to a "dispositive" motion to suppress and that the defense’s request was improperly based on issues of guilt and innocence, specifically entrapment, rather than the legality of the search. Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both parties, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion and the Probable Cause Inquiry**

The Appellee argues that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress barred the defense from raising issues that pertained to guilt or innocence, characterizing the defense’s arguments as an improper attempt to litigate an entrapment defense. While it is true that a suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits, see *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 100 S. Ct. 2439, 65 L. Ed. 2d 468 (1980), the defense correctly points out that the "dispositive" agreement governed the *consequence* of the ruling, not the *substantive scope* of the Fourth Amendment inquiry.

The core issue in a motion to suppress is whether the police had probable cause to believe a crime was being committed. Probable cause is not established by the mere fact of a tip; it requires a demonstration of the informant’s basis of knowledge and veracity. See *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 84 S. Ct. 1509, 12 L. Ed. 2d 723 (1964); *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410, 89 S. Ct. 584, 21 L. Ed. 2d 637 (1969). Therefore, an attack on the informant’s veracity is squarely within the scope of a suppression hearing. The defense’s argument that the informant "set him up" is not solely an entrapment defense; it is a direct challenge to the informant’s credibility and reliability. If the informant fabricated the tip or was motivated to frame the Appellant, the tip cannot support probable cause. Thus, the defense’s arguments were relevant to the suppression issue, not merely to guilt or innocence.

**B. The "New Information" and the Informant’s Veracity**

The trial court relied on the officers’ testimony regarding the informant’s "past tips" and "accuracy" to establish reliability, citing *State v. Brown*, 830 S.W.2d 575, 578 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1992, no pet.). However, the defense presented "new information" that the informant was present at the scene of the arrest and that her belongings were found in the Appellant’s car. This evidence suggests the informant was not a neutral observer but a participant in the events.

Under *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 591 (1957), the government’s privilege to withhold an informant’s identity must yield when the informant’s testimony is relevant and helpful to the defense. The defense has demonstrated a particularized need for disclosure here. The informant’s potential status as a participant who "set up" the Appellant goes directly to the veracity prong of the *Aguilar-Spinelli* test. An informant who is an active participant in the alleged crime has a motive to lie or exaggerate the defendant’s involvement to justify their own role or to secure a benefit from law enforcement.

The Appellee argues that the "new information" does not prove the tip was untruthful. However, the defense is not required to prove the tip was false at this stage; they need only show that the informant’s testimony could be helpful to the defense in challenging the probable cause determination. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 64–65. The trial court’s reliance on the informant’s general history of reliable tips was insufficient to disregard the specific evidence of potential bias or fabrication in this instance. See *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436, 439 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001) (holding disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is relevant to the suppression issue).

**C. Conclusion on the Motion to Disclose**

The defense successfully established that the informant’s identity was necessary to a fair determination of the probable cause issue. The informant was a material witness to the circumstances surrounding the tip and the arrest. The defense’s showing that the informant may have "set up" the Appellant raised a legitimate question regarding the informant’s motive to fabricate, which is a critical component of the reliability analysis. By denying the motion to disclose, the trial court prevented the defense from adequately challenging the veracity of the informant, thereby denying the Appellant a fair opportunity to contest the probable cause for the search.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The defense’s arguments were properly directed at the reliability of the informant, which is a fundamental component of the probable cause inquiry. The "dispositive" nature of the hearing did not strip the Appellant of his right to challenge the factual basis for the search. The "new information" provided a sufficient basis to require disclosure under *Roviaro*.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The defense’s reliance on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. But the defense never demonstrated how the informant’s identity would aid in challenging the *probable cause* determination. It asserted the informant “set him up,” but that is a claim of entrapment or innocence—a trial defense wholly unrelated to whether the tip provided a reasonable basis for the search. An informant’s potential participation in the crime does not automatically undermine the reliability of a tip given to law enforcement; it merely suggests a motive for the defendant’s guilt or innocence, which is irrelevant to probable cause. As the Eleventh Circuit held in *United States v. Smith*, 126 F.3d 1231, 1236 (11th Cir. 1997), disclosure is required under *Roviaro* only when the informant’s testimony is “necessary to a determination of the suppression issue.” Here, the defense failed to meet that standard because its arguments addressed guilt or innocence, not the legality of the search.

The defense also cites *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436 (Tex. Crim. Instead, it only sought to undermine the government’s case at trial. By contrast, in *State v. Brown*, 830 S.W.2d 575, 578 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1992, no pet.), this Court affirmed that an informant’s past reliability is sufficient for probable cause absent evidence that the informant’s credibility was compromised *at the time of the tip*. The defense presented no such evidence; it only pointed to post-tip events that bear on guilt, not reliability. The trial court correctly distinguished *Brown* and *Garcia* by recognizing that the defense’s arguments fell outside the scope of the suppression hearing.

The defense’s claim that it needed disclosure to challenge the informant’s “credibility” is a semantic sleight of hand. Credibility for probable cause is assessed based on the tip’s content and the informant’s track record—not on whether the informant later became a participant in the crime. If the defense’s theory were correct, every suppression hearing would become a trial on the merits whenever an informant is present at the scene. That is not the law. The Fourth Amendment does not require disclosure of an informant’s identity merely because the informant may have been involved in the underlying offense; it requires disclosure only when the informant’s testimony is necessary to determine whether the search was lawful. Here, it was not.

The court of appeals’ reversal improperly conflated probable cause with guilt or innocence. The trial court properly denied disclosure because the defense failed to show that the informant’s identity was essential to a fair determination of the suppression issue. By allowing the defense to litigate entrapment and innocence during a suppression hearing, the court of appeals undermined the narrow scope of such proceedings and invited chaos into the judicial process. For these reasons, we urge this Court to reverse the court of appeals and uphold the trial court’s ruling.

### A. The Defense's Arguments Were Properly Directed at the Probable Cause Inquiry, Not Guilt or Innocence

The prosecution contends that the defense's motion to disclose was an improper attempt to litigate guilt or innocence during a suppression hearing. This characterization fundamentally misreads the defense's arguments and ignores the core issue in any probable cause determination: the reliability of the informant.

The prosecution correctly notes that a suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits. However, the prosecution erroneously concludes that therefore the defense cannot challenge the factual basis for probable cause. This reasoning is circular and would eviscerate a defendant's Fourth Amendment rights. The very purpose of a suppression hearing is to determine whether the police had probable cause to conduct the search. As the Court of Appeals correctly noted, probable cause is not established by the mere fact of a tip; it requires a demonstration of the informant's basis of knowledge and veracity. See *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 84 S. Ct. 1509, 12 L. Ed. 2d 723 (1964); *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410, 89 S. Ct. 584, 21 L. Ed. 2d 637 (1969).

The prosecution argues that the defense's "new information"—that the informant was present at the scene and her belongings were found in the defendant's car—does not implicate the informant's reliability "at the time the tip was made." This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates the temporal element of reliability with the motivational element. While the informant's history of accurate tips may establish baseline reliability, evidence of the informant's participation in the events giving rise to the search directly speaks to the informant's motive to fabricate or exaggerate. An informant who "sets up" a defendant has a powerful incentive to provide information that justifies the defendant's arrest and secures benefits for the informant. This motive exists at the time the tip is given and directly affects the tip's reliability.

Second, the prosecution's argument proves too much. Under the prosecution's reasoning, a defendant could never challenge an informant's reliability through evidence of the informant's conduct during the relevant events because such evidence would necessarily postdate the tip. This would render the probable cause inquiry a purely historical exercise, ignoring the crucial question of whether the informant had any reason to lie. The law does not require such a rigid and impractical approach.

The defense's argument that the informant "set him up" is not solely an entrapment defense, as the prosecution suggests. It is a direct challenge to the informant's credibility and reliability. If the informant fabricated the tip or was motivated to frame the defendant, the tip cannot support probable cause. Thus, the defense's arguments were relevant to the suppression issue, not merely to guilt or innocence.

### B. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Hearing Did Not Limit the Substantive Scope of the Inquiry

The prosecution emphasizes that the hearing was "dispositive" and that the defense agreed to this characterization. However, as the Court of Appeals correctly noted, the "dispositive" agreement governed the consequence of the ruling, not the substantive scope of the Fourth Amendment inquiry.

The prosecution's own statements at the hearing demonstrate this point. The prosecution argues that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. In *Roviaro*, the Supreme Court held that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity must yield when the informant's testimony is relevant and helpful to the defense or is essential to a fair determination of the cause. 353 U.S. at 60-61. The Court emphasized that the scope of the privilege is limited and that disclosure may be required when the informant is a participant in the crime charged. The Court stated: "We believe that no fixed rule with respect to informant is workable. Here, the defense presented "new information" that the informant was present at the scene of the arrest and that her belongings were found in the defendant's car. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the State’s appeal from the Court of Appeals’ decision reversing the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The State argues that the Court of Appeals erred by allowing the defense to litigate issues of guilt and innocence—specifically entrapment—under the guise of a probable cause challenge during a suppression hearing. The Appellant contends that the "new information" regarding the CI’s presence and belongings in his car established a particularized need for disclosure to challenge the informant’s veracity. After reviewing the rebuttal arguments of both parties, the Court affirms the judgment of the Court of Appeals.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the "Dispositive" Motion**

The State contends that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress limited the inquiry to the legality of the search, barring the defense from introducing evidence related to the Appellant’s guilt or innocence. The State relies on *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 100 S. Ct. 2439, 65 L. Ed. 2d 468 (1980), for the proposition that suppression hearings are not trials on the merits.

While the Court agrees with the general principle articulated in *Payner*, the State’s application of it here is overly broad. The record reflects that the "dispositive" agreement governed the *consequence* of the ruling—specifically, that a ruling for the State would result in a plea and a ruling for the defense would result in a dismissal. It did not act as a stipulation that the informant was reliable or that the defense waived its right to challenge the factual basis of the probable cause. As the Appellant correctly argues, the "dispositive" label defined the procedural endpoint, not the evidentiary boundaries of the Fourth Amendment inquiry. To rule otherwise would allow the State to trade away the defendant's constitutional right to challenge probable cause in exchange for a procedural benefit.

**B. The Nexus Between Participation, Motive, and Probable Cause**

The central legal dispute is whether the defense’s theory that the informant "set him up" is a challenge to probable cause or an entrapment defense. The State argues that because the informant’s alleged participation occurred during the crime, it relates only to guilt or innocence, not to the reliability of the tip at the time it was made. The State cites *State v. Brown*, 830 S.W.2d 575, 578 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1992, no pet.), asserting that an informant’s past reliability is sufficient to establish probable cause absent evidence of compromise at the time of the tip.

This argument misunderstands the relationship between participation and veracity. Under the *Aguilar-Spinelli* test, see *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 84 S. Ct. 1509, 12 L. Ed. 2d 723 (1964); *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410, 89 S. Ct. 584, 21 L. Ed. 2d 637 (1969), probable cause based on an informant’s tip requires a demonstration of the informant’s basis of knowledge and veracity. Veracity is not solely a function of an informant’s track record; it also encompasses the absence of a motive to fabricate.

If the informant was an active participant who "set up" the Appellant, she possessed a significant motive to lie to law enforcement to justify her own role, shift blame, or secure favorable treatment. This motive exists at the time the tip is provided. Therefore, evidence that the informant was a participant is not merely evidence of entrapment; it is evidence of potential bias that directly attacks the *veracity* prong of the probable cause analysis. The trial court’s reliance on the informant’s general history of reliable tips, as endorsed by *State v. Brown*, was insufficient to overcome this specific evidence of potential bias in this instance. See *State v. Garcia*, 57 S.W.3d 436, 439 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001) (holding disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is relevant to the suppression issue).

**C. The Standard for Disclosure Under *Roviaro***

The State argues that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 77 S. Ct. 623, 1 L. Ed. 2d 591 (1957), does not require disclosure because the defense failed to show the informant’s identity was necessary to determine the legality of the search. The State relies on *United States v. Smith*, 126 F.3d 1231, 1236 (11th Cir. 1997), for the proposition that disclosure is limited to suppression issues.

The Court finds the Appellant’s reliance on *Roviaro* to be well-founded. *Roviaro* establishes that the government’s privilege to withhold an informant’s identity must yield when the informant’s testimony is relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of the cause. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60–61. The Supreme Court specifically noted that disclosure is frequently required when the informant is a participant in the crime charged. Id. at 65.

Here, the defense presented "new information" that the informant’s belongings were found in the Appellant’s car and that she was present at the scene. This evidence suggests the informant was more than a mere tipster; she was a participant. Her testimony regarding the events leading up to the arrest—and specifically regarding whether the Appellant possessed the cocaine independently or as a result of her "set up"—is critical to assessing the credibility of the tip. Because the informant’s veracity is the foundation of the probable cause affidavit, her identity is necessary to a fair determination of the suppression issue. The defense is not required to prove the tip was false at this stage; it need only show a "particularized need" for the informant’s testimony to challenge the basis for the search. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 64–65. That standard has been met.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State’s attempt to characterize the defense’s challenge as a purely procedural entrapment defense ignores the fundamental requirement that probable cause be based on reliable information. The evidence that the informant may have been a participant who set up the Appellant raises a legitimate question regarding her motive to fabricate, which is inextricably linked to the veracity of the tip. The trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose without considering the impact of this "new information" on the probable cause determination. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed the trial court’s ruling.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed, and the case is remanded to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
