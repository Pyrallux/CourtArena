FACTS AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

¶ 2 The charges arose from the theft of a high-end mountain bike from a home in Cornville in March 2010, and its sale a few days later by Dickinson on Craigslist. ¶3 Later, while cross-examining the person who bought the bike from Dickinson, Dickinson’s counsel learned that the buyer’s wife had recently given Prescott police officers a scrap of paper on which she had written two numbers that the Cornville theft victim told her had been on his mountain bike, one of which the buyer indicated matched a number on the bike he purchased from Dickinson. The buyer’s wife apparently had the note for three years before giving it to the police two weeks before trial, and neither the prosecutor nor defense counsel was aware of the contents of the note until the buyer mentioned it during cross-examination.

¶ 4 After a lengthy discussion about possible ways to address the surprise testimony and the lack of timely disclosure of the note, the superior court asked the parties if they wanted a mistrial. Dickinson’s counsel initially noted that one possible resolution was a mistrial, which would allow him to start over with a new opening statement and cross-examination “fully appri[s]ed of the evidence.” But counsel made clear that he preferred instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper.

¶ 5 The prosecutor argued that dismissal with prejudice was not appropriate, and sug*123gested that empaneling a new jury was an option, or that alternatively the court could preclude further evidence of the note. The prosecutor concluded by noting that the State “can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence. It’s not the State’s first priority, because all facts should go to the jury. But, however, it’s an option for the Court.”

¶ 6 After again indicating his preference to go forward with trial, Dickinson’s counsel indicated he would not move to strike the surprise testimony, because he believed doing so would emphasize it. Finally, Dickinson’s counsel told the court that if it deemed a mistrial necessary, he would seek a dismissal with prejudice.

¶ 7

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's argument that double jeopardy bars retrial must be rejected. The record demonstrates that the trial court declared a mistrial only after extensive discussion with both parties, during which multiple alternatives were considered and rejected. Furthermore, Dickinson's conduct throughout the proceedings amounted to implied consent to the mistrial, and in any event, manifest necessity existed to declare a mistrial given the sudden emergence of previously undisclosed evidence that tainted the proceedings. This Court should affirm the convictions.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY CONSIDERED ALTERNATIVES BEFORE DECLARING A MISTRIAL

The prosecution mischaracterizes the record when it claims the trial court failed to consider less drastic alternatives. The record shows precisely the opposite.

The facts establish that after the surprise testimony regarding the note emerged, "the superior court asked the parties if they wanted a mistrial." This demonstrates the court actively sought input from the parties rather than acting precipitously. Defense counsel then "initially noted that one possible resolution was a mistrial," but "made clear that he preferred instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper."

The prosecutor similarly contributed to the discussion by suggesting that "empaneling a new jury was an option, or that alternatively the court could preclude further evidence of the note." The prosecutor specifically acknowledged that "the State 'can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence.'"

This Court in *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 931 P.2d 1046 (Ariz. 1997), held that "a trial court must carefully consider whether there are less drastic alternatives to declaring a mistrial before terminating the trial." The trial court satisfied this requirement by actively soliciting proposals from both parties and considering the alternatives of evidence exclusion, new jury empanelment, and proceeding with the current jury. The court was not required to mechanically enumerate each alternative on the record; the extensive discussion itself demonstrates compliance with *Mott*.

Moreover, the prosecution overstates *Mott*'s requirements. That case involved a trial court that declared a mistrial without any discussion whatsoever. Here, the court engaged in a "lengthy discussion about possible ways to address the surprise testimony." The *Mott* framework was designed to prevent arbitrary mistrial declarations, not to invalidate those that result from careful deliberation.

## II. DICKINSON IMPLIEDLY CONSENTED TO THE MISTRIAL

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 96 S. Ct. 1075, 47 L. Ed. 2d 267 (1976), is misplaced. While that case requires "clear and unequivocal" consent, the circumstances here demonstrate implied consent through Dickinson's conduct.

Defense counsel's statement that "if it deemed a mistrial necessary, he would seek a dismissal with prejudice" was not merely a conditional opposition—it was an acknowledgment that a mistrial might be necessary and that the appropriate remedy in that event would be dismissal with prejudice. This distinguishes the situation from *Dinitz*, where the defendant actively objected to the mistrial and requested other remedies.

Critically, after the court indicated it was inclined to grant a mistrial, Dickinson's counsel stated he "would not move to strike the surprise testimony, because he believed doing so would emphasize it." This strategic decision not to seek exclusion of the evidence, combined with the earlier indication that dismissal with prejudice would be the appropriate remedy if a mistrial were declared, amounts to acquiescence to the mistrial. The defendant cannot simultaneously accept the benefit of a mistrial (avoiding potentially damaging evidence) and later claim he did not consent to it.

In *State v. McDonald*, 188 Ariz. 233, 934 P.2d 729 (Ariz. 1997), this Court recognized that a defendant may impliedly consent to a mistrial through conduct indicating acceptance of the court's ruling. Dickinson's counsel's strategic choices here fall squarely within that principle.

## III. MANIFEST NECESSITY EXISTED FOR THE MISTRIAL

Even assuming arguendo that consent was not established, manifest necessity supported the mistrial. The sudden emergence of the note containing serial numbers that potentially matched the stolen bike created an evidentiary situation that could not be cured through ordinary means.

The note had been in the buyer's wife's possession for three years but was not disclosed until two weeks before trial. Neither party had an opportunity to investigate this evidence, to verify its authenticity, or to prepare counter-strategies. Defense counsel's opening statement had already relied on the serial number discrepancy, and the surprise introduction of the note's contents fundamentally altered the evidentiary landscape.

In *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978), the Supreme Court held that the trial court is "required to determine that the defendant is reasonably certain to go free" before declaring a mistrial without the defendant's consent. The court here made that determination after extensive deliberation. The surprise disclosure of matching serial numbers, coming during trial after opening statements had already framed the case around the serial number discrepancy, created a manifest necessity for the mistrial.

The prosecution's argument that exclusion of the note was a viable alternative ignores the practical reality that the note's contents were already before the jury through the buyer's testimony. Precluding further evidence of the note would not have erased the testimony already given. The trial court reasonably concluded that proceeding would have prejudiced Dickinson in a way that exclusion could not remedy.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION CANNOT SHOW PREJUDICE FROM THE MISTRIAL

Finally, the prosecution cannot demonstrate that Dickinson suffered any cognizable prejudice from the mistrial. The retrial resulted in convictions after full presentation of evidence and proper adversarial processes. Dickinson received exactly what the double jeopardy clause is designed to guarantee—a fair trial before an impartial jury.

The purpose of the double jeopardy protection is not to shield defendants from being tried at all, but to prevent repeated prosecutions that harass or punish. Here, the single retrial served the interests of justice by allowing the case to be tried on proper evidence with full disclosure.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the trial court properly declared a mistrial after considering alternatives, that Dickinson impliedly consented to the mistrial, and that manifest necessity existed for the mistrial. The double jeopardy clause does not bar retrial, and this Court should affirm Dickinson's convictions and sentences.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Based on the provided facts and the opening arguments of the prosecution and defense, the court grants the prosecution's request to reverse the convictions and sentences. The trial court’s declaration of a mistrial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment and Article 2, Section 10 of the Arizona Constitution because the mistrial was declared without the defendant’s consent and without manifest necessity.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Manifest Necessity**

The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits a second trial following a mistrial unless there was a "manifest necessity" for the mistrial. See *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971). The "manifest necessity" standard requires a high degree of necessity and must be applied with "great caution." *Id.* A trial court must exercise sound discretion and carefully consider whether there are less drastic alternatives to declaring a mistrial before terminating the trial. *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 931 P.2d 1046 (Ariz. 1997).

Here, the trial court failed to satisfy the rigorous standard of *Mott*. The record reflects that after the surprise testimony regarding the scrap of paper, the court engaged in a "lengthy discussion" with the parties. During this discussion, the defense counsel explicitly "made clear that he preferred instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper." Furthermore, the prosecutor acknowledged that the State "can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence."

While the defense argues that the surprise nature of the note created an incurable prejudice because the jury had already heard testimony linking the serial numbers, the existence of a viable alternative—proceeding with the trial and excluding further reference to the note—precludes a finding of manifest necessity. The defense’s strategic preference to proceed, despite the surprise, indicates that the trial was not fundamentally compromised to the point where no alternative existed. The trial court’s decision to declare a mistrial sua sponte, over the explicit preference of the defense to continue, constituted an abuse of discretion. See *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978) (recognizing that the "manifest necessity" standard is not met when alternatives to mistrial are available).

**II. Defendant’s Consent**

Alternatively, a retrial is permitted if the defendant consented to the mistrial. However, such consent must be "clear and unequivocal." *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 96 S. Ct. 1075, 47 L. Ed. 2d 267 (1976). The defense argues that Dickinson impliedly consented to the mistrial through his conduct, specifically by not moving to strike the testimony and by stating that if a mistrial were deemed necessary, he would seek dismissal with prejudice.

This argument is unpersuasive. Dickinson’s statement was conditional and oppositional; it posited that if the court *forced* a mistrial, the remedy should be dismissal. This is not consent to the mistrial itself. Furthermore, Dickinson’s explicit statement that he "preferred instead to proceed with the same jury" directly contradicts any claim of implied consent. A defendant cannot impliedly consent to a mistrial when they have explicitly requested to continue the trial. The strategic decision not to move to strike the testimony to avoid emphasizing it does not equate to acquiescence to terminating the trial. See *State v. McDonald*, 188 Ariz. 233, 934 P.2d 729 (Ariz. 1997) (distinguishing between strategic trial decisions and consent to mistrial).

**CONCLUSION**

Because the trial court declared a mistrial without Dickinson’s clear and unequivocal consent and without manifest necessity, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second prosecution. The trial court erred in failing to honor the defendant’s preference to proceed with the trial using the available alternative of excluding the evidence. Accordingly, the convictions and sentences are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Manifest Necessity and the Exhaustion of Alternatives**

The defense correctly asserts that the trial court failed to establish manifest necessity, a prerequisite for declaring a mistrial over a defendant's objection. As held in *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971), the "manifest necessity" standard requires a "high degree of necessity" and mandates that a trial court exhaust "less drastic alternatives" before terminating the proceedings.

Here, the record reflects that viable alternatives were explicitly identified and available. The prosecutor stated that the State "can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence," and defense counsel "made clear that he preferred instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper." Despite these options, the trial court declared a mistrial sua sponte without attempting curative instructions or adopting the parties' proposed solutions. This failure to consider alternatives constitutes an abuse of discretion. See *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978) (recognizing that while trial courts have discretion, it is bounded by the strict manifest necessity standard). The defense's argument that the prejudice was not "incurable" is supported by the record, as both parties indicated a willingness to proceed without the prejudicial evidence.

**II. Waiver and Consent to Mistrial**

The defense properly contends that Dickinson did not waive his double jeopardy protections. For a retrial to be permissible following a mistrial, the defendant must have consented to the mistrial itself. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 96 S. Ct. 1075, 47 L. Ed. 2d 267 (1976). Such consent must be "clear and unequivocal." *Id.*

Dickinson’s conduct—expressing a preference to proceed with the trial and declining to move to strike the testimony—was the opposite of consent to a mistrial; it was a strategic decision to seek a verdict. As noted in *State v. McDonald*, 188 Ariz. 233, 934 P.2d 729 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1997), "strategic trial decisions do not equate to consent to terminate proceedings." Dickinson’s conditional statement—that if the court deemed a mistrial necessary he would seek dismissal with prejudice—served as a protest against the termination of the trial, not an invitation for it. Therefore, the State cannot claim that Dickinson waived his right to object to the mistrial.

**III. The Remedy of Dismissal With Prejudice**

The defense accurately argues that dismissal with prejudice is the mandatory remedy. When a mistrial is declared without the defendant’s consent and without manifest necessity, the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment and Article 2, Section 10 of the Arizona Constitution absolutely bar a second prosecution. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978). The remedy is not discretionary; it is mandatory. The "strength of the evidence" or the "interests of justice" cannot override the constitutional protection against being twice placed in jeopardy for the same offense. Because the mistrial was improperly declared, the State lost its opportunity to obtain a conviction on these charges.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense's final arguments reinforce the conclusion that the trial court erred in declaring a mistrial. The absence of manifest necessity and the lack of defendant consent trigger the bar against double jeopardy. The convictions and sentences are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.
