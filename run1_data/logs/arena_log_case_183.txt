**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR THE APPELLEE, R.R.**  

May it please this Honorable Court:  

Today, we confront a fundamental question of Louisiana law: whether a person who has voluntarily assumed the role of a parent for a child may later demand reimbursement of child support payments from an alleged biological father—without first disavowing the child. The answer is unequivocally **no**.  

J.M.Y. seeks to rewrite Louisiana’s established legal framework by asserting a right to reimbursement under Civil Code Article 227. But Article 227 does not create a cause of action for reimbursement; it establishes a presumption of paternity to protect the child’s stability. Louisiana jurisprudence has long held that a person who has supported a child as their own cannot later seek repayment from a biological father. To permit such a claim would undermine the very purpose of paternity law—to ensure children are raised in stable, predictable environments—and would open the floodgates to litigation that destabilizes familial relationships.  

Louisiana law is clear: **there is no statutory or common law right to reimbursement of child support payments from a biological father when the payer has acted as a parent**. In *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109, the court squarely rejected a claim for reimbursement by a man who had supported a child for years. The court explained: "Louisiana does not recognize a cause of action for the recovery of child support payments from a biological father where the paying party has voluntarily assumed the role of parent." Similarly, in *P.J.L. v. C.J.L.*, 03-1520 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/3/04), 869 So.2d 844, the court emphasized that allowing reimbursement would "frustrate the child’s right to a stable family unit" and "reward the very conduct that disrupts familial bonds."  

Article 227 itself confirms this principle. It creates a presumption of paternity to protect children—not to enable financial recoupment by a presumed parent. The article states that a man who supports a child as his own is presumed to be the father, and that presumption may only be rebutted by "clear and convincing evidence" of non-paternity. But nowhere does Article 227 suggest that a presumed father may then seek reimbursement from the biological father. To the contrary, Louisiana courts have consistently held that the presumption of paternity under Article 227 is a shield for the child’s welfare, not a sword for financial recovery.  

J.M.Y. argues that "no legal provision bars such reimbursement." But that is simply false. Louisiana law imposes a duty on those who assume parental responsibilities to fulfill them without recourse for reimbursement. As the Fourth Circuit noted in *State ex rel. A.P. v. B.P.*, 96-2194 (La. App. 4 Cir. 10/15/97), 702 So.2d 102, "a person who voluntarily assumes the role of father cannot later disavow that role and seek reimbursement from the biological father." This principle applies even if the biological father is later identified. To hold otherwise would incentivize parents to seek reimbursement after years of support, creating chaos for children and courts alike.  

The trial court correctly dismissed J.M.Y.’s suit for lack of a right of action and cause of action. There is no legal theory under which J.M.Y. may recover child support payments from R.R. because Louisiana law does not recognize such a claim. The Court should affirm the judgment below to preserve the integrity of Louisiana’s family law and protect children from the instability that reimbursement suits would bring.  

Thank you, Your Honors.

## INTRODUCTION

The record establishes the following undisputed facts:

6. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. for past child support payments made.
7. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from A.F.Y. for past child support payments made.
8. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for future child support payments.

These facts, taken as true for purposes of the exceptions, must be viewed in the light most favorable to J.M.Y. as the appellant opposing the exceptions. *See Herbert v. Baton Rouge General Hospital*, 258 La. 453, 246 So.2d 821 (1971).

---

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN GRANTING THE EXCEPTION OF NO CAUSE OF ACTION

#### A. Standard of Review

The exception of no cause of action raises a question of law: whether the petition states a valid cause of action under the law. *City of New Orleans v. Board of Commissioners of Orleans Levee District*, 93-0690 (La. 7/5/94), 640 So.2d 237. In reviewing the grant of an exception of no cause of action, the court must accept all well-pleaded facts as true and determine whether the petition states a valid cause of action under any theory of law. *Id.* at 241.

#### B. J.M.Y. States a Valid Cause of Action for Paternity Testing and Determination

Louisiana Civil Code Article 231 provides that the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of a child born during the marriage. However, this presumption is rebuttable. Article 231 states: "The father may disavow the child by bringing a suit within one year of the birth of the child." This Court has recognized that the biological father may also have standing to contest paternity in certain circumstances. *See Succession of Robinson*, 99-0905 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/17/00), 762 So.2d 753.

The petition below states a cause of action for paternity testing under Louisiana law. J.M.Y. has alleged facts sufficient to invoke the jurisdiction of the court to determine biological paternity. The Louisiana legislature has enacted specific provisions for determining paternity, including the Louisiana Paternity Act, La. R.S. 9:401 et seq., which establishes procedures for determining paternity through blood or genetic testing.

In *Doe v. Roe*, 98-0385 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/16/98), 722 So.2d 113, this Court recognized that a party has a right to seek judicial determination of paternity when there is a question as to the identity of the biological father. J.M.Y.'s petition, which seeks genetic testing to determine whether R.R. is the biological father, states a valid cause of action under these principles.

#### C. J.M.Y. States a Valid Cause of Action for Reimbursement

While Louisiana law may not recognize a cause of action for reimbursement in all circumstances, the petition states a cause of action that should survive the exception of no cause of action.

The petition alleges that J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support based on his presumed paternity. If R.R. is determined to be the biological father, then J.M.Y. may have a claim for reimbursement under general principles of unjust enrichment. Article 2298 of the Louisiana Civil Code provides: "A person who has been enriched without cause at the expense of another person is bound to compensate him therefor." If J.M.Y. has paid child support that should have been paid by R.R., this may constitute unjust enrichment.

The cases cited by R.R. are distinguishable. In *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109, the party seeking reimbursement had voluntarily assumed the role of parent with full knowledge of the circumstances. The instant case is different—J.M.Y. was married to the mother at the time of the child's birth and was presumed to be the father under the law. He paid child support pursuant to a court order, not voluntarily.

Similarly, in *P.J.L. v. C.J.L.*, 03-1520 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/3/04), 869 So.2d 844, the court addressed a situation where a presumed father sought to disavow paternity and seek reimbursement simultaneously. The court found such a claim to be contrary to public policy. However, J.M.Y. has not yet disavowed the child—he merely seeks to determine the biological truth and, if R.R. is the father, to seek reimbursement for support paid.

The distinction is critical: Louisiana law protects the stability of the family unit, but it does not prohibit a presumed father from seeking to determine biological paternity or from seeking reimbursement when the true biological father is identified. *See State ex rel. A.P. v. B.P.*, 96-2194 (La. App. 4 Cir. 10/15/97), 702 So.2d 102 (distinguishing between situations where a party has voluntarily assumed parental role versus situations where paternity is contested).

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN GRANTING THE EXCEPTION OF NO RIGHT OF ACTION

#### A. Standard of Review

The exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the subject matter of the suit. *Louisiana Associated General Contractors, Inc. v. State*, 95-1870 (La. 3/8/96), 669 So.2d 1185. The question is whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Id.*

#### B. J.M.Y. Has a Right of Action to Determine Paternity and Seek Reimbursement

J.M.Y. has a direct legal interest in determining whether he is the biological father of the child. As the man presumed to be the father under Civil Code Article 231, and as a person who has paid court-ordered child support, J.M.Y. has a right to seek judicial determination of biological paternity and, if warranted, reimbursement of support payments.

In *In re Succession of Brown*, 96-1539 (La. App. 4 Cir. 2/12/97), 688 So.2d 733, the Court recognized that a presumed father has a legal interest in establishing biological paternity. This interest is sufficient to confer standing to bring a paternity action.

Furthermore, if R.R. is determined to be the biological father, J.M.Y. has a right to seek reimbursement of child support payments made. The payment of support pursuant to a court order does not preclude reimbursement when the true biological father is identified. *See generally, Cazes v. Cazes*, 11-2239 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/12/12), 101 So.3d 507 (discussing reimbursement rights in paternity contexts).

---

## REBUTTAL TO APPELLEE'S ARGUMENTS

### I. The Appellee's Characterization of Louisiana Law Is Overbroad

R.R. asserts that "Louisiana does not recognize a cause of action for the recovery of child support payments from a biological father where the paying party has voluntarily assumed the role of parent." This statement, while accurate in certain contexts, does not accurately state the law as applied to the facts of this case.

J.M.Y. did not "voluntarily assume the role of parent" in the sense contemplated by the jurisprudence. He was married to the mother at the time of the child's birth and was legally presumed to be the father. He was then ordered by a court to pay child support. This is not a case where a man voluntarily supported a child knowing he might not be the biological father. This is a case where a presumed father, acting under court order, seeks to determine biological truth.

### II. Article 227 Does Not Preclude J.M.Y.'s Claims

R.R. argues that Civil Code Article 227 creates a presumption of paternity to protect the child, not to enable financial recoupment. This is true, but it does not address whether a presumed father may seek to rebut the presumption or seek reimbursement when the biological father is identified.

Article 227 provides that a man who represents himself as the father of a child born to his wife is presumed to be the father. This presumption may be rebutted. J.M.Y. seeks to test the accuracy of this presumption through genetic testing. If the presumption is rebutted and R.R. is shown to be the biological father, the legal landscape changes—the presumption that protected the child and J.M.Y.'s obligation to support would be legally altered.

### III. The Cases Cited by R.R. Are Distinguishable

The cases relied upon by R.R. are distinguishable on their facts:

- *K.M. v. M.M.* involved a man who had voluntarily supported a child for years with full knowledge that he might not be the biological father. The court found no cause of action for reimbursement. Here, J.M.Y. was married to the mother, was presumed to be the father, and paid support pursuant to court order.
- *P.J.L. v. C.J.L.* involved a presumed father who sought to simultaneously disavow paternity and seek reimbursement—a situation the court found to be contrary to public policy. J.M.Y. has not disavowed the child; he seeks to determine biological truth.
- *State ex rel. A.P. v. B.P.* involved a situation where a party had voluntarily assumed the parental role. The court held that such a party could not later disavow the role and seek reimbursement. This case is different—J.M.Y. has not disavowed anything; he seeks to establish biological truth.

The petition states valid causes of action for paternity determination and, contingent upon a finding that R.R. is the biological father, for reimbursement of child support payments. The trial court erred in dismissing these claims at the exception stage.

**STATE OF LOUISIANA**

**DECIDED:** [Current Date]

**BEFORE THE COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of J.M.Y. ("Appellant") from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. ("Appellee"). The sole issue presented for this preliminary ruling is whether the trial court legally erred in dismissing Appellant's petition seeking paternity testing and reimbursement of child support payments.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The exception of no cause of action questions whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged in the petition. *City of New Orleans v. Board of Commissioners of Orleans Levee District*, 93-0690 (La. 7/5/94), 640 So.2d 237. In reviewing the granting of this exception, the court must accept all well-pleaded facts as true and determine whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any theory of law. *Id.* at 241. The exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Louisiana Associated General Contractors, Inc. v. State*, 95-1870 (La. 3/8/96), 669 So.2d 1185.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Erred in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action Regarding Paternity Determination**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in dismissing his claim for paternity testing. The Appellee contends that Appellant voluntarily assumed the role of parent and is therefore barred from seeking relief. However, the Appellant correctly identifies that he was married to the mother at the time of the child's birth, invoking the presumption of paternity under Louisiana Civil Code Article 231, rather than the voluntary presumption under Article 227 cited by the Appellee.

The Appellant has a legitimate interest in establishing the biological truth of his paternity. As recognized in *Succession of Robinson*, 99-0905 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/17/00), 762 So.2d 753, a presumed father has a legal interest in establishing biological paternity. Furthermore, the Louisiana Paternity Act, La. R.S. 9:401 et seq., provides specific procedures for such determinations. The Appellant's petition alleges sufficient facts to invoke the court's jurisdiction to determine biological paternity. *See Doe v. Roe*, 98-0385 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/16/98), 722 So.2d 113. Therefore, the trial court erred in dismissing this claim via the exception of no cause of action.

**B. The Trial Court Erred in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action Regarding Reimbursement**

The Appellee argues that Louisiana law categorically bars reimbursement of child support payments from a biological father, relying heavily on *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109, and *P.J.L. v. C.J.L.*, 03-1520 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/3/04), 869 So.2d 844. The Appellee asserts that allowing such claims would undermine the stability of the family unit.

However, the Appellant has successfully distinguished the controlling jurisprudence. In *K.M. v. M.M.*, the court barred reimbursement where the party had "voluntarily assumed the role of parent." Here, the Appellant argues that he did not voluntarily assume the role in the same manner; rather, he was the husband of the mother and was presumed to be the father by operation of law (Civil Code Art. 231). He paid child support pursuant to a court order, not purely by voluntary choice. This distinction is legally significant. The Appellee's reliance on Article 227, which applies to unmarried men who represent themselves as the father, is misplaced given the marital context of this case.

Furthermore, the Appellant has pleaded a valid cause of action for unjust enrichment under Louisiana Civil Code Article 2298. The petition alleges that if R.R. is the biological father, R.R. has been enriched without cause at the Appellant's expense. While public policy concerns regarding family stability are valid, they do not automatically negate a claim for unjust enrichment where the plaintiff has paid support under a mistaken presumption of paternity. The Appellant cites *Cazes v. Cazes*, 11-2239 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/12/12), 101 So.3d 507, in support of the proposition that reimbursement rights may exist in paternity contexts.

At this preliminary stage, the Court must accept the Appellant's allegations as true. *Herbert v. Baton Rouge General Hospital*, 258 La. 453, 246 So.2d 821 (1971). The Appellant has alleged a factual scenario where he is not the biological father, yet has paid support to the benefit of the biological father and mother. This states a valid cause of action for unjust enrichment, subject to proof at trial. The trial court erred in determining that no cause of action exists as a matter of law.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court erred in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The Appellant has stated valid causes of action for paternity determination and, contingent upon a finding of non-paternity, for reimbursement under the theory of unjust enrichment.

**JUDGMENT**

IT IS ORDERED that the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action is REVERSED. This matter is REMANDED to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL ON BEHALF OF APPELLEES R.R. AND A.F.Y.

## RESPONSE TO APPELLANT'S CLAIMS AND OPPOSITION TO REVERSAL OF TRIAL COURT JUDGMENT

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

Appellees R.R. and A.F.Y. respectfully submit this defense rebuttal in opposition to the Appellant J.M.Y.'s appeal and in support of affirming the trial court's original judgment granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. Contrary to the preliminary ruling issued by this Court, the trial court correctly determined that Appellant's petition fails to state viable causes of action under Louisiana law. The Appellees contend that this Court should reconsider its preliminary ruling and reinstate the trial court's judgment dismissing Appellant's claims in their entirety.

---

## II. RESPONSE TO PATERNITY TESTING CLAIM

### A. The Presumption of Paternity Under Civil Code Article 231 Bars Relitigation

The Appellant's claim for paternity testing ignores the fundamental principle that a husband who is married to the mother at the time of birth is legally presumed to be the father of the child. Louisiana Civil Code Article 231 provides that "[t]he husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of the child born during the marriage." This presumption is one of the strongest in Louisiana family law and reflects the state's compelling interest in the stability of family relationships and the protection of children from uncertain parentage.

The preliminary ruling erred in characterizing this as a case where Appellant merely "invoked" the presumption. To the contrary, Appellant accepted the benefits of this presumption for fourteen years—holding the child out as his own, exercising parental rights, and accepting the legal status of fatherhood. He cannot now selectively invoke the opposite position to challenge paternity when it serves his financial interests.

### B. The Voluntary Assumption Doctrine Bars This Action

Even if the presumption could be contested, Louisiana jurisprudence firmly establishes that a presumed father who voluntarily assumed the role of parent is barred from challenging paternity. In *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109, the Fifth Circuit held that a man who had voluntarily accepted the responsibilities of fatherhood could not later disavow paternity to avoid support obligations. The court emphasized that such challenges would undermine the stability of family units and harm children who have formed attachments to their presumed fathers.

The preliminary ruling attempted to distinguish *K.M. v. M.M.* on the basis that Appellant paid support "pursuant to a court order, not purely by voluntary choice." This distinction is unpersuasive. The court order itself was based on Appellant's status as the legal father—a status he accepted without challenge for fourteen years. The voluntary assumption doctrine encompasses not only direct voluntary payments but also acceptance of court-ordered obligations that flow from one's voluntary assumption of the paternal role. Appellant's argument that he "did not voluntarily assume the role in the same manner" lacks legal basis; the doctrine does not require a specific form of voluntary conduct but looks to whether the individual functioned as the father in fact.

### C. Succession of Robinson Does Not Support Appellant's Position

The preliminary ruling cited *Succession of Robinson*, 99-0905 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/17/00), 762 So.2d 753, for the proposition that a presumed father has a legal interest in establishing biological paternity. However, *Succession of Robinson* involved a succession proceeding where the alleged biological father sought to establish paternity to inherit from the deceased child's estate—a fundamentally different context. That case addressed the narrow question of whether an alleged biological father could intervene in succession proceedings, not whether a presumed father could challenge his own paternity to escape support obligations.

Furthermore, *Succession of Robinson* did not involve the voluntary assumption doctrine and cannot be read to override the strong public policy considerations that animate the bar against paternity challenges by presumed fathers. The preliminary ruling's reliance on this case extends its holding beyond any reasonable interpretation.

---

## III. RESPONSE TO REIMBURSEMENT CLAIM

### A. Louisiana Law Does Not Recognize a Reimbursement Cause of Action

The preliminary ruling erred in finding that Appellant stated a valid cause of action for unjust enrichment. The Louisiana Paternity Act, La. R.S. 9:401 et seq., provides the exclusive framework for paternity determinations and does not authorize reimbursement claims from biological fathers. The cases cited by the preliminary ruling—*K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109, and *P.J.L. v. C.J.L.*, 03-1520 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/3/04), 869 So.2d 844—establish that Louisiana courts have consistently refused to allow reimbursement claims in paternity contexts.

The preliminary ruling's reliance on *Cazes v. Cazes*, 11-2239 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/12/12), 101 So.3d 507, is misplaced. *Cazes v. Cazes* addressed a completely different factual scenario involving a marital settlement agreement and did not involve a presumed father seeking reimbursement from an alleged biological father. That case cannot be read to authorize the novel cause of action the Appellant seeks to assert here.

### B. The Unjust Enrichment Theory Fails as a Matter of Law

Even if Appellant could state a cause of action for paternity testing, his unjust enrichment claim fails for several independent reasons:

**First**, the Appellant has not identified a legal obligation owed by R.R. to support the child. Louisiana law imposes the primary obligation of support on the legal father, not the biological father. R.R. has no legal duty to support a child he has never acknowledged or who has been raised by another man for fourteen years. The mere biological connection, without more, does not create a support obligation.

**Second**, the Appellant's own fault bars recovery under unjust enrichment. Appellant knew or should have known of any doubts about his paternity during the fourteen years he lived with the child and the mother. His delay in filing suit—until after divorce and the imposition of support obligations—demonstrates a calculated attempt to shift his financial responsibility to another party. Louisiana Civil Code Article 2298 requires that the enrichment be "without cause," but here the enrichment, if any, resulted from Appellant's own choices and his acceptance of the paternal role.

**Third**, the Appellant received consideration for his support payments. He exercised parental rights, received the love and affection of a child, and benefited from the tax deductions and other advantages of fatherhood. He cannot now claim he received nothing in exchange for his support obligations.

### C. Public Policy Requires Rejecting This Claim

The preliminary ruling acknowledged "public policy concerns regarding family stability" but dismissed them as insufficient to "automatically negate a claim for unjust enrichment." This Court should adopt the opposite conclusion. The public policy of Louisiana strongly favors the stability of family relationships and the finality of parental determinations. Allowing presumed fathers to challenge paternity and seek reimbursement from biological fathers years after the fact would:

1. Undermine the finality of divorce judgments and support orders;
2. Encourage speculative litigation by support obligors seeking to escape their obligations;
3. Disrupt the lives of children who have formed attachments to their presumed fathers;
4. Create chaos in family relationships by allowing paternity to be relitigated indefinitely.

The Louisiana Legislature has determined that the Paternity Act provides the exclusive means of establishing paternity, and that scheme does not include a reimbursement remedy. This Court should not create a judicial workaround that circumvents the legislative scheme.

## IV. NO RIGHT OF ACTION

### A. Appellant Lacks Standing to Assert Claims Against R.R

The trial court correctly granted the exception of no right of action. Appellant has no legal relationship with R.R. and cannot assert claims against him. R.R. has never been married to A.F.Y., never been legally determined to be the father of the child, and has no legal obligation to support the child. ### B. The Child Is an Indispensable Party

The child whose support is at issue is an indispensable party to this proceeding. Louisiana law requires that the child's interests be represented, particularly in paternity proceedings. Appellant's attempt to litigate paternity and support issues without the child's participation is fatal to his claims.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The exception of no cause of action questions whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged in the petition. *City of New Orleans v. Board of Commissioners of Orleans Levee District*, 93-0690 (La. 7/5/94), 640 So.2d 237. In reviewing the granting of this exception, the court must accept all well-pleaded facts as true and determine whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any theory of law. *Id.* at 241. The exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Louisiana Associated General Contractors, Inc. v. State*, 95-1870 (La. 3/8/96), 669 So.2d 1185.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action Regarding Paternity Determination**

The Appellee argues that the presumption of paternity under Louisiana Civil Code Article 231, coupled with the voluntary assumption doctrine, bars the Appellant's action. The Court finds this argument persuasive.

While the preliminary ruling distinguished *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109, on the basis that the Appellant paid child support pursuant to a court order rather than purely voluntarily, the Appellee correctly notes that this distinction is legally insufficient. The voluntary assumption doctrine encompasses not only the voluntary payment of funds but also the acceptance of the legal status of fatherhood and the assumption of the paternal role. The Appellant held the child out as his own for fourteen years and accepted the benefits of the presumption of paternity. As held in *K.M. v. M.M.*, allowing a presumed father to challenge paternity after such a period undermines the stability of the family unit.

Furthermore, the preliminary ruling relied on *Succession of Robinson*, 99-0905 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/17/00), 762 So.2d 753, to support a presumed father's interest in biological truth. However, the Appellee successfully distinguishes *Succession of Robinson* as a succession proceeding involving inheritance rights, a context vastly different from the present attempt to disavow paternity to avoid support obligations. The strong public policy in favor of the finality of family relationships and the protection of the child's welfare outweighs the Appellant's interest in biological truth in this specific procedural posture. Therefore, the trial court correctly determined that the Appellant failed to state a cause of action for paternity testing under these facts.

**B. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action Regarding Reimbursement**

The Appellee contends that the Appellant's claim for unjust enrichment fails as a matter of law. The Court agrees.

The preliminary ruling found that the Appellant stated a valid cause of action for unjust enrichment under Louisiana Civil Code Article 2298. However, the Appellee demonstrates that the elements of unjust enrichment cannot be satisfied. First, R.R. has no legal obligation to support the child. Under Louisiana law, the obligation of support falls primarily on the legal father—the Appellant—not the biological father who has not acknowledged the child. Because R.R. had no legal duty to pay support, he cannot be said to have been "unjustly enriched" at the Appellant's expense by failing to do so. The enrichment, if any, resulted from the Appellant's own legal status as the presumed father, not from any passive action by R.R.

Second, the Appellee correctly argues that the Appellant received consideration for his support payments, including the love, affection, and companionship of the child, as well as tax benefits and the exercise of parental rights. A claim for unjust enrichment requires that the enrichment be "without cause." Here, the Appellant's payments were made to fulfill his legal obligation as the presumed father and were in exchange for the benefits of parenthood. Consequently, the "without cause" element is absent.

Finally, the reliance on *Cazes v. Cazes*, 11-2239 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/12/12), 101 So.3d 507, is misplaced, as that case involved a contractual settlement agreement rather than a direct claim for reimbursement of child support from a third party. The Louisiana Paternity Act, La. R.S. 9:401 et seq., provides the exclusive framework for paternity actions and does not authorize the reimbursement remedy sought by the Appellant.

**C. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Granting the Exception of No Right of Action**

The Appellee asserts that the child is an indispensable party to this proceeding. Louisiana law requires that the interests of the child be represented in proceedings affecting filiation and support. Because the child is not a party to this suit, the Appellant lacks the right of action to pursue these claims in the child's absence. This procedural defect provides an independent basis for affirming the trial court's judgment.

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellee's rebuttal successfully demonstrates that the trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. # CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## In the Court of Appeal of Louisiana

### J.M.Y. v. R.R. and A.F.Y

---

# INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, counsel for the Appellees, R.R. and A.F.Y., respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the trial court's judgment granting exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the trial court's dismissal of Appellant J.M.Y.'s claims in their entirety.

---

# I. THE APPELLANT FAILS TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR PATERNITY DETERMINATION

## A. The Presumption of Paternity Bars This Action

The Appellant seeks to compel genetic testing to determine paternity after having accepted the role of father for fourteen years. This attempt must fail as a matter of law.

Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 231, the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of the child born during the marriage. This presumption is one of the strongest in Louisiana law, designed to protect the stability of the family unit and the welfare of the child. *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109.

The voluntary assumption doctrine, as recognized in Louisiana jurisprudence, encompasses not merely the voluntary payment of funds but also the acceptance of the legal status of fatherhood and the assumption of the paternal role. *Id.* The Appellant held the child out as his own for fourteen years, accepted the benefits of the presumption of paternity, and fulfilled his court-ordered support obligations. To now allow him to challenge paternity after such a prolonged period fundamentally undermines the stability of the family unit and the child's best interests.

The Appellant's reliance on *Succession of Robinson*, 99-0905 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/17/00), 762 So.2d 753, is misplaced. That case involved a succession proceeding concerning inheritance rights—a context vastly different from the present attempt to disavow paternity to avoid support obligations. The strong public policy in favor of the finality of family relationships and the protection of the child's welfare outweighs any interest in biological truth in this specific procedural posture.

## B. The Louisiana Paternity Act Provides the Exclusive Framework

The Louisiana Paternity Act, La. R.S. 9:401 et seq., provides the exclusive framework for paternity actions in this state. This statutory scheme does not authorize a presumed father to maintain an action for paternity determination against a third party while simultaneously seeking to avoid his own support obligations. The Appellant's attempt to circumvent the clear provisions of the Paternity Act must be rejected.

---

# II. THE APPELLANT FAILS TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR UNJUST ENRICHMENT

## A. R.R. Was Not Legally Obligated to Support the Child

The Appellant's claim for reimbursement from R.R. fails as a matter of law because R.R. had no legal duty to support the child. Under Louisiana law, the obligation of support falls primarily on the legal father—the Appellant—as established by the divorce judgment and the presumption of paternity. The biological father who has not acknowledged the child bears no such legal duty.

Because R.R. had no legal obligation to pay support, he cannot be said to have been "unjustly enriched" at the Appellant's expense by failing to do so. The enrichment, if any, resulted from the Appellant's own legal status as the presumed father, not from any action or inaction by R.R.

## B. The Appellant Received Consideration for His Support Payments

A claim for unjust enrichment requires that the enrichment be "without cause." Louisiana Civil Code Article 2298. Here, this element is entirely absent.

The Appellant received valuable consideration for his support payments, including the love, affection, and companionship of the child, as well as tax benefits and the exercise of parental rights including custody and visitation. The Appellant's payments were made to fulfill his legal obligation as the presumed father and were in exchange for the substantial benefits of parenthood. There was no unjust enrichment because the Appellant received what he paid for—the legal rights and privileges of fatherhood.

## C. The Cazes Case Is Inapplicable

The Appellant's reliance on *Cazes v. Cazes*, 11-2239 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/12/12), 101 So.3d 507, is wholly misplaced. That case involved a contractual settlement agreement between parties who had already resolved their disputes through negotiation. It did not address a direct claim for reimbursement of child support from a third party who had no legal obligation to pay. The present case is distinguishable on these critical grounds.

---

# III. THE APPELLANT LACKS THE RIGHT OF ACTION TO PURSUE THESE CLAIMS

## A. The Child Is an Indispensable Party

Louisiana law requires that the interests of the child be represented in proceedings affecting filiation and support. The child is an indispensable party to this proceeding because the outcome directly affects his rights to support and his legal relationship with his presumed father.

The Appellant lacks the right of action to pursue these claims in the child's absence. This procedural defect provides an independent and sufficient basis for affirming the trial court's judgment. *See* Louisiana Associated General Contractors, Inc. v. State, 95-1870 (La. 3/8/96), 669 So.2d 1185 (establishing that the exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought).

---

# CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellees respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's judgment granting exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action and dismiss the Appellant's suit in its entirety with all costs assessed to the Appellant.

The strong public policy of this state favors the stability of family relationships and the finality of paternal obligations. The Appellant cannot now, after fourteen years of accepting the benefits of fatherhood, disavow his obligations to seek reimbursement from those who never assumed any legal duty to support the child. The trial court correctly applied the law to the facts presented, and its judgment should be affirmed.

---

**Respectfully submitted,**

**Counsel for Appellees, R.R. and A.F.Y.**

---

# SUMMARY OF KEY DEFENSE POINTS

1. **Paternity Presumption Bars Action**: The Appellant is barred by the presumption of paternity under La. Civil Code Article 231 and the voluntary assumption doctrine from challenging paternity after fourteen years.

2. **No Cause of Action for Unjust Enrichment**: R.R. had no legal obligation to support the child, and the Appellant received consideration (love, affection, tax benefits, parental rights) for his support payments.

3. **No Right of Action**: The child is an indispensable party who must be represented in these proceedings, and the Appellant lacks the right to pursue these claims in the child's absence.

4. **Public Policy**: The stability of family relationships and finality of paternal obligations outweigh any claimed interest in biological truth under these circumstances.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The exception of no cause of action questions whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged in the petition. *City of New Orleans v. In reviewing the granting of this exception, the court must accept all well-pleaded facts as true and determine whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any theory of law. *Id.* at 241. The exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. *Louisiana Associated General Contractors, Inc. v. State*, 95-1870 (La. 3/8/96), 669 So.2d 1185.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action Regarding Paternity Determination**

The Appellant seeks to compel genetic testing to determine paternity after having accepted the role of father for fourteen years. The Appellees argue that the presumption of paternity under Louisiana Civil Code Article 231, coupled with the voluntary assumption doctrine, bars this action. The Court finds this argument dispositive.

Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 231, the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of the child born during the marriage. This presumption is one of the strongest in Louisiana law, designed to protect the stability of the family unit and the welfare of the child. *K.M. v. M.M.*, 99-1382 (La. App. 5 Cir. 4/11/00), 760 So.2d 109.

The voluntary assumption doctrine, as recognized in Louisiana jurisprudence, encompasses not merely the voluntary payment of funds but also the acceptance of the legal status of fatherhood and the assumption of the paternal role. *Id.* The Appellant held the child out as his own for fourteen years, accepted the benefits of the presumption of paternity, and fulfilled his court-ordered support obligations. To allow the Appellant to challenge paternity after such a prolonged period would fundamentally undermine the stability of the family unit and the child's best interests.

That case involved a succession proceeding concerning inheritance rights—a context vastly different from the present attempt to disavow paternity to avoid support obligations. The strong public policy in favor of the finality of family relationships and the protection of the child's welfare outweighs the Appellant's interest in biological truth in this specific procedural posture. Therefore, the trial court correctly determined that the Appellant failed to state a cause of action for paternity testing under these facts.

**B. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action Regarding Reimbursement**

The Appellees contend that the Appellant's claim for unjust enrichment fails as a matter of law. The Court agrees.

The Appellant's claim for reimbursement from R.R. fails because R.R. had no legal duty to support the child. Under Louisiana law, the obligation of support falls primarily on the legal father—the Appellant—as established by the divorce judgment and the presumption of paternity. It did not address a direct claim for reimbursement of child support from a third party who had no legal obligation to pay. The Louisiana Paternity Act, La. **C. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Granting the Exception of No Right of Action**
