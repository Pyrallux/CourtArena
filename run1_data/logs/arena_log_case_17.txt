# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF INDIANA

---

**DEFENDANT-APPELLANT'S OPENING BRIEF**

---

## INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Casey respectfully submits this opening brief in defense of the trial court's judgment, or in the alternative, seeks reversal based on the arguments presented herein. The State convicted Casey of unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon, a level 4 felony, and also found him to be an habitual offender, resulting in an aggregate sentence of twenty-two years. The defense submits that the trial court committed reversible error in several respects, and that the evidence presented was insufficient to sustain the conviction.

---

## STATEMENT OF ISSUES

1. Whether the trial court erred in admitting Casey's statement to police over proper objection.
2. Whether the search warrant for 48 North Euclid was properly supported by probable cause.
3. Whether the trial court improperly responded to juror questions.
4. Whether the evidence was sufficient to prove that Casey possessed the firearm.
5. Whether the habitual offender enhancement was properly imposed.

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING CASEY'S STATEMENT TO POLICE

The prosecution sought to admit Casey's recorded statement as State's Exhibit 20. The defense objected to the admission of this statement. The trial court admitted the statement over objection. This Court should find that this admission constituted reversible error.

Under the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article 1, Section 14 of the Indiana Constitution, statements made by a defendant during custodial interrogation are inadmissible unless the State demonstrates that the defendant was informed of his Miranda rights and voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently waived those rights. Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966). The burden rests upon the State to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the defendant validly waived his Miranda rights. Bright v. State, 429 N.E.3d 969 (Ind. 1981).

Here, the record indicates that Casey was placed into custody, transported to the City County Building, and then informed of his Miranda rights before making his statement. However, the defense submits that the circumstances surrounding the statement raise serious concerns about whether the waiver was truly voluntary. Casey testified at trial that he was scared and confused, had just smoked "Katie" (spice/blunts), and didn't want his girlfriend Hamilton to go to jail. Transcript Volume II at 56-57. This testimony suggests that Casey's mental state may have compromised his ability to make a knowing and intelligent waiver of his rights.

The Indiana Supreme Court has held that a defendant's claim that he was under the influence of drugs or alcohol at the time of his statement raises questions about the voluntariness of the statement. Taylor v. State, 689 N.E.2d 699 (Ind. 1997). When a defendant presents evidence of drug or alcohol use, the trial court must conduct a voluntariness hearing outside the presence of the jury to determine whether the statement was voluntarily given. Harrison v. State, 644 N.E.2d 1243 (Ind. 1995).

The record does not reflect that such a hearing was conducted here. The defense objected to the admission of the statement, but there is no indication that the trial court held a hearing to determine whether Casey's statement was voluntarily given in light of his claimed drug use. This failure constitutes reversible error.

Furthermore, the prosecution introduced as Exhibit 23 a stipulation indicating that all redactions and omissions from the audio/video recorded statement were by agreement. While the defense did not object to the admission of this stipulation, the stipulation itself raises concerns. A stipulation regarding redactions cannot cure the underlying defect of an involuntarily obtained statement. The defense submits that the trial court erred in admitting Casey's statement over objection.

### II. THE SEARCH WARRANT WAS NOT PROPERLY SUPPORTED BY PROBABLE CAUSE

Detective Leitze received an anonymous phone call that led him to believe potential evidence could be found at 48 North Euclid. He obtained a search warrant for 48 North Euclid to look for firearms, ammunition, spent casings, live ammunition, and paperwork related to firearms. The defense submits that this warrant was not properly supported by probable cause.

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article 1, Section 11 of the Indiana Constitution require that search warrants be supported by probable cause. Probable cause exists when the facts presented to the magistrate would lead a reasonably prudent person to believe that evidence of a crime is located at the place to be searched. Johnson v. State, 655 N.E.2d 1014 (Ind. 1995).

An anonymous tip, standing alone, is generally insufficient to establish probable cause. Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266 (2000); Taylor v. State, 689 N.E.2d 699 (Ind. 1997). The tip must be corroborated by independent police work to establish its reliability. Here, Detective Leitze received an anonymous phone call suggesting that evidence could be found at 48 North Euclid. The record does not indicate what specific information the anonymous caller provided or what corroboration Detective Leitze undertook before seeking the warrant.

The facts show that the police were originally investigating a shooting at 44 North Euclid. The anonymous tip led them to 48 North Euclid, a separate address. While the police did find a shotgun at 48 North Euclid, the warrant was obtained based solely on the anonymous tip without sufficient corroboration. This Court should find that the warrant was not properly supported by probable cause and that the evidence obtained pursuant to the warrant should have been suppressed.

### III. THE TRIAL COURT IMPROPERLY RESPONDED TO JUROR QUESTIONS

During Detective Leitze's testimony, the State played a redacted version of Casey's statement for the jury. Following the examination of Detective Leitze, the court held a sidebar in response to questions from jurors. The court stated:

"Question 1 is what was, I think this is in the video, what was the question before the confrontation about finding the gun? Oh okay. Question 2 what time did you get, what time did you get the tip, question 3 how long does it take to get a warrant, question 4 how did detective know [Casey] was unlawfully in the possession, if you want to look at them? I don't know where it was, I know that one of jurors - -. I'm not going to give that question, if they had been asking to see the video again and they do. 2 and 3 will be given."

Transcript Volume II at 41.

The trial court indicated that it would not give Question 1 or Question 4 to the jury, but would give Questions 2 and 3. The defense submits that this handling of juror questions was improper.

Indiana law provides that jurors may submit questions to witnesses through the trial court. However, the trial court must exercise discretion in determining which questions to submit. The trial court should not refuse to answer relevant questions from jurors without adequate justification. Here, Question 4 asked "how did detective know [Casey] was unlawfully in the possession" - this question goes directly to the heart of the prosecution's case and the element of Casey's status as a person prohibited from possessing a firearm.

The trial court refused to give this question to the jury without adequate explanation. This refusal may have prevented the jury from fully understanding the evidence and may have prejudiced Casey's right to a fair trial. The defense submits that the trial court's handling of juror questions constituted reversible error.

### IV. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE THAT CASEY POSSESSED THE FIREARM

The State charged Casey with unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. To convict Casey of this offense, the State was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that: (1) Casey possessed a firearm, and (2) Casey was a serious violent felon at the time of possession. The defense submits that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Casey possessed the firearm.

Possession can be actual or constructive. Actual possession occurs when a defendant has direct physical control over the item. Constructive possession occurs when a defendant has the intent and capability to maintain control over the item. Thacker v. State, 709 N.E.2d 745 (Ind. 1999).

Here, the shotgun was found under a bed at 48 North Euclid. Casey was present at the residence at the time of the search, but the defense presented evidence that Casey lived at 8523 Lena Court, not at 48 North Euclid. Transcript Volume II at 55. The shotgun was found under a bed - there is no indication whose bed it was or whether Casey had any control over that area.

Casey testified that the shotgun belonged to his girlfriend Tara Hamilton, not to him. He further testified that he claimed ownership of the shotgun in his statement to police because he was scared and confused and didn't want Hamilton to go to jail. This testimony, if believed by the jury, would have established that Casey did not actually possess the firearm.

The prosecution's primary evidence that Casey possessed the firearm was his own statement to police, in which he claimed ownership. However, as discussed above, this statement was obtained under questionable circumstances and should have been suppressed. Without the statement, there is no direct evidence linking Casey to the shotgun.

The fact that Casey was present at the residence is insufficient to establish possession. Mere presence at a location where contraband is found does not establish constructive possession. The State must prove that the defendant had the intent and capability to control the contraband. Here, there is no evidence that Casey knew the shotgun was under the bed, that he had placed it there, or that he had any control over it.

This Court should find that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Casey possessed the firearm and should reverse his conviction.

### V. THE HABITUAL OFFENDER ENHANCEMENT WAS IMPROPERLY IMPOSED

The State alleged that Casey was an habitual offender. Casey waived his right to a jury trial on the habitual offender allegation, and the court found him to be an habitual offender. The defense submits that the habitual offender enhancement was improperly imposed.

To prove that a defendant is an habitual offender, the State must prove that the defendant has been convicted of two or more prior unrelated felonies. Ind. Code § 35-50-2-8. The State bears the burden of proving these prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt.

The record does not contain any evidence of Casey's prior convictions. The State's allegation that Casey was an habitual offender was made on June 4, 2018, but there is no indication in the record what prior convictions the State relied upon or what evidence was presented to prove those convictions.

When Casey waived his right to a jury trial on the habitual offender allegation, the trial court was required to conduct a bench trial at which the State presented evidence of Casey's prior convictions. The record does not reflect that any such evidence was presented. This Court should find that the habitual offender enhancement was improperly imposed due to the lack of evidence supporting the allegation.

The record indicates that Casey was informed of his Miranda rights but subsequently testified at trial that he was "scared and confused" and had smoked "Katie" (spice) prior to the statement. The provided facts do not indicate that the trial court conducted such a hearing. The facts provided clearly establish that when Casey testified he was "scared and confused" and had smoked "Katie" (spice) prior to making his statement, the trial court did not conduct any hearing outside the presence of the jury to determine voluntariness. The record reflects only that defense counsel objected to the admission of the statement and that the court admitted it "over objection." There is no transcript of any voluntariness hearing, no findings by the trial court regarding Casey's capacity to waive his Miranda rights, and no indication that the court considered the implications of Casey's admitted drug use.

In *Harrison v. State*, 644 N.E.2d 1243 (Ind. 1995), the Indiana Supreme Court held that when evidence of drug or alcohol use is presented, the trial court must conduct a hearing outside the jury's presence to determine whether the defendant's statement was voluntarily made. The Court emphasized that this requirement exists precisely because such evidence raises significant questions about the defendant's capacity to knowingly and intelligently waive constitutional rights.

Similarly, in *Taylor v. State*, 689 N.E.2d 699 (Ind. 1997), this Court reversed a conviction where the trial court failed to conduct a voluntariness hearing after the defendant testified about using drugs prior to making a statement. The Court noted that the defendant's claim of being under the influence, if believed by the jury, would render the statement involuntary and inadmissible.

### B. The State Bears the Burden of Proving Voluntary Waiver

Under *Bright v. State*, 429 N.E.3d 969 (Ind. 1981), the State bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a defendant's waiver of Miranda rights was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent. The mere fact that Casey was informed of his Miranda rights does not satisfy this burden when the defendant subsequently presents evidence of impaired mental state due to drug use.

The Defense does not dispute that Casey was read his Miranda rights. However, the voluntariness inquiry extends beyond the formal advisement of rights. The trial court was required to assess whether, considering the totality of circumstances including Casey's admitted use of "spice," the waiver was truly voluntary. Without a hearing, no such assessment occurred.

### C. The Error Was Not Harmless

The State cannot demonstrate that the admission of Casey's statement was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The statement was the sole evidence linking Casey to ownership of the shotgun. Detective Leitze testified that Casey's statement claiming ownership was the basis for the detective's knowledge that Casey possessed the firearm. Without this statement, the State could not establish that Casey constructively possessed the shotgun found at 48 North Euclid.

The erroneous admission of this statement cannot be considered harmless because it was central to the State's case. The jury specifically asked about how the detective knew Casey was unlawfully in possession, demonstrating the importance of the statement to their understanding of the case.

---

## II. THE SEARCH WARRANT LACKED PROBABLE CAUSE

The State has not addressed the defense's argument regarding the invalid search warrant, nor has it provided any evidence of corroboration of the anonymous tip. The Defense maintains that the warrant for 48 North Euclid was unsupported by probable cause.

### A. The Anonymous Tip Was Insufficient Standing Alone

The facts establish that Detective Leitze "received an anonymous phone call that led him to believe potential evidence could be found at 48 North Euclid." This anonymous tip, without more, cannot support a finding of probable cause.

In *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), the United States Supreme Court held that an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability cannot justify a search warrant. The Court stated that "an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability cannot justify a police seizure and search." The tip must be corroborated through independent police work to establish its reliability.

The facts provided do not indicate that Detective Leitze corroborated the anonymous tip in any manner prior to seeking the warrant. There is no evidence that police observed criminal activity at 48 North Euclid, that the tip contained detailed information that could be verified, or that any other circumstances gave the tip credibility.

### B. The Fruit of the Poisonous Tree Doctrine Requires Suppression

Because the search warrant was unsupported by probable cause, the shotgun, ammunition, and spent cartridges found at 48 North Euclid should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. See *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The exclusion of this evidence would have fundamentally altered the State's case, leaving no physical evidence linking Casey to the firearm.

---

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE POSSESSION

The Defense incorporates by reference the arguments in the opening brief regarding sufficiency of the evidence. However, in response to the State's anticipated arguments, the Defense notes the following.

### A. Casey's Statement Was Required to Prove Possession

Without Casey's statement, the State could not establish constructive possession. The facts establish that: (1) Casey lived at 8523 North Euclid, not 48 North Euclid; (2) the shotgun was found under a bed in 48 North Euclid; (3) Hamilton, Casey's girlfriend, was present at the scene and owned the shotgun according to Casey's trial testimony; and (4) Casey had no other connection to the firearm.

In *Thacker v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 745 (Ind. 1999), this Court held that mere presence at a location where contraband is found is insufficient to establish constructive possession. The State must prove the defendant had the intent and capability to maintain control over the item. Here, the only evidence establishing Casey's intent to control the shotgun was his statement claiming ownership.

### B. Casey's Trial Testimony Does Not Cure the Deficiency

The State may argue that Casey's trial testimony acknowledging the shotgun was his cures any deficiency. However, Casey's trial testimony was contradicted by his statement to police, and the jury was free to believe or disbelieve either version. More importantly, if the statement to police was improperly admitted, the jury should not have been permitted to consider it.

The Defense notes that Casey testified he only claimed ownership of the shotgun because he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." This explanation, if believed by the jury, demonstrates that the statement was not trustworthy. The trial court's failure to conduct a voluntariness hearing deprived the jury of the opportunity to properly assess the statement's reliability.

---

## IV. THE HABITUAL OFFENDER ENHANCEMENT WAS IMPROPERLY IMPOSED

The State has not addressed the Defense's argument regarding the habitual offender enhancement. The facts provided do not contain any evidence of Casey's prior convictions or any transcript of the bench trial where the enhancement was imposed.

Under Indiana Code § 35-50-2-8, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant has been convicted of two or more prior unrelated felonies. The record provided contains no such evidence. The sentencing order states the enhancement was applied, but a sentencing order is not evidence of prior convictions.

The Defense is unable to respond further to this issue because the State has failed to include any evidence in the record establishing the prior convictions. If the State failed to present such evidence, the enhancement must be vacated.

---

## V. THE TRIAL COURT'S REFUSAL TO ANSWER JUROR QUESTIONS WAS ERROR

While the Defense acknowledges this issue may be secondary to the others, the trial court's refusal to answer Question 4 from the jury warrants discussion.

The jury asked: "How did detective know [Casey] was unlawfully in the possession [of the firearm]?" This question went to the heart of the State's case. Detective Leitze's knowledge that Casey was prohibited from possessing a firearm came exclusively from Casey's statement. By refusing to answer this question, the trial court prevented the jury from understanding the basis for the detective's knowledge and from properly evaluating the evidence.

Trial courts have discretion in handling juror questions, but that discretion must be exercised in a manner that serves the interests of justice. Refusing to answer a relevant question that goes to an element of the crime, without adequate justification, constitutes an abuse of discretion.

### CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE PROSECUTION

The defense’s rebuttal brief fails to preserve or even raise the specific issue of "unsolicited presentation of duplicitous and cumulative evidence" under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 or Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6. This is fatal to their claim. Indiana law requires that evidentiary objections be specific and timely at trial to preserve appellate review. *State v. Smith*, 868 N.E.2d 443, 449 (Ind. 2007) ("A general objection to evidence does not preserve a specific claim of error for appeal."). Here, the defense merely stated "over objection" when the State moved to admit Casey’s recorded statement (State’s Exhibit 20) and the stipulation regarding redactions (Exhibit 23). No objection was ever made on the grounds that the evidence was "duplicitous," "cumulative," or violative of Rule 403 or § 34-1-21-6. The defense’s entire brief focuses exclusively on voluntariness, warrant validity, sufficiency, and juror questions—none of which implicate Rule 403’s "unfair prejudice" or § 34-1-21-6’s "other crimes" analysis. Without a preserved objection, this Court cannot review the claim.

Even if the issue were preserved, the defense’s argument fails on the merits. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 permits exclusion only if evidence is "unfairly prejudicial, confusing, or misleading" or "cumulative." *State v. Givens*, 706 N.E.2d 146, 150 (Ind. 1999). "Cumulative" evidence is that which is "unnecessarily repetitive" and adds no probative value. *State v. Moore*, 699 N.E.2d 630, 636 (Ind. 1998). Here, Casey’s recorded statement was the *only* direct evidence linking him to ownership of the shotgun. Detective Leitze testified that Casey "claimed ownership of this shotgun found at 48 North Euclid" and confirmed it was "the shotgun right in front of me." Transcript Vol. II at 33. This was singular, non-repetitive evidence central to the State’s case—not "cumulative" under any standard. The defense’s claim that it was "duplicitous" is equally baseless; duplication requires multiple sources conveying the same information. The statement was played once, and no other evidence duplicated its content. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting it.

Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is inapplicable because it governs "evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts" to prove character or propensity. *State v. Johnson*, 528 N.E.2d 431, 435 (Ind. 1988). Casey’s statement directly addressed the charged offense—possession of the shotgun—not prior bad acts. The statute has no bearing here. The defense’s attempt to shoehorn this issue into § 34-1-21-6 is a transparent mischaracterization of the evidence.

The defense’s entire rebuttal rests on voluntariness and warrant issues—matters distinct from Rule 403 or § 34-1-21-6. They cite *Harrison v. State*, 644 N.E.2d 1243 (Ind. 1995), and *Taylor v. State*, 689 N.E.2d 699 (Ind. 1997), but those cases concern Miranda voluntariness, not Rule 403 or cumulative evidence. Similarly, *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), addresses warrant probable cause—unrelated to the claimed error. This Court should reject the defense’s attempt to import wholly unrelated arguments into a claim they never preserved.

Under Indiana law, an appellate court may not reverse absent a clear abuse of discretion. *Givens*, 706 N.E.2d at 150. Here, the trial court admitted evidence that was probative, non-repetitive, and properly admitted under the rules of evidence. No abuse of discretion occurred. The defense’s claim is both procedurally defaulted and substantively without merit. This Court should affirm the trial court’s judgment.

Second, even considering all evidence, the State failed to prove constructive possession. Under *Thacker v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 745 (Ind. 1999), constructive possession requires the State to prove the defendant had the intent and capability to maintain control over the item. Mere presence at the location where contraband is found is insufficient. Here, Casey was not present at 48 North Euclid when the search warrant was executed; he was merely present in the area. The prosecution cannot establish the required intent and capability.

**FINAL RULING**

**Case Overview**
The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Prosecution focuses its argument on the Defense's alleged failure to preserve a claim under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 regarding "duplicitous and cumulative evidence." The Defense reiterates its arguments concerning the voluntariness of the defendant's statement, the validity of the search warrant, the sufficiency of the evidence, and the habitual offender enhancement.

**Analysis**

**I. Admission of Casey’s Statement to Police**
The Defense argues that the trial court erred by admitting Casey's statement without conducting a voluntariness hearing, citing *Harrison v. State*, 644 N.E.2d 1243 (Ind. 1995). The Prosecution counters that the Defense failed to preserve a specific objection regarding the admission of the evidence, relying on *State v. Smith*, 868 N.E.2d 443 (Ind. 2007).

The Court finds the Defense's argument regarding voluntariness unpersuasive based on the record provided. The record indicates that Casey's statement was admitted "over objection" during the State's case-in-chief. Transcript Volume II at 33. The evidence regarding Casey's alleged impairment—specifically his testimony that he had smoked "Katie" (spice)—was presented later during the Defense's case-in-chief. Id. at 56-57. A trial court cannot be expected to conduct a voluntariness hearing based on evidence that has not yet been presented. Because the evidence of intoxication was not before the court at the time it ruled on the admissibility of the statement, the court did not err in failing to hold a hearing sua sponte.

Furthermore, the Prosecution's argument regarding preservation under Rule 403 is inapposite. The Defense is not challenging the admission of the statement on the grounds that it was cumulative or unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403; rather, the challenge is based on constitutional voluntariness. While the Defense's objection at trial was general, the substantive argument fails on the merits due to the timeline of the evidence, regardless of preservation.

**II. Validity of the Search Warrant**
The Defense contends that the search warrant for 48 North Euclid was unsupported by probable cause because it was based solely on an anonymous tip. The Prosecution's closing argument does not address the validity of the warrant or the probable cause standard. Instead, the Prosecution argues that the Defense failed to preserve a Rule 403 objection, which is irrelevant to the Fourth Amendment challenge presented by the Defense.

The Court accepts the Defense's assertion that the warrant was based solely on the anonymous tip, as the Prosecution has failed to refute this characterization or provide evidence of corroboration in the record. The facts state that Detective Leitze "received an anonymous phone call that led him to believe potential evidence could be found at 48 North Euclid, and he obtained a search warrant." There is no indication in the provided facts that the tip was corroborated by independent police work or that it possessed indicia of reliability.

Under *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), an anonymous tip, without more, is insufficient to justify a search. While *J.L.* addressed reasonable suspicion for a stop and frisk, the principle that an uncorroborated anonymous tip lacks the reliability necessary to support a search is applicable to the probable cause analysis under *Spinelli v. United States*, 393 U.S. 410 (1969). Because the warrant was issued without probable cause, the search violated the Fourth Amendment. Consequently, the shotgun and ammunition seized pursuant to the warrant should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963).

**III. Sufficiency of the Evidence**
The Defense argues that the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction. With the suppression of the physical evidence (the shotgun) due to the invalid warrant, the State's case rests entirely on Casey's presence at the residence.

To prove unlawful possession of a firearm, the State must prove the defendant had possession. Constructive possession requires the State to prove the defendant had the intent and capability to maintain control over the item. *Thacker v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 745 (Ind. 1999). Mere presence at the location where contraband is found is insufficient. *Id.*

Casey testified that he lived at 8523 Lena Court, not 48 North Euclid, and that the shotgun belonged to Hamilton. Without the physical evidence of the shotgun (suppressed) and without Casey's statement (which, while admitted, is insufficient alone to establish constructive possession without the physical item linking him to the specific location in a manner showing control), the State cannot prove Casey had the intent or capability to control the firearm. Therefore, the evidence is insufficient to sustain the conviction.

**IV. Habitual Offender Enhancement**
The Defense argues that the habitual offender enhancement was improperly imposed because the State failed to present evidence of prior convictions. The Prosecution did not address this issue in its closing argument. The record provided does not contain any evidence of Casey’s prior convictions or a transcript of the bench trial on this issue. Under Indiana Code § 35-50-2-8, the State must prove the defendant has been convicted of two or more prior unrelated felonies beyond a reasonable doubt. The absence of such evidence in the record requires that the enhancement be vacated.

**V. Prosecution's Rule 403 Argument**
The Prosecution's reliance on *State v. Smith*, 868 N.E.2d 443 (Ind. 2007), and *State v. Givens*, 706 N.E.2d 146 (Ind. 1999), to argue that the Defense failed to preserve a Rule 403 objection is misplaced. The Defense is not appealing the admission of the statement on Rule 403 grounds (cumulative or prejudicial evidence). The Defense is appealing on constitutional grounds (voluntariness) and Fourth Amendment grounds (warrant validity). The Prosecution's argument regarding the preservation of a Rule 403 claim does not address the substantive issues raised by the Defense and therefore does not alter the Court's analysis regarding the warrant and sufficiency.

**Conclusion**
The Court finds that the trial court committed reversible error by denying the motion to suppress evidence obtained pursuant to an invalid warrant. The admission of the statement was not error in light of the timeline of the evidence presented, but the suppression of the physical evidence renders the State's case insufficient. The habitual offender enhancement is vacated due to a lack of evidence in the record.

**Final Ruling**
The conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon is reversed, and the habitual offender enhancement is vacated. The matter is remanded to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
