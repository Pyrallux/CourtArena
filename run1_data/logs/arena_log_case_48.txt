# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

May it please the Court: Respondent Oakley respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's order granting Oakley presumed parent status under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1). The family court correctly applied the reasoning of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), which held that the paternity presumption statute must be read and applied gender-neutrally to comport with constitutional equal protection principles and the reality of modern family structures. Additionally, even absent the statutory presumption, the family court properly found that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental rights based on Turner's extensive representations that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent, her inclusion of Oakley in her will, and the parties' mutual intent to raise the child together.

The plaintiff's argument rests on a rigid textualism that ignores both controlling precedent and the fundamental transformation of family law in this jurisdiction. This Court should affirm.

---

## I. The Family Court Correctly Applied McLaughlin v. Jones

The family court properly relied on *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), which held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read gender-neutrally. In *McLaughlin*, this Court explained that the statutory presumption of paternity applies to any spouse of a biological parent who was married to that parent within ten months of the child's birth, regardless of the spouse's gender. Id. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The court reasoned that interpreting the statute to apply only to male spouses would unconstitutionally discriminate against same-sex couples in violation of the Equal Protection Clause. Id. at 564-65 ¶ 15, 382 P.3d at 122-23.

The plaintiff argues that *McLaughlin* improperly "rewrote" the statute. This argument fails. The *McLaughlin* court did not amend the statute; it interpreted the statute in accordance with constitutional requirements. As the Arizona Supreme Court has recognized, statutes must be interpreted in a manner that avoids constitutional infirmity. See *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. 530, 535, 892 P.2d 215, 220 (App. 1994) ("Statutes should be construed to give them constitutional validity if reasonably possible.").

Moreover, the facts here are indistinguishable from *McLaughlin*. In that case, as here, a same-sex couple attempted to conceive a child through artificial insemination, listed both partners on the birth certificate, and intended to co-parent the child. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 562-63, 382 P.3d at 120-21. The *McLaughlin* court held that the non-biological spouse was a presumed parent under § 25-814(A)(1). Id. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The family court correctly applied this binding precedent.

---

## II. The Cases Cited by Plaintiff Are Distinguishable

The plaintiff relies on *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. 287, 766 P.2d 1129 (App. 1988), *In re Pima County Juvenile Action No. J-74743*, 173 Ariz. 257, 841 P.2d 1187 (App. 1992), and *In re Marriage of H.*, 231 Ariz. 193, 291 P.3d 1150 (App. 2012). These cases are inapposite.

In *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, the court addressed whether a woman who had lived with the biological mother and child could adopt the child without the biological father's consent. 159 Ariz. at 289, 766 P.2d at 1131. The court held that the woman could not claim "presumptive paternity" because she was not a man. Id. at 290-91, 766 P.2d at 1132-33. Critically, however, the court was interpreting a different statute—Arizona's adoption laws—not the paternity presumption statute at issue here. Furthermore, *Baby Boy D.* predates the recognition of same-sex marriage in Arizona and the constitutional mandate that statutes be applied without gender discrimination.

Similarly, *In re Pima County Juvenile Action No. J-74743* addressed whether a woman could establish paternity under a statute specifically governing paternity proceedings. 173 Ariz. at 260, 841 P.2d at 1190. The court there was interpreting statutory language that explicitly referred to "father" in the context of establishing biological paternity for child support purposes. Id. at 261, 841 P.2d at 1191. That case did not involve a married couple attempting to conceive a child together, nor did it address the constitutional concerns that animated *McLaughlin*.

*In re Marriage of H.* addressed whether a biological father could claim parental rights over a child born during a marriage that had been legally invalidated. 231 Ariz. at 195-96, 291 P.3d at 1152-53. The court held that the father could not claim rights because the marriage was void ab initio. Id. at 198, 291 P.3d at 1155. This case has no bearing on the gender-neutral application of § 25-814(A)(1) to same-sex spouses.

None of these cases involved the precise statutory interpretation question presented here: whether a statute that uses the term "man" must be read gender-neutrally when applied to same-sex spouses who conceived a child together during a valid marriage. *McLaughlin* is the controlling authority, and the family court correctly applied it.

---

## III. Even Absent the Statutory Presumption, Oakley Has Parental Rights Through Equitable Estoppel

Independent of the statutory presumption analysis, the family court properly found that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental rights. This alternative ground provides an independent basis for affirmance.

Equitable estoppel applies when: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends that the other party rely on the conduct; (3) the other party is ignorant of the facts; and (4) the other party relies on the conduct to his or her detriment. *See Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 558, 905 P.2d 520, 524 (App. 1995).

The evidence presented at the evidentiary hearing established all four elements. Turner knew that Oakley was not the biological mother and had not formally adopted C.T. Nevertheless, Turner repeatedly represented to Oakley, to medical providers, to social media audiences, and in her own will that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as C.T.'s sole custodian if Turner died. Turner listed Oakley on C.T.'s birth certificate in the section designated "father." Turner referred to Oakley as C.T.'s mother on social media. These representations were not accidental; they reflected the parties' mutual intent to raise C.T. together as co-parents.

Oakley relied on Turner's representations to her detriment. Oakley participated in the conception process, was present at C.T.'s birth, cut the umbilical cord, and held herself out as C.T.'s parent. Oakley formed a parental bond with C.T. based on Turner's assurances. To now allow Turner to disclaim Oakley's parental rights would work a profound injustice.

The *McLaughlin* court recognized the applicability of equitable estoppel in precisely these circumstances. There, the court held that the biological mother was equitably estopped from denying her wife's parental rights because the parties had entered into an agreement guaranteeing equal parenting rights, the wife was a beneficiary of the mother's will, and the wife had been a de facto parent of the child. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 565-67, 382 P.3d at 123-24. The same analysis applies here.

The plaintiff argues that equitable estoppel cannot create parental rights where none exist under the statute. This argument confuses the analytical framework. Equitable estoppel does not "create" parental rights; it prevents a party from denying parental rights that that party has voluntarily conferred. Turner cannot now retract the representations on which Oakley relied. *See In re Marriage of H.*, 231 Ariz. at 198, 291 P.3d at 1155 (recognizing that equitable estoppel may apply to preserve parental relationships formed through mutual intent and reliance).

---

## IV. The Policy Considerations Support Recognizing Oakley's Parental Rights

The plaintiff urges this Court to adopt a construction of § 25-814(A)(1) that would systematically deny parental rights to the non-biological spouse in same-sex marriages. Such a result would be perverse.

When Turner and Oakley decided to conceive a child together, they did so with the mutual understanding that Oakley would be C.T.'s parent. Turner encouraged Oakley's participation in the conception process, welcomed Oakley's presence at the birth, and affirmatively represented to the world—including medical providers, government officials, and her own social network—that Oakley was C.T.'s mother. The child's best interests also counsel in favor of affirming. C.T. has known Oakley as a parent since birth. Oakley has formed a parental bond with C.T. Denying Oakley parental status would deprive C.T. of a parent and harm the child's emotional and psychological well-being. Arizona law recognizes that the best interests of the child are paramount in custody determinations. A.R.S. § 25-403.01. This principle supports recognizing Oakley's parental rights.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the family court erred in granting Respondent Oakley presumed parent status under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and in finding Petitioner Turner equitably estopped from rebutting that presumption. Specifically, we must determine whether the statutory language "a man is presumed to be the father" precludes its application to a female spouse in a same-sex marriage, and whether equitable estoppel can independently support parental rights in this context.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Application of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)**

The family court applied the reasoning of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), to hold that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. The Petitioner argues that this constitutes judicial overreach, citing the statute’s explicit use of the terms "man" and "father." Petitioner relies on *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. 287, 766 P.2d 1129 (App. 1988), for the proposition that courts lack the power to rewrite gender-specific statutes.

While Petitioner’s textualist argument is grounded in traditional principles of statutory construction, it fails to account for the binding precedent established by *McLaughlin*. As a panel of this Court, we are bound by the decisions of other panels of the same court unless they are clearly erroneous or overruled. *McLaughlin* directly addressed the constitutionality and interpretation of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) in the context of same-sex marriage. The *McLaughlin* court determined that applying the statute only to men would violate equal protection principles and that the statute must therefore be interpreted gender-neutrally. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

The Petitioner’s reliance on *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. 287, 766 P.2d 1129 (App. 1988), is distinguishable. *Baby Boy D.* addressed adoption statutes and predated the legal recognition of same-sex marriage. As the Respondent notes, statutory interpretation must evolve to comport with constitutional mandates. See *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. 530, 535, 892 P.2d 215, 220 (App. 1994) (holding that statutes should be construed to give them constitutional validity if reasonably possible). Because *McLaughlin* has already established that the statute is applicable to same-sex spouses, the family court did not err in following that controlling authority.

**B. Equitable Estoppel**

Even if the statutory presumption were found to be inapplicable, the family court’s alternative ruling based on equitable estoppel provides an independent basis for affirmance. To establish equitable estoppel, the Respondent must show: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends that the other party rely on the conduct; (3) the other party is ignorant of the facts; and (4) the other party relies on the conduct to his or her detriment. *Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 558, 905 P.2d 520, 524 (App. 1995).

The record supports the family court’s finding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental rights. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as the child’s sole custodian, listed Oakley on the birth certificate, and publicly represented Oakley as the child’s mother on social media. These actions demonstrate Turner’s knowledge and intent for Oakley to be recognized as a parent. Oakley relied on these representations by participating in the insemination process, attending the birth, and forming a parental bond with the child. Allowing Turner to now deny Oakley’s parental status would prejudice Oakley and harm the child, contrary to the best interests of the child standard found in A.R.S. § 25-403.01. This reasoning aligns with the *McLaughlin* court’s affirmation of equitable estoppel in similar circumstances. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 565-67 ¶¶ 16-23, 382 P.3d at 123-24.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The family court correctly applied the binding precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), in determining that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies gender-neutrally. Furthermore, the court’s finding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental rights is supported by the evidence and established law. *Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 905 P.2d 520 (App. 1995). Therefore, the family court’s order granting Oakley presumed parent status is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the family court's ruling is affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

Respondent Oakley respectfully submits this rebuttal to Petitioner's arguments seeking reversal of the family court's ruling granting Oakley presumed parent status. Petitioner's arguments misapprehend the binding nature of precedent, misapply principles of statutory construction, and attempt to relitigate issues already settled by controlling authority. The family court's ruling should be affirmed.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. McLaughlin v. Jones Remains Binding Precedent

Petitioner argues that *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), is not binding because the Arizona Supreme Court granted review on April 18, 2017. This argument fails for two independent reasons.

First, the mere grant of review does not automatically vacate or supersede a Court of Appeals decision. As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, "we are bound by the decisions of other panels of the same court unless they are clearly erroneous or overruled." The Arizona Supreme Court had not issued a ruling overturning *McLaughlin* at the time the family court rendered its decision. The doctrine of vertical stare decisis requires that this Court follow binding precedent from prior panels unless and until that precedent is explicitly overruled by a higher court. *State v. Smaggus*, 163 Ariz. 215, 219, 786 P.2d 1128, 1132 (App. 1989) (holding that one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by the decisions of other panels unless overruled by the Supreme Court).

Second, Petitioner conflates the procedural status of *McLaughlin* with its substantive validity. The family court correctly applied *McLaughlin* as it existed at the time of decision. If Petitioner believed *McLaughlin* was improperly decided, the appropriate remedy was to await a ruling from the Arizona Supreme Court, not to ask this Court to disregard binding authority. Petitioner cannot selectively ignore precedent that does not favor her position while claiming reliance on precedent that does.

### B. The Presumption Statute Must Be Applied Gender-Neutrally

Petitioner relies heavily on *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. 287, 766 P.2d 1129 (App. 1988), arguing that it dispositively holds the presumption of paternity inapplicable to women. This argument fails because *Baby Boy D.* was decided in a fundamentally different legal context and has been effectively superseded by subsequent developments.

The *Baby Boy D.* court premised its holding on the assumption that the Arizona Legislature intended the statute to apply only to men. 159 Ariz. at 290-91, 766 P.2d at 1132-33. However, this Court in *McLaughlin* explicitly reconsidered this interpretation and held that applying the statute only to men would violate equal protection principles under both the United States and Arizona Constitutions. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The *McLaughlin* court determined that the statute must be interpreted gender-neutrally to pass constitutional muster. Id.

Petitioner's argument that *Garcia* requires ambiguity before constitutional avoidance applies misreads that decision. In *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. 530, 535, 892 P.2d 215, 220 (App. 1994), this Court held that "statutes should be construed to give them constitutional validity if reasonably possible." This principle applies with full force here: if the statute can be constitutionally applied in a gender-neutral manner, that construction must be adopted. The *McLaughlin* court already performed this analysis and concluded that gender-neutral application is required. This Court is bound by that determination.

Petitioner's reliance on *In re Marriage of H.*, 231 Ariz. 193, 291 P.3d 1150 (App. 2012), is misplaced. That case addressed the legislature's authority to create presumptions, not whether those presumptions must be applied consistently with constitutional equal protection requirements. The legislature may create statutory presumptions, but it may not create classifications that violate equal protection. *Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460 (2012). The *McLaughlin* court correctly recognized this limitation.

### C. Equitable Estoppel Provides An Independent And Valid Basis For Affirmance

Even if this Court were to find the statutory presumption inapplicable—which it should not—equitable estoppel independently supports the family court's ruling. Petitioner's argument that equitable estoppel "cannot be used to create a presumption of paternity where none exists under the statute" misstates the nature of the family court's ruling.

The family court did not use equitable estoppel to *create* a presumption of paternity. Rather, the court applied equitable estoppel to *prevent Turner from denying* a parental relationship that she voluntarily created, acknowledged, and affirmatively represented to both Oakley and the public. This distinction is critical.

The elements of equitable estoppel are: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends that the other party rely on the conduct; (3) the other party is ignorant of the facts; and (4) the other party relies on the conduct to his or her detriment. *Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 558, 905 P.2d 520, 524 (App. 1995). The record overwhelmingly supports the family court's finding that all four elements are satisfied.

**Turner's Knowledge and Intent**: Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as the child's sole custodian in the event of Turner's death. She listed Oakley on the birth certificate as the child's father. She publicly represented Oakley as the child's mother on social media. These are not ambiguous acts; they constitute affirmative representations that Oakley was a parent to the child.

**Oakley's Reliance**: Oakley participated in the artificial insemination process, including reviewing donor profiles and attending appointments. She was present at the child's birth and cut the umbilical cord. She formed a parental bond with the child, believing she was a co-parent based on Turner's representations.

**Detrimental Reliance**: Oakley would suffer significant detriment if Turner were permitted to deny her parental rights after actively participating in the child's conception and upbringing. More importantly, the child would suffer harm by being denied a relationship with a parent the child has known since birth.

Petitioner argues that *Baby Boy D.* rejected the use of equitable estoppel to establish paternity. However, the *Baby Boy D.* court addressed a fundamentally different scenario: a man claiming paternity of a child born to a married woman with whom he had no relationship. 159 Ariz. at 291, 766 P.2d at 1133. Here, by contrast, Turner and Oakley were married, intentionally conceived the child together, and Turner affirmatively represented Oakley as the child's parent. The *McLaughlin* court recognized this distinction, holding that equitable estoppel was appropriate where the biological mother had "affirmatively represented to her wife and to the world that her wife was a parent of the child." 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 20, 382 P.3d at 123.

### D. The Best Interests Of The Child Require Affirmance

Arizona law requires that all custody determinations be made based on the best interests of the child. A.R.S. § 25-403.01. Petitioner's position would deny the child a relationship with a parent who has been present since birth, who participated in the child's conception, and who has formed a parental bond with the child. This result serves no child's best interests.

The family court properly considered the child's best interests in applying both the statutory presumption and equitable estoppel. This Court should do the same.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent Oakley respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's ruling granting Oakley presumed parent status. The statutory presumption of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies gender-neutrally under *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016). Alternatively, Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental rights based on her affirmative representations that Oakley was the child's parent. Either basis independently supports affirmance.

**CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE PLAINTIFF/PROSECUTION**  

Your Honor, this Court must reverse the family court’s ruling because the presumption of paternity statute, A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), is explicitly gender-specific and cannot be rewritten to apply to women. The defense’s arguments misapply binding precedent, misread constitutional principles, and improperly seek to judicially legislate where the legislature has spoken with clear language. We urge this Court to uphold the rule of law and hold that the family court erred in applying a statute designed for men to a woman.  

**I. THE STATUTE’S PLAIN LANGUAGE LIMITS THE PRESUMPTION TO MEN, AND COURTS CANNOT REWRITE IT.**  

A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) states unambiguously: “A man is presumed to be the father of a child” if married to the mother within ten months of birth. The statute uses the word “man” and “father” repeatedly. This is not a suggestion—it is a clear legislative directive. When statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts must apply it as written. *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. 530, 535, 892 P.2d 215, 220 (App. 1994) (“Statutes must be interpreted according to their plain meaning”). Here, the statute’s text leaves no room for judicial reinterpretation. The defense’s attempt to “gender-neutralize” the statute is a textbook example of judicial overreach.  

This Court’s decision in *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. 287, 290–91, 766 P.2d 1129, 1132–33 (App. 1988), directly controls. There, this Court held the presumption of paternity “applies only to men” because the statute’s language “refers only to a ‘man’ and a ‘father’.” *Baby Boy D.* is binding precedent, and the defense cannot escape it by citing *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), which was later granted review by the Arizona Supreme Court. The defense argues *McLaughlin* remains binding, but this is legally unsound. *State v. Smaggus*, 163 Ariz. 215, 219, 786 P.2d 1128, 1132 (App. 1989), confirms that “one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by the decisions of other panels unless overruled by the Supreme Court.” Crucially, the Supreme Court’s grant of review in *McLaughlin* means the decision is *not final* and cannot serve as binding authority. Lower courts cannot rely on an appellate opinion pending review by the highest court. The family court erred by treating *McLaughlin* as controlling when it was not yet settled law.  

**II. THE STATUTE DOES NOT VIOLATE EQUAL PROTECTION, AND NO CONSTITUTIONAL AVOIDANCE IS REQUIRED.**  

The defense claims a gender-neutral reading is required to avoid equal protection violations. This argument fails for two reasons. First, the statute does not discriminate; it reflects a traditional legal framework where marriage creates a presumption of paternity for the husband. This is a well-established legislative choice, not an unconstitutional classification. *In re Marriage of H.*, 231 Ariz. 193, 198, 291 P.3d 1150, 1155 (App. 2012) (“Statutory presumptions in family law are presumptively valid when they serve legitimate state interests”). Second, constitutional avoidance applies only when a statute is ambiguous. *Garcia*, 181 Ariz. at 535, 892 P.2d at 220. Here, the statute’s language is crystal clear—“man” and “father” are not open to interpretation. When a statute is unambiguous, courts must enforce it as written, regardless of whether the application seems outdated or inconvenient. The defense’s reliance on *Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460 (2012), is misplaced; that case addressed Eighth Amendment sentencing, not parental presumptions, and has no bearing on statutory interpretation here.  

**III. EQUITABLE ESTOPPEL CANNOT CREATE A PRESUMPTION WHERE THE STATUTE EXPRESSLY LIMITS IT TO MEN.**  

Even if this Court rejects the statutory presumption, the defense’s argument that equitable estoppel independently supports Oakley’s status is legally flawed. Equitable estoppel cannot override a clear statutory requirement. *Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. at 291, 766 P.2d at 1133, explicitly rejected using estoppel to “create a presumption of paternity where none exists under the statute.” The family court’s ruling here does precisely that: it uses estoppel to circumvent the statute’s gender-specific language. The defense claims *Baby Boy D.* is distinguishable because Turner and Oakley were in a committed relationship, but that distinction is irrelevant. The statute does not create a presumption for “committed partners” or “de facto parents”—it applies only to men. As this Court held in *Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 558, 905 P.2d 520, 524 (App. 1995), equitable estoppel requires “detrimental reliance,” but reliance on a misrepresentation about legal rights cannot alter the statute’s plain terms. Turner’s actions—listing Oakley on the birth certificate or drafting a will—do not create a legal parentage status where the law says only men can be presumed fathers.  

**CONCLUSION**  

This Court must reverse the family court’s ruling. The law is clear: A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) is gender-specific, and courts cannot rewrite statutes to fit modern circumstances. *Baby Boy D.* remains binding precedent, and *McLaughlin* was not final when the family court relied on it. Equitable estoppel cannot override a statutory presumption that expressly applies only to men. The legislature—not this Court—must decide whether to expand the presumption to women. Until then, the rule of law requires strict adherence to the statute’s text. We respectfully request this Court reverse the family court and hold that Oakley cannot claim the presumption of paternity under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1).

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for special action review challenging the family court’s order granting Respondent Oakley presumed parent status under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and finding Petitioner Turner equitably estopped from rebutting that presumption. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties, the Court must determine whether the family court erred in its application of the presumption of paternity statute and equitable estoppel principles.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Binding Nature of *McLaughlin v. Jones***

The Petitioner argues that *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), is not binding precedent because the Arizona Supreme Court granted review in that matter. Specifically, the Petitioner contends that the grant of review renders the decision non-final and strips it of controlling authority. We disagree.

The rule regarding the binding nature of appellate precedent is well established. As stated in *State v. Smaggus*, 163 Ariz. 215, 219, 786 P.2d 1128, 1132 (App. 1989), "one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by the decisions of other panels unless and until those decisions are overruled by the Supreme Court." A grant of review by the Supreme Court is a procedural step indicating the higher court’s willingness to hear the case; it is not a decision on the merits and does not constitute an overruling of the Court of Appeals’ opinion. *State v. Smaggus*, 163 Ariz. at 219, 786 P.2d at 1132. Until the Supreme Court issues a mandate reversing or vacating the opinion, the decision remains binding on lower courts and other panels of the Court of Appeals. Therefore, the family court did not err in relying on *McLaughlin* as controlling authority at the time of its ruling.

**B. Application of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)**

The Petitioner contends that the plain language of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), which presumes a "man" is the "father" of a child born within ten months of marriage, limits the statute’s application to men. She relies on *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.*, 159 Ariz. 287, 766 P.2d 1129 (App. 1988), where this Court held that the presumption of paternity applies only to men. However, this argument fails in light of the binding precedent set by *McLaughlin*.

In *McLaughlin*, this Court held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally to avoid violating equal protection principles. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The Petitioner’s reliance on *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. 530, 535, 892 P.2d 215, 220 (App. 1994), to argue that the statute is unambiguous and must be applied as written, is misplaced. While *Garcia* states that statutes should be construed according to their plain meaning, it also acknowledges the principle of constitutional avoidance: if a statute, as applied, creates a constitutional violation, the court must construe it in a manner that upholds the constitution. *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. at 535, 892 P.2d at 220. *McLaughlin* performed this analysis and determined that a gender-neutral reading is required to prevent the statute from being invalid under equal protection. Because *McLaughlin* is binding precedent, the family court correctly applied the statute gender-neutrally to Oakley.

**C. Equitable Estoppel**

Even if the statutory presumption were inapplicable, the family court’s finding of equitable estoppel provides an independent basis for affirmance. The Petitioner argues that *In re Adoption of Baby Boy D.* precludes the use of equitable estoppel to create a presumption of paternity where none exists. However, the family court did not use equitable estoppel to create a presumption; rather, it used estoppel to prevent Turner from denying a parental relationship she had previously affirmed.

To establish equitable estoppel, the Respondent must show: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends that the other party rely on the conduct; (3) the other party is ignorant of the facts; and (4) the other party relies on the conduct to his or her detriment. *Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 558, 905 P.2d 520, 524 (App. 1995).

The record supports the family court’s finding. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as the child’s sole custodian, listed Oakley on the birth certificate, and publicly represented Oakley as the child’s mother on social media. These actions demonstrate Turner’s knowledge and intent for Oakley to be recognized as a parent. Oakley relied on these representations by participating in the insemination process, attending the birth, and forming a parental bond with the child. The facts in *Baby Boy D.*—which involved a man seeking parental rights over a child born to a married woman with whom he had no prior relationship—are distinguishable from the present case involving a married couple who planned to raise a child together. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 20, 382 P.3d at 123.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The family court correctly applied the binding precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), in determining that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies gender-neutrally. Furthermore, the court’s finding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental rights is supported by the evidence and established law. *Smithe v. Smithe*, 183 Ariz. 554, 905 P.2d 520 (App. 1995). Therefore, the family court’s order granting Oakley presumed parent status is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the family court's ruling is affirmed.
