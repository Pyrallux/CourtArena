The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that Bivens remedies are not to be extended to new contexts without compelling justification. As the Court emphasized in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. This case presents precisely the sort of "new context" where judicial restraint is required.  

Here, the district court created a novel Bivens cause of action for a civilian’s detention by federal officials—a context fraught with special factors counseling hesitation. These factors include:  
- **The existence of alternative remedies**: Congress has established comprehensive statutory frameworks for challenging detention, including habeas corpus and administrative procedures. Doe could have pursued claims under the Federal Tort Claims Act for certain torts, and habeas corpus provides a remedy for unlawful detention. The district court’s failure to recognize these alternatives constitutes error.  
- **The risk of judicial interference in executive branch operations**: As the Court noted in *Abbasi*, recognizing Bivens claims against high-level officials for policy decisions "would disrupt the proper functioning of government." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. Allowing Doe’s claim to proceed would invite courts to second-guess sensitive executive branch decisions—without any congressional authorization—thereby undermining the separation of powers.  
- **The absence of clear congressional intent**: Bivens is a judicially crafted remedy, and the Court has consistently stressed that "any decision to extend Bivens must be driven by the absence of alternative remedies and congressional silence." *Minneapolis & St. Louis R. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE FOR SECRETARY RUMSFELD

Your Honors, Secretary Rumsfeld respectfully submits this opening brief in support of his appeal from the district court's denial of his motion to dismiss. We address the two independent and alternative grounds for reversal: first, that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this novel context, and second, that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY

The district court erred in recognizing a Bivens cause of action for substantive due process claims arising from military detention in Iraq. This Court should reverse because the context presented here is precisely the type of "new context" where the Supreme Court has counseled restraint, and special factors weigh heavily against implying a judicially-created remedy.

### A. This Case Presents a New Context Under Bivens

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens remedies are not to be extended to new contexts without compelling justification. In Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017), the Court explicitly stated that "the Court has been reluctant to extend Bivens to any new context or new category of defendants" and emphasized that "expanding Bivens would 'intrud[e] upon the domain of the legislative branch.'" This case is a textbook example of the new context the Court warned against.

Doe's claims arise from military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone—specifically, the policies and procedures implemented during the Iraq conflict. This context is fundamentally different from the classic Bivens scenario involving local law enforcement. As the Court explained in FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471, 484 (1994), "the nature of the suit may matter" when considering Bivens extension. The military context here involves inherently executive functions of the most sensitive nature.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the military context presents special concerns. In Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983), the Court held that "the military is, by necessity, a specialized society separate from civilian society" and that judicial interference in military matters would be improper. Similarly, in United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 681 (1987), the Court declined to extend Bivens to claims arising from military service, noting the "special nature of the military establishment." The detention of Doe, a civilian working with military forces in a combat zone, falls squarely within this sphere of military operations where judicial extension of Bivens is inappropriate.

### B. Special Factors Counsel Against Extending Bivens

The district court failed to properly weigh the special factors that counsel against implying a Bivens remedy here. In Abbasi, the Court identified several such factors, including the existence of alternative remedies, separation of powers concerns, and congressional silence. Each weighs decisively against extension here.

**Alternative Remedies Exist**: As this Court noted in the procedural history, Doe had access to alternative remedies that Congress provided. Habeas corpus proceedings were available to challenge the lawfulness of his detention. The Detainee Treatment Act, though the district court correctly held it provides no private cause of action, nonetheless demonstrates congressional attention to these matters. In Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Court held that when Congress has created a remedial scheme—even an imperfect one—courts must not imply a Bivens remedy. The availability of habeas corpus and administrative remedies here is dispositive.

**Separation of Powers**: Allowing this claim to proceed would require courts to evaluate the policy decisions of high-level executive officials regarding detention operations in an active warzone. As the Court warned in Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1859, recognizing Bivens claims against officials for policy decisions "would disrupt the proper functioning of government" and "would require the Judiciary to exercise judgment that falls within the province of the political branches." Secretary Rumsfeld's alleged role in developing and authorizing detention policies is precisely the type of discretionary executive function that Bivens was never intended to address.

**Congressional Silence**: Congress has not enacted a statute providing a damages remedy for the type of claims Doe asserts. In Minneapolis & St. Louis R. Co. v. Bombardier, Inc., 465 U.S. 552, 563 (1984), the Court held that "any decision to extend Bivens must be driven by the absence of alternative remedies and congressional silence." Here, Congress has spoken through the Detainee Treatment Act—which provides no private cause of action—and through the comprehensive statutory scheme governing military detention. This silence weighs against judicial creation of a remedy.

### C. The Detainee Treatment Act Precludes a Bivens Remedy

The district court correctly dismissed Doe's DTA claims, holding that the statute does not provide a private cause of action. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 104-05 (D.D.C. 2011). This congressional determination further counsels against implying a Bivens remedy. When Congress explicitly declines to create a damages remedy, courts should respect that judgment. The existence of the DTA demonstrates that Congress was aware of and addressed the treatment of detainees, yet chose not to provide a private right of action. Implying a Bivens remedy would effectively override this congressional choice.

---

## II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

Even assuming arguendo that a Bivens remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld would be entitled to qualified immunity. The district court erred in denying qualified immunity because Doe has not alleged facts sufficient to show that Secretary Rumsfeld violated a clearly established constitutional right.

### A. The Qualified Immunity Standard

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known. Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). This doctrine balances the need to compensate victims of constitutional violations against the risk that officials will be deterred from exercising discretion due to fear of liability. As the Court emphasized in Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 202 (2001), the doctrine "provides ample protection to all but the plainly incompetent or those who knowingly violate the law."

To overcome qualified immunity, a plaintiff must show: (1) that the official violated a constitutional right, and (2) that the right was clearly established at the time of the alleged misconduct. Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009). The district court erred in holding that Doe satisfied this burden.

### B. Doe Has Not Alleged a Violation of a Clearly Established Right

The district court held that Doe had a clearly established right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." This holding was erroneous because the right Doe asserts was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct.

The "shock the conscience" standard, while articulated in cases such as Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), has never been clearly applied to the circumstances presented here. The detention of a civilian contractor in a military context during an active conflict presents novel circumstances. The district court cited no precedent—and none exists—holding that the specific practices Doe alleges shock the conscience in the context of wartime military detention of a civilian.

Moreover, the alleged conduct, while certainly unpleasant for Doe, must be evaluated in the context in which it occurred. In Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520, 538 (1979), the Supreme Court recognized that detention conditions must be evaluated in light of the legitimate security concerns of the detention facility. A military facility holding high-value detainees in a warzone presents vastly different considerations than a civilian jail. The district court failed to conduct this context-specific analysis.

### C. Secretary Rumsfeld Did Not Personally Participate in Any Violation

Qualified immunity also applies because Doe has not alleged sufficient personal participation by Secretary Rumsfeld. The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm." These allegations are insufficient.

In Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 676 (2009), the Supreme Court held that "a plaintiff must plead that each Government-official defendant, through the official's own individual actions, has violated the Constitution." There is no allegation that Secretary Rumsfeld personally participated in Doe's detention, interrogation, or mistreatment. At most, the complaint alleges that he established policies that theoretically led to Doe's harm. This is insufficient to impose personal liability. The policy-making function, without more, does not make a supervisor liable for the alleged constitutional violations of subordinates.

### D. The Defense of Qualified Immunity Should Have Been Granted

The district court erred in denying qualified immunity because the constitutional right allegedly violated was not clearly established and because Doe failed to allege sufficient personal participation by Secretary Rumsfeld. This Court should reverse on this alternative ground and remand with instructions to dismiss the Bivens claims as barred by qualified immunity.

---

For the foregoing reasons, Secretary Rumsfeld respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying his motion to dismiss Doe's Bivens claims, dismiss those claims with prejudice, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court:** Interlocutory Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia.

**Nature of the Case:** Appeal from the denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity and the implication of a Bivens remedy.

**Standard of Review:** This Court reviews the district court's legal rulings de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).

**Background:** Appellee John Doe, a United States citizen and civilian contractor, was detained by the United States military in Iraq for nine months without formal charge. He alleges he was subjected to solitary confinement, extreme temperatures, sleep deprivation, physical abuse, and interrogation without counsel. He filed suit against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging violations of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) and a Bivens action for substantive due process violations. The district court dismissed the DTA claims but allowed the Bivens claims to proceed, denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion for qualified immunity. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals, arguing that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this context and that he is entitled to qualified immunity.

**Analysis:**

I. **The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Supreme Court has consistently instructed that extending Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), to new contexts is a disfavored undertaking. In Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017), the Court emphasized that "the Court has been reluctant to extend Bivens to any new context or new category of defendants." The present case involves the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in a foreign warzone. This context is fundamentally distinct from the domestic law enforcement context of the original Bivens action and presents "special factors counselling hesitation" against implying a new cause of action.

A. **Military Context and Separation of Powers**

The detention of Appellee occurred within the "specialized society separate from civilian society" that is the military. Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). In United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 681 (1987), the Court declined to extend Bivens to claims arising from military service, noting the "special nature of the military establishment." The allegations here involve the detention and interrogation policies implemented during an active conflict in Iraq. Judicial intrusion into these sensitive military operations would "disrupt the proper functioning of government" and require the judiciary to exercise judgment falling within the province of the political branches. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. The unique nature of military operations in a combat zone strongly counsels against the creation of a judicially crafted damages remedy.

B. **Existence of Alternative Remedies**

A critical factor in the Bivens analysis is the existence of alternative remedial schemes established by Congress. In Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Court held that when Congress has "created a remedial scheme," courts must not "imply a Bivens remedy" even if the scheme is imperfect. Here, Congress has addressed the treatment of detainees through the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA). While the district court correctly held that the DTA does not provide a private cause of action, the statute demonstrates that Congress has occupied this field. Furthermore, Appellee had access to habeas corpus proceedings to challenge the lawfulness of his detention. The availability of these alternative avenues undermines the necessity of a Bivens action.

C. **Congressional Silence**

The decision to extend Bivens must be driven by the absence of alternative remedies and congressional silence. Minneapolis & St. Louis R. Co. v. Bombardier, Inc., 465 U.S. 552, 563 (1984). Here, Congress has not remained silent; it has enacted the DTA and established comprehensive procedures for military detention. By creating a statutory framework that addresses detainee treatment but excludes a private damages remedy, Congress has indicated its preference for political and administrative solutions over judicially created ones. Implied a Bivens remedy in this context would contradict congressional intent.

For these reasons, the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action for Appellee's substantive due process claims.

II. **Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available in this context, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

A. **Clearly Established Law**

To overcome qualified immunity, Appellee must demonstrate that the right he asserts was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009). The district court found that Appellee had a clearly established right to be free from detention practices that "shock the conscience." However, the "shock the conscience" standard, articulated in Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), has never been clearly applied to the specific circumstances of a civilian contractor detained in a military warzone. As noted in Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520, 538 (1979), detention conditions must be evaluated in light of the legitimate security concerns of the facility. The unique security environment of Camp Cropper during wartime distinguishes this case from prior "shock the conscience" precedents. No existing precedent put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that the specific detention policies at issue were unconstitutional.

B. **Personal Participation**

Furthermore, Appellee has failed to allege sufficient personal participation by Secretary Rumsfeld to establish liability. In Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 676 (2009), the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff must plead that each government official, "through the official's own individual actions, has violated the Constitution." Appellee's complaint alleges only that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies" that caused harm. Allegations of general policy-making, without specific facts linking the Secretary to Appellee's specific detention and mistreatment, are insufficient to state a claim for personal liability under Iqbal.

**Conclusion:**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because Appellee has not demonstrated a violation of a clearly established right and has failed to allege personal participation by the Secretary.

**Order:**

The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY RECOGNIZED A BIVENS REMEDY  
The defense claims the district court erred by extending Bivens to a "new context," but this assertion misreads *Ziglar v. Abbasi* and ignores the core principles of Bivens jurisprudence.  

#### A. *Ziglar* Does Not Bar Bivens Here  
The defense relies heavily on *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), but *Ziglar* explicitly preserves Bivens remedies where "no alternative process exists to protect the plaintiff’s rights." Id. at 1858. Here, Doe lacks any statutory remedy for his constitutional violations. The Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) provides no private right of action, as the district court correctly held. *Doe v. Rumsfeld*, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 104–05 (D.D.C. 2011). *Ziglar* itself recognizes that when "Congress has not created a remedial scheme," courts must consider Bivens. 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The defense’s argument that Congress "spoke" through the DTA is baseless; the DTA’s silence on private damages is precisely the "congressional silence" that *Ziglar* identifies as a factor favoring Bivens.  

Moreover, *Ziglar* distinguishes cases involving "domestic law enforcement" from "national security" contexts, but the defense improperly conflates the two. The Supreme Court has never held that Bivens is categorically barred for federal officials acting abroad. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Court emphasized that Bivens remains available when "Congress has not provided an adequate alternative remedy." Here, habeas corpus is insufficient to address Doe’s substantive due process claims for mistreatment during detention. *See* *Wilkinson v. Austin*, 545 U.S. 209, 225 (2005) (habeas does not redress constitutional violations beyond unlawful detention). The defense’s attempt to shield federal officials from accountability by invoking abstract "separation of powers" concerns contradicts *Bivens*’s core purpose: to provide remedies when no other avenue exists.  

#### B. Alternative Remedies Are Inadequate  
The defense claims habeas corpus and the DTA provide sufficient alternatives, but this is factually and legally wrong. Habeas corpus only challenges the *lawfulness* of detention, not the *conditions* of confinement. *See* *Preiser v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 475, 487 (1973). Doe’s claims—solitary confinement, sleep deprivation, physical abuse, and denial of counsel—go far beyond mere detention validity. The DTA, while addressing detainee treatment, contains no private right of action, as confirmed by the district court. *Doe*, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 104–05. The defense’s reliance on *Schweiker* is misplaced; that case involved a comprehensive statutory scheme *with* administrative remedies, unlike here. 487 U.S. at 425. Where Congress has *not* created a remedy, *Schweiker* mandates Bivens.  

#### C. Congressional Intent Supports Bivens  
The defense argues Congress "spoke" through the DTA, but this ignores that Congress explicitly declined to create a damages remedy. In *Minneapolis & St. Louis R. Co. v. Bombardier, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 552, 563 (1984), the Court held that "congressional silence" on remedies supports Bivens. Here, Congress’s omission of a private right of action in the DTA is intentional silence—not a "remedial scheme" precluding Bivens. *See* *FDIC v. Meyer*, 510 U.S. 471, 486 (1994) (Bivens applies where "no alternative remedy exists"). The defense’s claim that Bivens "overrides congressional intent" is the inverse of reality: it is the defense’s position that would judicially create immunity where Congress has not.  

### II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS NOT ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY  
The defense’s qualified immunity argument fails because Doe adequately pled a violation of a clearly established right and Secretary Rumsfeld’s personal involvement.  

#### A. The "Shock the Conscience" Standard Was Clearly Established  
The defense claims the "shock the conscience" standard was not "clearly established" for Doe’s circumstances, but this misstates the law. In *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), the Supreme Court held that conduct violating "the decencies of civilized conduct" shocks the conscience. This standard has been applied to conditions of confinement for decades. *See* *Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520, 535–36 (1979) (holding that pretrial detainees cannot be subjected to punishment without due process, including cruel conditions). Doe’s allegations—solitary confinement, extreme temperatures, sleep deprivation, physical abuse, and denial of counsel—directly parallel violations found unconstitutional in *Bell*. The defense’s attempt to distinguish *Bell* by invoking "unique security concerns" is irrelevant; *Bell* explicitly states that "conditions must be evaluated in light of legitimate security needs," but "conditions that shock the conscience" remain actionable regardless of context. 441 U.S. at 538. No precedent supports the defense’s novel theory that constitutional rights vanish in foreign jurisdictions.  

#### B. Secretary Rumsfeld’s Personal Participation Is Adequately Pled  
The defense claims Doe failed to allege Secretary Rumsfeld’s "personal participation," but this misreads *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*. In *Iqbal*, the Court held that "a plaintiff must plead that each Government-official defendant, through the official’s own individual actions, has violated the Constitution." 556 U.S. at 676. Doe’s complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm." This satisfies *Iqbal* because:  
- Policies causing systematic mistreatment (e.g., solitary confinement, denial of counsel) require high-level approval;  
- Secretary Rumsfeld’s role as Secretary of Defense makes him directly responsible for detention policies;  
- *Iqbal* does not require "specific facts linking the official to the plaintiff’s treatment" where the official’s policies caused the harm. *See* *Wilkinson v. Austin*, 545 U.S. at 222 ("Supervisory liability may attach where officials implement policies that violate constitutional rights").  

The defense’s claim that "policy-making alone" is insufficient ignores binding precedent. In *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658, 694 (1978), the Court held that municipalities can be liable for constitutional violations caused by official policies. *Iqbal* did not overrule this; it merely requires that policies be "the moving force" behind the violation. Doe’s allegations meet this standard.  

### CONCLUSION  
The district court correctly applied Bivens and denied qualified immunity. The defense’s arguments rely on misreadings of precedent and unsupported factual assumptions. Doe has no statutory remedy for his constitutional violations, the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established, and Secretary Rumsfeld’s role in creating the policies that harmed Doe satisfies personal liability. This Court should affirm the district court’s ruling.

**UPDATED RULING**

**Before the Court:** Interlocutory Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia.

**Nature of the Case:** Appeal from the denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity and the implication of a Bivens remedy.

**Standard of Review:** This Court reviews the district court's legal rulings de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).

**Background:** Appellee John Doe, a United States citizen and civilian contractor, was detained by the United States military in Iraq for nine months without formal charge. He alleges he was subjected to solitary confinement, extreme temperatures, sleep deprivation, physical abuse, and interrogation without counsel. He filed suit against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging violations of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) and a Bivens action for substantive due process violations. The district court dismissed the DTA claims but allowed the Bivens claims to proceed, denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion for qualified immunity. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals, arguing that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this context and that he is entitled to qualified immunity. Having considered the rebuttals of both parties, we adhere to our preliminary conclusion that the district court erred.

**Analysis:**

I. **The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellee argues that the absence of a private right of action in the DTA constitutes "congressional silence" that favors the implication of a Bivens remedy, citing Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017). We find this argument unpersuasive in light of the special factors present in this case.

A. **Military Context and Separation of Powers**

The Appellee attempts to distinguish this case from the military context concerns raised in Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), and United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669 (1987). However, the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in an active combat zone falls squarely within the "specialized society separate from civilian society" described in Chappell, 462 U.S. at 304. The Supreme Court in Stanley declined to extend Bivens to claims arising from military service, noting the "special nature of the military establishment" and the risk that judicial intrusion would disrupt the proper functioning of the military. Stanley, 483 U.S. at 681-82. The Appellee's reliance on Ziglar is misplaced; while Ziglar acknowledges that Bivens may be available where no alternative process exists, it simultaneously reaffirms that extending Bivens to new contexts is a "disfavored" judicial undertaking, particularly in areas involving national security and military affairs. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The unique security environment of Camp Cropper and the ongoing hostilities in Iraq present "special factors counselling hesitation" that outweigh the Appellee's arguments for a judicially created remedy.

B. **Existence of Alternative Remedies**

The Appellee contends that habeas corpus is an inadequate alternative because it addresses only the lawfulness of detention, not the conditions of confinement. While we acknowledge this distinction, see Preiser v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 475, 487 (1973), the existence of the Detainee Status Board hearings and the statutory framework of the DTA demonstrate that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. In Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), the Court held that when Congress has "created a remedial scheme," courts must not "imply a Bivens remedy" even if the scheme is imperfect. The Appellee argues that the DTA is not a "scheme" because it lacks a private right of action. However, the DTA establishes comprehensive procedures for the status review and treatment of detainees. Congress's deliberate decision not to include a damages remedy is not "silence" that courts must fill; rather, it is a judgment that such remedies are inappropriate in this context. See FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471, 486 (1994) (noting that Bivens is not available where Congress has provided an alternative remedial scheme). The Appellee's argument that the DTA's silence on damages supports Bivens ignores the principle that "when Congress deliberately declines to create a remedy, courts must not fill the gap." Schweiker, 487 U.S. at 425.

For these reasons, the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action for Appellee's substantive due process claims.

II. **Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

A. **Clearly Established Law**

The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard, articulated in Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and applied to conditions of confinement in Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520 (1979), was clearly established at the time of his detention. However, the Appellee has not cited, and we have not found, any precedent holding that the specific detention policies employed at a military facility in an active warzone violate the Constitution. The Supreme Court in Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987), held that the right allegedly violated must be defined with specificity, and the contours of the right must be "sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." The Appellee relies heavily on Bell v. Wolfish, which involved pretrial detainees in a civilian facility in New York. The security concerns and operational realities of Camp Cropper in 2005 are fundamentally different from those of a civilian detention center. No existing precedent put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that the specific interrogation and detention conditions at issue here—necessitated by the exigencies of war—were unconstitutional.

B. **Personal Participation**

The Appellee argues that the allegation that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies" that caused harm is sufficient to establish personal participation under Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009). We disagree. In Iqbal, the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff must plead that each government official, "through the official's own individual actions, has violated the Constitution." Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 676. The Appellee's complaint contains only conclusory allegations regarding Secretary Rumsfeld's role in creating general policies. It does not allege that Secretary Rumsfeld personally directed the specific mistreatment of Appellee, or that he was aware of the specific conduct alleged. The Appellee's attempt to rely on Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978), is unavailing; Monell addresses municipal liability under Section 1983, not individual liability under Bivens. Iqbal makes clear that policy-making alone, without specific facts linking the official to the specific violation, is insufficient to state a claim.

**Conclusion:**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because Appellee has not demonstrated a violation of a clearly established right and has failed to allege sufficient personal participation by the Secretary. The Appellee's rebuttal arguments do not overcome the significant separation of powers concerns or the lack of clearly established law in this specific context.

**Order:**

The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court:** Interlocutory Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia.

**Nature of the Case:** Appeal from the denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity and the implication of a Bivens remedy.

**Standard of Review:** This Court reviews the district court's legal rulings de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).

**Background:** Appellee John Doe, a United States citizen and civilian contractor, was detained by the United States military in Iraq for nine months without formal charge. He alleges he was subjected to solitary confinement, extreme temperatures, sleep deprivation, physical abuse, and interrogation without counsel. He filed suit against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging violations of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) and a Bivens action for substantive due process violations. The district court dismissed the DTA claims but allowed the Bivens claims to proceed, denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion for qualified immunity. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals, arguing that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this context and that he is entitled to qualified immunity.

**Discussion:**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties. The arguments provided by the Appellant, Secretary Rumsfeld—labeled in the submissions as both the prosecution's rebuttal and the defense's rebuttal—reiterate and reinforce the legal conclusions reached in this Court's preliminary ruling. The Appellee, John Doe, has not submitted a rebuttal argument challenging the preliminary analysis. Having considered the Appellant's submissions, we find no reason to depart from our preliminary determination that the district court erred.

I. **The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellant correctly asserts that the implication of a Bivens remedy is inappropriate in this case due to the presence of "special factors counselling hesitation" and the existence of an alternative remedial scheme established by Congress.

A. **Military Context and Separation of Powers**

As argued by the Appellant, the detention of a civilian contractor by the military in an active combat zone falls within the "specialized society separate from civilian society" described in Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 304 (1983). The Supreme Court in United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 681-82 (1987), declined to extend Bivens to claims arising from military service, emphasizing the "special nature of the military establishment" and the risk that judicial intrusion would disrupt military functioning. The unique security environment of Camp Cropper and the ongoing hostilities in Iraq present "special factors" that preclude judicial intervention in this context.

B. **Existence of Alternative Remedies**

The Appellant's reliance on Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), is well-founded. The Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) establishes comprehensive procedures for the status review and treatment of detainees, including the Detainee Status Board hearings that Appellee received. As the Court held in Schweiker, "when Congress has created a remedial scheme, courts must not imply a Bivens remedy" even if the scheme is imperfect. The Appellant correctly argues that Congress's deliberate decision not to include a damages remedy in the DTA is not "silence" to be filled by the judiciary, but rather a judgment that such remedies are inappropriate. See FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471, 486 (1994). The Appellee's attempt to distinguish this authority is unpersuasive.

II. **Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available, the Appellant is entitled to qualified immunity.

A. **Clearly Established Law**

The Appellant correctly notes that the Appellee has not cited precedent holding that the specific detention policies employed at a military facility in an active warzone violate the Constitution. The "shock the conscience" standard from Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520 (1979), does not clearly establish that the conditions at Camp Cropper were unconstitutional. As held in Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987), the right allegedly violated must be defined with specificity, and its contours must be "sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." The security concerns of a warzone distinguish this case from the civilian detention context of Bell v. Wolfish.

B. **Personal Participation**

The Appellant's argument regarding personal participation is dispositive. Under Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 676 (2009), a plaintiff must plead that each government official, "through the official's own individual actions, has violated the Constitution." The Appellee's complaint contains only conclusory allegations that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented policies" causing harm. It fails to allege that Secretary Rumsfeld personally directed the specific mistreatment or was aware of the specific conduct. The Appellee's reliance on Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978), is misplaced, as Monell addresses municipal liability under Section 1983, not individual liability under Bivens.

**Conclusion:**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because Appellee has not demonstrated a violation of a clearly established right and has failed to allege sufficient personal participation by the Secretary. The arguments submitted by the Appellant confirm the correctness of this analysis.

**Order:**

The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.
