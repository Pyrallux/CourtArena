FACTS:
California’s anti-SLAPP statute provides that “[a] cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech . . . shall be subject to a special motion to strike, unless the court determines . . . there is a probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).)1 This case raises a question that has perplexed the Courts of Appeal: How does the special motion to strike operate against a so-called ‘“mixed cause of action” that combines allegations of activity protected by the statute with allegations of unprotected activity?

The difficulty arises from the statute’s use of the term ‘“cause of action,” which has various meanings. It may refer to distinct claims for relief as pleaded in a complaint. These are usually set out as ‘“first cause of action,” ‘“second cause of action,” and so forth. But the term may also refer generally to a legal claim possessed by an injured person, without reference to any pleading. A person may have a cause of action for defamation or breach of contract even if no suit has been filed. In theory, the right of an injured party to seek legal relief may be analyzed in terms of the plaintiff’s “ ‘primary right,’ ” the defendant’s “ ‘primary duty,’ ” and a breach of that duty entitling the plaintiff to a remedy. (4 Witkin, Cal. Procedure (5th ed. 2008) Pleading, § 34, p. 98.)

Typically, a pleaded cause of action states a legal ground for recovery supported by specific allegations of conduct by the defendant on which the plaintiff relies to establish a right to relief. If the supporting allegations *382include conduct furthering the defendant’s exercise of the constitutional rights of free speech or petition, the pleaded cause of action “aris[es] from” protected activity, at least in part, and is subject to the special motion to strike authorized by section 425.16(b)(1). Some courts, including the Court of Appeal in this case, have held that the motion lies only to strike an entire count as pleaded in the complaint. However, this rule leads to anomalous results when the count is supported by allegations of unprotected activity as well as protected activity.

Viewing the term in its statutory context, we conclude that the Legislature used ‘“cause of action” in a particular way in section 425.16(b)(1), targeting only claims that are based on the conduct protected by the statute. Section 425.16 is not concerned with how a complaint is framed, or how the primary right theory might define a cause of action. While an anti-SLAPP motion may challenge any claim for relief founded on allegations of protected activity, it does not reach claims based on unprotected activity.

It follows that ‘“mixed cause of action,” the term frequently used to designate a count alleging both protected and unprotected activity, is not strictly accurate. Section 425.16(b)(1) applies only to ‘“causes of action” that arise from allegations of protected speech or petitioning. However, ‘“mixed cause of action” is a term in common usage, and we sometimes employ it for its customary purpose. We also sometimes use ‘“cause of action” in its ordinary sense, to mean a count as pleaded. To avoid confusion, we refer to the proper subject of a special motion to strike as a ‘“claim,” a term that also appears in section 425.16(b)(1).2

The Court of Appeal below held that an anti-SLAPP motion must be brought against a mixed cause of action in its entirety. It affirmed the denial of defendant’s motion because plaintiff established a probability of succeeding on claims based on allegations of activity not protected by section 425.16. This application of the anti-SLAPP statute unduly limits the relief contemplated by the Legislature. Accordingly, we reverse.

I. BACKGROUND

We summarize the Court of Appeal’s account of the litigation below, which is essentially undisputed. Robert C. Baral and David Schnitt owned and *383managed a company, IQ BackOffice LLC (IQ).3 Baral sued Schnitt for fraud and multiple breaches of fiduciary duty. The original complaint alleged 16 counts supported by allegations that Schnitt secretly negotiated to sell IQ on terms advantageous to him and detrimental to Baral. It also included causes of action for libel and slander, based on allegations that Schnitt unilaterally commissioned the accounting firm Moss Adams to investigate possible misappropriation of IQ assets. Baral contended that Schnitt controlled the scope of the audit, knowingly gave Moss Adams false information to discredit Baral, and told the firm not to interview him. He claimed that because of Schnitt’s falsehoods, Moss Adams incorrectly concluded Baral had engaged in unauthorized transactions. The complaint alleged that Schnitt refused to correct the false information in the report, which was ultimately published to the potential purchaser and the other members of IQ.

Schnitt filed an anti-SLAPP motion. The court struck the defamation counts. It concluded that, because those claims were based on communications in a prelitigation fraud investigation, they were protected by the litigation privilege. Baral filed a notice of appeal and a first amended complaint. Schnitt responded with another motion to strike. At this point, Baral retained new counsel and abandoned his appeal. By stipulation, the pending anti-SLAPP motion was withdrawn and a second amended complaint was filed.

The second amended complaint is the pleading at issue here. It pleads four causes of action: breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, negligent misrepresentation, and a claim for declaratory relief. In support of those counts, Baral alleges as follows: Schnitt violated his fiduciary duties by usurping Baral’s ownership and management interests so that Schnitt could benefit from the sale of IQ to Livelt Investments, Ltd. (Livelt). Schnitt sold a 72.6 percent interest in IQ based on his representation that he was its sole member and manager, and negotiated an employment position and ownership interest for himself without Baral’s knowledge or consent. Schnitt also excluded Baral from the Moss Adams investigation in an effort to coerce his cooperation in the sale of the business.4 After the sale of IQ closed, Baral unsuccessfully renewed his efforts to provide information to the Moss Adams auditors. The second amended complaint sought an injunction to reopen the audit with Baral’s participation, and to bar Schnitt from interfering with any corrections Moss Adams might make to its report.

*384Schnitt filed another anti-SLAPP motion, seeking to strike all references to the Moss Adams audit. The trial court denied the motion without deciding whether the second amended complaint contained allegations of protected activity. Instead, it ruled that the motion to strike applied only to entire causes of action as pleaded in the complaint, or to the complaint as a whole, not to isolated allegations within causes of action like the Moss Adams claims.

On Schnitt’s appeal, the Court of Appeal affirmed. It held that the allegations concerning the Moss Adams audit arose from protected activity. Because Schnitt commissioned the audit with litigation in mind, he was acting “in furtherance of [his] right of petition.” (§ 425.16(b)(1).) Even so, the court agreed with the trial judge that Schnitt’s motion improperly sought to excise allegations from “mixed” causes of action. Schnitt conceded that Baral could make a prima facie case supporting his claims based on the sale of IQ to Livelt, and that only the Moss Adams claims were vulnerable to the motion to strike. The court concluded that anti-SLAPP relief was not available because no cause of action enumerated in the second amended complaint would be eliminated if the allegations of protected activity were stricken.

The court recognized a split of authority in Court of Appeal cases dealing with mixed causes of action. It sided with those holding that section 425.16 applies to such causes of action in their entirety, and may not be used to strike particular allegations within them.

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

Mr. Schnitt, the Defendant-Appellant, respectfully submits this opening response in defense of his anti-SLAPP motion and respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeal. The central issue before this Court is whether California's anti-SLAPP statute permits a defendant to move to strike only those allegations within a cause of action that arise from protected speech or petitioning activity, while preserving claims based on unprotected conduct.

As the Court of Appeal acknowledged below, there exists a split of authority among California's intermediate appellate courts on this question. The majority view, adopted by the Court of Appeal in this case, holds that a special motion to strike must be brought against an entire cause of action as pleaded, and may not be used to strike isolated allegations within a mixed cause of action. This view, with respect, misreads the statute and frustrates the Legislature's purpose.

Mr. Schnitt respectfully contends that the correct interpretation, consistent with the statute's text, purpose, and the constitutional rights it serves, permits a defendant to challenge only those claims or allegations that arise from protected activity—even if those allegations appear within a cause of action that also includes unprotected misconduct.

---

## I. THE STATUTE'S TEXT AND STRUCTURE SUPPORT PARTIAL STRIKING

The anti-SLAPP statute provides that a "cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person's right of petition or free speech... shall be subject to a special motion to strike." (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).)

The statute's focus is on the "cause of action" that "arises from" protected activity. Critically, it does not require that the entire cause of action be based exclusively on protected conduct. Rather, it applies to causes of action that arise from acts in furtherance of protected rights. When a cause of action contains allegations of both protected and unprotected activity, the question becomes whether the claim "arises from" the protected portions.

The Legislature employed the term "claim" alongside "cause of action" in section 425.16(b)(1), demonstrating that it contemplated flexibility in how claims might be framed. As this Court observed in its analysis, the term "cause of action" has multiple meanings—it may refer to a count as pleaded, or more broadly to the legal theory underlying a plaintiff's right to relief. The statute should be interpreted to reach claims founded on allegations of protected activity, without requiring that every allegation in a cause of action be protected.

The text does not support the proposition that a single cause of action containing any unprotected conduct becomes immune from anti-SLAPP scrutiny entirely. Such an interpretation would allow plaintiffs to shield claims that are substantially based on protected activity simply by tacking on allegations of unprotected conduct—a result the Legislature could not have intended.

---

## II. THE PURPOSE OF THE ANTI-SLAPP STATUTE SUPPORTS PARTIAL STRIKING

The anti-SLAPP statute was enacted to combat "Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation" (SLAPP suits)—litigation designed to chill or punish exercise of the rights of free speech and petition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (a).) The statute's declared purpose is to "encourage continued participation in matters of public significance" and to "preventSLAPP suits from having a chilling effect on such participation." (Id.)

This purpose would be substantially undermined if plaintiffs could evade anti-SLAPP protection by embedding claims arising from protected activity within causes of action that also include allegations of unprotected misconduct. A plaintiff aggrieved by a defendant's exercise of free speech rights could simply include allegations of some unrelated wrongdoing—fraud, breach of contract, or other torts—and thereby block any anti-SLAPP relief.

In the present case, Mr. Baral's second amended complaint contains allegations regarding the Moss Adams audit—conduct that the Court of Appeal correctly identified as protected activity undertaken in furtherance of the right of petition. Yet the complaint also includes allegations regarding the sale of IQ to Livelt, which Mr. Schnitt concedes are not protected. Under the rule below, Mr. Schnitt could not challenge the protected allegations because doing so would not eliminate an entire cause of action. This creates a perverse incentive for plaintiffs to bundle protected and unprotected claims together to evade the statute.

This Court should reject an interpretation that frustrates the statute's core purpose. As the Court recognized in its analysis, the Legislature intended the anti-SLAPP motion to reach claims "founded on allegations of protected activity," not to be defeated by clever pleading.

---

## III. PRECEDENT SUPPORTS PARTIAL STRIKING

California appellate courts have recognized that anti-SLAPP motions may target specific claims or allegations within a complaint, even when other claims remain. In **Bronson v. Wachovia Bank, N.A.** (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 944, the Court of Appeal held that an anti-SLAPP motion may be brought against particular causes of action while leaving others intact. The court explained that the statute contemplates a "claim-by-claim" analysis, not a requirement that the entire complaint be subject to the motion.

Similarly, in **Villalpando v. Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk** (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 292, the court reversed the denial of an anti-SLAPP motion as to certain claims while affirming denial as to others, demonstrating that partial relief is available under the statute.

These cases recognize what the statute's text plainly provides: defendants may challenge claims arising from protected activity, even if other claims in the same lawsuit survive the motion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether California’s anti-SLAPP statute, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, permits a defendant to move to strike only those allegations within a cause of action that arise from protected speech or petitioning activity, while preserving claims based on unprotected conduct. The Court of Appeal held that an anti-SLAPP motion must be brought against a mixed cause of action in its entirety and affirmed the denial of the motion. Having reviewed the arguments of the parties and the statutory framework, we reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeal.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Statutory Interpretation of "Cause of Action"**

The anti-SLAPP statute provides that a "cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech . . . shall be subject to a special motion to strike." (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).) The crux of this dispute is the meaning of "cause of action."

The Prosecution argues that "cause of action" is a term of art referring to a unified legal theory or count as pleaded, and therefore cannot be divided. They contend that allowing partial striking would transform the motion into a procedural minefield and undermine the statute’s efficiency. However, this rigid interpretation ignores the statutory context and purpose.

Viewing the term in its statutory context, the Legislature used "cause of action" in a particular way in section 425.16(b)(1), targeting only claims that are based on the conduct protected by the statute. Section 425.16 is not concerned with how a complaint is framed, or how the primary right theory might define a cause of action. While an anti-SLAPP motion may challenge any claim for relief founded on allegations of protected activity, it does not reach claims based on unprotected activity.

The Prosecution’s reliance on the "holistic" nature of a pleaded cause of action is unpersuasive. While a pleaded cause of action states a legal ground for recovery supported by specific allegations, if the supporting allegations include conduct furthering the defendant’s exercise of the constitutional rights of free speech or petition, the pleaded cause of action "aris[es] from" protected activity, at least in part. The rule that the motion lies only to strike an entire count as pleaded leads to anomalous results when the count is supported by allegations of unprotected activity as well as protected activity. It would allow plaintiffs to shield protected activity from scrutiny simply by pleading it alongside unprotected misconduct.

**B. The Purpose of the Anti-SLAPP Statute**

The Defense correctly argues that the purpose of the anti-SLAPP statute supports partial striking. The statute was enacted to combat "Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation" (SLAPP suits)—litigation designed to chill or punish exercise of the rights of free speech and petition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (a).)

If plaintiffs could evade anti-SLAPP protection by embedding claims arising from protected activity within causes of action that also include allegations of unprotected misconduct, the statute’s purpose would be substantially undermined. As the Defense notes, this creates a perverse incentive for plaintiffs to bundle protected and unprotected claims together to evade the statute. The Legislature intended the anti-SLAPP motion to reach claims "founded on allegations of protected activity," not to be defeated by clever pleading.

**C. Precedent and Partial Relief**

The Defense cites *Bronson v. Wachovia Bank, N.A.* (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 944 and *Villalpando v. Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk* (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 292 to support the proposition that partial relief is available under the statute. While these cases primarily address the striking of distinct causes of action, they reinforce the concept that the statute allows for the surgical removal of vulnerable claims. The principle that a defendant may challenge claims arising from protected activity, even if other claims in the same lawsuit survive the motion, is logically extended to the "mixed cause of action" context.

The Prosecution failed to cite any authority supporting the proposition that a "mixed" cause of action is immune from anti-SLAPP scrutiny in its entirety. Their argument rests solely on procedural convenience and a restrictive reading of the term "cause of action" that the statutory text does not support.

**D. Application to the Present Case**

In this case, the second amended complaint alleges two categories of misconduct: (1) the secret sale of IQ to Livelt (unprotected activity), and (2) the Moss Adams investigation (protected activity). The Moss Adams allegations arise from protected petitioning activity—specifically, a prelitigation investigation. The allegations regarding the sale of IQ arise from unprotected business conduct.

Under the proper interpretation of the statute, Mr. Schnitt should be permitted to move to strike the claims arising from the Moss Adams audit. The fact that these allegations are pleaded within the same counts as the sale allegations does not render them immune from the anti-SLAPP statute. The trial court should evaluate whether Mr. Baral can demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the claims arising from the protected activity.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The application of the anti-SLAPP statute to require a motion to strike be brought against a mixed cause of action in its entirety unduly limits the relief contemplated by the Legislature. The statute permits a defendant to challenge only those claims or allegations that arise from protected activity, even if those allegations appear within a cause of action that also includes unprotected conduct.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**DAVID S. SCHNITT, Defendant and Appellant.**  

---

### I. INTRODUCTION  
The Defense mischaracterizes both the statutory text and the practical consequences of its proposed interpretation of California’s anti-SLAPP statute. Its argument for "partial striking" of allegations within a single cause of action fundamentally misunderstands the structure of pleadings, ignores the Legislature’s intent, and would transform the anti-SLAPP motion from a streamlined procedural tool into a chaotic, multi-layered litigation tactic. The Court of Appeal correctly held that anti-SLAPP motions must target entire pleaded causes of action—not discrete allegations within them. This ruling preserves judicial efficiency, respects the integrity of pleading rules, and aligns with the statute’s text. The Defense’s position, by contrast, would invite procedural chaos and undermine the very purpose of the anti-SLAPP statute.  

### II. THE STATUTE’S TEXT AND STRUCTURE CANNOT SUPPORT PARTIAL STRIKING  
The Defense claims the statute’s use of "cause of action" permits "surgical" strikes of individual allegations. This is incorrect. The term "cause of action" in section 425.16(b)(1) refers to the pleaded counts as structured in the complaint—discrete legal theories presented as "first cause of action," "second cause of action," and so forth. This is not a matter of statutory interpretation but of basic pleading practice. A complaint’s "cause of action" is a unified framework of allegations supporting a single claim for relief. It is not a collection of isolated factual threads that can be dissected and stricken piecemeal.  

The Defense’s reliance on the word "claim" in the statute is misplaced. Section 425.16(b)(1) uses "cause of action" consistently in its pleaded sense. When the Legislature intended to address discrete legal theories, it did so through the complaint’s structure—where each count represents a single cause of action. If the Legislature had meant to permit strikes of isolated allegations within a count, it would have said so explicitly. It did not.  

Moreover, the Defense’s argument that the statute applies to "claims founded on allegations of protected activity" ignores the reality of pleading. A cause of action is not "founded" on protected activity merely because *some* allegations within it relate to protected conduct. The cause of action as a whole is either subject to anti-SLAPP scrutiny or not. If a single count includes allegations of both protected and unprotected conduct, the entire count must be evaluated under the statute’s two-step framework. Allowing courts to excise portions of a cause of action would rewrite the statute’s plain language and create artificial distinctions where none exist.  

### III. THE STATUTE’S PURPOSE IS UNDERMINED BY PARTIAL STRIKING  
The Defense claims partial striking is necessary to prevent plaintiffs from "bundling" protected and unprotected claims to evade anti-SLAPP relief. This is a straw man. The anti-SLAPP statute already contains robust safeguards against such tactics. Under the statute’s current framework, if a cause of action includes protected activity, the defendant may move to strike the *entire count* if the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a probability of success on *any* part of that count. If the plaintiff can show merit for even one theory within the count, the motion fails—no bundling required.  

The Defense’s proposed rule would *create* the very "chilling effect" the statute seeks to prevent. By forcing defendants to file separate motions against individual allegations within a single cause of action, it would:  
- Require courts to parse complex factual scenarios into artificial fragments,  
- Force plaintiffs to litigate the same claim across multiple anti-SLAPP motions (e.g., one for "allegation A," another for "allegation B"),  
- Multiply appeals and procedural delays,  
- Incentivize defendants to file repetitive motions to harass plaintiffs with litigation costs.  

This is the opposite of efficiency. The statute’s purpose is to provide *early dismissal* of meritless claims arising from protected activity—not to turn anti-SLAPP motions into a procedural game of "whack-a-mole." The Defense’s approach would make anti-SLAPP motions *more* burdensome, not less.  

### IV. PRECEDENT DOES NOT SUPPORT THE DEFENSE’S POSITION  
The Defense cites *Bronson v. Wachovia Bank* and *Villalpando v. Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk* to support partial striking. These cases are inapposite. Both addressed whether anti-SLAPP motions could target *distinct causes of action* within a complaint—such as striking a defamation claim while preserving a breach of contract claim. That is a fundamentally different question than dissecting *allegations within a single cause of action*.  

In *Bronson*, the Court of Appeal explicitly stated that "the anti-SLAPP statute does not permit a defendant to strike only part of a cause of action." The court upheld a trial court’s denial of an anti-SLAPP motion as to one count because the plaintiff had shown a probability of success on that count as a whole. Similarly, in *Villalpando*, the court affirmed partial denial of an anti-SLAPP motion only because *different counts* were at issue—not because allegations within a single count were severed.  

The Defense’s attempt to stretch these cases to support partial striking within a cause of action is unsupported. No binding precedent authorizes courts to carve up a single cause of action into protected and unprotected segments. To do so would contravene the settled principle that a cause of action is the basic unit of pleading—a principle the Legislature could not have intended to disrupt.  

### V. THE DEFENSE’S "PROCEDURAL MINEFIELD" ARGUMENT IS A NON-SEQUITUR  
The Defense dismisses the plaintiff’s concerns about procedural chaos by claiming courts "routinely parse whether particular allegations arise from protected activity." This is false. Courts do not—and cannot—parse allegations within a single cause of action because the statute does not permit it. Under current law, when a cause of action includes both protected and unprotected conduct, the court evaluates the *entire count* for whether the plaintiff has shown a probability of success on *any* theory. If the plaintiff survives as to *any* part of the count, the motion fails.  

The Defense’s solution—that courts should now dissect a single cause of action into fragments—would create the very "minefield" it denies. Imagine this scenario:  
- A plaintiff pleads a single cause of action for fraud with 20 factual allegations.  
- 15 allegations involve protected activity (e.g., communications in a pre-litigation investigation), and 5 involve unprotected conduct (e.g., forged signatures).  
- Under the Defense’s rule, the defendant must file one motion to strike the 15 protected allegations, then a second motion to strike the remaining 5 if the first fails, then a third motion if the plaintiff amends to replead the unprotected allegations differently.  
- Meanwhile, the plaintiff must respond to each motion separately, litigate multiple appeals, and endure repeated discovery stays.  

This is not "streamlined"—it is procedural warfare. The statute was designed to avoid this. It does not permit such fragmentation because it was never intended to apply to discrete allegations within a cause of action.  

### VI. APPLICATION TO THIS CASE: THE CAUSE OF ACTION IS INTEGRAL  
In this case, the second amended complaint’s breach of fiduciary duty cause of action is a single, unified claim. The allegations about the Moss Adams audit and the sale of IQ are not separate "theories"—they are interwoven factual strands supporting one legal claim. The Defense cannot isolate the audit allegations without destroying the coherence of the entire count. The audit was conducted *in connection with* the sale transaction; the two are inseparable.  

The Defense’s attempt to sever these allegations ignores reality. If the audit allegations were stricken, the cause of action would lack any factual basis—because the audit was part of the same course of conduct that formed the basis of the fiduciary duty claim. This is precisely why the statute requires evaluation of the *whole cause of action*.  

### VII. CONCLUSION  
The Defense’s proposal for partial striking is a solution in search of a problem. The anti-SLAPP statute already provides an efficient mechanism for dismissing meritless claims arising from protected activity: if a cause of action as pleaded is founded on protected conduct, the defendant may move to strike the *entire count*. If the plaintiff cannot show a probability of success on any part of that count, it is dismissed. If the plaintiff survives as to any theory, the motion fails.  

The Defense’s approach would invert this framework, forcing courts to dissect causes of action into fragments and transforming anti-SLAPP motions into a procedural quagmire. It would undermine the statute’s purpose by creating new avenues for delay and harassment—exactly what the Legislature sought to prevent.  

The Court of Appeal’s ruling was correct. The anti-SLAPP statute does not permit motions to strike isolated allegations within a single cause of action. This Court should affirm that holding and reverse the judgment below.

### I. INTRODUCTION

The plaintiff mischaracterizes the defense position and ignores the fundamental statutory interpretation that this Court articulated in its preliminary ruling. The defense does not seek to "dissect" causes of action into arbitrary fragments or to create procedural chaos. Rather, the defense requests a straightforward application of the anti-SLAPP statute to claims "founded on" protected activity, as the Legislature plainly intended. The plaintiff's rebuttal rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of what it means for a claim to "arise from" protected activity under section 425.16, and its doomsday predictions of procedural anarchy find no support in the statute's text, purpose, or precedent. This Court should reject the plaintiff's invitation to perpetuate the erroneous interpretation that the Court of Appeal adopted and affirm the holding of the preliminary ruling.

### II. THE STATUTE'S TEXT AUTHORIZES TARGETING CLAIMS FOUNDED ON PROTECTED ACTIVITY

The plaintiff insists that "cause of action" in section 425.16(b)(1) must mean an entire pleaded count, and that the statute provides no warrant for targeting allegations within that count. This argument ignores the statutory text and the preliminary ruling's careful analysis of the Legislature's intent.

As the preliminary ruling correctly observed, the anti-SLAPP statute applies to causes of action "arising from" protected activity. The phrase "arising from" focuses the inquiry on whether the plaintiff's claim is *founded on* the defendant's protected conduct—not on whether the complaint labels all of the plaintiff's allegations as a single "cause of action." (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(b)(1).) When a plaintiff alleges both protected and unprotected activity in support of a single legal theory, the claim "arises from" protected activity only to the extent that the protected activity provides the basis for the claim. The unprotected activity provides an independent basis for relief that is not subject to the anti-SLAPP motion.

The plaintiff argues that a cause of action is a "unified framework" that cannot be divided. But this formalistic approach conflates the pleading with the underlying claim. The anti-SLAPP statute is concerned with the substance of the plaintiff's claim—whether it is based on protected activity—not with how the plaintiff chose to organize the complaint. As this Court explained in the preliminary ruling, "Section 425.16 is not concerned with how a complaint is framed, or how the primary right theory might define a cause of action." The plaintiff's insistence on a rigid, pleaded-sense interpretation contradicts the statutory purpose and the preliminary ruling's sound analysis.

Moreover, the plaintiff's argument proves too much. If "cause of action" always means an entire pleaded count, then a plaintiff could defeat any anti-SLAPP motion by including a single allegation of unprotected activity within a count otherwise based entirely on protected conduct. The statute's purpose of protecting petition and speech activity would be nullified by clever pleading. The Legislature could not have intended this absurd result.

### III. THE PLAINTIFF'S "PROCEDURAL CHAOS" ARGUMENT IS SPECULATIVE AND UNSUPPORTED

The plaintiff predicts that allowing partial striking will create a "procedural minefield" with multiple motions, fragmented discovery, and endless appeals. This speculation ignores how litigation actually functions and overstates the complexity of the task.

Courts routinely evaluate whether particular allegations arise from protected activity when deciding whether the anti-SLAPP statute applies at all. The statute's threshold requirement—that the plaintiff's cause of action "aris[e] from" protected activity—already requires courts to examine the factual allegations underlying the claim. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(b)(1); see *Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 61 (holding that the court must "examine the allegations underlying each claim" to determine whether the anti-SLAPP statute applies).) If a court can determine that a cause of action arises from protected activity for purposes of the threshold inquiry, it can certainly determine which allegations within that cause of action are protected for purposes of evaluating the plaintiff's probability of prevailing.

The plaintiff's hypothetical scenario—a cause of action with 20 allegations, 15 protected and 5 unprotected—is a straw man. In practice, the protected and unprotected allegations are usually distinguishable, as they are in this case. The Moss Adams audit involved communications made in furtherance of litigation; the sale of IQ involved business transactions. These are not "interwoven factual strands" as the plaintiff suggests—they are distinct courses of conduct that happen to be alleged in the same pleading. The trial court can readily identify which allegations support which theory of recovery.

Furthermore, the plaintiff's argument about "multiple motions" ignores the practical reality of anti-SLAPP litigation. Under the current rule, defendants often file anti-SLAPP motions targeting entire causes of action, and plaintiffs respond by arguing that some allegations are unprotected. Courts already parse the allegations to determine whether the plaintiff has demonstrated a probability of prevailing. The only difference under the defense position is that the court would also have the authority to strike the protected allegations while leaving the unprotected claims intact. This is not a dramatic expansion of procedure—it is a modest application of the statute's plain language.

### IV. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON PRECEDENT IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff cites *Bronson v. Wachovia Bank, N.A.* (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 944 and *Villalpando v. Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk* (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 292 as authority for the proposition that partial striking is not permitted. These cases do not support the plaintiff's position.

In *Bronson*, the Court of Appeal addressed whether the anti-SLAPP statute permitted striking a single cause of action while preserving other causes of action in the same complaint. The court held that the statute allows partial relief at the cause-of-action level—defendants may move to strike some claims while others survive. (231 Cal.App.4th at 955-956.) This holding directly undermines the plaintiff's argument that the anti-SLAPP motion must target the complaint as a whole. If partial relief is available at the cause-of-action level, there is no logical reason to deny partial relief within a cause of action when the allegations supporting different theories of recovery are separable.

Similarly, in *Villalpando*, the Court of Appeal affirmed the denial of an anti-SLAPP motion as to one cause of action while reversing the denial as to another. (229 Cal.App.4th at 300-301.) This too demonstrates that the anti-SLAPP statute does not require all-or-nothing relief. The plaintiff's attempt to distinguish these cases on the ground that they involved "different counts" rather than "allegations within a single count" is unavailing. The principle underlying those decisions—that the anti-SLAPP statute permits targeted relief—is equally applicable here.

The plaintiff notes that the *Bronson* court stated that "the anti-SLAPP statute does not permit a defendant to strike only part of a cause of action." (231 Cal.App.4th at 956.) But this statement must be read in context. The *Bronson* court was addressing whether a defendant could strike a portion of a cause of action while preserving a different legal theory within the same count. The court concluded that because the plaintiff had demonstrated a probability of prevailing on the entire cause of action, the motion was properly denied. The court did not address whether allegations within a cause of action that arise from protected activity can be struck while allegations arising from unprotected activity survive.

More importantly, the plaintiff cites no binding authority from this Court establishing that partial striking is prohibited. The Court of Appeal cases on which the plaintiff relies are divided on the issue, as the preliminary ruling acknowledges. The preliminary ruling correctly resolved this split by interpreting the statute according to its text and purpose.

### V. THE PLAINTIFF'S CHARACTERIZATION OF THE CLAIMS IS INACCURATE

The plaintiff contends that the Moss Adams allegations and the sale allegations are "interwoven" and cannot be separated. This characterization is factually incorrect and legally irrelevant.

The second amended complaint alleges two distinct courses of conduct. First, the plaintiff alleges that the defendant secretly negotiated the sale of IQ to Livelt, excluding the plaintiff from the transaction and usurping his ownership interests. This is classic breach of fiduciary duty—unprotected business conduct. Second, the plaintiff alleges that the defendant commissioned the Moss Adams audit, controlled its scope, provided false information, and prevented the plaintiff from participating. This is conduct undertaken in anticipation of litigation—a classic exercise of the right to petition.

These are not "interwoven factual strands." They are separate acts occurring at different times, involving different parties, and giving rise to different legal theories. The sale allegations involve the defendant's negotiations with Livelt and his self-dealing in the transaction. The Moss Adams allegations involve the defendant's communications with an accounting firm in connection with a prelitigation investigation. The only connection is that both occurred during the same general period of discord between the parties.

The plaintiff argues that "if the audit allegations were stricken, the cause of action would lack any factual basis." This is nonsense. The breach of fiduciary duty claim is supported by the sale allegations—the defendant's negotiation of the sale, his representation that he was the sole member and manager, and his exclusion of the plaintiff from the transaction. The Moss Adams allegations are additional factual support, not the sole basis for the claim. Even if the Moss Adams allegations were stricken, the plaintiff would still have a claim for breach of fiduciary duty based on the sale.

### VI. THE STATUTE'S PURPOSE SUPPORTS PARTIAL STRIKING

The plaintiff argues that partial striking is unnecessary because the statute already protects defendants from "bundling." But the plaintiff's argument assumes that the current rule adequately protects defendants—which it does not.

Under the Court of Appeal's rule, if a cause of action includes any allegation of unprotected activity, the defendant cannot move to strike the protected allegations. The plaintiff can survive an anti-SLAPP motion by showing a probability of prevailing on the unprotected portion of the claim—even if the protected portion is utterly without merit. This creates a perverse incentive for plaintiffs to embed claims arising from protected activity within causes of action that also include unrelated misconduct, thereby shielding the protected activity from anti-SLAPP scrutiny.

The preliminary ruling correctly identified this problem: "If plaintiffs could evade anti-SLAPP protection by embedding claims arising from protected activity within causes of action that also include allegations of unprotected misconduct, the statute's purpose would be substantially undermined." The plaintiff's argument that the current rule adequately protects defendants ignores this fundamental flaw.

The plaintiff argues that partial striking would "create new avenues for delay and harassment." But the opposite is true. Under the defense position, defendants can move to strike only the claims arising from protected activity, leaving the plaintiff free to pursue claims based on unprotected conduct in the ordinary course of litigation. This is more efficient than requiring defendants to wait for full discovery on all claims before moving to strike the entire cause of action.

### VII. CONCLUSION

The plaintiff has not identified any flaw in the preliminary ruling's statutory analysis or demonstrated that partial striking is impractical or contrary to precedent. The defense position is straightforward: the anti-SLAPP statute applies to claims founded on protected activity, and defendants may move to strike those claims even when they are alleged alongside unprotected activity. This interpretation honors the statute's text, advances its purpose, and is consistent with the way courts already analyze anti-SLAPP motions.

The Court should affirm the preliminary ruling, reverse the Court of Appeal judgment, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue presented for decision is whether California’s anti-SLAPP statute, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, permits a defendant to move to strike only those allegations within a pleaded cause of action that arise from protected speech or petitioning activity, while preserving claims based on unprotected conduct. The Court of Appeal held that an anti-SLAPP motion must be brought against a mixed cause of action in its entirety and affirmed the denial of the motion. Having reviewed the rebuttal arguments of both parties, we reaffirm our preliminary conclusion: the judgment of the Court of Appeal must be reversed.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Statutory Interpretation: "Cause of Action" vs. "Claim"**

The Prosecution argues that "cause of action" is a term of art referring strictly to a pleaded count, and therefore cannot be dissected. They contend that the statute’s text provides no warrant for targeting allegations within a count. We find this formalistic reading unpersuasive in light of the statute’s broad purpose.

As the Defense correctly notes, the anti-SLAPP statute applies to causes of action "arising from" protected activity. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).) The phrase "arising from" directs the inquiry to the factual basis of the plaintiff’s claim—whether the claim is *founded on* the defendant’s protected conduct. The Legislature’s use of "cause of action" in this context targets the legal claim for relief that is rooted in protected activity, not merely the procedural label attached to it in the complaint.

The Prosecution’s rigid interpretation—that a cause of action is a "unified framework" that cannot be divided—conflates pleading form with substantive rights. As we stated in the preliminary ruling, "Section 425.16 is not concerned with how a complaint is framed, or how the primary right theory might define a cause of action." To hold that a plaintiff can immunize protected activity from scrutiny simply by pleading it alongside unprotected allegations would allow the artful pleader to defeat the statute’s purpose. The Legislature could not have intended such an absurd result.

**B. The "Procedural Chaos" Argument is Unpersuasive**

The Prosecution predicts that allowing partial striking will create a "procedural minefield" of multiple motions and fragmented discovery. This speculation is overstated and ignores the realities of anti-SLAPP jurisprudence.

Courts are already required to parse allegations to determine if a cause of action "arises from" protected activity for the threshold prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis. As cited by the Defense, *Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 61, holds that a court must "examine the allegations underlying each claim" to determine if the statute applies. If a court can distinguish between protected and unprotected allegations for the threshold inquiry, it is equally capable of doing so to strike the protected allegations while leaving the unprotected ones intact.

The Prosecution’s hypothetical of a cause of action with 20 interwoven allegations is a straw man. In cases like the one at bar, the protected and unprotected conduct often involves distinct courses of action. The procedural burden of severing distinct claims is far preferable to the substantive injustice of allowing a plaintiff to bypass the anti-SLAPP statute through "bundling."

**C. Precedent and the "All-or-Nothing" Rule**

The Prosecution relies on *Bronson v. Wachovia Bank, N.A.* (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 944 and *Villalpando v. Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk* (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 292 to argue that partial striking is prohibited. We are not convinced.

While *Bronson* states that "the anti-SLAPP statute does not permit a defendant to strike only part of a cause of action" (231 Cal.App.4th at 956), this statement must be read in context. The *Bronson* court was addressing a situation where the plaintiff had demonstrated a probability of prevailing on the entire cause of action. It did not address the scenario presented here: whether a plaintiff can avoid the statute by combining a meritless protected claim with a meritorious unprotected one.

Furthermore, *Bronson* and *Villalpando* acknowledge that partial relief is available at the cause-of-action level—defendants may move to strike some counts while others survive. (See *Bronson*, supra, 231 Cal.App.4th at 955-956; *Villalpando*, supra, 229 Cal.App.4th at 300-301.) The principle that the statute permits targeted relief logically extends to the "mixed cause of action" context. The rule advocated by the Prosecution—that the presence of any unprotected allegation immunizes the entire count—creates a perverse incentive for plaintiffs to draft complaints in a manner that evades the statute’s protections.

**D. Application to the Present Case**

The Prosecution contends that the Moss Adams audit allegations and the sale of IQ allegations are "interwoven" and inseparable. We disagree.

The second amended complaint alleges two distinct courses of conduct. First, it alleges that Schnitt secretly negotiated the sale of IQ to Livelt, excluding Baral and usurping his ownership interests. This is a classic breach of fiduciary duty based on unprotected business conduct. Second, it alleges that Schnitt commissioned the Moss Adams audit, controlled its scope, and provided false information. This is conduct undertaken in anticipation of litigation—a classic exercise of the right to petition.

These are not "interwoven factual strands" that cannot be disentangled. The sale of the company and the subsequent audit are separate acts involving different parties and different objectives. The breach of fiduciary duty claim is fully supported by the sale allegations alone. Even if the Moss Adams allegations were stricken, the plaintiff would still have a viable claim based on the secret sale. Therefore, severing the protected allegations would not destroy the coherence of the remaining claim.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution’s arguments for procedural efficiency cannot override the clear legislative intent behind the anti-SLAPP statute. The statute was designed to provide a quick and inexpensive mechanism to defeat meritless lawsuits arising from protected activity. Allowing plaintiffs to embed protected claims within mixed causes of action to evade the statute would undermine this purpose.

We hold that a defendant may move to strike those allegations within a cause of action that arise from protected activity, even if the cause of action also includes allegations of unprotected conduct. The trial court should evaluate whether Mr. Baral can demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the claims arising from the protected Moss Adams activity.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether California’s anti-SLAPP statute, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, permits a defendant to move to strike only those allegations within a pleaded cause of action that arise from protected speech or petitioning activity, while preserving claims based on unprotected conduct. The Court of Appeal held that an anti-SLAPP motion must be brought against a mixed cause of action in its entirety and affirmed the denial of the motion. Having reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties, we reaffirm our conclusion that the judgment of the Court of Appeal must be reversed.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Statutory Interpretation: "Cause of Action" and the "Arising From" Requirement**

The Defense argues persuasively that the term "cause of action" in section 425.16(b)(1) must be interpreted in light of the statute’s broad purpose to protect constitutional rights, rather than as a rigid procedural label. The statute applies to causes of action "arising from" protected activity. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).) As the Defense notes, the phrase "arising from" directs the inquiry to the factual basis of the plaintiff’s claim—whether the claim is *founded on* the defendant’s protected conduct.

The opposing view, that a cause of action is a "unified framework" that cannot be dissected, conflates pleading form with substantive rights. Section 425.16 is not concerned with how a complaint is framed, but with the substance of the conduct alleged. To hold that a plaintiff can immunize protected activity from scrutiny simply by pleading it alongside unprotected allegations would allow the artful pleader to defeat the statute’s purpose. The Defense illustrates this absurdity with the hypothetical of a plaintiff adding a single, unrelated allegation of misconduct—such as "jaywalking"—to a claim otherwise built on protected speech. Under the "unified framework" theory, the entire claim would be immune from scrutiny. The Legislature could not have intended such a result.

**B. The "Procedural Chaos" Argument is Unpersuasive**

The argument that allowing partial striking will create a "procedural minefield" of multiple motions and fragmented discovery is speculative and ignores the realities of anti-SLAPP jurisprudence. Courts are already required to parse allegations to determine if a cause of action "arises from" protected activity for the threshold prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis.

As cited by the Defense, *Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 61, holds that a court must "examine the allegations underlying each claim" to determine if the statute applies. If a court can distinguish between protected and unprotected allegations for the threshold inquiry, it is equally capable of doing so to strike the protected allegations while leaving the unprotected ones intact. The procedural burden of severing distinct claims is far preferable to the substantive injustice of allowing a plaintiff to bypass the anti-SLAPP statute through "bundling."

**C. Precedent and the "All-or-Nothing" Rule**

The Defense correctly addresses the reliance on *Bronson v. Wachovia Bank, N.A.* (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 944 and *Villalpando v. Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk* (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 292. While *Bronson* states that "the anti-SLAPP statute does not permit a defendant to strike only part of a cause of action" (231 Cal.App.4th at p. 956), this statement must be read in context. The *Bronson* court was addressing a situation where the plaintiff had demonstrated a probability of prevailing on the entire cause of action. It did not address the scenario presented here: whether a plaintiff can avoid the statute by combining a meritless protected claim with a meritorious unprotected one.

Furthermore, *Bronson* and *Villalpando* acknowledge that partial relief is available at the cause-of-action level—defendants may move to strike some counts while others survive. (See *Bronson*, supra, 231 Cal.App.4th at pp. 955-956; *Villalpando*, supra, 229 Cal.App.4th at pp. 300-301.) The principle that the statute permits targeted relief logically extends to the "mixed cause of action" context. The rule advocated by the opposing party—that the presence of any unprotected allegation immunizes the entire count—creates a perverse incentive for plaintiffs to draft complaints in a manner that evades the statute’s protections.

**D. Application to the Present Case**

The Defense contends that the Moss Adams audit allegations and the sale of IQ allegations are distinct and separable. We agree.

The second amended complaint alleges two distinct courses of conduct. First, it alleges that Schnitt secretly negotiated the sale of IQ to Livelt, excluding Baral and usurping his ownership interests. This is a classic breach of fiduciary duty based on unprotected business conduct. Second, it alleges that Schnitt commissioned the Moss Adams audit, controlled its scope, and provided false information. This is conduct undertaken in anticipation of litigation—a classic exercise of the right to petition.

These are not "interwoven factual strands" that cannot be disentangled. The sale of the company and the subsequent audit are separate acts involving different parties and different objectives. The breach of fiduciary duty claim is fully supported by the sale allegations alone. Even if the Moss Adams allegations were stricken, the plaintiff would still have a viable claim based on the secret sale. Therefore, severing the protected allegations would not destroy the coherence of the remaining claim.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The arguments for procedural efficiency cannot override the clear legislative intent behind the anti-SLAPP statute. The statute was designed to provide a quick and inexpensive mechanism to defeat meritless lawsuits arising from protected activity. Allowing plaintiffs to embed protected claims within mixed causes of action to evade the statute would undermine this purpose.

We hold that a defendant may move to strike those allegations within a cause of action that arise from protected activity, even if the cause of action also includes allegations of unprotected conduct. The trial court should evaluate whether Mr. Baral can demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the claims arising from the protected Moss Adams activity.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
