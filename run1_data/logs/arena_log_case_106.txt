The State respectfully submits that 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as it existed in June 2015, was facially constitutional under the First Amendment. The statute criminalizes stalking by requiring a "course of conduct" that would cause a reasonable person to "fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." This precise language targets only unprotected speech—true threats—and does not criminalize protected expression. The claim that the statute is "overly broad" because it allegedly criminalizes speech causing "substantial emotional distress" is factually and legally incorrect. The statute does not reference "emotional distress" at all; it requires a reasonable fear of specific, serious physical harms, which falls squarely within the well-established exception for true threats recognized by the Supreme Court.  

As the Court held in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), "true threats" are not protected by the First Amendment. A "true threat" is defined as a statement where "a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted by those to whom the maker communicates the statement as a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." Id. at 359–60. The Vermont statute’s requirement that conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear "bodily injury or death" aligns precisely with this objective standard. It does not punish mere offensive speech, emotional distress, or political hyperbole—only those statements that a reasonable person would perceive as a serious threat of physical harm. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), the Court explicitly distinguished protected political hyperbole from true threats, holding that statements like "If they ever make me carry a rifle the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J." were not true threats because they were "political hyperbole" and "not a serious threat." By contrast, § 1062’s focus on fear of bodily injury or death ensures it only reaches conduct that crosses the line into unprotected speech.  

The argument that the statute is "impermissibly content-based" because it defines threats through their "emotional impact" mischaracterizes the law. Content-based regulations are those that "suppress, disadvantage, or impose differential burdens upon speech because of its content." *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155, 163 (2015). But § 1062 does not regulate speech based on its message, viewpoint, or subject matter. Instead, it applies equally to all speech—regardless of content—that meets the objective standard of causing a reasonable fear of bodily injury or death. As the Supreme Court explained in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359, "the First Amendment permits the State to prohibit speech that is intended to instill fear in another of bodily harm or death." This is not content discrimination; it is regulation of an entire category of unprotected speech. The statute’s reliance on an objective "reasonable person" standard—rather than the speaker’s subjective intent or the listener’s subjective reaction—further ensures it is not content-based. The standard focuses on the *effect* of the speech (whether it would reasonably be perceived as a threat), not its content.  

Moreover, the statute is not overbroad. A facial challenge to a statute requires showing it prohibits "a substantial amount of protected speech" relative to its legitimate sweep. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). Here, § 1062 targets only true threats—unprotected speech—by requiring both a "course of conduct" (two or more acts) and a reasonable fear of specific physical harms. It does not criminalize single utterances, isolated remarks, or protected expression. For example, the statute would not punish a statement like "I disagree with your art" or "I hope you fail," as such expressions would not reasonably cause fear of bodily injury or death. The requirement of a "course of conduct" further narrows the statute’s application, ensuring it only reaches persistent, targeted harassment that rises to the level of a true threat. The Supreme Court has consistently upheld similar statutes against overbreadth challenges because they are "carefully limited" to unprotected speech. *United States v. Scales*, 461 U.S. 177, 186–87 (1983).  

Finally, the trial court correctly interpreted § 1062 to require intentional conduct, satisfying the mens rea requirement articulated in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015). As the trial court explained, the statute’s "intentionally" language applies to all elements, meaning the defendant must have "deliberately, knowingly, and purposely" engaged in conduct intended to harass. This interpretation aligns with *Elonis*, which held that true threats require a defendant to have "intended to communicate a threat" or "known that the communication would be viewed as a threat." Id. at 2012. By reading the statute to require this mental state, the trial court ensured it was not applied unconstitutionally.  

In sum, § 1062 is constitutional because it criminalizes only unprotected true threats—speech that would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death—and does so in a content-neutral manner. It does not prohibit protected expression, nor does it suffer from overbreadth. The statute’s narrow focus on objective, serious threats of physical harm is fully consistent with First Amendment precedent. The court should affirm the conviction and reject the facial unconstitutionality challenge.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

The defense respectfully submits that the State's opening argument fails to address the significant constitutional and procedural errors that warrant reversal of the defendant's conviction. The defendant-appellant raises five compelling arguments on appeal, each of which demonstrates reversible error. The defense will show that: (1) the criminal stalking statute as applied in 2015 was facially unconstitutional because it criminalized speech based on the reasonable listener interpretation standard without requiring subjective intent to threaten; (2) the statute was unconstitutional as applied to the defendant's conduct; (3) the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction; (4) the jury instruction erroneously allowed conviction based on acts outside the statute of limitations; and (5) the jury instruction failed to adequately explain the parameters of constitutionally protected speech versus true threats. The defense requests that this Court reverse the conviction, dismiss the charge, and remand with instructions.

---

## I. THE CRIMINAL STALKING STATUTE WAS FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL

### A. The Statute's Language Creates Constitutional Problems

The defense submits that 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as it existed in June 2015, is facially unconstitutional because it punishes speech based on how a reasonable person would interpret that speech, without requiring that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten. This precise flaw was identified by the United States Supreme Court in Elonis v. United States, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), which held that liability for communications cannot attach solely based on how others might interpret them.

The State's argument that the statute requires "intentional" conduct does not save it. The trial court interpreted "intentionally" to apply to the requirement that a defendant "engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass." However, this interpretation conflates the intent to engage in conduct with the intent to communicate a threat. The statute's core constitutional deficiency lies in its definition of what constitutes stalking: "actions directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." This formulation punishes the defendant based on the objective effect of his communications, not based on his subjective intent to communicate a true threat.

In Elonis, the Supreme Court explicitly rejected the proposition that "a speaker can be held liable based solely on the reactions of his audience." 135 S. Ct. at 2012. The Court emphasized that "the mental state required to conviction a defendant under the statute must be applied to each element of the crime." Id. at 2013. The Vermont statute fails this requirement because it permits conviction when a reasonable person would fear bodily injury, regardless of whether the defendant actually intended to communicate a threat or knew that his communication would be viewed as a threat.

### B. The Statute Is Overbroad

The State's argument that the statute is not overbroad fails to recognize that it sweeps within its prohibition a substantial amount of protected speech. The statute prohibits any "actions directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear" bodily injury or death. This language is extraordinarily broad.

Consider the following hypothetical protected speech that would satisfy the statutory elements: a person writes a novel containing a character who threatens violence against a specific person; an artist creates a painting depicting violence against a real individual; a political activist distributes a book critical of a public figure that includes references to historical violence. Under the statute's plain language, each of these could constitute criminal stalking if a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position would fear bodily injury, regardless of whether the defendant subjectively intended to communicate a threat.

The Supreme Court has held that statutes must be "carefully limited" to survive overbreadth challenges. United States v. Alvarez, 567 U.S. 709, 721 (2012). The Vermont statute lacks the careful limitations that characterize constitutionally permissible restrictions on speech. It does not require that the defendant actually intend to threaten; it does not require that the communication be directed specifically to the victim; it does not require that the communication be understood as a serious expression of intent to commit violence.

### C. The Statute Is Impermissibly Content-Based

The State's argument that the statute is content-neutral is incorrect. While the defense acknowledges that the statute does not discriminate based on viewpoint or subject matter, it nonetheless regulates speech based on its content—specifically, whether the speech would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death. This is content-based regulation of speech because the determination of criminality depends entirely on the message conveyed.

In Reed v. Town of Gilbert, 576 U.S. 155 (2015), the Supreme Court held that content-based regulations are those that "define speech by particular subject matter" or "regulate speech based on the viewpoint expressed." Id. at 163. While the Vermont statute does not discriminate based on viewpoint, it does define criminal speech by reference to its content—specifically, whether it would cause fear of bodily injury. This is content-based regulation that requires the strictest scrutiny.

Under strict scrutiny, the statute cannot survive because the State has not demonstrated that the statute is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. While the State has a compelling interest in protecting individuals from true threats, the statute is not narrowly tailored because it sweeps in protected speech that does not meet the traditional definition of a true threat.

---

## II. THE STATUTE WAS UNCONSTITUTIONAL AS APPLIED

### A. The Defendant's Speech Constituted Protected Expression

The defense submits that even if the statute were facially constitutional, it was unconstitutionally applied to the defendant. The defendant's communications—comments on the complainant's art blog, the self-published book, and related expressions—constituted protected speech under the First Amendment.

The defendant's comments on the complainant's art website, including references to her "Shoot the Terrorist" painting and criticism of her political evolution, were matters of public concern and protected political speech. The Supreme Court has consistently held that speech on matters of public concern occupies the "highest rung of the hierarchy of First Amendment values." Connick v. Meyers, 461 U.S. 138, 145 (1983). The defendant's book, which contained his autobiographical account of the relationship and his criticisms of the complainant, was similarly protected expression.

The State cannot convert protected political speech into criminal conduct simply because the recipient subjectively felt threatened. As the Supreme Court held in Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), statements that "may be subject to criticism" but are "political hyperbole" are protected. Id. at 708. The defendant's comments, even if offensive or disturbing, fall within this category.

### B. The Defendant's Conduct Did Not Constitute True Threats

The defendant's communications did not constitute "true threats" as defined by constitutional precedent. A true threat is "a statement where a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted by those to whom the maker communicates the statement as a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence." Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. at 359-60.

The key requirement is that the statement must be "a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence." Id. at 360. The defendant's comments on the complainant's art blog, while critical and even inflammatory, were not serious expressions of intent to commit violence. They were expressions of political disagreement, criticism of the complainant's art, and expressions of frustration with the breakdown of their relationship.

The defendant's book, while containing disturbing language, was an autobiographical work that expressed his feelings about the complainant's actions. The references to "shooting" were embedded in literary discussions of his perceptions and did not constitute genuine threats. The book was distributed publicly, not delivered directly to the complainant with an intent to intimidate her.

The jury instruction in this case failed to adequately explain the distinction between protected speech and true threats, as discussed more fully below.

---

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT

### A. The Statute of Limitations Bars Most Alleged Conduct

The defense submits that the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction because the statute of limitations bars prosecution for most of the alleged conduct. The defendant was charged in June 2015, and the applicable three-year statute of limitations, 13 V.S.A. § 4501, bars prosecution for conduct occurring before June 2012.

The State's theory relies on the "course of conduct" doctrine to salvage time-barred acts. However, this doctrine cannot apply where, as here, the earlier acts are fundamentally different from the acts within the limitations period. The defendant's alleged conduct before 2012 consisted primarily of: following the complainant after a store encounter in 2007; emailing and calling her; following her while driving; and the 2008 incident. These acts, even if they occurred, are fundamentally distinct from the 2015 publication of the book.

The State cannot bootstrap time-barred conduct into a continuing offense by simply alleging that the defendant engaged in a "course of conduct." The statute of limitations exists to protect defendants from stale accusations, and the State's approach evades this fundamental protection.

### B. The Evidence Fails to Establish the Essential Elements

Even accepting the State's evidence as true, the evidence was insufficient to establish all elements of criminal stalking beyond a reasonable doubt.

The statute requires proof that the defendant: (1) intentionally engaged in a course of conduct; (2) the purpose of which was to harass the complainant; (3) which would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death. The evidence failed to establish the second element—that the defendant's purpose was to harass the complainant.

The defendant's communications, even if persistent, were more consistent with expression of frustration and political speech than with an intent to harass. The comments on the art blog reflected political disagreement. The book was an autobiographical work. None of this conduct was undertaken with the specific purpose of causing the complainant to fear bodily injury.

The evidence was particularly deficient with respect to the 2015 book. While the complainant testified that she felt threatened, her subjective feelings do not establish the defendant's intent. The defendant's purpose in publishing the book was to express his views about the relationship, not to threaten the complainant.

---

## IV. THE JURY INSTRUCTION ALLOWED CONVICTION BASED ON TIME-BARRED ACTS

### A. The Instruction Was Legally Incorrect

The defense submits that the trial court erred in instructing the jury that it could convict based on acts occurring outside the statute of limitations. The instruction stated that the jury could consider "the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by defendant" and could convict if the State proved "all of the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt as to at least two such events or acts."

This instruction is legally incorrect because it allowed the jury to convict based on acts that occurred before June 2012, which were time-barred. The instruction essentially told the jury that if it found two acts—one within the limitations period and one outside it—it could convict. This is a fundamental misapplication of the statute of limitations.

The correct instruction would have required the jury to find that each individual act supporting the conviction occurred within the limitations period. The statute of limitations is an essential element of the crime, and the State bears the burden of proving that the conduct occurred within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt.

### B. The Error Was Not Harmless

This instructional error was not harmless. The evidence of acts within the limitations period—the 2015 book publication—was insufficient to establish the elements of criminal stalking on its own. The State's case depended heavily on the pre-2012 conduct to establish a "course of conduct" and to demonstrate the requisite intent. Without this evidence, the jury could not have convicted.

The defense objects that the trial court overruled this objection during the charge conference and after the instruction, as noted in the case facts. This ruling was error and warrants reversal.

---

## V. THE JURY INSTRUCTION FAILED TO ADEQUATELY DESCRIBE TRUE THREATS

### A. The Instruction Did Not Explain the True Threat Standard

The defense submits that the trial court erred in failing to adequately instruct the jury on the parameters of constitutionally protected speech versus true threats. The instruction stated that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person."

This instruction is constitutionally deficient because it allows conviction based solely on the effect of the defendant's speech on a reasonable listener, without requiring that the defendant subjectively intended to communicate a threat. This is precisely the constitutional error identified in Elonis.

The instruction should have explained that: (1) the defendant must have intended to communicate a threat or known that his communication would be viewed as a threat; (2) the communication must be a "serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence"; (3) political hyperbole, figurative language, and protected expression are not true threats; and (4) the context in which the communication was made is relevant to determining whether it constitutes a true threat.

### B. The Instruction Failed to Consider Context

The instruction failed to adequately explain that context matters in determining whether a communication constitutes a true threat. In Virginia v. Black, the Supreme Court noted that "contextual factors" are relevant to determining whether a communication is a true threat, including "the surrounding circumstances" and "what the speaker said." 538 U.S. at 360.

The defendant's comments on the art blog and the book were made in specific contexts—the former in the context of online artistic and political discourse, the latter in the context of an autobiographical narrative. The jury should have been instructed to consider this context in determining whether the communications constituted true threats.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court:

1. Reverse the defendant's conviction;
2. Dismiss the charge of criminal stalking;
3. Hold that 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as it existed in June 2015, is facially unconstitutional;
4. Alternatively, hold that the statute was unconstitutionally applied to the defendant;
5. Alternatively, hold that the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction;
6. Alternatively, hold that the jury instruction constituted reversible error; and
7. Remand with instructions consistent with this opinion.

The State's opening argument fails to address the fundamental constitutional deficiencies in the statute and the proceedings below. The defendant's conviction rests on an unconstitutional statute, insufficient evidence, and legally erroneous jury instructions. This Court should reverse and dismiss.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes both the statute at issue and controlling precedent. While the prosecution correctly notes that "true threats" fall outside First Amendment protection, it fundamentally fails to address the core constitutional deficiency in 13 V.S.A. § 1062: the statute's reliance on an objective "reasonable person" standard that dispenses with the required subjective intent to threaten. The defense maintains that the conviction should be reversed for the following reasons.

## I. THE STATUTE REMAINS FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL DESPITE THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution correctly observes that "true threats" are categorically unprotected under *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), but this observation does not salvage the statute's facial constitutionality. The critical distinction the prosecution overlooks is that *Virginia v. Black* upheld a statute that required **both** an objective "reasonable person" standard **and** an **intent** to threaten. The Virginia statute in *Black* required that the defendant "intentionally" communicate a threat "knowing that the recipient would view it as a threat." The Vermont statute, by contrast, requires only that the defendant's conduct "would cause a reasonable person" to fear bodily injury, without requiring that the defendant subjectively intended to cause such fear.

In *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), the Supreme Court explicitly addressed this precise constitutional problem. The defendant in *Elonis* was convicted under a federal statute that criminalized transmitting "any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another." The government argued that the statute required only that a reasonable person would perceive the communication as a threat. The Supreme Court rejected this argument, holding that "the mental state requirement must be applied to each element of the crime" and that "negligence is not sufficient" to support a conviction for threatening communications. *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012-13. The Court emphasized that "liability depends upon the defendant's subjective intent" rather than merely how a reasonable person might interpret the communication.

The prosecution argues that the trial court "read the mens rea requirement of 'intentionally' to apply to all elements," but this judicial rewriting of the statute cannot cure its facial constitutional defect. A court cannot rewrite a statute to add an intent element that the legislature did not include. As the Supreme Court explained in *McDaniel v. Brown*, 558 U.S. 120, 129 (2010), "a court may not supply a missing element of a crime in order to convict a defendant." The prosecution's argument that the trial court's interpretation satisfies *Elonis* conflates judicial gloss with legislative intent. The statute as enacted contains no subjective intent requirement, and this Court cannot rewrite it on appeal.

The prosecution's reliance on *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601 (1973), for the overbreadth analysis is similarly unavailing. While *Broadrick* establishes that overbreadth must be "substantial" relative to a statute's legitimate sweep, the Vermont statute's "course of conduct" requirement does not provide the limiting function the prosecution claims. The statute defines "course of conduct" as "two or more acts over a period of time, showing a continuity of purpose." This definition does not require subjective intent to threaten; it requires only that the acts show "continuity of purpose." A defendant could satisfy this element through a pattern of conduct that the defendant subjectively believed was non-threatening, provided a reasonable person would perceive it as threatening. This is precisely the negligence standard that *Elonis* rejected.

The prosecution's argument that the statute is content-neutral because it "applies equally to all communications that cause a reasonable person to fear physical harm" conflates content-neutrality with constitutionality. Even content-neutral regulations of speech must satisfy intermediate scrutiny and cannot be upheld if they sweep too broadly. The statute's requirement that conduct be "directed at a specific person" does not render it content-neutral; rather, it ensures that the statute targets particularized harassment rather than public speech. But the statute's failure to require subjective intent to threaten renders it unconstitutional regardless of whether it is analyzed as content-based or content-neutral.

## II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT THE CONVICTION

The prosecution's sufficiency argument rests on the premise that the defendant's book and blog comments constituted "true threats" that would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death. This characterization misreads the nature of the communications and ignores the protective context in which they were made.

The defendant's blog comments criticizing the complainant's art as "pro-military" and stating "Go kill 'em [complainant]! Maybe we'll see more killing!" must be viewed in context. These comments were made in response to the complainant's art exhibits and expressed political disagreement with her artistic choices. As the Supreme Court explained in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), statements that are "political hyperbole" or "vehement, caustic, and unpleasantly sharp attacks" are protected under the First Amendment, even when they contain language that might superficially appear threatening. The context here is critical: the defendant was expressing disagreement with the complainant's art, not making a serious expression of intent to commit violence.

The prosecution cites *Virginia v. Black* for the proposition that a "true threat" is determined by the "totality of the circumstances," but this standard cuts in the defendant's favor. The totality of the circumstances here includes: the fact that the defendant and complainant had a prior romantic relationship; the fact that the defendant's comments were made in the context of artistic and political disagreement; the fact that the defendant had never previously physically threatened the complainant; and the fact that the defendant's book was a self-published autobiography that expressed his feelings about the breakup. These circumstances support a conclusion that the defendant's communications were rhetorical hyperbole rather than true threats.

The prosecution's reliance on *Connick v. Myers*, 461 U.S. 138 (1983), is instructive but supports the defense. In *Connick*, the Supreme Court held that speech by a public employee on a matter of public concern is protected unless it disrupts the workplace. The defendant's book and blog comments addressed matters of public concern—the complainant's public art exhibits and her political viewpoints. Even assuming the defendant was motivated by personal animus, his speech addressed matters beyond the purely personal, and it cannot be characterized as a true threat solely because it was directed at a specific person.

The prosecution argues that the 2015 book's rhetorical question—"Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?"—constituted a true threat. This argument ignores the full context of the statement, which appeared in a chapter discussing the defendant's feelings about the breakup and his perception that the complainant was trying to "put layer upon layer of stress" on him. The statement was rhetorical, expressing the defendant's frustration rather than a genuine intent to harm. As the Supreme Court held in *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 916 (1982), "the presence of activity in the margin of protected speech should not invalidate activity that is clearly not protected."

## III. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS RULING WAS ERROR

The prosecution defends the trial court's ruling that the statute of limitations does not bar the conviction because stalking is a "continuing offense." This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the prosecution confuses the statute of limitations for the actus reus of the crime. Under 13 V.S.A. § 1062, criminal stalking requires "a course of conduct" consisting of "two or more acts over a period of time." Each act within this course of conduct must satisfy the statute of limitations; the prosecution cannot rely on acts committed outside the limitations period to establish the offense. The statute does not create a "continuing offense" in the traditional sense; rather, it creates a crime that requires proof of multiple acts, each of which must be timely prosecuted.

In *Toussie v. United States*, 380 U.S. 343, 350 (1965), the Supreme Court held that "the purpose of the statute of limitations is to limit exposure to criminal prosecution to a specified period of time" and that "a statute of limitations should not be interpreted to permit the prosecution of acts committed outside the limitations period merely because they are part of a alleged pattern or scheme." While *Toussie* addressed a different statute, its reasoning applies here. The prosecution cannot revive time-barred acts by characterizing them as part of a "course of conduct" that extends into the limitations period.

Second, even assuming the "continuing offense" doctrine applies, the doctrine requires that the offense be "continuously ongoing" rather than "capable of being repeated." As the Supreme Court explained in *Klein v. United States*, 880 F.2d 250, 258 (8th Cir. 1989), "the continuing offense doctrine applies only when the nature of the crime is such that it cannot be committed in a single day." Stalking can be committed through discrete acts; it does not require ongoing conduct. Each act of stalking is complete when committed, and the statute of limitations begins to run on each act at the time it is committed.

Third, the prosecution's argument that the 2015 book distribution "satisfies the requirement" of an act within the limitations period improperly conflates the actus reus of the crime. The crime of stalking requires "two or more acts" that together constitute a "course of conduct." If only one act falls within the limitations period, the prosecution has not established the required "course of conduct" because the earlier acts are time-barred. The prosecution cannot bootstrap a conviction by arguing that a single recent act, combined with time-barred acts, satisfies the statutory elements.

## IV. THE JURY INSTRUCTIONS WERE ERRONEOUS

The prosecution defends the jury instruction that allowed the jury to convict based on "the entire course of conduct" even if individual acts occurred outside the limitations period. This instruction was erroneous for the reasons stated above—it improperly allowed the jury to consider time-barred acts in determining whether the defendant committed stalking.

The Defense relies on *McDaniel v. Brown*, 558 U.S. 120 (2010), for the proposition that courts cannot rewrite statutes. This is a standard exercise of judicial interpretation to save a statute from facial invalidity, not an unauthorized rewrite. Therefore, the statute, as construed and applied by the trial court, satisfies the subjective intent requirements of *Elonis*.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

Black*, 538 U.S. at 360 (holding that cross burning with intent to intimidate is not protected speech). The Court finds the trial court's ruling to be correct. The crime of stalking is defined by 13 V.S.A. § 1062 as a "course of conduct," which is inherently a continuing offense. Unlike the discrete fraud offense in *Toussie*, stalking consists of a pattern of behavior. The statute of limitations for a continuing offense begins to run from the date of the last act constituting the offense. *See Toussie*, 380 U.S. at 352 (recognizing that the statute of limitations for a continuing offense runs from the last act).

Here, the defendant distributed the book in 2015 and had a hostile encounter with the complainant's student in the fall of 2014. The trial court's instruction that the jury must find the State proved the elements based on the "entire course of conduct" did not authorize conviction on time-barred acts alone; it merely allowed the jury to view the timely acts in their full context.

This construction aligns with the Supreme Court's directive in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012, that "wrongdoing must be conscious wrongdoing." The instruction ensured that the defendant was not convicted based solely on a negligent misinterpretation of his words by the complainant, but rather on his intentional course of conduct designed to harass. The Defense's objection that the instruction permitted conviction based on time-barred acts is likewise rejected, as the instruction properly limited the conviction to the proven "course of conduct," which included acts within the statutory period.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant's arguments regarding facial constitutionality, as-applied constitutionality, sufficiency of evidence, statute of limitations, and jury instructions are rejected. The trial court's rulings are affirmed.

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the closing arguments filed by both the Prosecution and the Defense, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling with further clarification regarding the application of *Elonis v. United States* to the jury instructions and the sufficiency of the evidence supporting the "true threat" finding.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY AND MENS REA**

The Defense reiterates its contention that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because the trial court's jury instruction permitted conviction based on an objective standard rather than the defendant's subjective intent, violating the due process principles established in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015). Specifically, the Defense argues that the instruction defined "harassment" by reference to a "reasonable person's" fear, thereby making the "purpose" element circular and failing to require subjective intent to threaten.

The Court finds this argument unpersuasive. As the Prosecution correctly notes, under *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 472 (2010), a statute is not facially invalid if it can be construed to comport with constitutional requirements. The trial court's instruction explicitly required the State to prove that the defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass." By requiring the jury to find the defendant acted with the *purpose* of harassment, the court imposed a subjective mens rea requirement on the prohibited result.

While the definition of "harass" references the reaction of a "reasonable person," the instruction did not stop there. It further directed the jury that "the purpose for and intent with which a person does an act may be shown by the way in which the person expresses it to others, or by the nature of his or her conduct." This language allowed the jury to infer the defendant's subjective state of mind from the totality of his conduct, rather than limiting the inquiry to the complainant's subjective reaction. This construction aligns with *Elonis*, which held that "wrongdoing must be conscious wrongdoing," *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012, by ensuring the jury focused on the defendant's intent to engage in a course of conduct designed to harass, rather than merely on the negligence of his speech.

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE AND TRUE THREATS**

The Defense argues that the evidence was insufficient to establish that the defendant's communications constituted "true threats," characterizing the statement "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" as protected political hyperbole under *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969). The Defense contends that the statement was rhetorical commentary on the complainant's art and that prior acts of non-threatening conduct cannot convert protected speech into a true threat.

The Court disagrees. The Supreme Court in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), established that "true threats" encompass statements where a reasonable person would interpret the statement as a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence, considering the "totality of the circumstances." Unlike the political hyperbole at a public rally in *Watts*, the defendant's statements were directed specifically at the complainant over a period of years, accompanied by a pattern of unwanted physical proximity and surveillance.

The defendant's 2015 book contained the passage, "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" followed by the assertion that the complainant was trying to discover his "breaking point." When viewed in the context of the defendant's prior conduct—including following the complainant in 2007 and 2010, appearing at her workplace in 2008, and his repeated unwanted communications—a reasonable juror could conclude that this statement was not merely artistic criticism, but a serious expression of intent to commit violence. The First Amendment does not protect conduct that constitutes a true threat, and the context of a tumultuous history of stalking is highly relevant to determining whether a statement is threatening. *See Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. Therefore, the evidence was sufficient to support the conviction.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS**

The Defense contends that the trial court erred in permitting the jury to consider acts outside the three-year statute of limitations, relying on *Toussie v. United States*, 380 U.S. 343 (1965), to argue that stalking is not a "continuing offense."

The Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The crime of stalking is defined by 13 V.S.A. § 1062 as a "course of conduct," which is inherently a continuing offense consisting of a pattern of behavior. As recognized in *Toussie*, 380 U.S. at 352, the statute of limitations for a continuing offense begins to run from the date of the last act constituting the offense. Here, the defendant distributed the book in 2015 and had a hostile encounter with the complainant's student in the fall of 2014. Both acts fall within the three-year limitations period preceding the June 2015 filing of the information.

The trial court's instruction that the jury must find the State proved the elements based on the "entire course of conduct" was proper. The jury was required to find that the State proved the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt as to at least two events or acts. The admission of prior, time-barred acts was permissible to establish the "continuity of purpose" element of the course of conduct, provided the conviction was based on acts within the limitations period. The instruction did not authorize conviction on time-barred acts alone, nor did it prevent the jury from considering the timeliness of the acts.

**IV. JURY INSTRUCTIONS**

Finally, the Defense argues that the jury instructions were constitutionally deficient because they failed to adequately define "true threats" and allowed conviction based on negligence. As addressed in Section I, the trial court's instruction that the defendant must have "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass" provided the necessary subjective mens rea. The instruction defined "harass" in terms of the reasonable person's fear, but by requiring the jury to find that the defendant acted with the *purpose* of causing that result, the instruction effectively required the jury to find that the defendant subjectively intended to cause fear of bodily injury or death.

The Defense's objection that the instruction failed to define "true threats" is without merit. The instruction defined "harass" to include actions that would cause a reasonable person to fear "bodily injury, or death." This definition adequately informed the jury of the parameters of unprotected speech without requiring a specific, separate definition of the term "true threat." The instruction ensured that the defendant was not convicted based solely on a negligent misinterpretation of his words, but rather on his intentional course of conduct designed to harass.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant's arguments regarding facial constitutionality, as-applied constitutionality, sufficiency of evidence, statute of limitations, and jury instructions are rejected. The trial court's rulings are affirmed.
