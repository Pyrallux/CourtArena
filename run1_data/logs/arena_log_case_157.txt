COURT ARENA LOG - Case 157
FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") (together, "defendants") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering ("IPO") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the "shares").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") listed on the New York Stock Exchange ("NYSE"). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission ("SEC") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer "in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed." App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that "[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends." App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, "[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote." App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that "[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws." App. 23. The prospectus further stated that "any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. ("Repsol") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the "governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights." Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A "classic example" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a "taking" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) ("It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights."). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) ("States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ...."); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., "Indirect Expropriation" and the "Right to Regulate" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the "General Expropriation Law." App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress "shall particularly refer to specific property" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that "[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved." App. 185-86. The law further declares that "[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...." App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive "the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation," such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that "[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator." App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an "Intervenor" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as "fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law." App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as "unfair" and a "bear trap." Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the "YPF Expropriation Law"). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply," App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating "public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons," App. 166. 
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to "exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

IV. Procedural History

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint, arguing, inter alia , that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. Â§ 1601 et seq. (the "FSIA"), and that Petersen's claims were barred by the "act of state doctrine." As is relevant here, the district court denied defendants' motions to dismiss with respect to the FSIA and act of state issues. They timely appealed the FSIA ruling and the district court subsequently certified the act of state issue for our interlocutory review.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, this Court faces a straightforward question: whether the district court correctly denied dismissal of plaintiffs’ breach of contract claims against Argentina and YPF under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) and the act of state doctrine. The answer is unequivocally yes. Petersen Energía’s claims arise solely from commercial contractual obligations—provisions in YPF’s bylaws that were integral to a public offering on the New York Stock Exchange—and thus fall squarely within the FSIA’s commercial activity exception. Crucially, these claims do not implicate sovereign acts but instead concern private, commercial conduct with direct, tangible effects in the United States. The act of state doctrine is inapplicable because it cannot shield breaches of private contractual agreements.  

### I. The Claims Are Rooted in Commercial Contractual Obligations, Triggering the FSIA’s Commercial Activity Exception  
The FSIA bars foreign sovereign immunity for claims "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act outside the United States in connection with commercial activity that has a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Here, Argentina’s actions—and YPF’s complicity—unquestionably fall within this exception.  

The bylaws at issue were not sovereign decrees but private contractual terms embedded in a U.S. capital markets transaction. YPF’s prospectus, filed with the SEC for its 1993 IPO, explicitly described these provisions as investor protections designed to attract U.S. capital. It stated that "[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws," and that any failure to comply would "result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." These terms were not imposed by Argentina as a sovereign; they were negotiated and publicly disclosed as part of a commercial offering to U.S. investors.  

When Argentina expropriated 51% of YPF’s shares in 2012 under Law 26,741, it did so without making a tender offer, as required by the bylaws. The Deputy Economy Minister openly declared that Argentina would not comply, calling the tender offer provisions "fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law" and dismissing them as "unfair" and a "bear trap." Argentina’s refusal to honor these contractual obligations was not a sovereign act—it was a commercial breach. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), "a foreign state engages in commercial activity when it carries on a commercial activity in the United States or when it engages in a commercial activity outside the United States that has a direct effect in the United States." In *Weltover*, Argentina’s issuance of bonds to U.S. investors was deemed commercial because it was "a transaction of a type that private parties might enter into," and its breach triggered the FSIA exception. The same logic applies here: Argentina’s failure to execute a tender offer—contractual terms expressly designed to protect U.S. investors—was a commercial act indistinguishable from the bond issuance in *Weltover*.  

Moreover, the breach had a direct effect in the United States. YPF’s refusal to enforce the bylaws’ penalty provisions—specifically, section 7(h), which mandates that shares acquired in breach "shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends"—enabled Argentina to cancel dividend payments owed to U.S.-based investors and precipitated Petersen’s default on loans secured by ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This foreclosure of ADRs in New York is a paradigmatic "direct effect" under the FSIA. See *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983) (holding that "a direct effect in the United States exists when the consequences of the foreign state’s conduct are felt within the United States"). The cancellation of dividends to U.S. investors and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York City are not speculative or attenuated; they are concrete, commercial consequences of Argentina’s breach, directly impacting U.S. markets and investors.  

As for YPF, it is a private corporation—not a foreign state—and thus not entitled to FSIA immunity at all. Its role in enabling Argentina’s breach—by refusing to withhold voting rights and canceling dividends—constitutes a straightforward commercial contract violation, properly subject to judicial review.  

### II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply Because This Is a Private Contractual Dispute, Not a Challenge to Sovereign Acts  
The act of state doctrine bars courts from questioning the validity of a foreign sovereign’s official acts within its own territory. See *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). But it has never been invoked to shield breaches of private contractual obligations. The doctrine applies only when a court must "pass judgment on the validity of the sovereign act of a foreign nation." Id. Here, Petersen does not challenge the legality of Argentina’s expropriation; it asserts a claim for breach of contract under private bylaws that existed *before* the expropriation.  

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the act of state doctrine does not extend to commercial contracts. In *OBB Personschaftsverband v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015), the Court clarified that the doctrine is "confined to situations where the validity of the sovereign act of a foreign nation within its own territory is directly in question." This case involves no such question. Petersen’s claim arises from YPF’s bylaws—a private agreement entered into during a commercial IPO—and concerns whether Argentina and YPF violated their contractual duties. As the district court correctly recognized, the expropriation itself is irrelevant to the breach of contract claim; the bylaws’ terms were binding contractual obligations that survived the expropriation.  

The act of state doctrine is a prudential rule of judicial restraint, not a jurisdictional bar. It cannot be used to dismiss claims based on commercial conduct. Here, the bylaws were created as part of a U.S.-listed securities offering, and their enforcement would not require the Court to "question the validity" of Argentina’s sovereign acts—it would merely require the Court to interpret and enforce a private contract. As the Second Circuit has held, "the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions or private contractual disputes." *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006).  

### Conclusion  
This case is not about whether Argentina’s expropriation was lawful—it is about whether Argentina and YPF breached their contractual promises to U.S. investors. The bylaws were commercial terms in a U.S. securities offering, and Argentina’s refusal to honor them caused direct harm in New York. Under *Weltover* and *First National City Bank*, this is a classic commercial activity exception to FSIA immunity. And under *Sabbatino* and *OBB*, the act of state doctrine cannot be weaponized to dismiss a private contractual dispute. The district court’s denial of dismissal was entirely correct. We ask this Court to affirm that ruling and let this breach of contract claim proceed to trial.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

We respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss under the FSIA and dismiss the claims against Argentina, and that we dismiss the appeal as to the act of state doctrine only to the extent the district court addresses the merits of that doctrine on remand.

---

## I. ARGENTINA IS ENTITLED TO SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY; THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

The FSIA provides that "a foreign state shall be immune from the jurisdiction of the courts of the United States" unless one of the statutory exceptions applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The burden rests on the plaintiff to establish that an exception applies. *See Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp.*, 488 U.S. 428, 432 (1989). The commercial activity exception, upon which Petersen relies, provides immunity shall not apply in any case:

in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or based upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere that has a direct effect in the United States.

28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

Critically, "commercial activity" is defined as "either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The Supreme Court has instructed that the inquiry is whether the foreign state is "engaged in a project of the same general nature" as a private party would undertake, rather than a sovereign function. *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). Expropriation is quintessentially a sovereign function—it is the power by which a sovereign state takes private property for public use, with compensation. *See id.* at 618 (distinguishing sovereign acts from commercial acts).

### B. Argentina's Acts Were Sovereign Expropriation, Not Commercial Activity

The heart of Petersen's claim is that Argentina failed to comply with tender offer obligations when it expropriated 51% of YPF's shares from Repsol in 2012. But the expropriation itself was a sovereign act—enacted through Law 26,741, the YPF Expropriation Law, passed by the Argentine Congress pursuant to Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution, and effectuated through the General Expropriation Law. This is not a case where a foreign sovereign entered the marketplace as a private participant. Argentina exercised its eminent domain power—the most sovereign of powers—to take property for public use.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Weltover* is instructive. In *Weltover*, the Court held that Argentina's issuance of bonds was commercial activity because it was "a transaction of a type that private parties might enter into." 504 U.S. at 618. But the Court explicitly distinguished sovereign acts like expropriation, stating that "the FSIA's immunity clause would not apply" to such sovereign takings. *Id.* at 618 n.5. Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares is precisely the type of sovereign act the FSIA was designed to protect against—conduct that private parties simply cannot engage in.

Petersen's attempt to recast this as a "commercial" dispute fails because it asks this Court to look behind the sovereign act. The tender offer obligations in YPF's bylaws, Petersen argues, were "private contractual terms" that survived the expropriation. But this framing ignores that section 28(A) of the bylaws—by its own terms—applies only to acquisitions by "the Government of Argentina." The provision was designed to govern sovereign conduct. It cannot be severed from the sovereign act it was meant to regulate. When Argentina expropriated shares, it did so as a sovereign exercising its eminent domain power—not as a private acquirer in the market.

As this Court held in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 595 (2d Cir. 2006), "the critical question is whether the specific act giving rise to the alleged liability is an act that a private party could perform, or whether it is an act that can only be performed by the sovereign." Expropriation can only be performed by the sovereign. Therefore, the commercial activity exception does not apply.

### C. The "Direct Effect" Analysis Does Not Alter This Conclusion

Petersen argues that the breach had "direct effects" in the United States—specifically, the cancellation of dividends and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York. But this analysis puts the cart before the horse. The FSIA requires that the action be "based upon" commercial activity. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The "based upon" requirement demands a direct causal link between the commercial activity and the claim. *See id.* at 618 (quoting H.R. Rep. No. 94-1487, at 17 (1976)).

Here, the claim is "based upon" Argentina's expropriation—a sovereign act. The downstream effects on dividends and ADRs are incidental to the sovereign taking, not the basis for the claim. The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that any effect in the United States triggers the exception. In *Amerada Hess*, the Court held that the FSIA "does not provide federal courts jurisdiction over all violations of international law" but only over the specific acts enumerated in § 1605. 488 U.S. at 438. Similarly, the "direct effect" branch of the commercial activity exception requires that the commercial act itself have a direct effect—not merely that the sovereign act underlying the claim has consequences in the United States.

Petersen's reliance on *First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611 (1983), is misplaced. In that case, the Court held that a foreign sovereign's commercial activity (issuing a letter of credit) had a direct effect in the United States. But the Court emphasized that the "act" at issue was the commercial act of issuing credit—not a sovereign taking. *Id.* at 620. Here, by contrast, the act giving rise to the claim is the sovereign expropriation. The "direct effect" analysis therefore does not bring this case within the commercial activity exception.

### D. YPF Is Entitled to Immunity as an Instrumentality of a Foreign State

YPF, while a private corporation, is an instrumentality of the Argentine Republic. Under the FSIA, "a political subdivision of a foreign state" or "an agency or instrumentality of a foreign state" is included within the definition of "foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(a). YPF is majority-owned by Argentina and operates as Argentina's national petroleum company. To the extent Petersen seeks to hold YPF liable for acts taken in its capacity as an instrumentality of the sovereign, YPF is entitled to the same immunity as Argentina. *See Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 769 (2019) (holding that agencies and instrumentalities of foreign states are immune under the FSIA).

---

## II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS ADJUDICATION OF THESE CLAIMS

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

The act of state doctrine provides that "the courts of this country will not examine the validity of a taking of property by a foreign sovereign within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). The doctrine reflects "the broad principle of international law that sovereigns do not review the acts of other sovereigns performed within their own territory." *Id.* at 422. As the Supreme Court has explained, the doctrine applies whenever "the validity of the sovereign act of a foreign nation within its own territory is directly in question." *OBB Personverkehrsverband v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015).

### B. This Case Requires Adjudication of the Validity of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

Petersen's breach of contract claim cannot be adjudicated without examining whether Argentina's expropriation was valid under Argentine law. The YPF Expropriation Law declared a public interest in the expropriation and authorized the taking of 51% of YPF shares from Repsol. To determine whether Argentina breached its contractual obligations under the bylaws, this Court would need to examine:

1. Whether the expropriation was properly authorized under Argentine law;
2. Whether Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares triggered the tender offer provisions;
3. Whether the penalties in section 7(h) apply to shares acquired through a lawful expropriation.

Each of these inquiries requires this Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts—the very essence of what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The district court erred in concluding that the doctrine does not apply because the bylaws are "private" contractual provisions. This reasoning conflates the nature of the instrument with the nature of the claim. The bylaws may have been drafted as private contractual terms, but they were designed to govern acquisitions by the Government of Argentina—the sovereign. Section 28(A) specifically provides that its terms apply to "all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument." The provision contemplates sovereign acquisitions. Enforcement of these provisions would require this Court to determine whether Argentina's acquisition was subject to the bylaws—and that determination necessarily involves examining the sovereign act of expropriation.

As this Court held in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d at 598, "the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions or private contractual disputes" where the claim does not require examination of the sovereign act. But where, as here, the claim cannot be resolved without examining the validity of a sovereign taking, the doctrine applies. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428.

### C. The Doctrine Applies Regardless of Whether Petersen Challenges the Expropriation's Legality

The district court reasoned that the act of state doctrine does not apply because Petersen does not challenge the "validity" of the expropriation. This reasoning is flawed. The doctrine applies not only when a plaintiff explicitly challenges the validity of a sovereign act, but also when adjudication of the claim requires the court to "pass judgment" on that act. *See Sachs*, 577 U.S. at 335.

Here, even if Petersen accepts the expropriation as valid, adjudication of the breach of contract claim would require this Court to interpret Argentine law to determine whether the tender offer provisions apply to the expropriation. This interpretation necessarily involves examining the sovereign act. The doctrine therefore applies.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The appeal before the Court concerns the denial of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF")'s motions to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction based on foreign sovereign immunity and Rule 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunity**

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. See Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp., 488 U.S. 428, 433 (1989). Under the FSIA, a foreign state is presumptively immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless a specific exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. Defendants argue that the district court erred in finding that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies to this case. We disagree.

The commercial activity exception strips immunity of a foreign state with respect to claims "based upon . . . an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere that has a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has held that the "commercial activity" exception should be defined by reference to the "nature" of the course of conduct or the particular transaction, rather than by its "purpose." Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). Thus, the inquiry is whether the particular actions that gave rise to the claim are of a type that private parties might engage in, regardless of the sovereign's motive. Id. at 614-15.

Here, the claims are based upon Argentina's failure to comply with the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws—specifically sections 7(d), 7(f), and 28(A)—and its subsequent exercise of voting rights and cancellation of dividends in contravention of section 7(h). These bylaws were established as part of YPF's privatization and initial public offering on the New York Stock Exchange, a quintessential commercial transaction. The obligation to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest is a standard contractual provision in corporate governance, one that private parties frequently adopt and enforce. Argentina's refusal to adhere to these contractual terms, and its decision to exercise voting rights despite the explicit penalties outlined in the bylaws, constitutes conduct of a type that private parties regularly perform. See Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614 (holding that issuing bonds was commercial activity). The fact that this conduct occurred in the context of a sovereign expropriation does not transform the nature of the act from commercial to sovereign where, as here, the specific acts complained of—breaching a shareholder agreement and manipulating corporate voting rights—are fundamentally commercial in nature.

Furthermore, the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied. A "direct effect" exists when the consequence of the foreign state's conduct is felt within the United States. First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983). The record indicates that Petersen's shares were held in the form of American Depository Receipts (ADRs) by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Argentina's breach of the bylaws enabled it to cancel dividend payments, which precipitated Petersen's default on its loans and the subsequent foreclosure of these ADRs in New York. This financial impact on investors and the transfer of assets within the Southern District of New York constitute a direct effect in the United States.

Defendants argue that the claims are "based upon" the sovereign act of expropriation, not commercial activity. However, the FSIA requires the court to look to the conduct upon which the claim is based. The conduct giving rise to the liability is the breach of the bylaws and the unlawful exercise of corporate rights, not the enactment of the expropriation decree itself. See Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618. Therefore, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss on this ground.

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants also argue that the act of state doctrine bars adjudication of Petersen's claims. The act of state doctrine is a prudential doctrine that precludes courts from sitting in judgment on the acts of the government of another done within its own territory. Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). The doctrine is confined to situations where the validity of the sovereign act of a foreign nation within its own territory is directly in question. OBB Personschaftsverband v. Sachs, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015).

In this case, Petersen does not challenge the validity of Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares under Argentine law. Rather, Petersen asserts a claim for breach of contract based on pre-existing bylaws that governed the relationship between YPF and its shareholders. Determining whether Argentina breached those bylaws does not require this Court to pass judgment on the validity of the expropriation itself; it merely requires the interpretation and enforcement of private contractual obligations. As the Second Circuit has recognized, the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions or private contractual disputes. Garb v. Republic of Poland, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006). Because Petersen's claims arise from private contractual rights rather than a challenge to the sovereign act of expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar adjudication.

However, consistent with the procedural posture of this case and the district court's certification, we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the FSIA is affirmed, and the appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

### I. THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION APPLIES BECAUSE THE CLAIMS ARE BASED ON COMMERCIAL CONDUCT, NOT SOVEREIGN ACTS  

#### A. The "Nature" of the Conduct, Not the Sovereign’s Purpose, Determines Commercial Activity  
The defense incorrectly asserts that Argentina’s expropriation of YPF shares transforms this dispute into a "sovereign act" immune from suit. But the Supreme Court has unequivocally held that the FSIA’s commercial activity exception turns on the "nature" of the specific conduct at issue—not the sovereign’s purpose or motive. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). Here, the claims are based on Argentina’s failure to comply with the tender offer provisions in YPF’s bylaws (sections 7(d), 7(f), and 28(A)) and its unlawful exercise of voting rights in violation of section 7(h)—conduct that is indistinguishable from that of a private party in a corporate transaction.  

The bylaws’ tender offer requirement is a standard commercial mechanism adopted by corporations worldwide to protect minority shareholders. As the district court correctly observed, these provisions are "standard contractual provisions in corporate governance, one that private parties frequently adopt and enforce." The defense’s attempt to recast these contractual obligations as "sovereign" ignores *Weltover*, where the Court held that Argentina’s bond issuance was commercial activity because it was "a transaction of a type that private parties might enter into." *Id.* at 618. Similarly, here, the bylaws’ terms—requiring tender offers for controlling acquisitions and imposing voting restrictions for breaches—are commercial safeguards that private companies routinely incorporate into their governance structures. The fact that these provisions were triggered by Argentina’s government action does not convert them into sovereign acts; it merely reflects a contractual condition triggered by a specific event.  

#### B. The "Based Upon" Requirement Is Satisfied by Commercial Conduct, Not the Expropriation Decree  
The defense misstates the "based upon" standard. The FSIA requires the court to examine the "particular transaction or act" giving rise to the claim—not the underlying sovereign act. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the claim is "based upon" Argentina’s breach of the bylaws and YPF’s failure to enforce section 7(h)—not the expropriation decree itself. The defense’s argument conflates the *cause* of the breach (expropriation) with the *basis* of the liability (contractual noncompliance). But as *Weltover* makes clear, "the fact that a sovereign act is the context for a commercial transaction does not transform the transaction itself into a sovereign act." *Id.* at 618 n.5.  

For example, if a private company signed a contract requiring it to make a tender offer upon acquiring 51% of another company’s shares, and then refused to comply after acquiring those shares through a lawful transaction, the resulting breach would unquestionably be commercial. The same principle applies here: Argentina entered into a contractual relationship with YPF shareholders by adopting the bylaws, and its refusal to honor those terms is commercial conduct. The defense’s reliance on *Amerada Hess* is misplaced; that case held that the FSIA does not cover "violations of international law," but Petersen’s claim is a straightforward breach of contract under New York law—governed by the bylaws’ explicit terms. *Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp.*, 488 U.S. 428, 438 (1989).  

#### C. The "Direct Effect" Requirement Is Clearly Met  
The defense wrongly claims that the "direct effect" analysis is irrelevant because the claim is "based upon" sovereign conduct. But the Second Circuit has confirmed that the cancellation of dividends paid to U.S.-based ADR holders and the foreclosure of those ADRs in New York City constitute a "direct effect" in the United States. *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983) (holding that a foreign sovereign’s commercial act causing financial consequences in the U.S. satisfies the "direct effect" requirement). Here, the record is clear: Petersen’s ADRs were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City; Argentina’s breach caused the cancellation of dividend payments that would have been made to U.S. investors; and this directly precipitated Petersen’s default and the foreclosure of its ADRs in New York. These are not "incidental" consequences—they are the *core* of the breach.  

#### D. YPF Is Not Entitled to Immunity for Its Commercial Conduct  
YPF is an instrumentality of Argentina, but the FSIA does not shield it for commercial activities. *Jam v. Int’l Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 769 (2019). YPF’s role in maintaining the bylaws and enforcing corporate governance standards for its NYSE-listed ADRs was a commercial function. When YPF failed to ensure Argentina complied with the bylaws or allowed Argentina to exercise voting rights in breach of section 7(h), it acted commercially—not sovereignly.  

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY BECAUSE THE CLAIMS INVOLVE PRIVATE CONTRACTUAL DISPUTES, NOT SOVEREIGN VALIDITY  

#### A. The Doctrine Bars Adjudication Only When the Court Must Pass Judgment on a Sovereign Act  
The defense claims that adjudicating this breach of contract claim would require this Court to "pass judgment" on the validity of Argentina’s expropriation under Argentine law. But this is categorically false. As the Second Circuit held in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006), "the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions or private contractual disputes where the claim does not require examination of the sovereign act." Here, Petersen does *not* challenge the validity of Argentina’s expropriation under Argentine law. Its claim is solely whether Argentina and YPF breached their pre-existing contractual obligations under the bylaws—a question that requires only interpretation of the contract’s terms, not evaluation of the sovereign act itself.

The bylaws are private contracts governing shareholder rights, and their enforcement does not require the Court to second-guess Argentina’s exercise of eminent domain. As the district court correctly noted, "Petersen does not challenge the validity of Argentina’s expropriation of YPF shares under Argentine law. Rather, Petersen asserts a claim for breach of contract based on pre-existing bylaws that governed the relationship between YPF and its shareholders." The Court need only ask: Did Argentina violate the contractual terms it agreed to? That is a classic contract dispute, not a challenge to sovereign authority.  

#### B. *Sabbatino* and *Sachs* Confirm the Doctrine’s Inapplicability  
The defense cites *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), and *OBB Personverkehrsverband v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015), but both cases are inapposite. *Sabbatino* applies only when the "validity of the sovereign act of a foreign nation within its own territory is directly in question." Here, the question is purely contractual: whether Argentina breached its bylaws. *Sachs* reinforces this principle, holding that the act of state doctrine does not bar claims arising from "commercial transactions" or "private contractual disputes." *Id.* at 335. The defense’s attempt to stretch the doctrine to cover breach-of-contract claims is contrary to Second Circuit precedent and Supreme Court guidance.

The district court correctly denied the motions to dismiss. The claims are based on commercial contractual obligations—not sovereign acts—and the FSIA exception applies. The act of state doctrine is inapplicable because the Court need not question the validity of Argentina’s expropriation; it need only interpret and enforce a private contract. ## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of this dispute and ignores the fundamental principle that a foreign state's sovereign acts—including expropriations—are presumptively immune from suit in United States courts. While the prosecution attempts to frame this case as a simple breach of contract dispute, the underlying reality is that Petersen's claims arise directly from Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain. The prosecution cannot circumvent the FSIA and the act of state doctrine by recasting a sovereign expropriation as a commercial transaction. This Court should reverse the district court's order and dismiss the claims.

---

## II. THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

### A. The Claims Are Based Upon Sovereign Expropriation, Not Commercial Activity

The prosecution erroneously argues that the claims are "based upon" Argentina's breach of the bylaws rather than the expropriation itself. This argument fails because the very existence of the alleged breach flows directly from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriating YPF shares pursuant to Law 26,741. Without the expropriation, there would be no claim. The Supreme Court has held that where the claim is "based upon" a sovereign act, the commercial activity exception does not apply. *Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp.*, 488 U.S. 428, 438 (1989).

The prosecution's reliance on *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), is misplaced. In *Weltover*, the Court found commercial activity because the bonds were issued as part of a commercial borrowing transaction, not as an exercise of sovereign power. Here, by contrast, Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares was effectuated through a sovereign expropriation law enacted pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution—the quintessential exercise of eminent domain. This is not a transaction "of a type that private parties might enter into." *Id.* at 618. No private party can expropriate property from another without consent.

The prosecution's argument that the "nature" of the conduct determines commercial activity ignores the critical distinction between conduct that is sovereign in nature and conduct that is commercial in nature. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. Argentina's enactment of the YPF Expropriation Law and its exercise of the rights attendant to the expropriated shares are inherently sovereign acts. The fact that Argentina subsequently exercised voting rights does not transform the underlying expropriation into a commercial transaction. The chain of events begins and ends with sovereign action.

### B. The Prosecution's "Direct Effect" Argument Is Irrelevant

The prosecution's lengthy discussion of the "direct effect" requirement is beside the point. The "direct effect" analysis under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2) applies only after the court determines that the claim is "based upon" commercial activity. Because the claims are based upon sovereign expropriation—the exercise of the taking power—the court never reaches the direct effect analysis. The prosecution puts the cart before the horse.

Even if the Court reaches the direct effect analysis, the prosecution overstates the impact. The alleged financial harm to Petersen is an incidental consequence of a sovereign act, not the basis for the claim. The Supreme Court has held that the "direct effect" must be more than incidental or consequential. *See id.* at 618 (requiring "some direct effect"). The foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs occurred because Petersen overleveraged itself to acquire shares in a foreign company—a risk inherent in international investment. This does not constitute a direct effect sufficient to strip Argentina of sovereign immunity.

### C. YPF Is Entitled to Immunity as an Instrumentality of Argentina

The prosecution incorrectly argues that YPF is not entitled to sovereign immunity for its commercial activities. However, the FSIA provides immunity to both foreign states and their "instrumentalities." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(a). An "instrumentality" is a corporate entity that is a separate legal person, but the FSIA explicitly provides that an instrumentality is "deemed to be a foreign state" for purposes of the statute. 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b).

YPF's adoption of the bylaws and its corporate governance activities were undertaken in furtherance of Argentina's sovereign privatization program. The bylaws were not adopted in the ordinary course of commercial corporate governance; they were adopted as part of a sovereign restructuring of Argentina's economy. When YPF failed to prevent Argentina from exercising its sovereign rights, it was not engaging in commercial conduct—it was failing to intervene in a sovereign act. The prosecution's attempt to hold YPF liable for not preventing Argentina from exercising its eminent domain power misunderstands the nature of YPF's role as a state-owned entity.

---

## III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS ADJUDICATION

### A. The Doctrine Applies Because the Claims Challenge Argentina's Sovereign Acts

The prosecution argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because Petersen is not challenging the validity of the expropriation. This argument is frivolous. The entire premise of Petersen's claim is that Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares was subject to the tender offer requirements in the bylaws—and that Argentina's failure to comply with those requirements constitutes a breach of contract. But determining whether Argentina was required to comply with the bylaws necessarily requires this Court to evaluate whether Argentina's acquisition was a valid exercise of its sovereign expropriation power.

If this Court were to hold that Argentina breached the bylaws by failing to make a tender offer, it would effectively be holding that Argentina's sovereign expropriation was subject to the private contractual terms of YPF's bylaws. This would constitute precisely the kind of "judgment on the acts of the government of another done within its own territory" that the act of state doctrine prohibits. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964).

The prosecution's reliance on *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), is unavailing. In *Garb*, the court found the act of state doctrine inapplicable because the plaintiff did not challenge the validity of the Polish government's acts. Here, by contrast, Petersen's claim implicitly challenges the manner in which Argentina exercised its sovereign power. Petersen is not merely seeking to enforce private contractual rights; it is seeking to impose additional conditions on Argentina's exercise of eminent domain—conditions that Argentina never agreed to in its sovereign capacity.

### B. The Doctrine Bars Claims That Would Require Examination of Sovereign Acts

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Sabbatino* and *OBB Personverkehrsverband v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 327 (2015), but these cases support the defense. In *Sabbatino*, the Supreme Court held that the act of state doctrine applies "when the validity of the sovereign act of a foreign nation within its own territory is directly in question." 376 U.S. at 428. Here, the validity of Argentina's expropriation is directly in question because Petersen's claim depends on the premise that the expropriation was subject to the bylaws' tender offer requirements.

Similarly, in *Sachs*, the Supreme Court emphasized that the act of state doctrine applies when adjudication would require the court to "pass judgment on the conduct of a foreign sovereign." 577 U.S. at 335. This is exactly what Petersen asks this Court to do. Petersen asks this Court to judge Argentina's conduct in acquiring YPF shares without complying with the bylaws—to find that Argentina acted wrongfully in exercising its sovereign expropriation power. This is the core of what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The prosecution's argument that this is merely a contract dispute ignores the reality that the contract at issue—YPF's bylaws—was adopted in connection with a sovereign privatization and is now being invoked to impose conditions on a sovereign expropriation. The bylaws cannot be read in isolation from the sovereign context in which they were adopted. Argentina adopted the bylaws as part of its sovereign decision to privatize YPF; it did not agree to be bound by those bylaws when exercising its sovereign power of eminent domain.

### C. The YPF Expropriation Law Does Not Support Petersen's Claims

The prosecution argues that the YPF Expropriation Law does not prohibit a post-expropriation tender offer. But this argument misses the point. The question is not whether the law prohibits such a tender offer; the question is whether Argentina is required to make a tender offer under the bylaws. The YPF Expropriation Law says nothing about the bylaws—it addresses only the expropriation of shares from Repsol. Argentina's sovereign exercise of eminent domain is not subject to the private contractual provisions of YPF's bylaws.

If this Court were to hold that the bylaws apply to Argentina's sovereign expropriation, it would be creating a novel and untenable rule: that a foreign sovereign, by participating in a privatization, agrees to subject all future exercises of its sovereign power to the private contractual terms adopted in connection with that privatization. This would hamstring foreign sovereigns' ability to exercise their inherent sovereign powers and would discourage privatizations that benefit both the sovereign and private investors.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons set forth in the defendants-appellants' opening brief, this Court should reverse the district court's order denying the motions to dismiss. The claims are based upon sovereign acts—the expropriation of YPF shares—and are barred by both the FSIA and the act of state doctrine.

**UPDATED PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms its previous preliminary ruling. The defense's arguments, while articulating a strong position regarding the sovereign nature of expropriation, do not overcome the application of the commercial activity exception to the specific conduct at issue, nor do they establish that the act of state doctrine bars this breach of contract claim.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunity**

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. See Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp., 488 U.S. 428, 433 (1989). We reaffirm that the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

**A. The "Nature" of the Conduct is Commercial**

The defense argues that the claims are "based upon" the sovereign act of expropriation and that because expropriation is a power unique to sovereigns, the "nature" of the act is sovereign. We disagree. The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial activity" exception is to be defined by reference to the "nature" of the course of conduct or the particular transaction, rather than by its "purpose." Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992).

While the defense correctly notes that the *context* of this dispute is the expropriation of YPF shares, the specific conduct upon which Petersen's claims are based is the breach of the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws and the unlawful exercise of voting rights and cancellation of dividends in contravention of section 7(h). These acts—failing to make a tender offer, voting shares, and cancelling dividend payments—are actions "of a type that private parties might enter into." Id. at 614. Private corporations frequently adopt bylaws with tender offer requirements and penalties for non-compliance; private shareholders frequently vote shares and declare or withhold dividends. The fact that Argentina acquired the shares through a sovereign exercise of eminent domain does not transform the subsequent commercial management of those shares—or the breach of the contractual obligations governing that management—into a sovereign act. As the prosecution argues, the "nature" of the conduct at issue is the enforcement of corporate governance standards, which is fundamentally commercial.

**B. The Claims Are "Based Upon" Commercial Activity**

The defense contends that the claim is "based upon" the expropriation because, without the expropriation, there would be no breach. This "but-for" causation argument misinterprets the FSIA. In Weltover, the Court rejected the argument that the sovereign purpose of the bond issuance transformed the commercial nature of the rescheduling of payments. 504 U.S. at 618. Similarly, here, the claim is not based on the validity of the expropriation decree itself, but on the separate and distinct obligation to comply with the bylaws upon acquiring a controlling interest. The "particular transaction" giving rise to the claim is the breach of the bylaws, not the enactment of Law 26,741. See id. at 618. Therefore, the "based upon" requirement is satisfied.

**C. Direct Effect in the United States**

The defense argues that the "direct effect" analysis is irrelevant because the claim is based on sovereign activity. Having determined that the claim is based on commercial activity, we proceed to the "direct effect" requirement. The defense further asserts that the financial harm to Petersen was incidental. We disagree. The record shows that Petersen's shares were held in the form of American Depository Receipts (ADRs) by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Argentina's breach of the bylaws enabled it to cancel dividend payments, which directly precipitated Petersen's default on its loans and the subsequent foreclosure of these ADRs in New York. This constitutes a direct effect within the meaning of the FSIA. See First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983).

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

The defense renews its argument that the act of state doctrine bars adjudication because enforcing the bylaws would require this Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's expropriation. We are not persuaded.

The act of state doctrine is a prudential doctrine that precludes courts from sitting in judgment on the acts of the government of another done within its own territory. Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). However, the doctrine is confined to situations where the validity of the sovereign act is directly in question. OBB Personenverkehr v. Sachs, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015).

Petersen does not challenge the validity of Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares under Argentine law. The claim is strictly one for breach of contract based on pre-existing bylaws that governed the relationship between YPF and its shareholders. As the Second Circuit held in Garb v. Republic of Poland, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006), the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions or private contractual disputes where the claim does not require examination of the sovereign act. Determining whether Argentina breached the bylaws does not require this Court to invalidate the expropriation; it merely requires interpretation of the contractual terms that Argentina voluntarily agreed to when it privatized YPF and listed its shares on the NYSE. The defense's argument that enforcing the bylaws effectively imposes conditions on Argentina's sovereign power is a policy argument for the legislature, not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the FSIA is affirmed, and the appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**SO ORDERED.**

## CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR PLAINTIFF/PETITIONER

Your Honors, this case is not about the validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation. It is not about whether Argentina has the right to take property in service of national interest—that question is settled under Argentine law. This case is about a simple, straightforward breach of contract. Argentina and YPF voluntarily entered into commercial agreements with investors, including Petersen, when they privatized YPF and listed its shares on the New York Stock Exchange. They promised specific protections in those agreements, and then they broke those promises. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) and the act of state doctrine do not shield them from accountability for these commercial violations.  

### I. THE CLAIMS ARE BASED ON COMMERCIAL CONDUCT, NOT SOVEREIGN ACTIVITY  

The defense insists this is a "sovereign expropriation" case. That is false. The Supreme Court has made clear that the FSIA's commercial activity exception turns on the "nature" of the conduct, not its purpose or context. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). Here, Petersen's claims arise from Argentina's and YPF's breach of specific contractual obligations: failing to make a tender offer after acquiring control of YPF, refusing to enforce penalties for non-compliance, and unlawfully exercising voting rights. These are not sovereign acts—they are commercial acts that private parties routinely perform.  

Consider the core allegations:  
- Argentina ignored the bylaws' tender offer requirement when it acquired control of YPF.  
- YPF failed to ensure Argentina complied with its contractual obligations.  
- Both defendants permitted Argentina to vote shares and cancel dividends in violation of section 7(h) of the bylaws.  

None of these actions are unique to sovereigns. Private corporations adopt tender offer provisions to protect minority shareholders. Private shareholders vote shares and declare dividends. Private companies enforce contractual penalties for non-compliance. As this Court recognized in *Weltover*, when a government "issues bonds, reschedules payments, or engages in other transactions of a type that private parties might enter into," it is acting commercially—even if the broader context involves sovereign policy goals. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. Here, Argentina and YPF did not merely "expropriate"—they breached pre-existing commercial contracts they had voluntarily created.  

The defense's "but-for" argument—that there would be no claim without the expropriation—is legally irrelevant. *Weltover* squarely rejected this reasoning. In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court held that even if a sovereign act (bond issuance) preceded the breach, the "particular transaction" giving rise to the claim was the commercial rescheduling of payments, not the sovereign act itself. *Id.* at 618. The same logic applies here: the claim is based on Argentina's failure to follow the tender offer procedures and its unlawful exercise of voting rights—commercial acts that occurred *after* the expropriation decree. The YPF Expropriation Law itself says nothing about the bylaws or tender offers. It only addresses the expropriation of Repsol's shares. The breach occurred when Argentina, as a shareholder, ignored the contractual terms it had agreed to when privatizing YPF.  

### II. THE DIRECT EFFECT REQUIREMENT IS SATISFIED  

The defense claims the "direct effect" analysis is irrelevant because the claims are "sovereign." But as this Court rightly held, the FSIA's commercial activity exception applies here, making the direct effect analysis essential. *First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983). Petersen's ADRs were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Argentina's breach directly caused the cancellation of dividend payments that Petersen relied on to meet loan obligations. This led to the foreclosure of those ADRs in New York. This is not "incidental" harm—it is a direct, substantial effect within the United States. The Supreme Court has held that "direct effect" exists when the breach "impacts U.S. commerce in a concrete way." *Id.* at 620. Here, the foreclosure of NYSE-listed securities in New York is precisely the type of direct effect that strips Argentina of sovereign immunity.  

### III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY  

The defense argues that enforcing the bylaws would require this Court to "pass judgment on the validity" of Argentina's expropriation. That is not the claim. Petersen is not challenging Argentina's right to expropriate property under Argentine law. It is not arguing that the expropriation was invalid or that Argentina must compensate Repsol differently. It is simply seeking to enforce the contractual obligations Argentina and YPF voluntarily assumed when they invited global investors—like Petersen—to participate in YPF's privatization.  

The act of state doctrine applies only when a court must "invalidate the sovereign act of a foreign nation." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). But as the Second Circuit held in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006), the doctrine "does not bar claims that arise from commercial transactions or private contractual disputes where the validity of the sovereign act is not at issue." Here, the claim is purely contractual: Did Argentina and YPF breach their own bylaws? That question does not require this Court to examine whether Argentina's expropriation was lawful under Argentine law. It requires only interpretation of the contractual terms that governed shareholder rights after the expropriation.  

The defense's argument—that enforcing the bylaws would "impose conditions on Argentina's sovereign power"—is a policy argument, not a legal one. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, the act of state doctrine is not a "shield for sovereign misconduct" in commercial contexts. *OBB Personenverkehr v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015). Petersen is not seeking to invalidate Argentina's sovereign act. It is seeking to hold Argentina and YPF accountable for breaking promises they made to investors in the commercial marketplace.  

### CONCLUSION  

Argentina and YPF did not just "expropriate"—they also promised investors protections in their bylaws. They then ignored those promises, causing real harm to Petersen in New York. The FSIA's commercial activity exception applies because the claims are based on commercial conduct: tender offers, voting rights, and dividend payments—actions private parties routinely perform. The direct effect requirement is satisfied because the foreclosure of NYSE-listed securities occurred in New York. And the act of state doctrine does not apply because this case is about enforcing contractual obligations, not questioning the validity of Argentina's sovereign act.  

This Court should affirm the district court's denial of dismissal. To do otherwise would send a dangerous message: that foreign sovereigns can hide behind immunity when they break commercial contracts they voluntarily entered into. Investors would lose confidence in the stability of international markets. The rule of law demands accountability for breaches of commercial agreements—no matter who signs them.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENDANTS-APPELLANTS

## ARGUMENT SUMMARY

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits that the plaintiff-appellee's closing argument, while artfully crafted, fails to overcome the fundamental reality that this case arises from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation—a classic exercise of eminent domain that is shielded by both the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine. The plaintiff seeks to transform a straightforward governmental taking into a commercial dispute by focusing on the aftermath of the expropriation while ignoring the sovereign act that made that aftermath possible. This Court should reverse the district court's order and grant the defendants' motions to dismiss.

---

## I. THE CLAIMS ARISE FROM SOVEREIGN EXPROPRIATION, NOT COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY

The plaintiff attempts to cabin this dispute to the "commercial" realm by focusing on Argentina's post-expropriation conduct—its failure to make a tender offer, its exercise of voting rights, and its cancellation of dividends. This argument ignores the fundamental truth that but for the sovereign act of expropriation, none of this conduct would have occurred. The plaintiff cannot escape the reality that Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares was accomplished through Law 26,741, a sovereign act of eminent domain enacted pursuant to Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law.

The defense acknowledges that the Supreme Court in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), instructed courts to examine the "nature" of the conduct rather than its "purpose" when determining whether the commercial activity exception applies. However, the defense submits that the Court in *Weltover* did not contemplate a situation where the entirety of the plaintiff's claim flows directly from a sovereign act of expropriation. In *Weltover*, the sovereign had issued bonds—a commercial instrument—and the dispute centered on the rescheduling of payments on those bonds. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff does not hold a commercial instrument issued by Argentina; rather, its injury flows directly from Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain.

The plaintiff argues that "private parties might enter into" the conduct at issue—tender offers, voting shares, and declaring dividends. This observation, while technically accurate, proves too much. The question under the FSIA is not whether private parties could theoretically engage in similar conduct, but whether the specific conduct at issue is of a type that private parties *did* engage in in the particular circumstances of this case. Argentina did not acquire 51% of YPF through a private tender offer or arm's-length transaction; it acquired those shares through a sovereign act of expropriation effectuated by statute. The subsequent management of those expropriated shares, while perhaps "commercial" in character, cannot be divorced from the sovereign act that gave Argentina those shares in the first instance.

The plaintiff's reliance on the "but-for" causation argument is equally unavailing. While the defense agrees that but-for causation is not the sole test under the FSIA, the Supreme Court in *Waltover* did not hold that causation is irrelevant. The Court held that the claim must be "based upon" the commercial activity, not merely "connected to" a sovereign act. Here, the plaintiff's claim is fundamentally intertwined with the sovereign act of expropriation. The tender offer provisions in the bylaws were triggered *only* because Argentina exercised its sovereign power to take the shares. Without the expropriation, there would be no breach. The claim is therefore "based upon" the sovereign act.

---

## II. THE DIRECT EFFECT REQUIREMENT IS NOT SATISFIED

Even if this Court were to find that the claims are "based upon" commercial activity, the plaintiff has not demonstrated the requisite "direct effect" in the United States to invoke the commercial activity exception.

The plaintiff argues that the foreclosure of its ADRs in New York constitutes direct effect. However, this harm was not the direct result of any commercial activity by Argentina; it was the indirect result of Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The plaintiff entered into loan agreements with private lenders and pledged its YPF shares as collateral. When Argentina expropriated the shares from Repsol, the value of the plaintiff's collateral plummeted, leading to the plaintiff's default and subsequent foreclosure. This chain of causation is too attenuated to satisfy the "direct effect" requirement.

The Supreme Court has held that "direct effect" requires that the sovereign act "immediately affect" a United States person or entity. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the plaintiff's harm was not immediate; it was mediated through multiple intervening factors—the plaintiff's own financial decisions, the actions of private lenders, and the functioning of private markets. The fact that the plaintiff's ADRs were held in New York does not transform this indirect harm into a direct effect.

Furthermore, the plaintiff's argument that the cancellation of dividend payments constitutes direct effect is similarly flawed. The dividend payments were cancelled because Argentina exercised its ownership rights over the expropriated shares—a right that flows from the sovereign act of expropriation. The plaintiff, as a minority shareholder, has no right to compel Argentina to pay dividends on shares that Argentina owns through a sovereign taking. Any harm from the cancellation of dividends is a consequence of the expropriation, not a separate commercial activity.

---

## III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS CLAIM

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory are not subject to review by the courts of another country." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). This doctrine reflects "the mutual respect for the sovereign authority of independent nations" and "the importance of preserving the freedom of foreign sovereigns to act without fear of costly litigation." *Id.* at 423-24.

The plaintiff argues that this case involves only a breach of contract claim that does not require this Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's expropriation. This argument mischaracterizes the nature of the claim. The plaintiff seeks to enforce the bylaws' tender offer requirements against Argentina—requirements that would effectively impose conditions on Argentina's sovereign exercise of eminent domain. If this Court were to order Argentina to comply with the tender offer provisions, it would be telling Argentina that it cannot exercise its sovereign power of expropriation except on terms dictated by this Court. This is precisely the kind of interference with sovereign authority that the act of state doctrine was designed to prevent.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), is misplaced. In *Garb*, the Second Circuit held that the act of state doctrine did not apply where the plaintiff sought to enforce a contract that was "wholly independent" of the sovereign act at issue. Here, by contrast, the tender offer provisions in the bylaws were specifically designed to address the scenario where Argentina would attempt to renationalize YPF. These provisions are not independent of the sovereign act of expropriation; they were created *in response to* the possibility of such an act. Enforcing these provisions would require this Court to judge the adequacy of Argentina's compensation under its own laws—a task expressly prohibited by the act of state doctrine.

The plaintiff argues that it is not challenging the validity of the expropriation. But this argument cannot be sustained. The plaintiff seeks to impose obligations on Argentina that would not exist but for the expropriation. By requiring Argentina to make a tender offer, the plaintiff is effectively challenging the terms on which Argentina acquired the shares. If the expropriation is valid, Argentina acquired the shares as owner, and the bylaws' tender offer requirements do not apply. If the plaintiff can circumvent the expropriation by enforcing the bylaws, then the practical effect is to invalidate the expropriation. This is exactly what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

---

## IV. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO THE PLAINTIFF'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

### A. The "Nature" of the Conduct

The plaintiff argues that Argentina's failure to make a tender offer, its exercise of voting rights, and its cancellation of dividends are "commercial" acts because private parties engage in similar conduct. This argument conflates the nature of the act with the identity of the actor. While private parties may engage in tender offers and dividend payments, Argentina engaged in these acts as part of its sovereign exercise of eminent domain. The context in which Argentina acted is determinative. Argentina did not acquire the shares through a commercial transaction; it acquired them through a sovereign taking. The subsequent management of those shares, including the exercise of voting rights and the decision whether to pay dividends, flows from Argentina's status as the owner of the expropriated shares. This is not commercial activity; it is the exercise of ownership rights that flow from sovereign authority.

### B. The "But-For" Argument

The plaintiff argues that the "but-for" causation argument was rejected in *Waltover*. This is a misreading of *Waltover*. In *Waltover*, the Court held that the mere fact that a sovereign act preceded the commercial activity at issue does not immunize the commercial activity. However, the Court did not hold that causation is irrelevant. The Court simply held that the claim must be "based upon" the commercial activity, not merely "connected to" a sovereign act. Here, the claim is not merely connected to the expropriation; it is fundamentally dependent on it. Without the expropriation, there would be no breach of the tender offer provisions. The claim is therefore "based upon" the sovereign act.

### C. The "Direct Effect" Argument

The plaintiff argues that the foreclosure of its ADRs in New York constitutes direct effect. This argument fails for the reasons stated above. The foreclosure was an indirect consequence of the sovereign act of expropriation, mediated through the plaintiff's own financial decisions and the actions of private lenders. The fact that the ADRs were held in New York does not transform this indirect harm into a direct effect within the meaning of the FSIA.

## V. CONCLUSION

Your Honors, this case presents a fundamental question: whether a foreign sovereign can be haled into United States court for breach of contract when the alleged breach arises from the sovereign's exercise of eminent domain. The defense submits that the answer is no. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act was enacted to protect foreign sovereigns from precisely this kind of litigation. The act of state doctrine was developed to prevent courts from sitting in judgment on the acts of foreign governments within their own territories.

The plaintiff has dressed this case in commercial clothing, but it cannot hide the fact that its claims arise from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. Argentina exercised a power that is fundamental to state sovereignty—the power to take private property for public use with just compensation. The plaintiff may disagree with the terms of the expropriation, but that disagreement must be resolved in Argentine courts, not in the courts of the United States.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order and grant the defendants' motions to dismiss.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court's order denying the defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). The appeal regarding the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunity**

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. See Argentine Republic v. Amerada Hess Shipping Corp., 488 U.S. 428, 433 (1989). We agree with the district court that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies in this case.

**A. The "Nature" of the Conduct is Commercial**

The defense argues that the claims are "based upon" the sovereign act of expropriation and that because expropriation is a power unique to sovereigns, the "nature" of the act is sovereign. We disagree. The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial activity" exception is to be defined by reference to the "nature" of the course of conduct or the particular transaction, rather than by its "purpose." Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992).

While the defense correctly notes that the *context* of this dispute is the expropriation of YPF shares, the specific conduct upon which Petersen's claims are based is the breach of the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws and the unlawful exercise of voting rights and cancellation of dividends in contravention of section 7(h). These acts—failing to make a tender offer, voting shares, and cancelling dividend payments—are actions "of a type that private parties might enter into." Id. at 614. Private corporations frequently adopt bylaws with tender offer requirements and penalties for non-compliance; private shareholders frequently vote shares and declare or withhold dividends. The fact that Argentina acquired the shares through a sovereign exercise of eminent domain does not transform the subsequent commercial management of those shares—or the breach of the contractual obligations governing that management—into a sovereign act. As the prosecution argues, the "nature" of the conduct at issue is the enforcement of corporate governance standards, which is fundamentally commercial.

**B. The Claims Are "Based Upon" Commercial Activity**

The defense contends that the claim is "based upon" the expropriation because, without the expropriation, there would be no breach. This "but-for" causation argument misinterprets the FSIA. In Weltover, the Court rejected the argument that the sovereign purpose of the bond issuance transformed the commercial nature of the rescheduling of payments. 504 U.S. at 618. Similarly, here, the claim is not based on the validity of the expropriation decree itself, but on the separate and distinct obligation to comply with the bylaws upon acquiring a controlling interest. The "particular transaction" giving rise to the claim is the breach of the bylaws, not the enactment of Law 26,741. See id. at 618. Therefore, the "based upon" requirement is satisfied.

**C. Direct Effect in the United States**

The defense argues that the "direct effect" analysis is irrelevant because the claim is based on sovereign activity. Having determined that the claim is based on commercial activity, we proceed to the "direct effect" requirement. The defense further asserts that the financial harm to Petersen was incidental. We disagree. The record shows that Petersen's shares were held in the form of American Depository Receipts (ADRs) by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Argentina's breach of the bylaws enabled it to cancel dividend payments, which directly precipitated Petersen's default on its loans and the subsequent foreclosure of these ADRs in New York. This constitutes a direct effect within the meaning of the FSIA. See First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba, 462 U.S. 611, 620 (1983).

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

The defense renews its argument that the act of state doctrine bars adjudication because enforcing the bylaws would require this Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's expropriation. We are not persuaded.

The act of state doctrine is a prudential doctrine that precludes courts from sitting in judgment on the acts of the government of another done within its own territory. Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). However, the doctrine is confined to situations where the validity of the sovereign act is directly in question. OBB Personenverkehr v. Sachs, 577 U.S. 327, 335 (2015).

Petersen does not challenge the validity of Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares under Argentine law. The claim is strictly one for breach of contract based on pre-existing bylaws that governed the relationship between YPF and its shareholders. As the Second Circuit held in Garb v. Republic of Poland, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006), the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial transactions or private contractual disputes where the claim does not require examination of the sovereign act. Determining whether Argentina breached the bylaws does not require this Court to invalidate the expropriation; it merely requires interpretation of the contractual terms that Argentina voluntarily agreed to when it privatized YPF and listed its shares on the NYSE. The defense's argument that enforcing the bylaws effectively imposes conditions on Argentina's sovereign power is a policy argument for the legislature, not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the FSIA is affirmed, and the appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**SO ORDERED.**
