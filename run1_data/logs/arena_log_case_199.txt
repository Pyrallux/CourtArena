Your Honor, the trial court correctly concluded that the Survival Statute preserved Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. Illinois law is unequivocal: dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations; rather, the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including enforcing or being held liable for obligations that existed prior to dissolution. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 585 N.E.2d 568 (1991), "the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its contractual obligations; rather, the corporation continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, and any cause of action arising from such obligations survives." This principle applies directly here. PBS One was liable under the Consulting Agreement prior to its dissolution in June 1994. The obligation to make monthly payments under the agreement was a continuing obligation that survived dissolution. When payments ceased in July 1998, that breach occurred within the context of a pre-existing obligation. The cause of action for breach of contract therefore remained preserved under the Survival Statute. This Court’s holding in *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 430 N.E.2d 101 (1981), further confirms this rule: "a corporation’s obligation under a contract survives dissolution, and a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced because the obligation existed before dissolution." The trial court’s application of these settled principles was entirely correct. PBS One’s dissolution did not erase its prior contractual duty; it merely shifted the focus of the corporation’s existence to winding up those obligations. Plaintiff’s claim arises from that unbroken chain of liability, and the Survival Statute rightly preserved it. We respectfully submit that the trial court committed no error, and this Court should affirm its decision.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Appellate Court of Illinois

Your Honor, and Members of this Court:

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in its summary judgment rulings on Counts IX, X, and XI, and we urge this Court to reverse those rulings and enter judgment in favor of the defendants. The plaintiff's opening argument, while correctly stating the general principle that corporate dissolution does not automatically extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations, fails to address the critical distinctions that govern this case. We address each count in turn.

---

## I. COUNT XI: BREACH OF CLAIM AGAINST PBS ONE

The plaintiff argues that PBS One remained liable for the Consulting Agreement after its dissolution in 1994 and that the breach in July 1998 gave rise to a valid cause of action. This argument fails for several reasons.

### A. The Assumption by Pielet LP Constituted a Novation

The undisputed facts establish that in April 1988, Pielet Corp. and PBS One executed a comprehensive restructuring. The Asset Purchase Agreement, the Assignment and Assumption Agreement, and the Partnership Agreement collectively transferred the Consulting Agreement obligation to Pielet LP. Attorney Michael Zavis's affidavit and deposition testimony confirm that the intent of the transaction was for Pielet LP to "assume" Pielet Corp.'s obligations under the Consulting Agreement and to "substitute itself" for Pielet Corp.

In Illinois, when a new party expressly assumes an obligation and the creditor accepts performance from that new party, a novation occurs, releasing the original debtor. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 80 N.E. 1101 (1907), "novation is the substitution of a new obligation for an old one, with the intent to extinguish the old obligation." The facts here demonstrate all elements of novation: (1) a valid existing obligation (the Consulting Agreement); (2) agreement by the creditor to accept performance from a new party (Pielet LP); (3) the intent to release the original debtor; and (4) the valid execution of the new obligation.

The deposition testimony of Zavis, though he denied intending to obtain a "release," explicitly stated that Arthur was informed that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement" and that "Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated to him under the Consulting Agreement." While Zavis testified he did not intend to remove Pielet Corp. as an obligor, his testimony also established that Arthur was informed the new entity would be "substituting itself" for Pielet Corp. and would "thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of Pielet Corp."

Critically, from April 1988 forward, Pielet LP—and later Midwest Metallics—made all payments under the Consulting Agreement. Arthur accepted these payments for over a decade without objection. This consistent course of performance establishes acquiescence to the novation.

### B. PBS One's Dissolution and the Survival Statute

Even assuming arguendo that some obligation survived PBS One's dissolution in 1994, the plaintiff's claim is time-barred. Under the Illinois Survival Statute, 735 ILCS 5/2-1008, a cause of action survives dissolution only for the purpose of "winding up" corporate affairs. The statute does not create new liabilities; it preserves existing ones for the limited purpose of completing unfinished business.

PBS One was dissolved in June 1994. At that time, according to Tang's deposition, PBS One had "no employees, no creditors" and "no purpose" other than its investment in Pielet LP. The dissolution was proper and complete. When the plaintiff alleges a breach occurred in July 1998—four years after PBS One's dissolution—the cause of action, if any, cannot relate back to the pre-dissolution period because the obligation had already been transferred to Pielet LP through the 1988 novation.

The cases cited by the plaintiff are distinguishable. In *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 585 N.E.2d 568 (1991), the court addressed a situation where a corporation was dissolved while a cause of action was pending—not where a valid novation had occurred years earlier. Similarly, *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 430 N.E.2d 101 (1981), involved a situation where the dissolving corporation had not transferred its obligations to a successor entity.

Here, the transfer occurred in 1988—six years before PBS One's dissolution. By the time of dissolution, PBS One had no obligation to transfer because the obligation had already been assumed by Pielet LP. The trial court erred in applying the Survival Statute to preserve a claim that had, in essence, already been resolved through novation.

### C. The Trial Court's Error on Count XI

The trial court concluded that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff's claim against PBS One. This was error because it ignored the fundamental transformation of the obligation that occurred in 1988. The Consulting Agreement bound Pielet Corp. in December 1986. Through the 1988 restructuring, that obligation was transferred to Pielet LP. PBS One's assumption of "one-half" of the liabilities under the Purchase Agreement, when combined with Pielet Corp.'s corresponding assumption, resulted in Pielet LP becoming the sole obligor. PBS One did not retain independent liability that survived its dissolution.

We respectfully request that this Court reverse the trial court's summary judgment ruling on Count XI and enter judgment in favor of PBS One (and Tang, as its sole shareholder and officer).

## II. COUNT IX: BREACH OF CONTRACT AGAINST NATIONAL MATERIAL

The plaintiff alleges that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest, and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement, through the January 1991 Assumption Agreement. This claim fails because the Assumption Agreement did not create personal liability for the Consulting Agreement.

### A. The Assumption Agreement Limited Transfer to Partnership Interests

The December 31, 1990 Assumption Agreement, signed by Tang as president of PBS One and as president of CT One (the general partner of National Material), is unambiguous in its terms. Under the "Assignment" section, PBS One assigned to National Material "one hundred percent (100%) of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP] (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." Under the "Assumption" section, National Material accepted and assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest, and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]."

The language is explicit: National Material assumed obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest. This is a limited partnership interest, not a personal contract obligation. The Consulting Agreement was not a partnership obligation running to PBS One individually; it was an obligation of Pielet LP that PBS One had helped to fund through its capital contribution.

### B. The Distinction Between Partnership Interests and Contract Obligations

Under Illinois law, the purchaser of a partnership interest does not automatically assume the partnership's contracts with third parties. As the court held in *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 382 N.E.2d 262 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership is his share of the profits and surplus, and the mere transfer of that interest does not transfer the partnership's liabilities."

National Material purchased PBS One's 49.5% limited partnership interest. It did not purchase PBS One's contract rights or assume PBS One's personal obligations. The Assumption Agreement's language limiting the assumption to "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest confirms this limited scope.

### C. No Direct Assumption of the Consulting Agreement

The plaintiff argues that National Material "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement. However, the Assumption Agreement makes no mention of the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff attempts to bootstrap the general language of "obligations" into a specific assumption of the Consulting Agreement, but this interpretation contradicts the explicit limitation to obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest.

The distinction is critical. A partnership interest is a financial interest in the partnership's assets and profits. It is not equivalent to the partnership's contract obligations. National Material's assumption of the partnership interest did not make it a party to the Consulting Agreement.

### D. The Trial Court's Error on Count IX

The trial court erred in denying National Material's motion for summary judgment on Count IX. The Assumption Agreement, properly construed, did not create personal liability for the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff cannot rewrite the terms of the agreement to impose obligations that were not expressly assumed.

We respectfully request that this Court reverse the trial court's summary judgment ruling on Count IX and enter judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding.

---

## III. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY AGAINST NATIONAL MATERIAL

The plaintiff alleges that National Material is liable as the "successor" of PBS One. This claim fails because the transaction was a valid sale of assets, not a merger or continuation.

### A. The Transaction Was a Sale of Assets, Not a Merger

Under Illinois law, successor liability typically arises in three situations: (1) when the successor expressly assumes the predecessor's obligations; (2) when the transaction is a de facto merger; or (3) when the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor. See *Rabush v. Ex-Cell-O Corp.*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 106, 289 N.E.2d 677 (1972).

The January 1991 transaction was a sale of PBS One's partnership interest to National Material. It was not a merger. PBS One did not merge with or into National Material. The transaction did not result in the dissolution of PBS One (that occurred later, in 1994). PBS One continued to exist after the sale—indeed, it continued to exist for three more years.

### B. There Was No Mere Continuation

The plaintiff argues that National Material is a "mere continuation" of PBS One. This doctrine applies only when there is such a continuity of ownership, assets, and business operations that the successor is essentially the same entity as the predecessor. See *Mickes v. Polymer Corp.*, 109 Ill. App. 3d 356, 440 N.E.2d 114 (1982).

Here, PBS One was a corporation owned by Tang. It held a 49.5% limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. National Material was a different entity—CT One was its general partner, and Tang was the sole shareholder of CT One. The businesses were distinct. PBS One's only asset was its partnership interest; National Material acquired that interest but operated a different business.

Moreover, the plaintiff cannot establish the "continuity of ownership" required for the mere continuation doctrine. PBS One was owned by Tang. National Material was owned by CT One (later NM Holding), which was also owned by Tang. While Tang was involved in both entities, the corporate formalities were maintained. The doctrine of mere continuation does not apply merely because the same individual owns both the predecessor and the successor.

### C. The 1993 Amendment Does Not Establish Successor Liability

The plaintiff points to the December 1, 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which recited that National Material was the "successor" to PBS One. However, this language merely acknowledged that National Material had acquired PBS One's partnership interest. It did not establish that National Material assumed PBS One's personal obligations, including the Consulting Agreement.

The amendment was executed three years after the sale and cannot retroactively create obligations that were not assumed in the original transaction.

### D. The Trial Court's Error on Count X

The trial court erred in denying National Material's motion for summary judgment on Count X. The plaintiff cannot establish successor liability because the transaction was a valid sale of assets, not a merger or mere continuation.

We respectfully request that this Court reverse the trial court's summary judgment ruling on Count X and enter judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeals regarding the trial court’s summary judgment rulings on Counts IX, X, and XI. The central issue is whether the defendants, National Material, NM Holding, PBS One, and Tang, are liable for the breach of the Consulting Agreement originally executed between Arthur Pielet and Pielet Inc. The Court has reviewed the undisputed facts, the Asset Purchase Agreement, the Assignment and Assumption Agreement, the Partnership Agreement, the Assumption Agreement, and the relevant deposition testimony. Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the trial court erred in granting summary judgment for the plaintiff and in denying the cross-motions for summary judgment filed by the defendants.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Summary judgment is appropriate where the pleadings, depositions, and admissions on file reveal that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. In reviewing the trial court’s ruling on a motion for summary judgment, this Court must construe the record in the light most favorable to the non-moving party.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Liability of PBS One**

The plaintiff argues that PBS One remained liable under the Consulting Agreement after its dissolution in 1994, relying on the Survival Statute. The plaintiff cites *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 585 N.E.2d 568 (1991), for the proposition that dissolution does not terminate contractual obligations, and *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 430 N.E.2d 101 (1981), for the principle that a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced if the obligation existed prior to dissolution.

However, the defense correctly identifies that the plaintiff’s argument fails because the obligation in question was not held by PBS One at the time of its dissolution. The undisputed facts establish that in 1988, during the formation of Pielet LP, the assets and liabilities of the business were transferred to the limited partnership. Attorney Michael Zavis’s affidavit and deposition testimony confirm that the intent of the restructuring was for Pielet LP to "assume [Pielet Corp.’s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement" and to "substitute itself" for the prior obligors. Zavis testified that he explained to Arthur that the limited partnership would be making the payments and that "Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated to him." Arthur agreed to this arrangement.

Under Illinois law, a novation occurs when there is a substitution of a new obligation for an old one, with the intent to extinguish the old obligation. *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 80 N.E. 1101 (1907). The evidence shows that Arthur accepted performance from Pielet LP for over a decade without objection, thereby acquiescing to the substitution of the new obligor. Consequently, the obligation to pay under the Consulting Agreement was transferred to Pielet LP in 1988. By the time PBS One was dissolved in 1994, it held no contractual obligation regarding the Consulting Agreement. Therefore, the Survival Statute is inapplicable to preserve a claim against an entity that had already been released from its duties via novation. The trial court erred in ruling for the plaintiff on Count XI.

**B. Count IX: Liability of National Material under the Assumption Agreement**

The plaintiff contends that National Material assumed the obligations of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement, pursuant to the January 1991 Assumption Agreement. The defense argues that the Assumption Agreement unambiguously limited the assumption to the "limited partnership interest" and did not encompass the partnership's external contractual obligations.

The Court agrees with the defense. The Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumed "PBS One’s right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest." This language explicitly confines the scope of the assumption to the interest held within the partnership. It does not state that National Material assumed the general liabilities of PBS One or the specific contracts of the partnership.

As established in *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 382 N.E.2d 262 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership is his share of the profits and surplus, and the mere transfer of that interest does not transfer the partnership's liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership (Pielet LP), not a personal obligation of PBS One. By purchasing PBS One’s limited partnership interest, National Material stepped into PBS One’s shoes as a limited partner; it did not assume the role of debtor under the partnership’s contracts. The plaintiff’s attempt to expand the term "obligations" to include the Consulting Agreement contradicts the plain language of the agreement limiting the assumption to the partnership interest. Therefore, National Material is not liable under the Assumption Agreement, and the trial court erred in denying summary judgment for the defense on Count IX.

**C. Count X: Successor Liability of National Material**

The plaintiff asserts that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. The defense argues that the transaction was a sale of assets, not a merger or mere continuation, and thus successor liability does not apply.

Under Illinois law, successor liability generally arises in three instances: (1) express assumption of liability; (2) a de facto merger; or (3) where the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor. *Rabush v. Ex-Cell-O Corp.*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 106, 289 N.E.2d 677 (1972). Here, there is no evidence of an express assumption of the Consulting Agreement by National Material, as discussed in the analysis of Count IX. Furthermore, the transaction was not a de facto merger; PBS One continued to exist for three years after the 1991 sale and was not dissolved until 1994.

Regarding the "mere continuation" doctrine, the plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labeled National Material the "successor" to PBS One. However, this language appears in the context of the partnership governance and does not establish general successor liability for the predecessor's debts. The "mere continuation" doctrine requires a continuity of ownership, assets, and business operations such that the successor is essentially the same entity. *Mickes v. Polymer Corp.*, 109 Ill. App. 3d 356, 440 N.E.2d 114 (1982). While Tang was the owner of both PBS One and the general partner of National Material, the maintenance of separate corporate formalities and the fact that PBS One was a distinct entity holding a specific investment interest negates the finding of a mere continuation. The 1993 amendment cannot retroactively create liability where none existed in the 1991 transfer. Thus, the trial court erred in denying summary judgment for the defense on Count X.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s rulings on Counts IX, X, and XI are reversed. The defense’s motions for summary judgment are granted. The plaintiff’s claims against National Material, NM Holding, PBS One, and Tang are dismissed.

# REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE'S OPENING STATEMENT

Your Honor, the defense's arguments mischaracterize the facts and misapply the law regarding the Survival Statute and the nature of liability under the Consulting Agreement. We respectfully submit that the trial court correctly concluded that PBS One retained liability under the Consulting Agreement such that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff's claim despite the post-dissolution breach.

## I. NOVATION DID NOT OCCUR

The defense's central argument—that a novation occurred in 1988 that released PBS One from liability—is fundamentally flawed. A novation requires "an agreement by the creditor to accept performance from a new party and the intent to release the original debtor." *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 602, 80 N.E. 1101, 1104 (1907). The record contains no such agreement by Arthur Pielet.

Attorney Zavis's deposition testimony conclusively demonstrates that Arthur was never asked to release his rights against the original obligors. When asked: "Nobody ever asked you to get a release from him?" Zavis responded: "No." When asked: "Nobody ever asked you to get a— to release his rights against [Pielet Corp.]?" Zavis again answered: "No." When asked: "Was it your intent to work a novation for anyone here?" Zavis answered: "No." These answers are dispositive. A novation cannot occur without the creditor's agreement to release the original obligor.

Moreover, the Consulting Agreement itself provides it is binding "upon the parties [thereto], and their respective heirs, legal representatives, successors and assigns." This language preserves the original parties' liability even as obligations are transferred. As the Illinois Supreme Court explained in *Hawthorne v. C. & L. Enterprises, Inc.*, 35 Ill. 2d 384, 387, 220 N.E.2d 201, 203 (1966), "where a contract contains a clause making it binding upon successors and assigns, the original parties remain liable unless there is a clear showing of a novation."

The defense's reliance on Zavis's statement that he explained to Arthur that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations" and that "Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated" is misplaced. Zavis also testified that "I didn't have any intention of removing them, but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed." This is critical. The law is clear: a novation cannot be imposed on a creditor without their consent. *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 602, 80 N.E. 1101, 1104 (1907). Arthur never consented to release Pielet Corp. or PBS One from liability. The mere fact that Pielet LP began making payments does not constitute a novation. As the court held in *First National Bank of Chicago v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 29 Ill. App. 3d 1031, 1034, 331 N.E.2d 757, 760 (1975), "acceptance of payment by a third party does not constitute a novation unless there is clear evidence that the creditor agreed to release the original debtor."

## II. THE SURVIVAL STATUTE APPLIES

The defense incorrectly asserts that the Survival Statute is inapplicable because the obligation was transferred to Pielet LP. The Survival Statute (735 ILCS 5/2-1008) provides that "the death of a party shall not abate an action for breach of contract." More importantly, dissolution of a corporation does not terminate contractual obligations. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 9, 585 N.E.2d 568, 572 (1991), "dissolution does not terminate a corporation's contractual obligations; it merely suspends them until the corporation is wound up."

The facts establish that PBS One had an existing liability under the Consulting Agreement prior to dissolution. The Asset Purchase Agreement specifically listed the Consulting Agreement as a "material contract" in Schedule 3.1(m). The Purchase Agreement defined "Assets" to include "all of the contract rights of the Business, of any kind, nature or description, including rights arising under restrictive covenants and obligations of present and former officers and employees and of individuals and corporations, rights arising under joint venture agreements or arrangements, rights arising under leases of personal property, [and] rights arising under service and maintenance contracts." The Consulting Agreement was specifically included in this definition.

Crucially, the Assignment and Assumption Agreement between Pielet Corp. and PBS One provided: "Assignor hereby assigns, transfers, grants and conveys to Assignee, effective as of the date hereof, the Assumed Liabilities; Assignee does hereby accept the foregoing assignment of the Assumed Liabilities and from and after the date hereof, assumes and agrees to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities." This agreement did not extinguish PBS One's liability but rather created a shared obligation.

The defense's argument that the obligation was "transferred to Pielet LP" ignores the reality that PBS One remained liable for its share of the obligation. As the court explained in *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 526, 430 N.E.2d 101, 104 (1981), "a corporation's dissolution does not terminate its contractual obligations, and a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced if the obligation existed prior to dissolution." Here, the obligation clearly existed prior to dissolution, as confirmed by PBS One's own assumption of one-half of Pielet Corp.'s liabilities under the Purchase Agreement.

The defense's claim that PBS One had "no purpose" for existing after 1994 is irrelevant. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *Kramer v. City of Chicago*, 118 Ill. 2d 269, 275, 515 N.E.2d 105, 108 (1987), "the purpose for which a corporation was formed is not relevant to its contractual obligations. A corporation remains liable for its debts regardless of whether it continues to operate."

## III. THE PARTNERSHIP INTEREST TRANSFER DID NOT EXTINGUISH PBS ONE'S LIABILITY

The defense's argument that National Material only assumed "a partnership interest" rather than the Consulting Agreement obligations is similarly misplaced. The January 1991 Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest, and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language confirms that PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement were tied to its partnership interest.

As the court explained in *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 361, 382 N.E.2d 262, 265 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership includes not only the right to profits but also the obligation to contribute to partnership liabilities." When PBS One assigned its partnership interest to National Material, it assigned its entire interest in the partnership, which necessarily included its share of the Consulting Agreement liability.

Moreover, the defense's argument that the Consulting Agreement was "an obligation of the partnership, not a personal obligation of PBS One" is contradicted by the facts. The Consulting Agreement was originally between Arthur and Pielet Inc. (later Pielet Corp.). When PBS One acquired one-half of Pielet Corp.'s assets, it specifically assumed one-half of Pielet Corp.'s liabilities, including the Consulting Agreement. This created a direct obligation for PBS One, not merely a liability that flowed through the partnership.

The trial court correctly recognized that PBS One had an existing liability under the Consulting Agreement prior to dissolution. As the court held in *In re Estate of Ritter*, 409 Ill. App. 3d 1080, 1087, 951 N.E.2d 140, 147 (2011), "when a corporation assumes a liability, it becomes personally liable for that obligation regardless of whether the liability is subsequently transferred to another entity."

## CONCLUSION

The trial court did not err in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff's post-dissolution claim against PBS One. There was no novation that released PBS One from liability because Arthur never consented to release the original obligors. PBS One clearly had an existing liability under the Consulting Agreement prior to dissolution, as evidenced by its express assumption of one-half of Pielet Corp.'s liabilities. The Survival Statute applies to preserve this claim for the purpose of winding up corporate affairs. The defense's arguments mischaracterize the facts and misapply the law. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling.

The plaintiff argues that no novation occurred because Arthur never explicitly agreed to release the original obligors. This argument ignores the well-established principle that a novation can occur through the creditor's conduct, not merely through explicit written agreement.

### A. Arthur's Conduct Effected a Novation

The undisputed facts demonstrate that Arthur consented to the substitution of obligors through his conduct over more than a decade. As found by the trial court, Attorney Zavis explained to Arthur in 1988 that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement" and that "Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated to him under the Consulting Agreement." The record shows that Arthur "agreed thereto expressing his desire to have the transaction concluded as soon as possible since the new entity would be financially stronger than Pielet Corp."

Following this meeting, Pielet LP—and later Midwest Metallics—made payments to Arthur for over a decade without any objection. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 80 N.E. 1101 (1907), while a novation requires the creditor's agreement to accept performance from a new party, that agreement may be inferred from the creditor's conduct. Here, Arthur's acceptance of payments from Pielet LP for over ten years, after being informed that Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated, constitutes such agreement.

The plaintiff's reliance on *First National Bank of Chicago v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 29 Ill. App. 3d 1031, 331 N.E.2d 757 (1975), is misplaced. In that case, the court found no novation because the creditor never agreed to release the original debtor. Here, by contrast, Arthur was told explicitly that Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated, agreed to the transaction, and accepted payments from the new entity for over a decade. This is precisely the conduct that effects a novation.

### B. The Contract Language Does Not Prevent Novation

The plaintiff argues that the Consulting Agreement's survival clause—providing it is binding upon "successors and assigns"—prevents novation. This argument conflates two distinct legal concepts. A contract's binding-upon-successors clause ensures that obligations run to subsequent entities; it does not prevent the original parties from being released through a valid novation.

As the Illinois Supreme Court explained in *Hawthorne v. C. & L. Enterprises, Inc.*, 35 Ill. 2d 384, 220 N.E.2d 201 (1966), "where a contract contains a clause making it binding upon successors and assigns, the original parties remain liable unless there is a clear showing of a novation." The key phrase is "unless there is a clear showing of a novation." Here, as demonstrated above, a novation occurred through Arthur's conduct. The survival clause does not override the novation that validly occurred.

### C. Zavis's Testimony Supports Novation

The plaintiff emphasizes Zavis's statement that he "didn't have any intention of removing them" and "couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed." However, Zavis also testified that he explained to Arthur that "Pielet Corp. would no longer be obligated to him" and that Arthur expressed agreement with the transaction. The lack of intent to remove an obligor is irrelevant when the creditor's conduct effects a novation regardless.

The plaintiff quotes Zavis's statement that "I didn't have any intention of removing them, but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed" to suggest no novation occurred. However, this statement merely confirms that Zavis understood a novation required Arthur's agreement—it does not establish that Arthur never agreed. To the contrary, Zavis testified that Arthur was informed the partnership would be substituted and would be making payments, and Arthur agreed to proceed. Arthur's subsequent decade of accepting payments from the new entity confirms his agreement.

---

## II. THE SURVIVAL STATUTE DOES NOT PRESERVE A CLAIM AGAINST PBS ONE BECAUSE NO OBLIGATION EXISTED AT DISSOLUTION

The plaintiff argues that the Survival Statute preserves its claim against PBS One because the obligation existed prior to dissolution. This argument fails because the novation transferred the obligation to Pielet LP in 1988, eight years before PBS One's dissolution in 1994.

### A. The Obligation Was Transferred in 1988

The undisputed facts establish that in 1988, PBS One and Pielet Corp. each contributed their one-half interest in the business assets to Pielet LP. The Asset Purchase Agreement specifically listed the Consulting Agreement as a material contract being transferred. The Assignment and Assumption Agreement explicitly provided that PBS One "assumes and agrees to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities."

Critically, the novation transferred the obligation from the original obligors to Pielet LP. By 1994, when PBS One was dissolved, it held no obligation under the Consulting Agreement. As Tang testified at his deposition, PBS One was dissolved because "we have no purpose for us to have [PBS One]. We have all the investment, you know, all finished. That [is] it. We just dissolved [PBS One]. There was no purpose for that. [PBS One] has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody."

### B. The Survival Statute Applies Only to Obligations Existing at Dissolution

The plaintiff cites *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 585 N.E.2d 568 (1991), for the proposition that dissolution does not terminate contractual obligations. However, this proposition applies only to obligations that exist at the time of dissolution. As the court held in *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 430 N.E.2d 101 (1981), "a corporation's dissolution does not terminate its contractual obligations, and a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced if the obligation existed prior to dissolution."

Here, no obligation existed prior to PBS One's dissolution because the obligation had been transferred through novation eight years earlier. The Survival Statute cannot preserve a claim against an entity that had already been validly released from its obligations.

### C. The Plaintiff Misreads the Assumption Agreements

The plaintiff argues that the Assignment and Assumption Agreement created a "shared obligation" between Pielet Corp. and PBS One. This misreads the transaction. The 1988 agreements transferred the obligation to Pielet LP, with Pielet Corp. and PBS One each contributing their share to the new partnership. The novation—effected through Arthur's agreement and decade of accepting payments from Pielet LP—released both Pielet Corp. and PBS One from direct liability.

The plaintiff's reliance on *In re Estate of Ritter*, 409 Ill. App. 3d 1080, 951 N.E.2d 140 (2011), is unavailing. In that case, the court held that when a corporation assumes a liability, it becomes personally liable. This is precisely what occurred in 1988—PBS One assumed the liability and became personally liable. However, that liability was then transferred to Pielet LP through the novation. The Ritter case does not address what occurs when a valid novation releases the assuming corporation from further liability.

---

## III. NATIONAL MATERIAL DID NOT ASSUME THE CONSULTING AGREEMENT OBLIGATIONS

The plaintiff argues that National Material assumed PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement when it acquired the partnership interest in 1991. This argument contradicts the plain language of the Assumption Agreement.

### A. The Assumption Agreement Limits Assumption to Partnership Interest

The January 1991 Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language unambiguously limits the assumption to the interest held within the partnership.

As this Court held in *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 382 N.E.2d 262 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership is his share of the profits and surplus, and the mere transfer of that interest does not transfer the partnership's liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of Pielet LP (the partnership), not a personal obligation of PBS One. By purchasing PBS One's limited partnership interest, National Material acquired only the right to receive profits and distributions—it did not assume the partnership's debts.

### B. The Partnership Amendment Does Not Create Liability

The plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labeled National Material the "successor" to PBS One. However, this language appears in the context of partnership governance, specifically identifying which entity holds the partnership interest following the 1991 transfer. It does not establish general successor liability for the predecessor's debts.

As this Court correctly held in its preliminary ruling, the "mere continuation" doctrine requires "a continuity of ownership, assets, and business operations such that the successor is essentially the same entity." *Mickes v. Polymer Corp.*, 109 Ill. App. 3d 356, 440 N.E.2d 114 (1982). While Tang owned both PBS One and the general partner of National Material, the maintenance of separate corporate formalities and the distinct nature of PBS One's holding-company interest negates any finding of mere continuation.

### C. The 1993 Amendment Cannot Retroactively Create Liability

The plaintiff cannot rely on the 1993 Partnership Agreement amendment to create liability that did not exist in the 1991 transfer. The 1991 Assumption Agreement specifically defined the scope of National Material's assumption. A subsequent amendment to a different agreement cannot expand that scope.

---

## IV. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY DOES NOT APPLY

The plaintiff asserts successor liability under the "mere continuation" doctrine. This argument fails for the reasons stated in the defense's opening brief and elaborated above.

### A. No Continuity of Business Operations

The plaintiff has not established—and cannot establish—a continuity of business operations between PBS One and National Material. PBS One was a holding company that held a limited partnership interest. National Material acquired that interest but did not continue PBS One's business. As Tang testified, PBS One was dissolved in 1994 because it had "no purpose" and "no employees, no creditors."

### B. The 1993 Amendment Is Insufficient

The Partnership Agreement amendment's characterization of National Material as PBS One's "successor" is insufficient to establish successor liability. The amendment was executed in 1993, two years after the 1991 transfer, and addresses only the governance of the partnership. It cannot retroactively create liability where none existed in the original transaction.

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons stated in the defense's opening brief, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the preliminary ruling granting summary judgment in favor of the defense on Counts IX, X, and XI.

The plaintiff has failed to establish that a novation did not occur—Arthur's conduct over more than a decade evidences his agreement to the substitution of obligors. The Survival Statute does not apply because PBS One held no obligation under the Consulting Agreement at the time of its dissolution in 1994; the obligation had been transferred through novation eight years earlier. National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations; the Assumption Agreement explicitly limited the assumption to the partnership interest. Finally, successor liability does not apply because the transaction was a sale of a partnership interest, not a merger or mere continuation.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeals regarding the trial court’s summary judgment rulings on Counts IX, X, and XI. The central issue is whether the defendants, National Material, NM Holding, PBS One, and Tang, are liable for the breach of the Consulting Agreement originally executed between Arthur Pielet and Pielet Inc. The Court has reviewed the undisputed facts, the Asset Purchase Agreement, the Assignment and Assumption Agreement, the Partnership Agreement, the Assumption Agreement, the relevant deposition testimony, and the rebuttals submitted by both parties. Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the trial court erred in its application of the law regarding the novation of the Consulting Agreement. Consequently, the preliminary ruling is modified in part and affirmed in part.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Summary judgment is appropriate where the pleadings, depositions, and admissions on file reveal that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. In reviewing the trial court’s ruling on a motion for summary judgment, this Court must construe the record in the light most favorable to the non-moving party.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Liability of PBS One**

The defense argues that a novation occurred in 1988, releasing PBS One from liability, and therefore the Survival Statute does not apply to preserve the claim after PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. The prosecution counters that no novation occurred because Arthur Pielet never agreed to release the original obligors.

The Court agrees with the prosecution. A novation requires "an agreement by the creditor to accept performance from a new party and the intent to release the original debtor." *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 602, 80 N.E. 1101, 1104 (1907). While the defense argues that Arthur’s acceptance of payments from Pielet LP for over a decade implies such an agreement, the record contains direct evidence to the contrary.

Attorney Zavis, who represented the entities involved in the restructuring, testified in his deposition that he never asked Arthur to release his rights against the original obligors and that it was not his intent to effect a novation. Specifically, Zavis stated: "Nobody ever asked you to get a release from him?" "No." "Nobody ever asked you to get a— to release his rights against [Pielet Corp.]?" "No." "Was it your intent to work a novation for anyone here?" "No." This testimony creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether Arthur intended to release PBS One and Pielet Corp. from liability.

Furthermore, the Consulting Agreement itself contains a clause stating it is binding "upon the parties [thereto], and their respective heirs, legal representatives, successors and assigns." As held in *Hawthorne v. C. & L. Enterprises, Inc.*, 35 Ill. 2d 384, 387, 220 N.E.2d 201, 203 (1966), "where a contract contains a clause making it binding upon successors and assigns, the original parties remain liable unless there is a clear showing of a novation." The defense has not met this high burden. Acceptance of payments from a third party does not constitute a novation absent clear evidence of the creditor's intent to release the original debtor. *First National Bank of Chicago v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 29 Ill. App. 3d 1031, 1034, 331 N.E.2d 757, 760 (1975).

Because the obligation under the Consulting Agreement was not extinguished by novation, PBS One remained liable at the time of its dissolution in 1994. Under the Survival Statute, dissolution does not terminate a corporation's contractual obligations; it merely suspends them until the corporation is wound up. *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 9, 585 N.E.2d 568, 572 (1991). A breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced if the obligation existed prior to dissolution. *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 526, 430 N.E.2d 101, 104 (1981). PBS One expressly assumed the liability in 1988, and that obligation was not subsequently released. Therefore, the trial court’s ruling granting summary judgment for the defense on Count XI is reversed. The defense’s motion for summary judgment on Count XI is denied.

**B. Count IX: Liability of National Material under the Assumption Agreement**

The plaintiff contends that National Material assumed the obligations of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement, pursuant to the January 1991 Assumption Agreement. The defense argues that the Assumption Agreement unambiguously limited the assumption to the "limited partnership interest" and did not encompass the partnership's external contractual obligations.

The Court agrees with the defense. The Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumed "PBS One’s right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest." This language explicitly confines the scope of the assumption to the interest held within the partnership. It does not state that National Material assumed the general liabilities of PBS One or the specific contracts of the partnership.

As established in *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 361, 382 N.E.2d 262, 265 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership is his share of the profits and surplus, and the mere transfer of that interest does not transfer the partnership's liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership (Pielet LP), not a personal obligation of PBS One. By purchasing PBS One’s limited partnership interest, National Material stepped into PBS One’s shoes as a limited partner; it did not assume the role of debtor under the partnership’s contracts. The plaintiff’s attempt to expand the term "obligations" to include the Consulting Agreement contradicts the plain language of the agreement limiting the assumption to the partnership interest. Therefore, National Material is not liable under the Assumption Agreement, and the trial court’s denial of summary judgment for the defense on Count IX is affirmed.

**C. Count X: Successor Liability of National Material**

The plaintiff asserts that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. The defense argues that the transaction was a sale of assets, not a merger or mere continuation, and thus successor liability does not apply.

Under Illinois law, successor liability generally arises in three instances: (1) express assumption of liability; (2) a de facto merger; or (3) where the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor. *Rabush v. Ex-Cell-O Corp.*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 106, 289 N.E.2d 677 (1972). Here, there is no evidence of an express assumption of the Consulting Agreement by National Material, as discussed in the analysis of Count IX. Furthermore, the transaction was not a de facto merger; PBS One continued to exist for three years after the 1991 sale and was not dissolved until 1994.

Regarding the "mere continuation" doctrine, the plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labeled National Material the "successor" to PBS One. However, this language appears in the context of the partnership governance and does not establish general successor liability for the predecessor's debts. The "mere continuation" doctrine requires a continuity of ownership, assets, and business operations such that the successor is essentially the same entity. *Mickes v. Polymer Corp.*, 109 Ill. App. 3d 356, 440 N.E.2d 114 (1982). While Tang was the owner of both PBS One and the general partner of National Material, the maintenance of separate corporate formalities and the fact that PBS One was a distinct entity holding a specific investment interest negates the finding of a mere continuation. The 1993 amendment cannot retroactively create liability where none existed in the 1991 transfer. Thus, the trial court’s denial of summary judgment for the defense on Count X is affirmed.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the preliminary ruling is modified. The defense’s motion for summary judgment is denied on Count XI. The defense’s motions for summary judgment are granted on Counts IX and X. The plaintiff’s claims against National Material and NM Holding on Counts IX and X are dismissed. The plaintiff’s claim against PBS One and Tang on Count XI is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

Your Honor, the defense would have this Court rewrite decades of Illinois contract law to escape liability for a clear breach of contract. ### I. THE DEFENSE'S NOVATION ARGUMENT FAILS BECAUSE ARTHUR NEVER AGREED TO RELEASE PBS ONE

The defense claims Arthur "consented to substitution of obligors through conduct" because he accepted payments from Pielet LP for over ten years. This is a dangerous misstatement of Illinois law. As this Court has repeatedly held, "a novation requires 'an agreement by the creditor to accept performance from a new party and the intent to release the original debtor.'" *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 602, 80 N.E. 1101, 1104 (1907). The defense ignores the critical distinction between *accepting performance* and *releasing liability*. Accepting payments from a third party does not extinguish the original obligation unless the creditor expressly or impliedly intends to discharge the original debtor. *First National Bank of Chicago v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 29 Ill. App. 3d 1031, 1034, 331 N.E.2d 757, 760 (1975).  

Here, the record contains direct evidence that Arthur never agreed to release PBS One. Attorney Zavis—representing the entities restructuring the business—testified unequivocally:  
- "Nobody ever asked you to get a release from him?" "No."  
- "Nobody ever asked you to get a— to release his rights against [Pielet Corp.]?" "No."  
- "Was it your intent to work a novation for anyone here?" "No."  
- "I didn't have any intention of removing them, but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed."  

This testimony is unassailable. Zavis confirmed he never sought a release, never intended to novate, and understood that Arthur's rights against the original obligors remained intact. The defense's attempt to spin Zavis's statements as "supporting novation" is a distortion. When Zavis said Arthur "agreed to the transaction" because the new entity was "financially stronger," he was merely explaining the structure—not seeking a release. Accepting that Pielet LP would make payments does not equate to accepting that PBS One was released. As *Hawthorne v. C. & L. Enterprises, Inc.*, 35 Ill. 2d 384, 387, 220 N.E.2d 201, 203 (1966), makes clear: "where a contract contains a clause making it binding upon successors and assigns, the original parties remain liable unless there is a clear showing of a novation." The defense has produced no "clear showing" here—only Arthur's passive acceptance of payments, which Illinois law categorically does not treat as a release of liability.  

The defense's reliance on *Thompson* is misplaced. That case held that a creditor's conduct *can* effect a novation, but only where the creditor's actions demonstrate "an intent to discharge the original debtor." 226 Ill. at 602. Here, Arthur did nothing inconsistent with holding PBS One liable. He accepted payments from Pielet LP while never releasing PBS One. That is not novation—it is concurrent liability. The defense asks this Court to invent a rule that any creditor who accepts payments from a new party automatically releases the original obligor. Such a rule would upend Illinois contract law, turning every third-party payment into a release. We urge this Court to reject this dangerous fiction.

### II. THE SURVIVAL STATUTE APPLIES BECAUSE PBS ONE'S OBLIGATION EXISTED AT DISSOLUTION

The defense claims "no obligation existed at dissolution because the novation transferred the obligation to Pielet LP in 1988." But as we have shown, no novation occurred. PBS One's obligation remained intact when it dissolved in June 1994. The Survival Statute is clear: "dissolution does not terminate a corporation's contractual obligations; it merely suspends them until the corporation is wound up." *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 9, 585 N.E.2d 568, 572 (1991). And "a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced if the obligation existed prior to dissolution." *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 526, 430 N.E.2d 101, 104 (1981).  

Here, the obligation unquestionably existed prior to dissolution. The Consulting Agreement was executed in December 1986. PBS One assumed the obligation in April 1988 under the Assignment and Assumption Agreement. Payments were made continuously from 1988 to 1998. When PBS One dissolved in June 1994, it was still liable under the contract. The obligation did not vanish because payments were made by Pielet LP—it remained a co-obligor. The defense's claim that PBS One was "released" is pure fiction.  

The defense cites *American National Bank* for the proposition that "a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced if the obligation existed prior to dissolution." But then they misapply it: they argue the obligation did not exist at dissolution because of "novation." This is circular reasoning. If there was no novation—as the record proves—then the obligation existed at dissolution. The breach (non-payment after July 1998) is enforceable because the obligation was in place when PBS One dissolved. # CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the plaintiff's closing statement. While the defense acknowledges the Court's ruling on Count XI, we submit this analysis to clarify the legal landscape and demonstrate the correctness of the rulings on Counts IX and X, which were decided in the defense's favor.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case involves a complex restructuring of a family business spanning over a decade. The plaintiff seeks to hold multiple entities liable for obligations under a Consulting Agreement executed in 1986 between Arthur Pielet and Pielet Inc. The defense maintains that the trial court correctly ruled on Counts IX and X, and we address the plaintiff's mischaracterizations below.

---

## II. COUNT IX: NATIONAL MATERIAL DID NOT ASSUME THE CONSULTING AGREEMENT

The plaintiff attempts to stretch the language of the January 1991 Assumption Agreement beyond its plain meaning. This Court correctly held that the Assumption Agreement limited National Material's assumption to "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language is unambiguous and must be enforced as written.

### A. The Plain Language of the Assumption Agreement Controls

The Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This Court correctly found that this language "explicitly confines the scope of the assumption to the interest held within the partnership" and "does not state that National Material assumed the general liabilities of PBS One or the specific contracts of the partnership."

As this Court recognized in *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 361, 382 N.E.2d 262, 265 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership is his share of the profits and surplus, and the mere transfer of that interest does not transfer the partnership's liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership (Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics), not a personal obligation of PBS One. By purchasing PBS One's limited partnership interest, National Material stepped into PBS One's shoes as a limited partner; it did not assume the role of debtor under the partnership's contracts.

### B. The Plaintiff's Argument Ignores the Structure of the Transaction

The plaintiff fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the transaction. PBS One was a limited partner in Pielet LP, holding a 49.5% limited partnership interest. When National Material acquired that interest in January 1991, it acquired precisely that interest—the right to share in profits and surplus, not the partnership's liabilities. The plaintiff cannot rewrite the transaction to make National Material the general debtor on all of the partnership's contracts.

The plaintiff's attempt to expand the term "obligations" to include the Consulting Agreement "contradicts the plain language of the agreement limiting the assumption to the partnership interest." This Court got it right: National Material is not liable under Count IX.

---

## III. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY DOES NOT APPLY

The plaintiff asserts that National Material is liable as the "successor" to PBS One under the mere continuation doctrine. This Court correctly rejected this argument.

### A. No Express Assumption of Liability

As discussed in Section II, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement by National Material. The Assumption Agreement limited the assumption to the partnership interest, not external contractual obligations.

### B. No De Facto Merger

The transaction was not a de facto merger. Critically, "PBS One continued to exist for three years after the 1991 sale and was not dissolved until 1994." A de facto merger requires the predecessor to cease existence as a separate entity, which did not occur here.

### C. No Mere Continuation

The plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labeled National Material the "successor" to PBS One. However, this Court correctly held that "this language appears in the context of the partnership governance and does not establish general successor liability for the predecessor's debts."

The mere continuation doctrine "requires a continuity of ownership, assets, and business operations such that the successor is essentially the same entity." *Mickes v. Polymer Corp.*, 109 Ill. App. 3d 356, 440 N.E.2d 114 (1982). While Tang owned both PBS One and the general partner of National Material, "the maintenance of separate corporate formalities and the fact that PBS One was a distinct entity holding a specific investment interest negates the finding of a mere continuation."

Furthermore, "the 1993 amendment cannot retroactively create liability where none existed in the 1991 transfer." The plaintiff cannot use a 1993 document to impose liability that was clearly not assumed in 1991.

This Court correctly found that successor liability does not apply, and we ask this Court to affirm the ruling on Count X.

---

## IV. COUNT XI: THE DEFENSE'S POSITION AND RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The defense acknowledges that this Court has ruled against us on Count XI. However, we respond to the plaintiff's mischaracterizations as follows:

### A. The Defense Did Not Argue That Acceptance of Payments Constitutes Novation

The plaintiff mischaracterizes the defense's position by claiming we argued that "a creditor's mere acceptance of payments from a new party does not, by itself, release the original obligor" is a "dangerous misstatement of Illinois law." This is a distortion of the defense's argument.

The defense never claimed that mere acceptance of payments constitutes novation. Rather, the defense argued that the totality of the circumstances—including the explicit assumption by Pielet LP, the restructuring of the entire business, Arthur's awareness and participation in the meetings, and his acceptance of payments for over a decade—created a genuine issue of material fact as to whether a novation occurred.

### B. The Evidence Supports a Finding of Novation

The undisputed facts demonstrate:

1. **Explicit Assumption by Pielet LP**: Attorney Zavis's affidavit stated that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement, that the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement, and would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]."

2. **Arthur Was Informed and Agreed**: Zavis testified that he explained to Arthur "that the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement" and that Arthur "agreed thereto expressing his desire to have the transaction concluded as soon as possible since the new entity would be financially stronger than [Pielet Corp.]."

3. **Payments Made for Over a Decade**: Arthur received payments from Pielet LP (later renamed Midwest Metallics) from 1988 until July 1998—a period of ten years.

4. **Arthur Never Objected**: Arthur never objected to the payments or asserted that PBS One remained liable. He accepted the new entity's performance for over a decade.

While this Court found that Zavis's deposition testimony creates a genuine issue of material fact, the defense submits that the totality of the evidence strongly supports the novation defense. The plaintiff cannot simply ignore the explicit assumption by Pielet LP, Arthur's agreement to the transaction, and a decade of payments from the new entity.

### C. The Survival Statute Argument

The defense maintains its position that the Survival Statute does not apply where a valid novation occurred. If the obligation was effectively transferred to Pielet LP in 1988, then PBS One had no obligation at the time of its dissolution in 1994. The plaintiff's reliance on *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 585 N.E.2d 568 (1991), and *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 430 N.E.2d 101 (1981), assumes that an obligation existed at dissolution. That assumption is contested precisely because of the novation defense.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling on the appeals regarding the trial court’s summary judgment decisions on Counts IX, X, and XI. The Court has carefully reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the undisputed facts of record, and the applicable Illinois law. The central issue on appeal is whether the defendants, National Material, NM Holding, PBS One, and Tang, are liable for the breach of the Consulting Agreement. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms in part and reverses in part the trial court’s rulings.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

On review of a summary judgment ruling, the Court must construe the record in the light most favorable to the non-moving party and determine whether a genuine issue of material fact exists. Summary judgment is appropriate only where the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Liability of PBS One**

The trial court granted summary judgment for the defense on Count XI, finding that PBS One was not liable because its obligation had been extinguished by novation prior to its dissolution. The Court previously reversed this ruling, finding a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether a novation occurred. Having reviewed the closing arguments, the Court adheres to its determination that the defense has not met its burden to prove novation as a matter of law.

The defense argues that the "totality of the circumstances"—including the restructuring of the business, the explicit assumption by Pielet LP, and Arthur's acceptance of payments for over a decade—demonstrates a novation. The prosecution correctly counters that Illinois law requires "an agreement by the creditor to accept performance from a new party and the intent to release the original debtor" for a novation to be valid. *Thompson v. Postal Life Insurance Co.*, 226 Ill. 594, 602, 80 N.E. 1101, 1104 (1907). Mere acceptance of payments from a third party, without more, does not constitute a release of the original obligor. *First National Bank of Chicago v. First National Bank of Chicago*, 29 Ill. App. 3d 1031, 1034, 331 N.E.2d 757, 760 (1975).

The record contains deposition testimony from Attorney Zavis, the attorney for the restructuring entities, which directly contradicts the defense's assertion of intent to release. Zavis testified that he never asked Arthur to release his rights against the original obligors and that it was not his intent to effect a novation. Specifically, Zavis stated: "Nobody ever asked you to get a release from him?" "No." "Nobody ever asked you to get a— to release his rights against [Pielet Corp.]?" "No." "Was it your intent to work a novation for anyone here?" "No." This testimony creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding Arthur's intent to release PBS One.

Furthermore, the Consulting Agreement contained a clause binding the parties' "successors and assigns." Under *Hawthorne v. C. & L. Enterprises, Inc.*, 35 Ill. 2d 384, 387, 220 N.E.2d 201, 203 (1966), such a clause creates a presumption that the original parties remain liable unless there is a clear showing of a novation. The defense has not met this high burden.

Because the obligation was not extinguished by novation, it remained in effect when PBS One dissolved in 1994. Under the Survival Statute, dissolution does not terminate contractual obligations; it merely suspends them. *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1, 9, 585 N.E.2d 568, 572 (1991). A breach occurring after dissolution is enforceable if the obligation existed prior to dissolution. *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522, 526, 430 N.E.2d 101, 104 (1981). Therefore, the trial court's grant of summary judgment for the defense on Count XI is reversed.

**B. Count IX: Liability of National Material under the Assumption Agreement**

The trial court denied summary judgment for the defense on Count IX, but the Court previously ruled that summary judgment for the defense was appropriate. The defense's closing argument reinforces this conclusion. The plaintiff argues that National Material assumed the obligations of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement, via the January 1991 Assumption Agreement.

The Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One’s right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest." This language unambiguously limits the scope of the assumption to the interest held within the partnership. It does not state that National Material assumed the general liabilities of PBS One or the specific external contracts of the partnership.

Under *Kawasaki Heavy Industries, Ltd. v. Paramount Metals, Inc.*, 65 Ill. App. 3d 357, 361, 382 N.E.2d 262, 265 (1978), "a partner's interest in a partnership is his share of the profits and surplus, and the mere transfer of that interest does not transfer the partnership's liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership (Pielet LP), not a personal obligation of PBS One as a limited partner. By purchasing PBS One’s limited partnership interest, National Material stepped into PBS One’s shoes as a limited partner; it did not assume the role of debtor under the partnership’s contracts. The plaintiff's attempt to expand the term "obligations" to include the Consulting Agreement contradicts the plain language of the agreement. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count IX.

**C. Count X: Successor Liability of National Material**

The trial court denied summary judgment for the defense on Count X, but the Court previously ruled that summary judgment for the defense was appropriate. The defense's closing argument correctly applies the law regarding successor liability.

Successor liability generally arises in three instances: (1) express assumption of liability; (2) a de facto merger; or (3) where the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor. *Rabush v. Ex-Cell-O Corp.*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 106, 289 N.E.2d 677 (1972). As established in the analysis of Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement by National Material.

Regarding the "mere continuation" doctrine, the plaintiff relies on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which labeled National Material the "successor" to PBS One. However, this language appears in the context of partnership governance and does not establish general successor liability for the predecessor's debts. The "mere continuation" doctrine requires a continuity of ownership, assets, and business operations such that the successor is essentially the same entity. *Mickes v. Polymer Corp.*, 109 Ill. App. 3d 356, 440 N.E.2d 114 (1982). Here, PBS One continued to exist as a distinct entity for three years after the 1991 sale and was not dissolved until 1994. The maintenance of separate corporate formalities and the distinct nature of the investment interest held by PBS One negates a finding of a mere continuation. The 1993 amendment cannot retroactively create liability where none existed in the 1991 transfer. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count X.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's ruling insofar as it denied the plaintiff's claims against National Material and NM Holding on Counts IX and X, and grants summary judgment in favor of the defense on those counts. The Court reverses the trial court's ruling granting summary judgment for the defense on Count XI. The plaintiff's claim against PBS One and Tang on Count XI is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
