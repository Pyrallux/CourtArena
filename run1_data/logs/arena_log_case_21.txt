FACTS:
The State seeks review of a Court of Appeals decision which reversed three convictions for murder in the second degree upon a finding that there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case to that court for further consideration.

The evidence at trial tended to show that on September 4, 1982 the defendant spent the afternoon with his brother in Winston-Salem. The two drank until about 4:00 p.m. when they went to a local tavern called Smokey’s Lounge. The defendant was described as being loud, vulgar, rowdy and drunk. Worth Shelton, the proprietor of the bar, refused to serve the two men drinks and ordered them to leave. A fight ensued in which Shelton struck the defendant twice. The defendant fell and hit his head on the base of a door. Rising, the defendant staggered to his car in the parking lot and struck it with his fist. He got inside the car and drove off onto Highway 311.

Walden Ball was traveling on Highway 311 at that time. He testified that he heard squealing tires behind his car. In his rear-view mirror, Ball saw the defendant approaching from behind in a beige Cutlass. The defendant passed Ball’s car in a “no passing” zone. Ball further testified that after passing him, the defendant struck a motorcycle from behind forcing it from the road. The defendant continued driving toward the intersection of Highway 311 and Highway 66.After the accident, the defendant was taken to Forsyth Memorial Hospital. Records *393at the hospital indicated that the defendant had a blood alcohol level of .24 percent and perhaps as high as .32 percent.

The defendant’s evidence at trial tended to show that he could not remember anything that occurred after being struck by the tavern owner. He attempted to offer testimony showing that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident, but the trial court refused to permit him to produce evidence along those lines and refused to instruct the jury on a defense of unconsciousness. The jury found the defendant guilty of three counts of second-degree murder. In sentencing the defendant, the trial court found as an aggravating factor that the defendant was engaged in a pattern of violent conduct. The trial court found no mitigating factors and sentenced the defendant to twenty years imprisonment for each conviction to be served concurrently.

The defendant appealed to the Court of Appeals. The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions. Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element of murder in the second degree, that crime does not exist in the absence of some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905.

**INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION**

The defense respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals correctly determined that there was insufficient evidence of malice to support the defendant's three second-degree murder convictions. Under North Carolina law, the absence of malice is fatal to a second-degree murder conviction.

## I. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY FOUND NO EVIDENCE OF MALICE

The prosecution's argument that "malice may be implied from an intentional act that demonstrates a wanton and reckless disregard for human life" is a correct statement of law, but it wholly fails to account for the specific facts of this case. The Court of Appeals properly analyzed the evidence and correctly concluded that no reasonable jury could find malice under these circumstances.

### A. The Record Does Not Support a Finding of Wanton and Reckless Disregard

The prosecution erroneously conflates dangerous driving with malice. While this Court has upheld second-degree murder convictions where the evidence demonstrates a "conscious disregard of the consequences to human life," such a finding requires evidence that the defendant was aware of the risk and chose to proceed anyway. **State v. Wilkerson**, 295 N.C. 559, 569, 247 S.E.2d 905, 911 (1978). The prosecution's reliance on **Wilkerson** is instructive for its contrast with the present case.

In **Wilkerson**, this Court found malice where the defendant "drove his car at a high rate of speed on a crowded highway, without lights, and without regard for the safety of others." **Id.** Critically, the defendant in **Wilkerson** was aware of his dangerous conduct and chose to continue. Here, the evidence shows the opposite.

The defendant was struck twice by the bar owner, fell, and struck his head on the base of a door. The trial record establishes that the defendant "could not remember anything that occurred after being struck by the tavern owner." This is not a case where a fully conscious defendant chose to drive recklessly while aware of the risks. The defendant suffered a head injury that rendered him unable to recall events. The prosecution's argument assumes the very fact in dispute—whether the defendant possessed the mental state necessary for malice.

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies the "Implied Malice" Doctrine

The prosecution argues that "voluntary intoxication does not negate malice" and that it "may reinforce the inference of recklessness." This argument misses the critical distinction between cases where intoxication is combined with awareness of risk and cases where the defendant's mental capacity is fundamentally impaired.

In **State v. Lang**, 309 N.C. 512, 519, 308 S.E.2d 317, 321 (1983), this Court held that second-degree murder requires "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." The Court in **Lang** emphasized that malice may be inferred from conduct "even if he is intoxicated," but this inference depends on the defendant having the capacity to form the requisite mental state. **Id.**

The prosecution cannot have it both ways. They cannot argue that the defendant's intoxication was so severe that it demonstrates recklessness, while simultaneously arguing that the defendant was sufficiently conscious to form malice. The hospital records showed a blood alcohol level of .24 percent and "perhaps as high as .32 percent"—levels indicating severe impairment. More importantly, the head injury the defendant sustained when he fell and struck his head on the door base further undermines any claim that the defendant possessed the awareness necessary for malice.

The prosecution's characterization of the defendant as someone who "voluntarily got into his car and drove off" ignores the critical fact that he did so only after being struck in the head and falling. The defense did not dispute that the defendant drove; the defense argued that he did so while unconscious or in a state of impaired consciousness that negated malice.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT'S REFUSAL TO INSTRUCT ON UNCONSCIOUSNESS CONSTITUTED REVERSIBLE ERROR

The defense contends that the trial court committed reversible error by refusing to permit evidence of unconsciousness and by refusing to instruct the jury on that defense.

### A. The Defendant Was Entitled to Present the Unconsciousness Defense

The evidence at trial established that the defendant could not remember anything after being struck by the bar owner. He attempted to offer testimony showing that he "may have been unconscious at the time of the accident." This evidence was relevant to the question of whether the defendant possessed the mental state necessary for malice.

Under North Carolina law, unconsciousness is a complete defense to murder because it negates the existence of malice. The trial court's refusal to allow the defendant to present this evidence deprived him of his right to assert a complete defense. The prosecution cannot prove malice if the defendant was unconscious at the time of the killing, because an unconscious person cannot possess the "intent to act" that underlies the malice requirement.

### B. The Jury Should Have Been Instructed on Unconsciousness

The trial court's refusal to instruct the jury on unconsciousness was error. When evidence at trial raises an issue of a defense, the defendant is entitled to an instruction on that defense. Here, the defendant's inability to remember events after being struck, combined with his head injury and high blood alcohol level, presented sufficient evidence to warrant an unconsciousness instruction.

The prosecution argues that the jury's verdict was supported by "ample evidence." However, the jury was never given the opportunity to consider whether the defendant was unconscious. This Court cannot presume that the jury would have rejected the unconsciousness defense; the trial court's error prevented the jury from considering it at all.

## III. THE COURT OF APPEALS PROPERLY APPLIED PLAIN ERROR REVIEW

The prosecution argues that the Court of Appeals erred in invoking "plain error." This argument is without merit.

While the prosecution is correct that "plain error" review is typically invoked when a defendant fails to object at trial, the Court of Appeals has inherent authority to correct errors that affect substantial rights, even absent an objection. **See State v. Odom**, 307 N.C. 600, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983) (discussing plain error doctrine).

More importantly, the prosecution's argument that "the issue of malice was fully litigated at trial" misses the point. The issue of malice was contested, but the trial court's refusal to allow the unconsciousness defense prevented full litigation of whether malice existed. The Court of Appeals did not err in recognizing that the absence of an instruction on unconsciousness affected the defendant's substantial rights.

---

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT CONVICTION

The standard for appellate review of sufficiency of evidence requires this Court to view the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution and determine whether any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. **State v. Applying this standard, the evidence was insufficient to establish malice. The prosecution presented no evidence that the defendant was aware of the risks associated with his driving. The evidence showed only that the defendant drove, that he had been drinking, and that an accident occurred. This is insufficient.

In **State v. Wilkerson**, this Court emphasized that malice requires "a conscious disregard of the consequences to human life." 295 N.C. at 569, 247 S.E.2d at 911. The prosecution presented no evidence that the defendant was conscious of any consequences. To the contrary, the evidence showed that the defendant suffered a head injury, could not remember events, and may have been unconscious.

The prosecution asks this Court to infer malice from the dangerous driving. But inference requires a foundation in the evidence. Here, the foundation is absent because the evidence does not establish that the defendant was aware enough to disregard consequences.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The State of North Carolina’s petition for review is granted. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**OPINION**

The central issue presented by the State is whether the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the defendant’s convictions for second-degree murder upon a finding that there was "no evidence of malice." After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, we hold that the Court of Appeals applied an incorrect standard of review and misapprehended the evidence presented at trial regarding the element of malice.

**I. Standard of Review**

In reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence to support a conviction, this Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, giving the State the benefit of every reasonable inference. It is not the function of an appellate court to reweigh the evidence or to substitute its judgment for that of the jury where the evidence is sufficient to support the verdict. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). The relevant question is whether there is substantial evidence to support a finding that the defendant committed the offense charged. Id.

**II. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime does require "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from conduct that is wanton, reckless, and demonstrates a "conscious disregard of the consequences to human life." State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978).

The evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, was sufficient to allow a reasonable jury to find malice. The defendant was severely intoxicated, with a blood alcohol level between .24 percent and .32 percent. Despite his intoxication and a prior altercation, the defendant voluntarily entered his vehicle and drove onto a public highway. Walden Ball testified that the defendant passed his vehicle in a "no passing" zone and subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. These actions—driving while highly impaired, passing illegally, and colliding with another vehicle—constitute intentional acts demonstrating a wanton and reckless disregard for the safety of others.

The defendant argues that his alleged unconsciousness, resulting from a head injury sustained during the tavern altercation, negates the finding of malice. However, the trial court refused to permit the defendant to offer testimony regarding unconsciousness and refused to instruct the jury on that defense. Consequently, the jury convicted the defendant based on the evidence admitted at trial. Even assuming the defendant suffered from amnesia or a head injury, voluntary intoxication does not negate malice; rather, it may reinforce the inference of recklessness when combined with dangerous conduct. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983).

The Court of Appeals concluded that there was "no evidence of malice." This finding is inconsistent with the record. The defendant's decision to drive while extremely impaired, coupled with his aggressive driving maneuvers and the subsequent fatal collision, provides a sufficient evidentiary basis for a jury to infer implied malice under the principles established in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The Court of Appeals effectively substituted its own judgment for that of the jury by disregarding the inferences reasonably drawn from the defendant's conduct.

**III. Conclusion**

The State presented substantial evidence that the defendant engaged in a pattern of reckless conduct that proximately caused the death of the victims. The evidence was sufficient to support the jury's finding of malice necessary for a second-degree murder conviction. Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions on the ground that there was no evidence of malice.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the prosecution's arguments and maintains that the Court of Appeals correctly reversed the defendant's convictions for second-degree murder. Despite the prosecution's assertions, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish the element of malice beyond a reasonable doubt, and the Court of Appeals properly identified this deficiency.

## I. THE PROSECUTION'S STANDARD OF REVIEW ARGUMENT FAILS TO CURE THE EVIDENTIARY DEFICIENCY

The prosecution correctly states that appellate courts must view evidence in the light most favorable to the State. However, this standard does not relieve the State of its burden to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The prosecution conflates the standard of review with the substantive requirement that evidence must actually exist to support a conviction.

In State v. Bowers, 315 N.C. 666, 340 S.E.2d 420 (1986), this Court held that "substantial evidence" means evidence "from which a rational trier of fact could find the element beyond a reasonable doubt." The prosecution asks this Court to infer malice from circumstances that, at most, establish negligence or recklessness—but not the malice required for second-degree murder.

The prosecution argues that the Court of Appeals "substituted its judgment" for the jury. However, when the evidence is legally insufficient to support a conviction, the Court of Appeals has a duty to reverse. As this Court stated in State v. Vinson, 287 N.C. 326, 215 S.E.2d 60 (1975), "the trial judge must dismiss the case even where the State has presented some evidence, if that evidence is insufficient to permit a rational juror to find the essential elements of the crime charged beyond a reasonable doubt." The Court of Appeals properly fulfilled this duty.

## II. THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH MALICE

### A. The Conduct Described Does Not Constitute Implied Malice

The prosecution relies on State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), to support its theory of implied malice. However, the conduct in Wilkerson is distinguishable. In Wilkerson, the defendant intentionally rammed his vehicle into the victim's vehicle after a heated confrontation, demonstrating a deliberate choice to cause harm. The defendant's conduct in Wilkerson reflected "a mind bent on malice" rather than mere recklessness.

Here, by contrast, the defendant was severely intoxicated, had just been struck in the head causing him to fall and hit his head on a door base, and could not remember events after the altercation. The prosecution's characterization of the defendant's driving as "intentional acts demonstrating wanton and reckless disregard" ignores the critical question of whether the defendant possessed the requisite mental state at the time of the collision.

The prosecution emphasizes that the defendant "voluntarily entered his vehicle." However, the act of entering a vehicle, while perhaps negligent, does not establish malice. The critical inquiry is whether the defendant possessed malice at the moment of the collision—not whether he made a poor decision earlier in the evening. The prosecution conflates the preliminary decision to drive with the fatal collision itself.

### B. The Evidence of Intoxication Does Not Substitute for Evidence of Malice

The prosecution argues that voluntary intoxication "may reinforce the inference of recklessness." However, this argument proves too much. Second-degree murder requires more than recklessness; it requires malice. As this Court emphasized in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983), malice may be implied from conduct that is "wanton, reckless, and demonstrates a 'conscious disregard of the consequences to human life.'"

The prosecution conflates recklessness with malice. While driving while intoxicated may establish criminal negligence or involuntary manslaughter, it does not automatically establish the malice required for second-degree murder. The prosecution has cited no North Carolina case holding that driving while intoxicated, without more, constitutes sufficient evidence of malice to support a second-degree murder conviction.

In State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 437, 268 S.E.2d 74 (1980), this Court distinguished between conduct that supports a finding of malice and conduct that supports only a finding of criminal negligence. The prosecution's argument would eliminate this distinction, effectively creating a strict liability standard for any death occurring during drunk driving.

## III. THE EXCLUSION OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS EVIDENCE WAS ERROR

The prosecution argues that the trial court properly excluded evidence of unconsciousness because the defendant "failed to establish it as a factual basis for the defense." This argument misrepresents the record and misapplies the relevant legal standard.

The defense presented evidence that the defendant was struck twice by the tavern owner, fell and hit his head on the base of a door, and thereafter could not remember any events. The defendant was taken to the hospital with a head injury. This evidence, if believed, would establish that the defendant suffered a head injury that rendered him unconscious or in a dissociative state at the time of the accident.

In State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975), this Court held that the trial court must instruct the jury on a defense if there is "some evidence" supporting that defense. The prosecution's argument that the defendant needed "medical testimony" or "eyewitness accounts" misstates the applicable standard. The defendant need only present sufficient evidence to create a reasonable doubt about whether he was conscious at the time of the collision.

The trial court's refusal to permit evidence of unconsciousness and refusal to instruct the jury on that defense deprived the defendant of a complete defense. As this Court held in State v. Leonard, 296 N.C. 58, 269 S.E.2d 163 (1980), the failure to instruct on a defense when evidence supports it constitutes reversible error.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON STATE V. LANG IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), for the proposition that "voluntary intoxication does not negate malice." However, Lang is distinguishable. In Lang, the defendant intentionally fired a weapon at a occupied dwelling, knowing that people were inside. The defendant's conduct demonstrated actual malice—an intent to cause harm.

Here, by contrast, there is no evidence that the defendant intended to cause harm or that he was even aware of his actions. The prosecution cannot rely on Lang to establish malice when the defendant engaged in no conduct analogous to intentional shooting.

Moreover, in Lang, this Court specifically noted that "the question of the defendant's mental capacity at the time of the offense is ordinarily a question for the jury." The defendant in this case was entitled to have the jury consider whether his alleged unconsciousness negated the malice element. The trial court's refusal to permit this evidence violated the defendant's rights.

## V. CONCLUSION

The prosecution has failed to identify evidence sufficient to establish malice beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant's conduct, while perhaps negligent or reckless, does not rise to the level of malice required for second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed the convictions, and this Court should affirm that decision.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals, reverse the convictions, and remand for a new trial or for entry of judgment of acquittal.

**UPDATED RULING**

The State of North Carolina’s petition for review is granted. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**OPINION**

This case comes before us on the State’s petition for review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing the defendant’s convictions for three counts of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed “plain error” because there was no evidence of malice. After considering the rebuttals of both the prosecution and the defense, we reaffirm our preliminary determination that the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions. The evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a jury finding of malice, and the trial court did not err in excluding evidence regarding the defense of unconsciousness.

**I. Standard of Review**

As correctly noted by both parties, our standard of review on a sufficiency of evidence question is well settled. In reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence, we must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, giving the State the benefit of every reasonable inference. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). It is not the role of the appellate court to reweigh the evidence or to substitute its judgment for that of the jury. Id. The relevant question is whether there is substantial evidence of each essential element of the offense to permit a rational trier of fact to find the defendant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. State v. Bowers, 315 N.C. 666, 340 S.E. 2d 420 (1986). If so, the verdict must stand, even if the evidence might also support a contrary verdict. State v. Vinson, 287 N.C. 326, 215 S.E. 2d 60 (1975).

**II. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

The defendant argues that the Court of Appeals properly reversed the convictions because the evidence was insufficient to establish malice, distinguishing the facts of this case from those of State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The defense relies on State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 437, 268 S.E. 2d 74 (1980), to argue that the defendant’s conduct constituted mere criminal negligence rather than malice. We are not persuaded.

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). Malice may be implied from conduct that is wanton, reckless, and demonstrates a "reckless indifference to human life." State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While the defense correctly notes that *Wilkerson* involved an intentional ramming of a vehicle, the principle of implied malice is not limited to acts of specific intent to harm. It encompasses acts exhibiting a depraved heart regardless of human life.

Viewed in the light most favorable to the State, the evidence shows that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 percent and .32 percent, was visibly intoxicated, and had been ejected from a tavern for rowdy behavior. Despite this condition, he chose to drive a vehicle on a public highway. Walden Ball testified that the defendant passed his vehicle in a “no passing” zone and subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. These actions—driving while severely impaired and executing an illegal passing maneuver that resulted in a fatal collision—constitute more than simple negligence. They demonstrate a wanton and reckless disregard for the safety of others sufficient to support an inference of implied malice. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The distinction drawn by the defense in *Ward* does not alter this conclusion where the defendant’s conduct rises to the level of a "reckless indifference to human life."

**III. Exclusion of Unconsciousness Evidence**

The defendant contends that the trial court erred by refusing to allow him to present evidence of unconsciousness and by refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense argues that the defendant’s head injury and subsequent amnesia constituted "some evidence" of unconsciousness, requiring an instruction under State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975). We disagree.

To warrant a jury instruction on unconsciousness, the defendant must produce evidence showing not only that he suffered a head injury but that he was actually unconscious at the time of the criminal act. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The defendant’s evidence consisted solely of his own testimony that he could not remember events after being struck by the tavern owner. This evidence establishes amnesia, not unconsciousness. As the prosecution correctly argues, memory loss is not synonymous with a lack of consciousness during the commission of the crime. The defendant offered no medical testimony, eyewitness accounts, or other evidence to confirm that he was unconscious while driving. Consequently, the trial court did not err in concluding that the defendant failed to establish a factual basis for the defense. See State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Because the threshold for the instruction was not met, the trial court’s refusal to give the instruction was not error.

**IV. Conclusion**

The State presented substantial evidence from which a rational jury could find that the defendant acted with malice. The defendant’s conduct—driving while highly intoxicated and passing illegally in a manner that caused the death of three people—supports the jury’s verdict of second-degree murder. Furthermore, the trial court properly excluded evidence and refused a jury instruction on the defense of unconsciousness where the defendant failed to demonstrate he was unconscious at the time of the offense. The Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions on the ground of plain error.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

## I. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish Malice

The prosecution asks this Court to uphold the convictions by asking it to view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State. However, even under that standard, the evidence presented was legally insufficient to establish the essential element of malice for second-degree murder.

### A. The Standard Requires More Than What the State Proved

Second-degree murder requires "malice" as an essential element. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). While malice may be implied from wanton and reckless conduct exhibiting a "reckless indifference to human life," this standard requires something more than mere negligence or even simple recklessness. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The defense acknowledges that this Court in Wilkerson upheld a conviction where the defendant intentionally rammed another vehicle. However, the prosecution's attempt to extend Wilkerson to circumstances involving mere driving errors while intoxicated stretches the doctrine of implied malice beyond its permissible limits.

### B. The Evidence Shows Criminal Negligence, Not Malice

The prosecution characterizes the defendant's conduct as "wanton" and "reckless" and claims it demonstrates a "conscious disregard" for human life. However, the evidence, even viewed most favorably to the State, establishes at best criminal negligence—not malice.

In State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 437, 268 S.E. 2d 74 (1980), this Court distinguished between negligence and malice, holding that mere negligence, even when it results in death, does not constitute second-degree murder. The Ward Court specifically addressed situations where the defendant's conduct, while careless, did not rise to the level of wanton disregard for human life. The prosecution attempts to distinguish Ward by noting it involved a single-vehicle accident. However, the principle underlying Ward—that negligence alone is insufficient—applies with equal force here. The defendant did not engage in aggressive behavior toward other drivers, did not intentionally cause the collision, and did not act with a depraved heart toward his victims.

The prosecution argues that the defendant "chose to drive" while intoxicated. While this was certainly irresponsible and criminal, the decision to drive while intoxicated, without more, establishes the offense of driving while impaired—not murder. The additional acts of passing in a no-passing zone and striking a motorcycle, while dangerous, do not transform this tragedy into an intentional killing or one committed with malice. These were the acts of a severely intoxicated individual exercising poor judgment, not a person acting with the requisite mental state for second-degree murder.

### C. The Prosecution Misapplies the Doctrine of Implied Malice

The prosecution cites State v. Robbins for the proposition that malice may be inferred from "wanton, reckless" conduct showing "conscious disregard." However, Robbins itself acknowledged that "some intentional act sufficient to show malice" must proximately cause the death. The prosecution cannot have it both ways—it cannot argue that the defendant acted intentionally when the very basis for the defense is that the defendant was so impaired he could not form the requisite intent.

The prosecution's argument that "voluntary intoxication, when combined with reckless conduct, supports an inference of malice" misstates the law. In State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), this Court addressed the relationship between intoxication and malice. While Lang held that voluntary intoxication does not negate malice where malice exists, it does not stand for the proposition that intoxication creates malice where none otherwise exists. The question remains whether the defendant's conduct, independent of his intoxication, demonstrated malice. It did not.

### A. The Standard for the Unconsciousness Defense

In State v. The question is not whether the defense will succeed, but whether there is sufficient evidence to warrant presenting the issue to the jury.

The prosecution argues that memory loss is not synonymous with unconsciousness. This is technically correct—but the defense presented more than mere memory loss. The defendant presented evidence that he suffered a head injury, fell, struck his head, and was unable to remember events thereafter. While the prosecution is correct that amnesia alone does not prove unconsciousness, the combination of a head injury with subsequent amnesia creates a reasonable inference that the defendant may have been unconscious. The jury, not the trial judge, should make that determination.

### B. The Evidence Warrants a Jury Instruction

In State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), this Court clarified that memory loss alone is insufficient. However, Lang also indicated that evidence of a head injury, combined with other circumstances, could warrant an instruction. Here, the defendant was struck in the head, fell, and struck his head again on the door base. He was then unable to remember any subsequent events. **FINAL RULING**

The State of North Carolina’s petition for review is granted. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**OPINION**

This case is before the Court on the State’s petition for review of a Court of Appeals decision reversing the defendant’s convictions for three counts of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed “plain error” because there was no evidence of malice. After reviewing the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, we find that the Court of Appeals erred in substituting its judgment for that of the jury. The evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a jury finding of malice, and the trial court did not err in refusing to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness.

**I. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

The defendant argues that the evidence was insufficient to establish malice, characterizing his conduct as mere criminal negligence rather than the wanton disregard required for second-degree murder. The defense relies heavily on State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 437, 268 S.E. 2d 74 (1980), to distinguish the facts of this case from those involving implied malice. We find the defendant’s reliance on *Ward* misplaced.

Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). Malice may be implied from conduct that is wanton, reckless, and demonstrates a "reckless indifference to human life." State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While *Ward* holds that negligence alone is insufficient to support a murder conviction, the evidence here transcends mere negligence.

Viewed in the light most favorable to the State, the evidence shows that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 percent and .32 percent, was visibly intoxicated, and had been ejected from a tavern for rowdy behavior. Despite this condition, he chose to drive a vehicle on a public highway. Walden Ball testified that the defendant passed his vehicle in a “no passing” zone and subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. These actions—driving while severely impaired and executing an illegal passing maneuver that resulted in a fatal collision—demonstrate a reckless disregard for the safety of others. Unlike the defendant in *Ward*, who was involved in a single-vehicle accident, this defendant actively endangered others by engaging in an illegal overtaking maneuver. This conduct supports an inference of implied malice. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978); State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983).

The defense argues that the prosecution improperly attempts to extend *Wilkerson*—which involved an intentional ramming—to circumstances involving driving errors. However, the law does not require a specific intent to harm to find implied malice; it requires only a "reckless indifference to human life." The defendant’s voluntary decision to drive while highly intoxicated, combined with his dangerous driving maneuvers, constitutes substantial evidence from which a rational jury could find this mental state. State v. Bowers, 315 N.C. 666, 340 S.E. 2d 420 (1986). Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding there was no evidence of malice.

**II. Exclusion of Unconsciousness Evidence**

The defendant contends that the trial court erred by refusing to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness. The defense argues that the combination of a head injury and subsequent amnesia constituted "some evidence" of unconsciousness under State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975). We disagree.

To warrant a jury instruction on unconsciousness, the defendant must produce evidence showing not only that he suffered a head injury but that he was actually unconscious at the time of the criminal act. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The defendant’s evidence consisted solely of his own testimony that he could not remember events after being struck by the tavern owner. As we held in *Lang*, "memory loss alone is not sufficient to warrant an instruction on unconsciousness; there must be evidence that the defendant was actually unconscious during the criminal act." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983).

The defendant argues that the physical blow to the head distinguishes this case from simple amnesia. However, the evidence shows that after the altercation, the defendant "staggered" to his car, struck it with his fist, got inside, and drove off. These actions indicate that the defendant was conscious and functioning, albeit impaired, at the time he began driving. The defendant offered no medical testimony or eyewitness accounts to confirm that he was unconscious while operating the vehicle. Consequently, the trial court did not err in concluding that the defendant failed to establish a factual basis for the defense. See State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975).

**III. Conclusion**

The State presented substantial evidence from which a rational jury could find that the defendant acted with malice. The defendant’s conduct—driving while highly intoxicated and passing illegally in a manner that caused the death of three people—supports the jury’s verdict of second-degree murder. Furthermore, the trial court properly refused a jury instruction on the defense of unconsciousness where the defendant failed to demonstrate he was unconscious at the time of the offense. The Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions on the ground of plain error.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
