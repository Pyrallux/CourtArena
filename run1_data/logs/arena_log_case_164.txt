### OPENING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLANT  
**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III**  

May it please the Court: Today, this Court must correct a fundamental error in the trial court’s decision to modify and extend a temporary injunction enforcing a noncompetition agreement against Mr. Bradley. The trial court’s ruling was legally unsound because it failed to address critical defenses raised by Mr. Bradley—defenses that, if proven, would completely extinguish the employer’s right to enforce the covenant. Under settled Florida law, a trial court abuses its discretion when it grants injunctive relief without resolving factual disputes over whether the employer materially breached the underlying contract. **We respectfully submit that the trial court erred as a matter of law, and this Court must reverse.**  

#### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY IGNORING MR. BRADLEY’S DEFENSES OF MATERIAL BREACH  
Florida law is unequivocal: **an employer’s material breach of an employment contract releases the employee from post-termination obligations under a noncompetition covenant.** *Hill v. Health Care Servs. Corp.*, 753 So. 2d 63, 65 (Fla. 1st DCA 2000). Here, Mr. Bradley raised two specific, credible allegations of material breach:  
- The employer **wrongfully refused to pay earned commissions** owed to him under the employment agreement;  
- The employer **ordered him to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed upon**, constituting fraud and a breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing.  

The trial court’s failure to address these defenses was reversible error. At the injunction hearing, the employer merely denied the allegations—without providing any evidence to rebut them. Yet the court proceeded to modify and extend the injunction as if these factual disputes were irrelevant. This violates the core principle that **a temporary injunction may not issue where the moving party fails to establish a *substantial likelihood of success on the merits* due to unresolved defenses.** *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683, 685 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004) (holding that "courts must resolve factual conflicts regarding material breaches before granting injunctive relief"). By ignoring these defenses, the trial court effectively treated Mr. Bradley’s allegations as "unproven" rather than "disputed"—a standard inconsistent with Florida law.  

#### II. THE TRIAL COURT’S INJUNCTION LACKED A FOUNDATION IN THE LEGAL ELEMENTS FOR RELIEF  
A temporary injunction requires the movant to prove *all* elements, including **irreparable harm and the absence of an adequate remedy at law**. *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 953 (Fla. 1986). Here, the noncompetition agreement explicitly includes a **liquidated damages clause**—a provision that directly undermines the claim of irreparable harm. When a contract specifies a fixed remedy for breach, courts must presume monetary damages are adequate unless the movant proves otherwise. *Surgitech*, 498 So. 2d at 954 ("Liquidated damages clauses are presumptively valid and preclude a finding of irreparable harm"). The trial court made no findings on this point. Instead, it mechanically enforced the injunction without addressing whether the liquidated damages clause satisfied the "adequate remedy at law" requirement. This was legal error.  

#### III. THE TRIAL COURT VIOLATED FUNDAMENTAL PROCEDURAL SAFEGUARDS  
Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610 requires that injunction orders "state the findings of fact and conclusions of law" supporting the ruling. *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987). The trial court’s order here contains **no analysis whatsoever of Mr. Bradley’s material breach defenses**. It does not address the commission dispute, the alleged invoice fraud, or whether the employer’s conduct voided the covenant. Instead, it recites boilerplate language about "protecting confidential information" while ignoring the factual record. This failure to "state findings" renders the order legally insufficient. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302 ("An injunction lacking specific findings on disputed facts is an abuse of discretion").  

#### CONCLUSION  
The trial court’s modification and extension of the temporary injunction was a compound error: it disregarded settled law on material breach defenses, ignored the legal effect of the liquidated damages clause, and failed to provide the required factual findings. Mr. Bradley’s allegations—if true—would absolve him of all obligations under the noncompetition agreement. The trial court should have conducted a full evidentiary hearing to resolve these disputes before granting injunctive relief. Instead, it shortcut the process and violated Mr. Bradley’s due process rights. **For these reasons, this Court must reverse and remand for proceedings consistent with Florida law.**

# RESPONSE BRIEF FOR APPELLEE HEALTH COALITION, INC.

## IN THE DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA

---

May it please the Court: Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this response to the opening argument of Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III. While Mr. Bradley presents a colorful narrative of alleged employer misconduct, his argument fundamentally misapprehends the applicable legal standards and ignores the record evidence supporting the trial court's order. This Court should affirm the temporary injunction.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting injunctive relief. The noncompetition agreement Mr. Bradley signed is enforceable under Florida law, and he has failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion. Mr. Bradley's arguments rest on unproven allegations that the trial court was not required to credit, and his reliance on the liquidated damages clause misstates Florida law.

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY DENIED MR. BRADLEY'S DEFENSES

### A. Mr. Bradley's Allegations of Material Breach Are Disputed Factual Claims That Do Not Require the Denial of Injunctive Relief

Mr. Bradley contends that the trial court erred by failing to resolve his "material breach" defenses before granting injunctive relief. This argument mischaracterizes the nature of a temporary injunction proceeding and places an impossible burden on the trial court.

Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is an equitable remedy designed to preserve the status quo pending a full adjudication on the merits. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). The purpose is not to resolve contested factual disputes but to prevent irreparable harm during the pendency of litigation. The trial court is not required to make final determinations of fact at the injunction stage.

Mr. Bradley relies on *Hill v. Health Care Servs. Corp.*, 753 So. 2d 63 (Fla. 1st DCA 2000), but that case is distinguishable. In *Hill*, the appellate court reversed because the trial court had made explicit findings that the employer had materially breached the contract—findings that were supported by the record. Here, by contrast, the trial court made no such findings because the evidence was disputed. Mr. Bradley's allegations remain just that—allegations.

The employer specifically denied that any instruction was given to alter invoices, and the commission dispute involves questions of contract interpretation and fact that are properly resolved at trial, not at a preliminary injunction hearing. *Wolverine Tube, Inc. v. Metalcrafters*, 763 So. 2d 405, 407 (Fla. 5th DCA 2000) (holding that disputed facts regarding contract interpretation should not be resolved at the injunction stage).

### B. Mr. Bradley Has Not Established That Any Alleged Breach Was Material

Even accepting Mr. Bradley's version of events for purposes of argument, he has not demonstrated that any alleged breach was "material" under Florida law. A material breach must go to the essence of the contract, not merely constitute a minor or incidental dispute. *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000).

The commission dispute, even if true, involves amounts that appear to be contested rather than undisputed. A dispute over the precise amount of commissions due does not constitute a material breach releasing Mr. Bradley from his post-termination obligations. *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219, 1223 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000) (holding that disputes over bonus calculations do not constitute material breach releasing employee from restrictive covenants).

Similarly, Mr. Bradley's allegation that he was asked to alter invoices—even if credited—does not automatically void a noncompetition covenant. Mr. Bradley was free to refuse the instruction, and he did. The appropriate remedy for such alleged misconduct would be a cause of action for wrongful termination or a claim for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing—not automatic release from his contractual obligations.

---

## III. THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

Mr. Bradley argues that the presence of a liquidated damages clause in the noncompetition agreement automatically negates the employer's claim of irreparable harm. This argument misreads Florida law.

### A. Liquidated Damages and Injunctive Relief May Coexist

In *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951 (Fla. 1986), the Florida Supreme Court did not hold that liquidated damages clauses categorically preclude injunctive relief. Rather, the Court recognized that such clauses are presumptively valid and may be enforced as an alternative remedy. The Court specifically noted that injunctive relief remains available when necessary to protect legitimate business interests that cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages.

The noncompetition covenant here protects the employer's relationships with its "active, paid up customers"—a legitimate business interest that goes beyond mere financial compensation. Customer relationships, goodwill, and confidential business information represent intangible assets that cannot be readily valued or replaced through liquidated damages. * Siegel v. LePore*, 234 F.3d 1163, 1176 (11th Cir. 2000) (applying Florida law and recognizing that injunctive relief is appropriate to protect customer relationships).

### B. The Employer Has No Adequate Remedy at Law

The very nature of a noncompetition covenant demonstrates why liquidated damages are inadequate. If Mr. Bradley is permitted to solicit the employer's customers during the two-year restricted period, those relationships may be permanently damaged or destroyed. The employer cannot un-sell blood plasma products to customers who have been diverted to a competitor. Money damages would be speculative and difficult to prove. *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002) (holding that irreparable harm may be presumed when a covenant not to compete is violated because the injury is inherently difficult to quantify).

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT'S ORDER ADEQUATELY STATES ITS FINDINGS

Mr. Bradley argues that the trial court's order lacks the required findings under Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610. This argument is without merit.

### A. The Order Contains Sufficient Findings

The trial court's order, as modified and extended, specifically references the noncompetition agreement, identifies the restricted period, and notes that Mr. Bradley was employed as a salesperson and went to work for a competing company. The order enforces the covenant to protect the employer's business interests during the pendency of litigation.

While the order could have been more detailed, Rule 1.610 requires only that the order "state the findings of fact and conclusions of law" that are necessary to inform the parties of the basis for the ruling. *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987). The order here meets this standard. The boilerplate language Mr. Bradley criticizes is precisely the type of general findings that Florida courts have found sufficient in injunction proceedings. *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114, 1117 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003) (holding that trial courts are not required to make hyper-detailed findings at the temporary injunction stage).

### B. The Trial Court Properly Exercised Its Discretion

The standard of review for temporary injunctions is abuse of discretion. *Martinez v. Schedler*, 811 So. 2d 697, 700 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). The trial court heard argument, reviewed the contract, and determined that injunctive relief was appropriate. This Court should not substitute its judgment for that of the trial court merely because the trial court did not resolve every factual dispute in the manner Mr. Bradley would have preferred.

---

## V. THE EMPLOYER IS ENTITLED TO ENFORCE THE COVENANT

### A. The Covenant Is Reasonably Limited in Scope

Under Florida law, noncompetition agreements are enforceable if they are reasonable in duration, geographic scope, and scope of activity. *Wallace v. Baya*, 478 So. 2d 118, 121 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985). The agreement here prohibits solicitation of "active, paid up customers" for a period of two years—a duration and scope that Florida courts have consistently found reasonable. *Austin v. Fein*, 318 So. 2d 181, 184 (Fla. 3d DCA 1975) (holding that two-year covenants are presumptively reasonable).

### B. Mr. Bradley's Post-Employment Conduct Supports Injunctive Relief

The record establishes that Mr. Bradley went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and began selling blood plasma products. This conduct directly contravenes the covenant he signed. The employer's interest in preventing competitive use of customer relationships and confidential information is at its apex when the employee actually engages in the prohibited conduct. *Davis*, 832 So. 2d at 715 (holding that proof of actual competitive activity strengthens the case for injunctive relief).

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court affirm the temporary injunction. The trial court properly exercised its discretion, and Mr. Bradley has not demonstrated reversible error.

Respectfully submitted,

**[Appellee's Counsel]**

### PRELIMINARY RULING

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO. [CASE NUMBER]**

**OPINION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from a final order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III argues that the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction without addressing his affirmative defenses of material breach and without considering the effect of a liquidated damages clause. Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. contends that the injunction was proper to preserve the status quo and that the defenses raised were merely disputed factual issues inappropriate for resolution at the preliminary stage. Upon review of the arguments presented and the applicable law, we find that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction without making specific findings of fact regarding Appellant’s defenses. Accordingly, we reverse.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s order granting or denying a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Martinez v. Schedler*, 811 So. 2d 697, 700 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). A trial court abuses its discretion if its ruling is based on a misapplication of the law or a failure to make required findings of fact. *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would serve the public interest. *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 953 (Fla. 1986).

**II. MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSE**

Appellant argues that the trial court erred by failing to address his defense that Appellee materially breached the employment agreement, thereby releasing him from the non-compete obligations. Specifically, Appellant alleges that Appellee wrongfully refused to pay earned commissions and instructed him to alter invoices to defraud customers.

Under Florida law, an employer’s material breach of an employment contract releases the employee from post-termination restrictive covenants. *Hill v. Health Care Servs. Corp.*, 753 So. 2d 63, 65 (Fla. 1st DCA 2000). A material breach is one that "goes to the essence of the contract." *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000). While Appellee correctly notes that temporary injunctions are designed to preserve the status quo and not to resolve all factual disputes, *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002), a court cannot determine the "likelihood of success on the merits" while ignoring a defense that, if proven, would extinguish the movant's rights.

Appellee relies on *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219, 1223 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000), for the proposition that disputes over bonus calculations do not constitute a material breach. However, Appellant’s allegations regarding the instruction to commit fraud by altering invoices raise a more significant issue than a mere calculation dispute. Furthermore, the refusal to pay "earned" commissions, if proven, may constitute a failure of consideration. *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683, 685 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004) (holding that courts must resolve factual conflicts regarding material breaches before granting injunctive relief). The trial court’s failure to make findings on these specific allegations of material breach was error.

**III. LIQUIDATED DAMAGES AND IRREPARABLE HARM**

Appellant further contends that the presence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement precludes a finding of irreparable harm. We agree that this clause required specific analysis by the trial court.

In *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 954 (Fla. 1986), the Florida Supreme Court held that "liquidated damages clauses are presumptively valid and preclude a finding of irreparable harm" unless the movant demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate. Appellee argues that liquidated damages and injunctive relief may coexist because customer relationships are intangible assets that cannot be fully valued monetarily, citing *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). While this is a valid argument, the trial court was required to make an explicit finding on the record explaining why the liquidated damages clause was insufficient to make Appellee whole. The court’s failure to address this contractual provision constitutes legal error. *Surgitech*, 498 So. 2d at 954.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF FINDINGS**

Finally, Appellant argues that the trial court’s order violated Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610 by failing to state the necessary findings of fact and conclusions of law. Appellee counters that "boilerplate" findings are sufficient at the temporary injunction stage, citing *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114, 1117 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003).

While trial courts are not required to make "hyper-detailed" findings, they must address the specific legal and factual issues raised by the parties. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. Here, the order failed to address Appellant’s specific defenses of material breach and the applicability of the liquidated damages clause. An injunction that ignores the primary defenses raised by the non-movant cannot stand. The trial court abused its discretion by entering an order that did not demonstrate it considered the critical defenses raised by Appellant.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court abused its discretion by modifying and extending the temporary injunction without addressing Appellant’s defense of material breach and without analyzing the impact of the liquidated damages clause on the finding of irreparable harm. We reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**REBUTTAL BRIEF FOR APPELLANT MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III**  

**IN THE DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA**  

May it please the Court: Health Coalition, Inc. ("Appellee") mischaracterizes both the controlling law and the trial court’s procedural failures in granting the temporary injunction. Appellee’s argument ignores the trial court’s affirmative duty to address material defenses before issuing injunctive relief and misreads Florida precedent on liquidated damages clauses. For the reasons below, this Court should affirm the reversal of the injunction.  

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO ADDRESS MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSES  

Appellee claims the trial court was "not required to resolve contested factual disputes" at the injunction stage (*Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002)). This misstates the law. While temporary injunctions preserve the status quo, a court **cannot** determine "likelihood of success on the merits" while ignoring defenses that, if proven, would extinguish the movant’s rights. *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683, 685 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004). Here, Appellant raised two material breach defenses: (1) wrongful refusal to pay earned commissions, and (2) instructions to commit fraud by altering invoices.  

Appellee dismisses these as "unproven allegations," but the trial court was obligated to make findings on whether these defenses were colorable. *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987) (holding that a court abuses discretion by failing to "make required findings of fact"). The defense’s reliance on *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000), is inapposite. *Ursini* addressed disputes over *bonus calculations*—not allegations of intentional fraud or refusal to pay *earned* commissions. As *Bryant* makes clear, when an employee alleges an employer’s failure to pay earned wages or instructions to commit fraud, the trial court **must** resolve whether such conduct constitutes a material breach before granting an injunction.  

Appellee’s argument that "a dispute over commissions does not void a noncompete" is irrelevant. The issue is not whether the breach ultimately prevails at trial—it is whether the trial court properly considered the defense at the injunction stage. By failing to address these defenses, the trial court violated *Mason* and *Bryant*, rendering its order legally unsound.  

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY IGNORING THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE  

Appellee claims liquidated damages and injunctive relief may "coexist" because customer relationships are "intangible assets." This misreads *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951 (Fla. 1986). In *Surgitech*, the Florida Supreme Court held that liquidated damages clauses are "presumptively valid and preclude a finding of irreparable harm" **unless** the movant demonstrates monetary damages are inadequate. *Id.* at 954. The trial court’s failure to address this presumption was legal error.  

Appellee cites *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), but that case does not excuse the trial court’s omission. *Equity Holdings* merely confirms that injunctive relief may be available for intangible assets—but it requires the movant to *prove* monetary damages are inadequate. Here, the trial court made no such finding. It did not explain why the liquidated damages clause—which Appellee itself included in the contract—was insufficient to compensate for alleged harm. *Surgitech* requires this analysis; the trial court’s silence violated it.  

Appellee’s argument that "money damages would be speculative" is conclusory. The contract’s liquidated damages clause exists precisely to provide a predetermined remedy for breaches. By ignoring it, the trial court improperly elevated Appellee’s speculative claims over the parties’ contractual agreement.  

### III. THE TRIAL COURT’S FINDINGS VIOLATED RULE 1.610  

Appellee defends the order as "sufficient" under *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003). This is incorrect. *Bierter* permits "general" findings but requires them to address "the specific legal and factual issues raised by the parties." *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. Here, the trial court’s order:  
- Made no reference to Appellant’s material breach defenses;  
- Did not address the liquidated damages clause;  
- Failed to explain why irreparable harm existed despite the contract’s damages provision.  

This is not "boilerplate" but a complete failure to engage with the core defenses. *Mason* holds that an injunction "cannot stand" when the trial court ignores "the primary defenses raised by the non-movant." *Id.* at 1302. The trial court’s order violated this rule.  

### CONCLUSION  

Appellee’s arguments rest on a fundamental misunderstanding of Florida law. The trial court was not merely permitted—but **required**—to address Appellant’s material breach defenses and the liquidated damages clause before granting injunctive relief. Its failure to do so was an abuse of discretion. This Court should affirm the reversal of the injunction and remand for proceedings consistent with *Bryant*, *Surgitech*, and *Mason*.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL BRIEF FOR APPELLEE HEALTH COALITION, INC.

## IN THE DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA

---

May it please the Court: Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III ("Bradley") and his counsel have mischaracterized both the factual record and the applicable legal standards in their attempt to defend the trial court's reversal of the temporary injunction. This Court should not affirm the reversal; rather, this Court should reinstate the temporary injunction or clarify the proper standards for the trial court on remand. The arguments advanced by Appellant fail for the following reasons.

---

### I. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS DISCRETION IN GRANTING THE TEMPORARY INJUNCTION

#### A. The Standard for Temporary Injunctive Relief Does Not Require Resolution of All Defenses

The standard for obtaining a temporary injunction in Florida requires the movant to demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would serve the public interest. *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 953 (Fla. 1986). This standard is designed to preserve the status quo pending a full trial on the merits, not to resolve contested factual disputes. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002).

The trial court in this case properly applied this standard. The employment agreement containing the covenant not to compete was a valid contract. Health Coalition, Inc. ("Appellee") demonstrated that Bradley was employed as a salesperson for blood plasma products, that the employment agreement contained a valid two-year noncompete clause, and that Bradley went to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., in violation of that agreement. These facts establish a likelihood of success on the merits sufficient to support injunctive relief.

#### B. Bradley's Alleged Defenses Do Not Automatically Preclude Injunctive Relief

Appellant argues that his defenses of material breach required the trial court to deny the injunction. This argument misapprehends the nature of a temporary injunction proceeding. At this stage, the court need only determine whether the movant has demonstrated a likelihood of success—not whether the respondent will ultimately prevail on his defenses at trial. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 714.

Even accepting Bradley's allegations as true for purposes of the injunction hearing, he has not established that any alleged breach was "material" such that it would excuse his performance under the noncompete agreement. A material breach is one that "goes to the essence of the contract." *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000). The covenant not to compete is a separate agreement within the employment contract, and any alleged disputes over commissions or invoice instructions do not necessarily relate to the enforceability of the restrictive covenant.

#### C. The Case Law Cited by Appellant Does Not Support Reversal

Appellant relies heavily on *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), for the proposition that courts must resolve material breach defenses before granting injunctive relief. However, *Bryant* is distinguishable. In *Bryant*, the employee alleged that the employer had failed to pay wages owed under the contract—specifically, that the employer had improperly calculated and withheld earned wages. The court in *Bryant* held that the trial court should have made findings on whether the wage claims constituted a material breach. *Id.* at 685.

Here, by contrast, Bradley's allegations are far more attenuated. He claims that he was "instructed to alter certain invoices to charge higher prices than the customers had agreed to" and that this constituted "fraud on the customers." However, Bradley has presented no evidence—only his own self-serving allegations—that such instructions were given. The employer specifically denies that any such instruction was given. This is not a case where the employer admits to the conduct alleged but disputes its materiality; this is a case where the fundamental fact itself is disputed.

Similarly, Bradley's claim that the employer "wrongfully refused to pay certain commissions which he had earned" is disputed. The employer claims that Bradley voluntarily resigned and that any commission disputes relate to the calculation of bonuses, not a refusal to pay earned compensation. This Court in *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219, 1223 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000), held that disputes over bonus calculations do not constitute a material breach. The same principle applies here.

---

### II. THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

#### A. Liquidated Damages and Injunctive Relief May Coexist

Appellant argues that the presence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement precludes a finding of irreparable harm, citing *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951 (Fla. 1986). This argument overstates the holding of *Surgitech*. In *Surgitech*, the Florida Supreme Court held that liquidated damages clauses are "presumptively valid" but that they do not automatically preclude injunctive relief. *Id.* at 954. Rather, the court held that the presumption may be rebutted if the movant demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate.

The trial court in this case properly found that monetary damages would be inadequate because customer relationships are intangible assets that cannot be fully valued monetarily. This Court in *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), recognized that injunctive relief may be available to protect intangible assets, even when liquidated damages clauses exist in the contract. The trial court's finding on this issue was proper.

#### B. The Trial Court Made Sufficient Findings

 Appellant argues that the trial court's order failed to address the liquidated damages clause. However, the trial court's order did reference the covenant not to compete and the need to preserve the status quo. While the order could have been more detailed, Florida law does not require "hyper-detailed" findings at the temporary injunction stage. *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114, 1117 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003). The order was sufficient to demonstrate that the trial court considered the relevant factors.

---

### III. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS WERE ADEQUATE UNDER RULE 1.610

Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610 requires that temporary injunction orders state the findings of fact and conclusions of law that support the issuance of the injunction. However, this rule does not require the trial court to address every conceivable defense raised by the non-moving party. *Bierter*, 864 So. 2d at 1117.

The trial court's order in this case granted a temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete. The order referenced the existence of the employment agreement, the covenant not to compete, and Bradley's employment with a competitor. These findings are sufficient to support the injunction. The trial court was not required to make specific findings on every defense raised by Bradley, particularly where those defenses are disputed and would require evidentiary hearings to resolve.

---

### IV. THE PROPER REMEDY IS TO REINSTATE THE INJUNCTION OR CLARIFY STANDARDS FOR REMAND

Assuming arguendo that this Court agrees with the panel's conclusion that the trial court's findings were insufficient, the proper remedy is not simply to reverse and remand for further proceedings. This Court should either: (1) reinstate the temporary injunction with instructions to the trial court to make more detailed findings; or (2) clarify the specific findings required on remand so that the parties and the trial court have clear guidance.

The interests of justice require that the covenant not to compete be enforced pending trial. Bradley entered into a valid agreement not to compete for two years after the termination of his employment. He then went to work for a direct competitor in violation of that agreement. The harm to the employer's business relationships and confidential information is ongoing and cannot be remedied through monetary damages alone. The trial court should be directed to issue a temporary injunction preserving the status quo pending trial, with adequate findings to support that injunction.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should not affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction. Rather, this Court should either reinstate the injunction or clarify the standards for the trial court on remand. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting the injunction, and Bradley's arguments regarding material breach and liquidated damages do not require a different result.

### UPDATED RULING

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO. [CASE NUMBER]**

**OPINION**

ON APPEAL from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III challenges the trial court’s entry of a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. defends the court’s ruling. We have considered the rebuttals of both parties and affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Martinez v. Schedler*, 811 So. 2d 697, 700 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). A trial court abuses its discretion if it misapplies the law or fails to make required findings of fact. *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a threat of irreparable harm; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 953 (Fla. 1986).

**II. MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSE**

Appellee argues that the trial court properly exercised its discretion by granting the injunction without resolving Appellant’s defenses, asserting that temporary injunctions are meant to preserve the status quo, not resolve factual disputes. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). Appellee further contends that *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), is distinguishable because Appellant’s allegations of fraud and unpaid commissions are "attenuated" and "self-serving," unlike the wage claims in *Bryant*.

We are not persuaded. While we agree with Appellee that *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000), holds that disputes over bonus calculations do not constitute a material breach, Appellant’s allegations here go beyond mere calculation errors. Appellant alleges he was instructed to alter invoices to defraud customers and that he was refused payment of *earned* commissions. These allegations, if proven, constitute a material breach that goes to the essence of the contract. *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000).

As held in *Bryant*, courts must resolve factual conflicts regarding material breaches before granting injunctive relief. *Bryant*, 881 So. 2d at 685. The trial court cannot determine the "likelihood of success on the merits" while ignoring a defense that, if established, would extinguish the Appellee’s right to enforce the covenant. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. The fact that Appellant’s allegations are disputed does not relieve the trial court of its duty to make findings on whether these defenses are colorable. The trial court’s failure to address these specific allegations of material breach constituted an abuse of discretion.

**III. LIQUIDATED DAMAGES AND IRREPARABLE HARM**

Appellee argues that the liquidated damages clause does not preclude injunctive relief because customer relationships are intangible assets. *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). Appellee contends the trial court implicitly found that monetary damages were inadequate.

We acknowledge that liquidated damages and injunctive relief may coexist. However, *Surgitech* mandates that liquidated damages clauses are "presumptively valid and preclude a finding of irreparable harm" unless the movant demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate. *Surgitech*, 498 So. 2d at 954. The trial court’s order failed to make an explicit finding addressing this presumption or explaining why the liquidated damages clause was insufficient to make Appellee whole.

Appellee’s reliance on *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003), to argue that "hyper-detailed" findings are not required, is misplaced. While *Bierter* allows for general findings, it does not excuse a trial court from addressing a specific legal defense that directly impacts a required element of injunctive relief. *Surgitech* requires a specific analysis of the inadequacy of monetary damages when a liquidated damages clause is present. The trial court’s silence on this issue was legal error.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF FINDINGS**

Finally, Appellee asserts that the trial court’s findings were adequate under Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610 because they referenced the employment agreement and the need to preserve the status quo. We disagree.

As stated in *Mason*, a trial court must address the specific legal and factual issues raised by the parties. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. The trial court’s order failed to address Appellant’s primary defenses: material breach and the applicability of the liquidated damages clause. An injunction that ignores these defenses cannot stand. The trial court abused its discretion by entering an order that did not demonstrate it considered the critical defenses raised by Appellant.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court abused its discretion by modifying and extending the temporary injunction without addressing Appellant’s defense of material breach and without analyzing the impact of the liquidated damages clause on the finding of irreparable harm. We affirm the reversal of the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

A temporary injunction is not a vehicle for preserving the status quo while ignoring core defenses that would extinguish the movant’s right to enforce the covenant. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). The "likelihood of success on the merits" element *requires* the trial court to evaluate whether the movant’s claim is valid *in light of defenses that, if proven, would void the contract entirely*.  

Bradley alleged two material breaches: (1) being ordered to alter invoices to defraud customers, and (2) the wrongful refusal to pay earned commissions. These are not "attenuated" or "self-serving" claims, as the defense contends. They go to the very essence of the employment contract. *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000) (holding that breaches "going to the essence of the contract" excuse performance). A demand to commit fraud on customers—by falsifying invoices to charge higher prices than agreed—strikes at the core of ethical business conduct and contractual integrity. This is not a dispute over bonus calculations (as in *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219, 1223 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000)), but a direct allegation of illegal conduct by the employer. Similarly, the refusal to pay *earned* commissions is a breach that undermines the fundamental obligation of the employer to compensate for services rendered.  

*Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683, 685 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), squarely controls this issue. In *Bryant*, the court held that a trial court *must* resolve factual disputes over material breach defenses before granting injunctive relief. The trial court’s failure to even acknowledge Bradley’s allegations—let alone make findings on them—was an abuse of discretion. As this Court stated in Round 2: "The trial court cannot determine the 'likelihood of success on the merits' while ignoring a defense that, if established, would extinguish the Appellee’s right to enforce the covenant." *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987).  

The defense’s reliance on *Davis v. Monahan* is misplaced. *Davis* does not permit a trial court to "freeze" disputed facts in place; it requires the court to assess whether the movant has shown a *probable right to relief*. When material breach defenses exist, that "probable right" evaporates. The trial court’s refusal to engage with these defenses was not "preserving the status quo"—it was judicial abdication.  

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY FAILING TO ADDRESS THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE AND IRREPARABLE HARM  

The defense argues that liquidated damages clauses "do not preclude injunctive relief" and that the trial court "implicitly found" monetary damages inadequate. That is legally incorrect. *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 954 (Fla. 1986), holds that liquidated damages clauses create a *presumption* that monetary damages are adequate. To overcome this presumption, the movant must *explicitly demonstrate* why monetary relief is insufficient. The trial court’s order made *no such finding*. It did not reference the liquidated damages clause at all—let alone address why it was inadequate to compensate Health Coalition for Bradley’s alleged competition.  

The defense cites *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002), for the proposition that "intangible assets" justify injunctive relief. But *Equity Holdings* explicitly requires that the movant first rebut the *Surgitech* presumption. A trial court cannot simply declare "customer relationships are intangible" and assume irreparable harm exists. It must conduct a *specific analysis* of the liquidated damages clause. Here, the trial court did neither.  

The defense also claims *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114, 1117 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003), excuses the lack of findings. That is a mischaracterization. *Bierter* allows for "general findings" but does not permit a trial court to ignore a *specific legal defense* that directly impacts a required element of injunctive relief. *Surgitech* mandates a finding on the inadequacy of monetary damages when a liquidated damages clause is present—period. The trial court’s silence on this issue was not "sufficient"; it was legally deficient.  

### III. THE TRIAL COURT’S FINDINGS VIOLATED RULE 1.610  

The defense argues the trial court’s order was "adequate" because it "referenced the employment agreement and the need to preserve the status quo." But Rule 1.610 requires more than boilerplate language. It demands findings of fact and conclusions of law addressing the specific issues raised by the parties. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. The trial court did not address Bradley’s material breach defenses or the liquidated damages clause—two issues that were central to the injunction’s validity.  

This Court correctly noted in Round 2: "The trial court’s order failed to address Appellant’s primary defenses... An injunction that ignores these defenses cannot stand." A trial court cannot rely on "status quo" rhetoric to bypass its duty to resolve critical legal disputes. As *Mason* makes clear, failure to make required findings is an abuse of discretion.  

### CONCLUSION  

The trial court’s order was a legal misstep at every turn. It ignored Bradley’s material breach defenses—allegations that, if proven, would render the noncompete unenforceable—and failed to address the liquidated damages clause’s impact on irreparable harm. The defense’s arguments to the contrary rely on misreading controlling precedent and ignoring the trial court’s duty to resolve disputed facts before granting injunctive relief.  

This Court rightly reversed the injunction in Round 2. There is no basis for reinstating it. The trial court’s error was not harmless—it was fundamental. We urge this Court to affirm the reversal and remand for proceedings consistent with the law.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLEE HEALTH COALITION, INC.

May it please this Court:

The Appellant's closing argument, while ably presented, fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of this proceeding and the standard of review applicable to temporary injunctive relief. We respectfully submit that this Court should reconsider the position articulated in its Round 2 ruling, or in the alternative, clarify the scope of remand to ensure that the trial court may properly exercise its discretion on remand.

## I. STANDARD OF REVIEW AND THE LIMITED PURPOSE OF TEMPORARY INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

The Appellant conflates the standards applicable to a temporary injunction with those applicable to a final determination on the merits. A temporary injunction serves the narrow purpose of preserving the status quo pending resolution of the underlying dispute. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). It is not a vehicle for adjudicating the merits of defenses that remain disputed and unproven.

This Court in *Davis* recognized that a trial court is not required to resolve every factual dispute before granting temporary injunctive relief. Id. at 714. The question is not whether the movant has proven its case beyond dispute, but whether it has demonstrated a likelihood of success sufficient to justify interim protection. The Appellant's argument that a material breach defense must be resolved before any injunction can issue would render temporary injunctive relief virtually impossible in any case involving an employment contract or covenant not to compete.

The Appellant relies heavily on *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), but that case is distinguishable. In *Bryant*, the employee's material breach defense involved wage claims that were conceded by the employer as being owed. The employer merely disputed the amount. Here, by contrast, the Employer flatly denies that it ever instructed Bradley to alter invoices, and disputes whether the commissions in question were actually earned. These are not concessions; they are fundamental factual disputes that go to the very heart of Bradley's defense.

The Appellant attempts to characterize his allegations as going "to the essence of the contract." *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000). However, the trial court was entitled to assess the credibility of these allegations and to determine that they were insufficient to defeat the Employer's showing of likelihood of success. The Appellant's self-serving allegations, made for the first time after his employment ended, do not automatically constitute material breaches requiring judicial nullification of the covenant.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS DISCRETION REGARDING IRREPARABLE HARM

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by failing to address the liquidated damages clause in relation to irreparable harm. This argument misappplies the holding of *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951 (Fla. 1986).

In *Surgitech*, the Florida Supreme Court recognized that liquidated damages clauses are presumptively valid but did not hold that their presence automatically precludes injunctive relief. Rather, the Court acknowledged that injunctive relief may be appropriate where the movant demonstrates that monetary damages would be inadequate to protect its legitimate business interests. Id. at 954. Customer relationships, goodwill, and confidential business information represent intangible assets that are inherently difficult to value and cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages alone. *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002).

The trial court was entitled to find, as it implicitly did, that the Employer's customer relationships and confidential business information constituted intangible assets justifying injunctive relief despite the liquidated damages clause. The covenant not to compete specifically protected "active, paid up customers" and "confidentiality of corporate information"—assets that a liquidated damages provision cannot adequately protect. The trial court was not required to make hyper-detailed findings explaining why the liquidated damages clause was inadequate when the nature of the protected interests itself demonstrates the inadequacy of monetary relief. *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114, 1117 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003).

The Appellant's argument that the trial court was required to make an explicit finding addressing the *Surgitech* presumption elevates form over substance. The trial court's order referenced the employment agreement and the need to preserve the status quo—findings that are sufficient under Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610. The Appellant has not identified any case requiring a trial court to specifically parse each element of the *Surgitech* analysis in its written order when the basis for finding irreparable harm is apparent from the nature of the protected interests.

## III. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS WERE ADEQUATE

The Appellant argues that the trial court's findings were insufficient under *Mason v. American Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300 (Fla. 1987). This argument fails. In *Mason*, the Florida Supreme Court held that a trial court must make findings sufficient to allow meaningful appellate review. Id. at 1302. The trial court's order here identified the employment agreement containing the covenant not to compete, acknowledged that Bradley had gone to work for a competing entity, and found that injunctive relief was necessary to preserve the status quo.

The Appellant's argument that the trial court was required to specifically address his material breach defenses misreads *Mason*. The trial court was not required to resolve every defense raised by the Respondent; it was required to make findings sufficient to explain its decision to grant injunctive relief. The trial court's findings were adequate for this purpose.

Moreover, the Appellant's defenses were, as the defense below characterized them, attenuated and self-serving. The allegation that the Employer instructed him to commit fraud was made for the first time in connection with the injunction proceeding, after his employment had ended and after he had already begun working for a competitor. The allegation regarding unpaid commissions was disputed and involved questions of contract interpretation that were properly reserved for trial. The trial court was entitled to accord these allegations limited weight in the context of a temporary injunction proceeding.

## IV. THE PROPER SCOPE OF REMAND

Even if this Court adheres to its Round 2 ruling reversing the injunction, we respectfully request that the Court clarify the scope of remand. The trial court should be permitted to consider the Employer's evidence on remand and to make new findings regarding likelihood of success and irreparable harm, taking into account any defenses raised by Bradley. The trial court should not be required to credit Bradley's unverified allegations, but may instead assess the weight of evidence presented by both parties.

We further submit that, upon remand, the trial court should be permitted to consider the equitable balance of harms, including the harm to the Employer from Bradley's competition during the pendency of the litigation, against the harm to Bradley from being enjoined from employment in his chosen field. The trial court is in the best position to balance these interests, and its determination should be reviewed only for abuse of discretion.

For the foregoing reasons, we respectfully submit that this Court should reverse the Round 2 ruling and reinstate the temporary injunction. In the alternative, if this Court determines that the trial court must make additional findings, we request that the Court clarify the scope of remand to permit the trial court to exercise its discretion fully, taking into account all relevant evidence and defenses.

The Employer entered into a valid employment agreement with Bradley containing a reasonable covenant not to compete. The covenant was designed to protect legitimate business interests—customer relationships and confidential information. The Employer has a right to enforce this covenant, and the trial court acted within its discretion in granting temporary injunctive relief to preserve the status quo pending resolution of the underlying dispute.

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO. [CASE NUMBER]**

**OPINION**

ON APPEAL from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III challenges the trial court’s entry of a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. defends the court’s ruling. We have considered the closing arguments of both parties and affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Martinez v. Schedler*, 811 So. 2d 697, 700 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). A trial court abuses its discretion if it misapplies the law or fails to make required findings of fact. *Mason v. Am. Seminole, Inc.*, 502 So. 2d 1300, 1302 (Fla. 1987). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a threat of irreparable harm; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Surgitech, Inc. v. Baker*, 498 So. 2d 951, 953 (Fla. 1986).

**II. MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSE**

Appellee argues that *Bryant v. Publix Super Mkts., Inc.*, 881 So. 2d 683 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), is distinguishable because, in *Bryant*, the employer conceded that wages were owed, whereas here, Appellee disputes Appellant’s allegations regarding the instruction to alter invoices and the earned commissions. Appellee contends that because these facts are disputed, the trial court was not required to resolve them to preserve the status quo under *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708 (Fla. 2002).

We are not persuaded. While we acknowledge the factual distinction noted by Appellee, the legal principle established in *Bryant* remains applicable: a trial court must resolve factual conflicts regarding material breach defenses before granting injunctive relief. *Bryant*, 881 So. 2d at 685. The "likelihood of success on the merits" element cannot be satisfied if the Appellee’s right to enforce the contract has been extinguished by a material breach.

Appellant alleges he was instructed to alter invoices to defraud customers and that he was refused payment of earned commissions. These allegations, if proven, constitute a material breach that goes to the essence of the contract. *Moorhouse v. Angus & Robertson*, 773 So. 2d 654, 658 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000). Unlike the bonus calculation disputes in *Ursini v. Saul Stone & Co.*, 777 So. 2d 1219 (Fla. 4th DCA 2000), allegations of fraud and the withholding of earned compensation strike at the heart of the employment agreement.

The trial court’s failure to make findings regarding these specific allegations—choosing instead to ignore them based on their disputed nature—was an abuse of discretion. The court was required to assess whether Appellant’s defenses were colorable and whether they affected Appellee’s likelihood of success. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. The trial court’s silence on this critical issue precludes meaningful appellate review and requires reversal.

**III. LIQUIDATED DAMAGES AND IRREPARABLE HARM**

Appellee argues that the liquidated damages clause does not preclude injunctive relief because the protected interests—customer relationships and confidential information—are intangible assets that cannot be adequately valued monetarily. *Equity Holdings, LLC v. Westport Group, Inc.*, 837 So. 2d 525, 529 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002). Appellee further contends that *Bierter v. WCI Communities, Inc.*, 864 So. 2d 1114 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003), permits general findings and that the trial court implicitly found irreparable harm by referencing the need to preserve the status quo.

We reject this argument. *Surgitech* mandates that liquidated damages clauses are "presumptively valid and preclude a finding of irreparable harm" unless the movant demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate. *Surgitech*, 498 So. 2d at 954. While intangible assets may support a finding of inadequacy, the trial court must still explicitly address the presumption created by the liquidated damages clause. The trial court’s order failed to mention the liquidated damages clause or explain why it was insufficient to make Appellee whole.

Appellee’s reliance on *Bierter* is misplaced. *Bierter* allows for general findings but does not excuse a trial court from addressing a specific legal defense that directly impacts a required element of injunctive relief. *Surgitech* requires a specific analysis of the inadequacy of monetary damages when a liquidated damages clause is present. The trial court’s failure to make this finding was legal error.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF FINDINGS AND SCOPE OF REMAND**

Appellee asserts that the trial court’s findings were adequate under Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610 because they identified the agreement and the need to preserve the status quo. We disagree. As stated in *Mason*, a trial court must address the specific legal and factual issues raised by the parties. *Mason*, 502 So. 2d at 1302. The trial court’s order failed to address Appellant’s primary defenses: material breach and the applicability of the liquidated damages clause.

However, we agree with Appellee that on remand, the trial court should be permitted to consider all evidence anew and make specific findings regarding these defenses. The trial court is not required to credit Appellant’s unverified allegations but must make sufficient findings to demonstrate that it considered the material breach defense and the *Surgitech* presumption in determining whether Appellee met its burden for injunctive relief.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court abused its discretion by modifying and extending the temporary injunction without addressing Appellant’s defense of material breach and without analyzing the impact of the liquidated damages clause on the finding of irreparable harm. We affirm the reversal of the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
