FACTS:
Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard, an attorney licensed to practice in this state, appropriated for her personal use approximately $1,300 in personal injury settlement funds belonging to her client. Recognizing petitioner’s continuing recovery from alcohol and drug addiction, the Review Department of the State Bar Court recommended the minimum penalty prescribed by the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct (Rules Proc. of State Bar, div. V; all further references to standards are to this source) for wilful misappropriation of entrusted funds: a one-year actual suspension. (Std. 2.2(a).) Petitioner challenges the one-year suspension, urging that a three-month suspension proposed by the *218State Bar referee who initially heard her case is sufficient to protect the public, the courts, and the legal profession.1

Upon our independent review of the record, we have concluded that an actual suspension of six months is sufficient discipline in petitioner’s case. Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum. She made restitution. She has made a substantial showing in mitigation by demonstrating an apparently successful recovery from chemical dependency problems that have plagued most of her adult life. Charges were not brought against petitioner until nearly three years after the misappropriation and restitution and her rehabilitation. We believe that a six-month suspension is adequate to protect the public and the profession in these circumstances.

I. Facts, Findings, and Conclusions

Petitioner stipulated to the findings of fact and conclusions of law made by the referee. The following is taken from her stipulation, the documents attached to it, and other undisputed evidence in the record before the State Bar Court:

Petitioner was admitted to practice in 1982. Around July 1984, client Susan Marsh consulted petitioner about a claim for personal injuries suffered by the client in an accident. Petitioner and Marsh signed a retainer agreement establishing a contingent fee arrangement and authorizing petitioner to sign Marsh’s name to any paper pertaining to the case, including a settlement check.2 Marsh expressed a desire to settle the case without litigation. An experienced paralegal associated with petitioner conducted settlement negotiations under her supervision. Around February 1985, the case was settled for $3,500.

The settlement check was made jointly payable to petitioner and Marsh. Using the authority vested in her by the retainer agreement, petitioner then endorsed Marsh’s name on the check as well as her own. She paid $1,000 to the law office with which she was associated for medical expenses it had advanced on the client’s behalf and deposited the balance of $2,500 into her *219personal bank account. She then spent this money for personal purposes. Marsh received none of the settlement proceeds.

Petitioner left the law office where she was working about a week after she took the settlement funds. She was unemployed from March 1 through July 1985. During this time, Marsh called the law office on several occasions to find out why she had not yet received her share of the settlement. In late April 1985, petitioner contacted Marsh by phone and Marsh demanded her share of the settlement money. Petitioner apologized for the delay and told Marsh that she would make sure that Marsh received her money. 

The State Bar brought charges against petitioner in a notice to show cause filed on March 31, 1988. Based on petitioner’s admissions in the stipulation, the referee concluded she had violated the Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act by wilfully misappropriating client funds and failing to communicate with her client. (Rules Prof. Conduct, former rule 8-101 (new Rules of Professional Conduct became operative on May 27, 1989); Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 6068, subd. (a), 6103.) He also concluded petitioner’s conduct constituted moral turpitude under Business and Professions Code section 6106.

In mitigation, petitioner offered evidence that: (1) she was admitted to the bar in 1982 and had no prior record of discipline;  and (3) she was a victim of alcohol and chemical dependency resulting from psychological problems beginning in childhood. Petitioner had begun drinking while a teenager and had abused drugs and alcohol virtually all of her adult life. She is the child of alcoholics who deserted her in childhood. She experienced severe emotional distress from the unexpected death in 1984 of the grandmother who raised her and a series of broken relationships with others. She “bottomed out” in 1986 and received help from Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and Cocaine Anonymous. She has remained an active participant in AA and The Other Bar, assisting others who suffer from chemical dependency. Petitioner had not used alcohol or drugs between 1986 and the time of her hearing before the referee in *220late 1988, approximately two and one-half years.3 The referee found petitioner’s evidence compelling and concluded her conduct had resulted from alcoholism. He noted petitioner had performed well as a lawyer since 1986 when she stopped drinking and using drugs. He credited expert testimony presented by petitioner to the effect that she fell in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful, particularly since she had been sober for more than two years. He concluded that petitioner’s chemical dependency was well under control and predicted that, under appropriate supervision, it would continue to be so. He recommended a three-year suspension with five years of probation and three months of actual suspension.

The review department adopted the findings and conclusions of the referee, but recommended petitioner’s period of actual suspension be increased to one year in accordance with the minimum discipline prescribed by standard 2.2(a) for wilful misappropriation of client funds. Ten reviewing members voted for the recommendation; two dissented because they felt the penalty too lenient and favored disbarment; and two dissented because they felt the penalty too harsh and favored the referee’s recommendation of three months’ actual suspension. One of the members voting with the majority stated that he would have adopted the referee’s recommendation but for the mandatory language of the standard.4

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the independent review of the record and the arguments presented by the prosecution and defense, the Court hereby grants the prosecution’s request for a six-month actual suspension.**

**I. STATEMENT OF DECISION**

Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard stipulated to wilfully misappropriating $1,300 in client settlement funds for personal use. Under the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct, specifically Standard 2.2(a), the prescribed minimum penalty for such conduct is a one-year actual suspension. However, both the prosecution and the defense urge this Court to depart from this mandatory minimum based on significant mitigating factors, including Petitioner's recovery from chemical dependency and lack of prior disciplinary record. The defense argues for a three-month suspension, while the prosecution argues for a six-month suspension.

After considering the stipulated facts, the mitigating evidence, and the applicable legal precedents, the Court finds that a six-month actual suspension is the appropriate discipline. This sanction balances the need to protect the public and maintain the integrity of the profession against the compelling evidence of Petitioner's rehabilitation and the isolated nature of her misconduct.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Standard for Misappropriation and Mitigating Factors**

Wilful misappropriation of client funds is a grave violation of an attorney’s fiduciary duties, warranting severe discipline. Standard 2.2(a) prescribes a one-year actual suspension as the minimum penalty. However, this Court has recognized that standards are not inflexible mandates but guideposts that must yield to an individualized assessment of the circumstances.

The record establishes that Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum of $1,300. She made restitution and has no prior disciplinary history since her admission to the bar in 1982. Most significantly, the misconduct was directly linked to Petitioner's long-standing struggle with alcohol and drug addiction, stemming from childhood trauma and exacerbated by the death of her grandmother in 1984. Since "bottoming out" in 1986, Petitioner has maintained continuous sobriety for over two and a half years, actively participates in Alcoholics Anonymous and Cocaine Anonymous, and has received expert testimony confirming she is in the highest percentile for successful recovery.

**B. Application of Precedent**

The prosecution and defense both rely on *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), and *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), to support their arguments for a departure from the standard minimum.

In *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), the Court held that "the purpose of discipline is to protect the public, not to punish the attorney." The Court reduced a suspension where the attorney demonstrated significant rehabilitation and the misconduct stemmed from chronic addiction. Similarly, in *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), the Court imposed a six-month actual suspension for misappropriation despite the standard minimum of one year, emphasizing that the disciplinary process must balance deterrence with the reality of an attorney’s recovery efforts.

These precedents support the prosecution's position that a suspension below the one-year minimum is justified where, as here, the attorney has no prior discipline, has achieved substantial rehabilitation, and expert testimony confirms a low risk of relapse. The mitigating factors in this case—specifically the expert testimony regarding Petitioner's high likelihood of continued recovery and her active assistance to others in recovery—are compelling and distinguish this case from those involving inherent dishonesty unrelated to addiction.

**C. Determination of the Appropriate Sanction**

The defense contends that a three-month suspension, as recommended by the referee, is sufficient. While the Court acknowledges the referee's findings and the strength of Petitioner's mitigation, misappropriation of client funds remains an offense that strikes at the heart of the attorney-client relationship. A three-month suspension risks undermining public confidence in the profession by minimizing the severity of the breach of trust, even where the attorney is rehabilitated.

Conversely, the Review Department’s recommendation of a one-year suspension fails to give adequate weight to the extraordinary mitigation presented. As noted by the prosecution, imposing the full one-year suspension would serve little protective purpose beyond punishment, contravening the principle established in *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974).

The prosecution’s proposed six-month suspension strikes the appropriate balance. It serves as a significant sanction that denounces the misconduct and deters similar conduct by others, while recognizing that Petitioner's recovery is robust and that she poses a minimal risk to the public. This duration aligns with the sanction imposed in *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), where similar mitigating factors were present.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the prosecution's argument for a six-month actual suspension is consistent with the record, the Standards for Attorney Sanctions, and controlling precedent. The defense's request for a three-month suspension is insufficient to address the gravity of the offense, and the Review Department's recommendation of a one-year suspension is excessively harsh given the totality of the mitigating circumstances.

**IT IS ORDERED that Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.**

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the rebuttal arguments presented by the prosecution and defense, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling granting a six-month actual suspension.**

**I. STATEMENT OF DECISION**

Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard seeks a further reduction of her suspension from six months to three months, relying on the referee’s recommendation and distinguishing the precedent cited by the prosecution. The prosecution opposes this reduction, arguing that controlling precedent establishes a six-month floor for misappropriation of client funds, regardless of mitigating factors. After careful consideration of the parties' arguments and the applicable law, the Court finds that the defense has not demonstrated sufficient legal grounds to depart from the six-month sanction established in *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982). While Petitioner’s mitigation is substantial, it does not justify reducing the suspension below the minimum threshold required to maintain public confidence in the profession.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The "Six-Month Floor" Established by *In re Rose***

The defense argues that *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), is distinguishable because the respondent in that case had a prior disciplinary record, whereas Petitioner does not. While this factual distinction exists, it does not undermine the controlling legal principle articulated by the prosecution regarding the appropriate sanction for misappropriation.

As the prosecution asserts, *In re Rose* explicitly held that "a suspension of less than six months for misappropriation of client funds would fail to adequately reflect the seriousness of the offense, even where the misconduct is isolated and the attorney has demonstrated rehabilitation." This language establishes a six-month suspension as the baseline for any willful misappropriation, serving as a protective floor for the integrity of the profession. The Court’s determination in *In re Rose* that six months was the appropriate reduced sanction—despite the presence of mitigation—indicates that the gravity of the offense itself mandates this minimum period of actual suspension. The defense’s request for a three-month suspension would directly contravene this holding.

**B. The Limits of Mitigation Under *In re Waddell***

The defense relies heavily on *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), to argue that the Court has discretion to reduce discipline below standard minimums where misconduct is directly attributable to chemical dependency. The Court agrees with this principle in the abstract; indeed, the current ruling exercises that discretion by reducing the suspension from the one-year minimum prescribed by Standard 2.2(a) to six months.

However, the defense’s interpretation of *In re Waddell* as support for a three-month suspension is misplaced. The prosecution correctly notes that in *In re Waddell*, the Court reduced the discipline to six months, not below it. The principle derived from *In re Waddell* is that addiction-related mitigation justifies a departure from the standard *maximum* or *mandatory* minimums, but it does not override the separate jurisprudential floor established for misappropriation cases in *In re Rose*. The defense’s argument that the "causal connection" between addiction and misconduct warrants a further reduction ignores the reality that the attorneys in both *Waddell* and *Rose* likely had similar causal links, yet the Court still imposed six months.

**C. Weight of the Referee’s Recommendation**

The defense urges the Court to adopt the referee’s recommendation of three months, emphasizing the referee's opportunity to assess witness credibility and the "compelling" nature of Petitioner's mitigation. The Court acknowledges the referee's detailed findings and the significant weight they are due. However, the referee's recommendation is not binding on this Court, particularly where it conflicts with controlling precedent.

The Review Department rejected the referee's three-month recommendation, specifically noting the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a). While the defense argues this rejection was based solely on the standard's text, the prosecution correctly points out that the Review Department's consensus reflects an understanding that the referee's proposal was inconsistent with the sanction imposed in analogous cases like *In re Rose*. The Court’s independent review confirms that while the referee’s assessment of Petitioner’s rehabilitation is accurate, the legal conclusion that three months is sufficient discipline for misappropriation is erroneous as a matter of law.

**D. Public Confidence and Deterrence**

Finally, the defense argues that a three-month suspension would strengthen public confidence by recognizing rehabilitation. The Court disagrees. As stated in *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), reducing the suspension below six months risks signaling that misappropriation is a minor infraction. The protection of the public requires that the sanction denounce the breach of trust with sufficient severity. A three-month suspension, while acknowledging Petitioner’s recovery, fails to adequately communicate the seriousness of diverting client funds to the public and the bar.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has failed to overcome the prosecution's argument that *In re Rose* establishes a six-month floor for misappropriation sanctions. The factual distinction regarding prior discipline is insufficient to justify a sanction below the minimum required to reflect the gravity of the offense. Petitioner’s significant mitigation has been fully accounted for by reducing the suspension from one year to six months. Further reduction to three months is unwarranted by precedent and detrimental to the public interest.

**IT IS ORDERED that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.**

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Matter of LISA MARGARET HOWARD, Petitioner

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that this Court should reduce Petitioner's actual suspension from six months to three months. While we appreciate the Court's recognition of Petitioner's substantial mitigation by reducing the discipline from the mandatory one-year minimum to six months, we submit that the Court has the inherent discretion—and indeed the obligation under controlling precedent—to impose the three-month suspension recommended by the referee. The prosecution's arguments regarding the so-called "six-month floor" misread the applicable law and ignore the unique circumstances that distinguish this case from those upon which the prosecution relies.

---

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The record establishes the following undisputed facts that support a three-month suspension:

Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard was admitted to practice law in 1982. In 1984, she represented client Susan Marsh in a personal injury matter. The case settled for $3,500, with the check made jointly payable to petitioner and Marsh. Petitioner, acting under the express written authority granted in the retainer agreement, endorsed Marsh's name on the check and deposited the proceeds into her personal account. She then used approximately $1,300 for personal purposes, failing to remit any portion to her client.

The misappropriation involved a single act, a relatively small sum of $1,300, representing the balance of settlement funds after the law office received $1,000 for medical expenses it had advanced. Petitioner made full restitution to her client. The misconduct occurred in 1985, and charges were not brought until March 31, 1988—nearly three years later.

In mitigation, petitioner established that she was admitted to the bar in 1982 with no prior disciplinary record. She presented compelling evidence that she was a victim of alcohol and chemical dependency resulting from psychological problems beginning in childhood. She is the child of alcoholics who deserted her in childhood. She experienced severe emotional distress from the unexpected death in 1984 of the grandmother who raised her. She "bottomed out" in 1986 and received help from Alcoholics Anonymous and Cocaine Anonymous. She has remained an active participant in AA and The Other Bar, assisting others who suffer from chemical dependency. At the time of her hearing before the referee in late 1988, she had not used alcohol or drugs for approximately two and one-half years. The referee found she fell in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful.

### III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

#### A. The Court Has Discretion to Impose a Three-Month Suspension

The prosecution erroneously asserts that *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), establishes an invariable six-month floor for all misappropriation cases. This reading of *Rose* is too broad and ignores the actual holding of that case.

In *Rose*, the Court reduced the discipline from one year to six months specifically because of mitigating circumstances—including the attorney's demonstration of rehabilitation. The Court stated that "a suspension of less than six months for misappropriation of client funds would fail to adequately reflect the seriousness of the offense, *even where the misconduct is isolated and the attorney has demonstrated rehabilitation*." This language, however, must be read in the context of *Rose*'s specific facts: the attorney in that case had a prior disciplinary record involving similar misconduct. The Court was concerned that a shorter suspension would be inadequate to address a pattern of behavior.

Petitioner has no prior disciplinary record. She committed a single, isolated act of misappropriation involving $1,300—a relatively modest sum. The absence of prior discipline is a meaningful distinction that undermines the prosecution's argument that the six-month floor applies without exception. As the defense noted in its initial briefing, the *Rose* Court itself recognized that the severity of prior misconduct influences the appropriate sanction. Where, as here, the attorney has no prior discipline and has demonstrated extraordinary rehabilitation, the reasoning underlying the six-month floor does not apply.

#### B. *In re Waddell* Authorizes Discipline Below the Standard Minimum

The prosecution's reliance on *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), is misplaced. While the prosecution correctly notes that the Court in *Waddell* reduced discipline to six months, the prosecution ignores the broader principle articulated in that case: "the discipline may be reduced below the standard minimum" where misconduct is directly attributable to chemical dependency.

The *Waddell* Court held that where an attorney's misconduct is "directly attributable to chemical dependency," the Court has discretion to reduce discipline below the standard minimum. This principle was explicitly recognized in the Court's preliminary ruling, which acknowledged that "addiction-related mitigation justifies a departure from the standard maximum or mandatory minimums." The defense submits that this principle applies with full force here.

Petitioner's misconduct was directly attributable to her chemical dependency. The referee found that her conduct resulted from alcoholism. She has demonstrated sustained recovery since 1986. The causal connection between her addiction and her misconduct is undisputed. Under *Waddell*, this causal connection authorizes a departure below the standard minimum.

The prosecution argues that *Waddell* only reduced to six months and therefore establishes six months as the floor. This argument conflates the specific disposition of *Waddell* with the scope of the Court's discretion. The *Waddell* Court exercised its discretion to impose six months in *those particular circumstances*. The holding does not establish six months as the minimum possible sanction in all cases involving addiction-related misconduct. To the contrary, the Court explicitly recognized discretion to go below the standard minimum.

#### C. The Referee's Recommendation Is Entitled to Significant Weight

The referee, who had the opportunity to observe witness credibility firsthand, recommended a three-month suspension. The referee found petitioner's evidence "compelling" and concluded that her conduct had resulted from alcoholism. The referee noted petitioner had performed well as a lawyer since 1986 when she stopped drinking and using drugs. He credited expert testimony presented by petitioner to the effect that she fell in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful.

The prosecution argues that the referee's recommendation is not binding on this Court. While technically correct, this argument understates the weight due to the referee's assessment. The referee was in the best position to evaluate the credibility and sincerity of petitioner's rehabilitation. His recommendation reflects a careful balancing of the competing interests: protecting the public, maintaining professional standards, and recognizing genuine rehabilitation.

The Review Department rejected the referee's three-month recommendation based solely on the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a). However, as this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the standard's mandatory minimum must be read in conjunction with the Court's discretion under *Waddell* to reduce discipline below that minimum where addiction-related mitigation warrants. The Review Department's rejection did not address the merits of the referee's recommendation; it merely applied the standard mechanically.

#### D. The Delay Between Misconduct and Charges Warrants Consideration

The prosecution argues that the three-year delay between the misappropriation and the filing of charges does not justify a reduction below six months. While the defense does not argue that delay alone warrants a further reduction, the delay is relevant to the overall assessment of discipline.

As the Court noted in *In re Waddell*, delay "provides an opportunity to observe the attorney's actual rehabilitation." Here, the delay allowed the Court to observe petitioner's sustained recovery for nearly three years before charges were even filed. During this period, petitioner demonstrated her commitment to sobriety and her ability to practice law ethically. This observation supports the referee's conclusion that petitioner presents minimal risk of future misconduct.

---

### IV. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S CLAIMS

The prosecution's closing statement mischaracterizes the defense's position and the applicable law in several respects.

First, the prosecution argues that the six-month floor in *Rose* applies regardless of whether the attorney has prior disciplinary history. This argument ignores the context in which *Rose* was decided. The Court in *Rose* was concerned about the attorney *who had a prior disciplinary record*. The language relied upon by the prosecution—"even where the misconduct is isolated and the attorney has demonstrated rehabilitation"—must be read in conjunction with the specific facts of that case. An attorney with no prior discipline who commits a single isolated act is meaningfully different from an attorney with prior misconduct. The prosecution's attempt to elevate language from a fact-specific decision into a universal rule is improper.

Second, the prosecution argues that *Waddell* does not authorize suspensions below six months. This argument ignores the explicit language of *Waddell*, which states that "the discipline may be reduced below the standard minimum" where misconduct is directly attributable to chemical dependency. The prosecution cites only the disposition of *Waddell*—which happened to be six months—while ignoring the principle that authorized the reduction. The principle, not the specific number, is the holding of the case.

Third, the prosecution argues that a three-month suspension would "signal that misappropriation is a minor infraction." This argument overstates the significance of the difference between three months and six months. Both are significant sanctions that convey the seriousness of misappropriation. The question is not whether misappropriation is serious—of course it is—but whether three months is sufficient to serve the purposes of discipline in *these particular circumstances*. Given petitioner's single act, small sum, full restitution, no prior discipline, and extraordinary rehabilitation, three months is adequate.

Finally, the prosecution argues that the six-month suspension "appropriately balances rehabilitation with denunciation of the misconduct." The defense submits that this balance is better struck at three months. The purposes of attorney discipline are protection of the public, maintenance of professional standards, and rehabilitation of the attorney. A three-month suspension serves all three purposes: it demonstrates that misappropriation will not be tolerated, it maintains professional standards by imposing a meaningful sanction, and it recognizes petitioner's successful rehabilitation. Six months is excessive under these circumstances.

### V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reduce Petitioner's actual suspension from six months to three months. The Court has the discretion to do so under *In re Waddell*, which authorizes reduction below the standard minimum where misconduct is directly attributable to chemical dependency. Petitioner's misconduct was directly attributable to her addiction, and she has demonstrated extraordinary rehabilitation. The referee, who had the best opportunity to assess her credibility, recommended three months. A three-month suspension serves the purposes of attorney discipline while recognizing the unique circumstances of this case.

The prosecution's arguments regarding the so-called "six-month floor" misread controlling precedent. *In re Rose* addressed an attorney with prior disciplinary history; petitioner has none. *In re Waddell* explicitly authorized discipline below the standard minimum where addiction is the cause of misconduct. The Court should exercise its discretion to impose the three-month suspension recommended by the referee.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments and rebuttals presented by counsel, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling granting a six-month actual suspension.**

**I. STATEMENT OF DECISION**

Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard requests a reduction of her actual suspension from six months to three months. She argues that the "six-month floor" established by *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), is inapplicable because she lacks a prior disciplinary record, and that *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), grants this Court the discretion to impose a sanction below six months where misconduct is attributable to chemical dependency. The prosecution opposes this reduction, maintaining that *In re Rose* establishes an absolute minimum for misappropriation and that *In re Waddell* supports a reduction only to six months, not below.

After careful consideration, the Court finds the prosecution’s arguments to be more persuasive and legally sound. The defense’s attempt to distinguish *In re Rose* based on the absence of prior discipline fails to account for the broad language of that holding regarding isolated misconduct. Furthermore, the defense’s interpretation of *In re Waddell* as authorizing a three-month suspension is inconsistent with the actual sanction imposed in that case. Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce the suspension below six months.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The "Six-Month Floor" Remains Controlling Despite Mitigation**

The defense contends that *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982), is distinguishable because the respondent in that case had a prior disciplinary record, whereas Petitioner does not. The defense argues that the "six-month floor" articulated in *Rose* was intended to address patterns of misconduct rather than isolated acts.

This argument is unpersuasive. The Court in *In re Rose* explicitly stated that "a suspension of less than six months for misappropriation of client funds would fail to adequately reflect the seriousness of the offense, even where the misconduct is isolated and the attorney has demonstrated rehabilitation." The phrase "even where the misconduct is isolated" directly addresses the factual scenario presented by Petitioner: a single act of misappropriation without a history of similar behavior. By including this language, the *Rose* court established that the gravity of the offense itself—misappropriation of client funds—necessitates a minimum suspension of six months, regardless of the attorney's prior disciplinary history. The defense’s reliance on the absence of prior discipline cannot override this explicit jurisprudential limit.

**B. *In re Waddell* Does Not Authorize a Suspension Below Six Months**

The defense relies on *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), to argue that the Court has discretion to reduce discipline below the standard minimum where misconduct is directly attributable to chemical dependency. While the Court agrees that *In re Waddell* recognizes the mitigating force of chemical dependency, the defense overextends the holding of that case.

In *In re Waddell*, the court reduced the discipline from one year to six months. It did not reduce the discipline to three months. The defense argues that the "principle" of *Waddell* allows for a reduction to three months, but the prosecution correctly points out that the actual disposition of *Waddell* stopped at six months. If the court in *Waddell*—faced with addiction-related mitigation—believed a suspension of less than six months was appropriate, it presumably would have imposed it. The fact that it settled on six months reinforces the notion that six months is the lowest acceptable sanction for serious misconduct, even where compelling mitigation exists. The defense’s request for a three-month suspension asks this Court to go further than the precedent cited allows.

**C. The Referee’s Recommendation Cannot Override Controlling Law**

The defense places significant weight on the referee’s recommendation of a three-month suspension, emphasizing the referee's unique opportunity to assess Petitioner’s credibility and rehabilitation. The Court acknowledges the referee’s thorough findings regarding Petitioner’s recovery from chemical dependency and her low risk of reoffense. However, a referee’s recommendation on discipline is not binding on this Court, particularly where it conflicts with controlling precedent.

The Review Department rejected the referee’s recommendation specifically because it conflicted with the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a) and the holdings of *In re Rose*. The defense characterizes this rejection as "mechanical," but the Review Department’s action reflects a proper application of the legal standards governing attorney discipline. While Petitioner’s rehabilitation is commendable, it does not empower this Court to disregard the minimum sanction established by the Supreme Court for the willful misappropriation of client funds.

**D. Public Confidence and the Deterrent Effect of the Sanction**

Finally, the defense argues that a three-month suspension would "strengthen public confidence" by recognizing the reality of Petitioner’s rehabilitation. The Court disagrees. As noted in the preliminary ruling and reiterated by the prosecution, reducing the suspension below six months risks signaling to the public that misappropriation is a minor infraction that can be quickly remedied. The protection of the public requires a sanction that unequivocally denounces the breach of trust. A six-month suspension achieves this by balancing the severity of the offense with the significant mitigation presented by Petitioner. A three-month suspension, in the Court’s view, would be insufficient to maintain public confidence in the integrity of the profession.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has not demonstrated that the unique circumstances of this case warrant a departure from the six-month minimum suspension established by *In re Rose*, 32 Cal. 3d 587 (1982). The absence of prior discipline does not render the "six-month floor" inapplicable, nor does *In re Waddell*, 12 Cal. 3d 21 (1974), authorize a reduction below that threshold. Petitioner’s substantial mitigation has been fully utilized to reduce the suspension from the one-year minimum recommended by the Review Department to six months. Further reduction is legally unwarranted.

**IT IS ORDERED that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.**
