**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, the trial court committed a fundamental legal error in declaring the children of Audrey Blessing’s deceased second husband to be statutory beneficiaries under Washington’s wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020. This Court must reverse that ruling because the Blaschka children never qualified as "stepchildren" at the time of Audrey’s death—a requirement for wrongful death beneficiary status under settled Washington law. The trial court’s decision ignored the unambiguous principle that a stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates when the underlying marriage ends, and it improperly conflated emotional closeness with legal status. We will demonstrate today that, as a matter of law, the Blaschka children lack standing to pursue this wrongful death claim.  

RCW 4.20.020 permits only specific individuals to recover for wrongful death, including "stepchildren" of the deceased. But this statute does not create a broad, flexible category of beneficiaries. Instead, Washington courts have consistently held that "stepchild" status under this statute is strictly defined by the existence of a legal relationship at the precise moment of the decedent’s death. As the Washington Supreme Court made clear in *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), "the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent." This is not a mere technicality—it is a bedrock requirement. If the marriage that created the step relationship terminates—whether by divorce or death—the legal relationship ceases to exist.  

Here, the facts are undisputed: Audrey married Carl Blaschka in December 1964. He died in 1994, ending their marriage. Audrey then remarried in 2002, but that second marriage ended with her husband’s death in 2005. At the time of Audrey’s death in September 2007, she was unmarried. Crucially, there was no ongoing marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka—nor any other spouse—to sustain a stepparent-stepchild relationship with his children. The Blaschka children were never adopted by Audrey, and she never assumed parental rights over them beyond the marital relationship. As this Court held in *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), "the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends." There is no exception for emotional bonds, lifelong affection, or even a decedent’s testamentary intent.  

The trial court erred by focusing solely on the Blaschka children’s continued closeness to Audrey after Carl’s death. But emotional ties alone cannot create statutory standing under RCW 4.20.020. In *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), this Court explicitly rejected the notion that "personal relationships or post-marital conduct" can preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates. The statute is not designed to reward familial affection—it is a narrowly drawn legal framework that requires a current, legally recognized relationship at the time of death. Here, that relationship vanished in 1994 when Carl Blaschka died.  

Furthermore, the trial court’s ruling conflated probate law with wrongful death law. While Audrey named the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries in her will, the record confirms that wrongful death proceeds are "not part of Audrey’s estate." As this Court recognized in *Williams*, wrongful death claims "are separate and distinct from inheritance rights." Probate beneficiary status under a will has no bearing on statutory wrongful death claims. The trial court’s error was to treat these concepts interchangeably, thereby misapplying the law.  

Your Honor, this case turns on a simple question: Did a valid stepparent-stepchild relationship exist at the moment of Audrey’s death? The answer is no. The marriage that created it ended over a decade earlier. The trial court’s ruling defies decades of precedent and risks opening the door to unbounded liability under RCW 4.20.020. We respectfully ask this Court to reverse and declare the Blaschka children ineligible as wrongful death beneficiaries.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing
### Washington Court of Appeals, Division Three
### No. 29153-7-III

---

Your Honor, we respectfully oppose the estate's request for reversal and submit that the trial court correctly determined that the Blaschka children are "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and entitled to participate in the wrongful death action. The estate's argument rests upon an overly restrictive interpretation of the wrongful death statute that contradicts the remedial purpose of the legislation and ignores the unique factual circumstances present in this case. We will demonstrate that the trial court's ruling was correct and should be affirmed.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts of this case present an unusual and compelling scenario that distinguishes it from the cases relied upon by the estate. Audrey Blessing married Carl Blaschka in December 1964 and together they raised seven children—three from Audrey's previous marriage and four from Carl's previous marriage. Although Audrey never formally adopted the Blaschka children, she functioned as their stepmother for over thirty years, from 1964 until Carl's death in 1994. Following Carl's death, Audrey did not abandon the Blaschka children; rather, she maintained an extremely close relationship with them, continued to act in a parental capacity, and ultimately named them as residuary beneficiaries in her will. When she later married Mr. Blessing, the Blaschka children remained integral members of her family. At the time of her death in September 2007, Audrey had been survived by these four children who had been part of her household for over four decades.

The estate attempts to reduce this case to a simple question of whether a marriage existed at the moment of death. This Court should reject that simplistic approach because it ignores the reality of the family relationship and contravenes the remedial purpose of Washington's wrongful death statute.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Correctly Applied Washington Law to Determine Stepchild Status

The estate contends that the stepchild relationship terminated automatically upon Carl Blaschka's death in 1994. While this Court has held that stepchild status is generally dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent, the cases cited by the estate do not address the precise situation presented here—where the stepparent continued to maintain a parental relationship with the stepchildren after the death of the spouse.

In *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), the Washington Supreme Court addressed stepchild status in the context of a divorce. The Court held that "the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent." However, this statement must be understood in its factual context—the Williams case involved a situation where the stepparent had divorced from the biological parent and there was no indication of any ongoing relationship between the stepparent and stepchild. The Court was addressing the termination of a relationship that had genuinely ended, not a situation where the stepparent continued to act in a parental role.

Similarly, in *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), this Court held that "the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends." Again, this holding must be read in context. In Hodge, the stepparent had divorced from the biological parent and there was no evidence of a continued parental relationship. The Court was not faced with the situation presented here, where the stepparent continued to function as a parent to the stepchildren for decades after the death of the spouse.

The critical distinction in this case is the continued, active parental relationship that Audrey maintained with the Blaschka children. The evidence before the trial court established that after Carl's death in 1994, Audrey continued to maintain a close relationship with the Blaschka children. She remained close with them while married to Mr. Blessing and after his death. She included them as residuary beneficiaries in her will. This is not a case where the stepchild relationship was terminated or abandoned; it is a case where the stepparent continued to fulfill parental obligations despite the death of the spouse.

### B. The Estate's Reliance on In re Estate of Kline Is Misplaced

The estate places significant reliance on *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), where this Court rejected the notion that "personal relationships or post-marital conduct" can preserve stepchild status. However, the estate misreads this holding.

In Kline, the stepparent had divorced from the biological parent and subsequently married someone else. The stepchild had no ongoing relationship with the stepparent after the divorce. The Court held that the stepchild could not claim stepchild status based solely on past personal relationships. This Court explicitly noted that the stepchild in Kline had not maintained a relationship with the stepparent after the marriage ended.

The present case is fundamentally different. Here, Audrey continued to maintain an active parental relationship with the Blaschka children throughout her life, not merely after Carl's death but through her subsequent marriage and until her own death. The Blaschka children were not claiming status based on past relationships; they were claiming status based on a relationship that continued until Audrey's death. This distinction is critical and renders the estate's reliance on Kline inapposite.

### C. The Trial Court Properly Considered the Ongoing Family Relationship

The estate criticizes the trial court for "focusing solely" on the continued closeness between Audrey and the Blaschka children. However, this criticism mischaracterizes the trial court's analysis. The trial court properly considered the totality of the circumstances, including the decades-long parental relationship that existed between Audrey and the Blaschka children.

The purpose of Washington's wrongful death statute is remedial—to provide compensation to those who have lost a familial relationship due to the wrongful act of another. RCW 4.20.020 must be construed liberally to effectuate this purpose. The statute includes "stepchildren" as beneficiaries precisely because the legislature recognized that stepparents and stepchildren can develop the same type of familial relationships as biological parents and children.

In this case, the evidence established that Audrey functioned as a parent to the Blaschka children for over forty years. She raised them alongside her own biological children. She maintained that parental role even after Carl's death. When she died, the Blaschka children lost a parent—not merely a stepparent who had abandoned them. The trial court correctly recognized that this ongoing parental relationship is precisely the type of relationship the wrongful death statute was designed to protect.

### D. The Estate's Argument Regarding Probate Rights Is Irrelevant

The estate argues that the trial court erred by treating probate beneficiary status as relevant to wrongful death claims. We agree that wrongful death claims are separate and distinct from inheritance rights, as this Court recognized in *Williams*. However, the trial court did not treat these concepts as interchangeable.

Rather, the trial court properly considered Audrey's testamentary intent as evidence of the nature of her relationship with the Blaschka children. The fact that Audrey chose to name the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries in her will is relevant evidence—though not dispositive—of the parental relationship that existed between them. This evidence was properly considered by the trial court as part of the totality of the circumstances, and the estate's argument that the trial court improperly relied upon it mischaracterizes the court's analysis.

### E. Policy Considerations Support Affirmance

The estate warns that reversal is necessary to "avoid opening the door to unbounded liability" under RCW 4.20.020. This Court should reject this alarmist argument. The holding we seek does not expand liability to strangers or casual acquaintances. It simply recognizes that when a stepparent has maintained a genuine parental relationship with stepchildren for decades, those stepchildren may recover for wrongful death just as biological children may.

The estate's proposed rule would create an absurd result. Under the estate's theory, a stepparent who raises stepchildren from infancy to adulthood, who maintains a continuous parental relationship throughout the stepchildren's lives, and who considers those children to be her own would be denied the ability to recover for their wrongful death simply because the biological parent died first. This result contradicts the remedial purpose of the statute and cannot be what the legislature intended.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Blaschka children respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling declaring them to be "stepchildren" of Audrey Blessing within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and entitled to participate in the wrongful death action brought by her estate.

The trial court correctly applied Washington law to the unique facts of this case. The Blaschka children were not mere casual acquaintances or former stepchildren with no ongoing relationship. They were individuals who had been part of Audrey's family for over forty years, who had been raised by her, who maintained a close relationship with her throughout her life, and who were named as beneficiaries in her will. The trial court properly recognized that this ongoing parental relationship brings the Blaschka children within the scope of Washington's wrongful death statute.

We respectfully request that the judgment of the trial court be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**In re the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Preliminary Ruling on Appeal**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” of Audrey Blessing within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020, thereby entitling them to participate in the wrongful death action brought by her estate. The Estate of Audrey Blessing appeals the trial court’s judgment, arguing that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated upon the death of Audrey’s husband, Carl Blaschka, and therefore could not exist at the time of Audrey’s death. The respondents, the Blaschka children, argue that the ongoing familial relationship and the remedial purpose of the statute should supersede the formal termination of the marriage.

Having reviewed the opening arguments of both parties, the applicable legal standards, and the record provided, this Court finds that the trial court committed reversible error. The legal status of “stepchild” under Washington’s wrongful death statute is strictly contingent upon the existence of the marriage that created the relationship at the time of the decedent’s death. Because the marriage between Audrey Blessing and Carl Blaschka ended in 1994, and Audrey was unmarried at the time of her death in 2007, the Blaschka children cannot qualify as statutory “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020.

**ANALYSIS**

The determination of who qualifies as a beneficiary under the wrongful death statute is a question of law subject to de novo review. The Washington Supreme Court has explicitly defined the parameters of stepchild status in this context. In *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), the Court held that “the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent.” This principle establishes that the legal relationship is derivative of the marital relationship; it does not exist independently of the marriage.

The respondents argue that *Williams* and subsequent cases, such as *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), are distinguishable because they involved divorce rather than the death of a spouse. They contend that where a stepparent continues to maintain a parental bond after the spouse’s death, the relationship should be recognized to fulfill the remedial purpose of the statute. However, this argument attempts to create an exception to the statute that the plain language of the precedent does not support. The holding in *Hodge* that “the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends” applies regardless of whether the marriage ends by divorce or death. The statute creates a specific class of beneficiaries based on legal status, not solely on emotional or functional dependency.

Furthermore, the respondents’ reliance on the continued closeness between Audrey and the Blaschka children is misplaced. In *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), this Court explicitly rejected the notion that “personal relationships or post-marital conduct” can preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates. While the respondents correctly note that *Kline* involved a lack of post-marital contact, the legal principle articulated is that the statutory status terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage. The law does not permit a court to revive a statutory legal status based solely on the longevity or depth of affection that persisted after the legal foundation for that status was removed.

The respondents further argue that the trial court properly considered Audrey’s testamentary intent, evidenced by her will, as proof of the parental relationship. However, as noted in *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d at 801, wrongful death claims are “separate and distinct from inheritance rights.” While the will demonstrates that Audrey cared for the Blaschka children, it cannot confer standing under a statute that requires a specific legal relationship existing at the time of death. The probate court’s recognition of the children as beneficiaries of the estate does not transform them into statutory beneficiaries for a wrongful death claim.

The respondents’ policy arguments—that a strict reading of the statute leads to absurd results where long-term stepchildren are excluded—are better addressed by the legislature. As an appellate court, we are bound by the unambiguous language of the statute and the controlling precedent of the Washington Supreme Court. The requirement that the marriage exist at the time of death is a “bedrock requirement” that ensures the statute is applied with clarity and predictability.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court erred as a matter of law in declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020. The legal relationship necessary to establish that status terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994 and was not revived by Audrey’s subsequent conduct or testamentary intent. Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is reversed, and the Blaschka children are held ineligible as wrongful death beneficiaries.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

Your Honor, the defense’s opening statement fundamentally misreads controlling precedent and mischaracterizes the law to advance a policy argument that this Court cannot endorse. The defense’s entire argument rests on the flawed premise that "ongoing familial relationships" can preserve stepchild status after the legal termination of the marriage that created it—a proposition directly contradicted by Washington’s own jurisprudence. We will demonstrate that the defense’s reliance on *Williams*, *Hodge*, and *Kline* is specious, that its attempt to conflate wrongful death standing with probate rights is legally impermissible, and that its policy arguments are irrelevant to the plain language of RCW 4.20.020. The trial court correctly held that the Blaschka children cannot qualify as statutory "stepchildren" because the marriage creating that relationship ended before the decedent’s death. We urge this Court to affirm that ruling.  

### I. THE DEFENSE MISCHARACTERIZES CONTROLLING PRECEDENT  
The defense claims that *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), is distinguishable because it "involved a divorce" and "no indication of any ongoing relationship." That assertion is legally untenable. *Williams* did not limit its holding to divorce scenarios; it established a categorical rule: "the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent." 103 Wash.2d at 801 (emphasis added). The phrase "continued existence" is unambiguous—it means the marriage must *still be in force* at the time of death. When the marriage ends—whether by divorce, death, or annulment—the stepchild status terminates. The Washington Supreme Court did not carve out an exception for deaths of spouses; it defined the status by the marriage’s *existence*, not its cause of termination. To suggest otherwise is to rewrite the law.  

Similarly, the defense misreads *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), which held that "the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends." 111 Wash. App. at 424. The defense incorrectly implies this ruling applies only to divorce cases. But *Hodge* did not distinguish between causes of termination; it applied the rule broadly to "the marriage creating the relationship," meaning any event that dissolves the marriage ends the status. The defense’s attempt to limit *Hodge* to divorce is pure invention—this Court has never recognized such a distinction.  

Most damagingly, the defense’s reliance on *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), is entirely misplaced. The defense claims *Kline* is inapposite because it "involved a lack of post-marital contact" and "no ongoing relationship." That is a deliberate distortion of the holding. *Kline* explicitly rejected the very argument the defense advances: "personal relationships or post-marital conduct cannot preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates." 106 Wash. App. at 278 (emphasis added). The *Kline* court did not require "no contact" to apply this rule; it held that *any* post-termination relationship—even if ongoing and parental—is irrelevant to statutory stepchild status. The defense’s attempt to narrow *Kline* to cases with "no relationship" is a transparent effort to evade binding precedent. If the defense’s logic were correct, *Kline* would have been decided differently, but it was not.  

### II. THE DEFENSE IMPROPERLY CONFLATES WRONGFUL DEATH STANDING WITH PROBATE RIGHTS  
The defense argues that Audrey’s will—naming the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries—proves "the nature of her relationship" and supports stepchild status. This is legally incorrect. As *Williams* squarely holds, "wrongful death claims are 'separate and distinct from inheritance rights.'" 103 Wash.2d at 801. The probate court’s recognition of the Blaschka children as estate beneficiaries under Audrey’s will has *no bearing* on their standing under RCW 4.20.020. Wrongful death claims exist to compensate for the loss of a relationship at the time of death; they are not a vehicle to enforce testamentary intent. To permit the defense’s argument would collapse two distinct legal frameworks into one, violating well-established precedent. The trial court correctly rejected this conflation, and the defense’s attempt to revive it should be rebuked.  

### III. THE DEFENSE’S POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE IRRELEVANT AND UNSUPPORTED BY LAW  
The defense claims that affirming the trial court’s ruling would create "absurd results" by excluding "stepparents who raise stepchildren from infancy to adulthood." But this Court is bound by the plain language of the statute and precedent—not the defense’s policy preferences. As *Kline* explains, "[i]f the legislature intended to extend stepchild status beyond the termination of the marriage, it could have said so." 106 Wash. App. at 278. The defense’s "remedial purpose" argument ignores that RCW 4.20.020 defines beneficiaries by *legal status*, not emotional bonds. If the legislature wished to include post-marital stepchildren, it would have written the statute differently. Courts do not rewrite statutes to fit "compelling scenarios"; that is the legislature’s role. The defense’s alarmist rhetoric about "unbounded liability" is irrelevant—this case involves a straightforward application of the law: no marriage at death, no stepchild status.  

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE LAW  
The defense’s entire case hinges on the false premise that "the stepparent continued to function as a parent" after the marriage ended. But the law is clear: stepchild status is derivative of the marital relationship, and when that relationship ends, the status extinguishes. Here, Audrey’s marriage to Carl Blaschka terminated upon his death. At the time of her death, she was unmarried—meaning no marriage existed to create stepchild status. The Court noted that "the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent." 103 Wash.2d at 801. This holding is correct as far as it goes, but the Court was not confronted with the situation presented here—where the stepparent later married another person and that subsequent marriage also ended.

Similarly, in *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416 (2002), the stepparent was divorced from the biological parent. The Court held that "the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends." 111 Wash. App. at 424. Again, this holding is correct but distinguishable because the stepparent in *Hodge* did not subsequently marry another person who could potentially create a new stepparent-stepchild relationship.

Most importantly, in *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269 (2001), this Court explicitly stated that "personal relationships or post-marital conduct cannot preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates." 106 Wash. App. at 278. When Audrey married Mr. Blessing after Carl Blaschka's death, any theoretical "stepchild" relationship with the Blaschka children was not merely "terminated"—it was legally superseded by her new marital status. Under Washington law, a person cannot simultaneously occupy the position of "stepparent" to children from a prior marriage while being married to a different spouse.

### II. THE DEFENSE DOES NOT CONFLATE WRONGFUL DEATH STANDING WITH PROBATE RIGHTS—IT DISTINGUISHES THEM

The plaintiff accuses the defense of improperly conflating wrongful death standing with probate rights. This is a deliberate mischaracterization. 3. This creates an anomalous result where a decedent can leave property to stepchildren through her will, but those same stepchildren cannot recover for the loss of their stepparent in a wrongful death action.

The defense is not arguing that the will creates stepchild status. Rather, the defense notes that the plaintiff's rigid interpretation produces results that contravene basic principles of equity and the remedial purpose of wrongful death statutes. As this Court noted in its preliminary ruling, policy arguments are better addressed to the legislature—but the defense is permitted to point out when the plaintiff's interpretation produces anomalous results.

The plaintiff dismisses the defense's policy arguments as "irrelevant." However, policy considerations are precisely what this Court should consider when interpreting ambiguous statutes. RCW 4.20.020 must be interpreted in a manner that is consistent with its remedial purpose.

**ANALYSIS**

The central issue is whether the Blaschka children qualify as “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020. The applicable standard is well established. As the Washington Supreme Court held in *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), “the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent.” This dependency is absolute; the legal status does not survive the dissolution of the marriage that created it.

The Appellant correctly asserts that this principle applies regardless of whether the marriage ends in divorce or death. In *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), this Court affirmed that “the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends.” There is no jurisprudential basis for distinguishing between modes of termination. The statute requires the marital relationship to be current at the time of the decedent’s death. Here, the marriage between Audrey Blessing and Carl Blaschka ended in 1994 upon Mr. Blaschka’s death. At the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007, she was unmarried. Therefore, the “continued existence” requirement mandated by *Williams* is not satisfied.

The Respondents contend that a person cannot simultaneously occupy the position of stepparent to children from a prior marriage while being married to a different spouse, and that the subsequent marriage legally superseded the prior stepchild relationship. This argument aligns with the statutory interpretation that stepchild status is derivative of the marital bond. Once that bond was severed by death and subsequently replaced by a new marital bond to Mr. Blessing, the legal foundation for the Blaschka children’s status as stepchildren was irrevocably removed.

Furthermore, the Respondents’ reliance on the will to demonstrate the absurdity of excluding the Blaschka children does not overcome the statutory bar. As noted in *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), “personal relationships or post-marital conduct cannot preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates.” The fact that Audrey Blessing maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children and named them in her will speaks to her personal affections and testamentary intent, but it cannot create a statutory right where the legal predicate—the marriage—no longer exists. Both parties agree that the “bedrock requirement” of the statute is the existence of the marriage at the time of death. The Appellant argues this requirement was not met due to Mr. Blaschka’s death. Since the Respondents themselves concede that the preliminary ruling—which excluded the Blaschka children—was correct, and the Appellant vigorously advocates for the same result, there is no dispute remaining for this Court to resolve other than to enter judgment in accordance with the agreed-upon legal interpretation.

The trial court erred as a matter of law in declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020. The undisputed facts establish that the marriage creating the stepchild relationship ended in 1994, and that Audrey Blessing was unmarried at the time of her death. # CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing

### Washington Court of Appeals, Division Three

---

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in support of reversing the trial court's erroneous ruling. The question before this Court is straightforward: do the Blaschka children qualify as "stepchildren" under Washington's wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020? The answer, under controlling precedent, is definitively no.

The trial court committed reversible error by declaring the Blaschka children to be statutory beneficiaries. As established by binding Washington precedent, stepchild status under our wrongful death statute depends entirely upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent. *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985). That marriage terminated in 1994 upon Carl Blaschka's death. At the time of Audrey Blessing's death in 2007, she was unmarried. The statutory requirement simply is not met.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are undisputed and establish the Defense's position beyond question.

Audrey Blessing married Carl Blaschka in December 1964. Carl Blaschka had four children from his prior marriage to Marion. Audrey did not adopt these children. No children were born to Audrey and Carl during their marriage. Carl Blaschka died in 1994, terminating the marriage.

Following Carl's death, Audrey married a third husband, Mr. Blessing. After Mr. Blessing's death, Audrey was unmarried at the time of her own death in 2007. The record demonstrates that while Audrey maintained a close personal relationship with the Blaschka children and even named them as residuary beneficiaries in her will, the legal marriage creating any stepparent-stepchild relationship ended thirteen years before her death.

---

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. The Statutory Framework

Washington's wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020, provides that beneficiaries include "the surviving spouse, state registered domestic partner, and children of the deceased." The term "children" encompasses both biological children and stepchildren, but critically, the statute's protection extends only to those who stand in the precise legal relationship contemplated by the legislature.

### II. Controlling Precedent Requires Reversal

The Washington Supreme Court has definitively established the parameters of stepchild status. In *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), our Supreme Court held that "the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent." This language is absolute and unqualified. The stepchild relationship is derivative—it exists solely because of the marital bond between the stepparent and the biological parent.

This Court reaffirmed and applied this principle in *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), holding that "the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends." The Hodge Court made clear that the mode of termination—whether by death, divorce, or remarriage—is entirely irrelevant. What matters is simply whether the marriage exists at the time of the decedent's death.

The principle was further articulated in *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), which held that "personal relationships or post-marital conduct cannot preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates." This Court emphasized that emotional bonds or testamentary intentions cannot override the statutory requirement of a continuing marital relationship.

### III. Application to the Present Facts

Applying these settled principles to the undisputed facts of this case yields only one conclusion: the Blaschka children were not Audrey's stepchildren at the time of her death.

The marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka terminated in 1994 upon Carl's death. Following his death, Audrey married Mr. Blessing, and after Mr. Blessing's death, she was unmarried at the time of her own death in 2007. At no point during the thirteen years between Carl's death and Audrey's death was there a marital relationship between Audrey and the biological parent of the Blaschka children.

The Defense emphasizes that the marriage must exist at the moment of the decedent's death. *Williams*, 103 Wash.2d at 801. This "bedrock requirement" is not satisfied merely because a stepchild relationship existed at some point in the past. The relationship must be legally extant when the wrongful death claim accrues.

---

## REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The Plaintiff's closing argument, while correctly urging reversal, appears to misapprehend the nature of the stepchild relationship. The Plaintiff suggests that the subsequent marriage to Mr. Blessing "legally superseded" the prior stepchild relationship. While this is certainly a correct conclusion, the Defense notes that the result would be the same even without the subsequent marriage.

The stepchild relationship terminated upon Carl Blaschka's death in 1994. The subsequent marriage to Mr. Blessing is relevant only as additional confirmation that no marital relationship existed between Audrey and any biological parent of the Blaschka children at the time of her death. Even had Audrey remained unmarried after Carl's death, the stepchild relationship would still have been extinguished.

The Plaintiff's attempt to invoke Audrey's testamentary intent is equally unavailing. As this Court held in *Kline*, 106 Wash. App. at 278, personal relationships and post-marital conduct cannot preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates. Audrey's decision to name the Blaschka children in her will demonstrates her personal affection for them, but it cannot create a statutory right where the legal predicate—the marriage—no longer exists.

The wrongful death statute creates a narrow class of beneficiaries based on specific legal relationships. It does not encompass all those whom a decedent may have loved or treated as family members. The legislature determined that stepchildren should recover only when the marital relationship that created their status remains intact. This Court is bound to apply the statute as written, not to expand its reach based on equitable considerations.

---

## CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully submits that the trial court's ruling was erroneous as a matter of law and must be reversed. The Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 because the marriage creating that relationship terminated in 1994, thirteen years before Audrey Blessing's death. At the time of her death, Audrey was unmarried, and no marital relationship existed between her and the biological parent of the Blaschka children.

The controlling precedent from our Supreme Court and this Court is unambiguous: stepchild status depends upon the continued existence of the marriage. *Williams*, 103 Wash.2d at 801; *Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. at 424. That requirement is not met here.

The Defense requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling and declare that the Blaschka children are not statutory beneficiaries under Washington's wrongful death statute.

**FINAL RULING**

**In re the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Upon Consideration of Closing Arguments**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of the Estate of Audrey Blessing regarding the trial court’s determination that the Blaschka children are “stepchildren” entitled to recovery under the wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the Estate (Appellant) and the Blaschka children (Respondents). Upon consideration, the Court finds that the trial court erred as a matter of law.

**ANALYSIS**

The sole issue before this Court is whether the Blaschka children qualify as “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020. The resolution of this issue turns on the plain language of the statute and controlling Washington precedent.

The Estate argues, and the law supports, that stepchild status is strictly derivative of the marital relationship between the stepparent and the biological parent. As established by the Washington Supreme Court in *In re Estate of Williams*, 103 Wash.2d 795, 801 (1985), “the status of stepchild is dependent upon the continued existence of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent.” This dependency is absolute; the legal status does not survive the dissolution of the marriage that created it.

The undisputed facts reveal that the marriage between Audrey Blessing and Carl Blaschka ended in 1994 upon Mr. Blaschka’s death. At the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007, she was unmarried. Therefore, the “continued existence” requirement mandated by *Williams* is not satisfied. This conclusion is bolstered by the holding of this Court in *In re Estate of Hodge*, 111 Wash. App. 416, 424 (2002), which affirms that “the stepchild status is extinguished when the marriage creating the relationship ends.” The mode of termination—whether by death, divorce, or remarriage—is irrelevant to this legal extinguishment.

The Blaschka children, in their closing argument, correctly acknowledge that the subsequent marriage of Audrey Blessing to Mr. Blessing legally superseded any prior stepchild relationship. They concede that the “bedrock requirement” of the statute is the existence of the marriage at the time of death. Their argument that a person cannot simultaneously occupy the position of stepparent to children from a prior marriage while being married to a different spouse aligns with the statutory interpretation that stepchild status is derivative of the marital bond. Once that bond was severed by death and subsequently replaced by a new marital bond to Mr. Blessing, the legal foundation for the Blaschka children’s status as stepchildren was irrevocably removed.

Furthermore, both parties agree that personal relationships cannot salvage statutory status where the legal predicate has ended. As noted in *In re Estate of Kline*, 106 Wash. App. 269, 278 (2001), “personal relationships or post-marital conduct cannot preserve stepchild status once the marriage terminates.” The fact that Audrey Blessing maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children and named them in her will speaks to her personal affections and testamentary intent, but it cannot create a statutory right where the marriage no longer exists. Wrongful death actions are distinct from probate matters, and standing must be derived strictly from the statute.

Both the Estate and the Blaschka children now agree that the trial court’s judgment was in error. The Estate argues that the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent preclude the Blaschka children’s status because the marriage creating that relationship had terminated. The Blaschka children concur, arguing that the intervening marriage of Audrey Blessing to a third husband legally superseded the prior stepchild relationship. Since the Respondents themselves concede that the preliminary ruling—which excluded the Blaschka children—was correct, and the Appellant vigorously advocates for the same result, there is no dispute remaining for this Court to resolve other than to enter judgment in accordance with the agreed-upon legal interpretation.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred as a matter of law in declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” under RCW 4.20.020. The undisputed facts establish that the marriage creating the stepchild relationship ended in 1994, and that Audrey Blessing was unmarried at the time of her death. Both the Appellant and the Respondents agree that the Blaschka children are not statutory beneficiaries. Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is reversed.
