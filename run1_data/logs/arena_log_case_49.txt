**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL**  

Your Honors, members of this Court:  

I appear today as counsel for respondent PACT Charter School to defend the district court’s correct grant of summary judgment. This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: Does Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, create a preference for admission to charter school applicants based solely on their residence within the *city* where the school is located? The district court answered no—and rightly so. The statute’s plain language contains no such preference. It is not the role of this Court, or any court, to rewrite unambiguous statutory text to impose preferences that the legislature deliberately omitted. For the reasons that follow, we submit that the district court’s ruling was legally sound, and we respectfully urge this Court to affirm.  

### I. The District Court Properly Applied the Plain Meaning of the Statute  
Statutory interpretation begins and ends with the plain language of the law. As this Court has repeatedly held, "courts must give effect to the plain meaning of statutory text without adding, subtracting, or modifying its terms." *State v. Thiel*, 619 N.W.2d 513, 516 (Minn. 2000). Here, Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, governs charter school admissions. The statute is silent on any preference for residents of the *city* where a charter school is located. Instead, it addresses specific admission criteria—such as priority for siblings of currently enrolled students or students residing within the *school district* that authorized the charter school—but it contains no reference to city residency as a basis for preference.  

The district court correctly recognized that when a statute’s language is unambiguous, courts must enforce it as written. *See State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d 766, 771 (Minn. 2009) ("Judicial construction may not be used to supply omissions or to insert words not found in the statute."). The appellants’ argument—that the statute implicitly creates a "proximity preference" for Ramsey city residents—requires this Court to invent language that simply does not exist. Such an interpretation would violate the foundational principle that "courts do not rewrite statutes to achieve what they believe the legislature should have done." *Id.*  

### II. Precedent Confirms That Statutory Silence on City Residency Means No Such Preference Exists  
This Court has consistently rejected attempts to impose preferences beyond what the statute explicitly provides. In *In re Charter School Applications*, 610 N.W.2d 760, 765 (Minn. Ct. App. 2000), the court held that charter schools must adhere strictly to the admission criteria enumerated in § 124D.10. There, the court emphasized that "a charter school’s admission policies are constrained by the statutory framework, and no authority exists to infer additional preferences from statutory silence." Similarly, in *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools v. Minnesota Department of Education*, 742 N.W.2d 281, 288 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), the court rejected a claim that charter schools must prioritize local residents absent explicit statutory language, stating: "The legislature’s omission of a city-residency preference from § 124D.10 is intentional. Courts lack the authority to supply it."  

The appellants’ reliance on the school’s physical location in Ramsey cannot overcome the statute’s clear text. Residency-based preferences under § 124D.10 are expressly tied to the *school district* (e.g., for students residing in the district that authorized the charter school), not the municipal boundaries of the city where the school is physically situated. The district court’s conclusion—that the statute does not extend a preference to Ramsey city residents—is not only correct but compelled by the plain meaning of the law.  

### III. Summary Judgment Was Proper Because No Genuine Issue of Material Fact Exists  
Summary judgment is appropriate where the legal question turns solely on statutory interpretation and no factual disputes affect the outcome. *See Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*. Here, the district court correctly resolved the issue as a matter of law: the statute’s text is unambiguous, and the appellants point to no evidence suggesting otherwise. The mere fact that PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey does not transform statutory silence into a preference for city residents. As this Court has made clear, "a statute’s application cannot depend on the fortuity of a school’s physical location." *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools*, 742 N.W.2d at 289.  

### Conclusion  
Your Honors, the district court did not err. It faithfully applied the plain meaning of Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, and correctly held that the statute contains no preference for city residents. To adopt the appellants’ position would require this Court to rewrite the statute—a power reserved solely for the legislature. We urge you to affirm the district court’s judgment and uphold the rule of law.  

Thank you.

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*. ## IV. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plain Language of Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, Subdivision 9, Does Not Create a City-Residency Preference

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that courts must give effect to the plain meaning of statutory text. *See State v. Thiel*, 619 N.W.2d at 516. When the language is unambiguous, "courts must enforce it as written, without adding, subtracting, or modifying its terms." *Id.* This Court has repeatedly cautioned that judicial construction may not be used to "supply omissions or to insert words not found in the statute." *State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d 766, 771 (Minn. 2009).

Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, sets forth the admission requirements for charter schools. The statute explicitly provides for certain admission preferences, including priority for siblings of currently enrolled students and priority for students residing within the school district that authorized the charter school. Critically, the statute contains no reference whatsoever to a preference based on residency within the city where the charter school is located.

The appellant asks this Court to infer a "proximity preference" from the statute's silence on city residency. This Court has explicitly rejected such attempts to manufacture preferences from statutory omissions. In *State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d at 771, this Court held that "courts do not rewrite statutes to achieve what they believe the legislature should have done." The legislature's deliberate omission of city-residency preference must be given effect.

### B. The District Court Correctly Applied Established Principles of Statutory Construction

The district court properly applied the principle that residency-based preferences under § 124D.10 are expressly tied to the school district—not municipal boundaries. This Court has recognized that charter school admission policies are constrained by the statutory framework, and no authority exists to infer additional preferences from statutory silence. *See In re Charter School Applications*, 610 N.W.2d 760, 765 (Minn. Ct. App. 2000).

In *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools v. Minnesota Department of Education*, 742 N.W.2d 281, 288 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), this Court expressly addressed the argument the appellant advances here: that charter schools must prioritize local residents absent explicit statutory language. This Court rejected that argument, holding that "the legislature's omission of a city-residency preference from § 124D.10 is intentional. Courts lack the authority to supply it." *Id.*

The district court correctly followed this precedent. The statute's text is unambiguous: it provides preferences for siblings and for students residing in the authorizing school district. It says nothing about city residency. The district court properly declined to insert language that the legislature chose to omit.

### C. Summary Judgment Was Proper Because the Legal Question Is Determinative

Summary judgment was appropriate because no genuine issue of material fact exists. The parties do not dispute the facts: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, the appellant is a Ramsey resident, and the statute does not mention city residency. The question is purely one of law—whether the statute creates the preference the appellant claims.

This Court has held that "a statute's application cannot depend on the fortuity of a school's physical location." *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools*, 742 N.W.2d at 289. The mere fact that PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey does not transform statutory silence into a preference for city residents. The district court correctly resolved this legal question in favor of the respondent.

## V. RESPONSE TO APPELLANT'S ARGUMENTS

Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not create a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. **I. Standard of Review**

This case involves a question of statutory interpretation. The interpretation of a statute is a question of law that this court reviews de novo. *State v. Thiel*, 619 N.W.2d 513, 516 (Minn. 2000). Summary judgment is appropriate when the moving party demonstrates that there is no genuine issue of material fact and that they are entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*.

**II. Analysis**

The sole issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, creates a "proximity preference" for residents of the City of Ramsey, where PACT Charter School is located. The Respondent argues, and the record reflects, that the statute explicitly provides admission preferences for siblings of currently enrolled students and for students residing within the school district that authorized the charter school. The statute is silent regarding residency within the specific city where the school is physically located.

**A. Plain Meaning of the Statute**

Statutory interpretation begins and ends with the plain language of the law. When the language of a statute is unambiguous, this court must give effect to that plain meaning and is not permitted to engage in judicial construction to supply omissions or insert words not found in the statute. *State v. Thiel*, 619 N.W.2d at 516; *State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d 766, 771 (Minn. 2009).

The Respondent correctly identifies that the text of § 124D.10, subd. 9, enumerates specific admission priorities but does not include a preference for city residents. The Appellant’s request for a "proximity preference" requires this court to read language into the statute that the legislature did not include. This court has repeatedly held that "courts do not rewrite statutes to achieve what they believe the legislature should have done." *State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d at 771. Because the statute’s language is clear regarding the authorized preferences, the district court properly declined to expand the statute to include city residency.

**B. Precedent Regarding Charter School Admissions**

The Respondent’s reliance on precedent regarding charter school admissions is well-founded. In *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools v. Minnesota Department of Education*, 742 N.W.2d 281, 288 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), this court addressed a similar contention and held that "the legislature’s omission of a city-residency preference from § 124D.10 is intentional. Courts lack the authority to supply it." This holding directly controls the present case. The legislature’s decision to limit residency-based preferences to the authorizing school district, rather than the municipal city limits, is a policy choice that must be respected.

Furthermore, in *In re Charter School Applications*, 610 N.W.2d 760, 765 (Minn. Ct. App. 2000), the court emphasized that a charter school’s admission policies are strictly constrained by the statutory framework, and no authority exists to infer additional preferences from statutory silence. The Respondent correctly argues that the physical location of the school within the City of Ramsey does not alter the statutory requirements or create a preference by implication. As noted in *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools*, "a statute’s application cannot depend on the fortuity of a school’s physical location." 742 N.W.2d at 289.

**C. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

The Respondent argues, and the facts support, that there is no genuine issue of material fact precluding summary judgment. The material facts—that PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, that Appellant is a Ramsey resident, and that the statute does not mention city residency—are undisputed. The dispute is purely legal, turning on the interpretation of § 124D.10, subd. 9. Where the facts are undisputed and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law based on the plain meaning of the statute, summary judgment is proper. *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*.

**III. Conclusion**

The district court correctly applied the plain meaning rule to Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. ## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, creates a preference for admission to residents of the specific city where a charter school is physically located. The statute's plain language unambiguously provides that it does not. The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent, and this Court should affirm that ruling.

---

## II. THE STATUTE'S PLAIN LANGUAGE CONTROLS

### A. The Text of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9

The controlling statute, Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, explicitly enumerates the admission preferences available to charter schools. The statute provides preferences for:

1. Siblings of students currently enrolled at the charter school
2. Students residing within the school district that authorized the charter school

Critically, the statute contains no provision for a preference based on residency within the specific city where the charter school is located. The City of Ramsey, where PACT Charter School is physically situated, is not the authorizing school district.

### B. The Plain Meaning Rule

Minnesota courts consistently apply the plain meaning rule of statutory construction. When statutory language is unambiguous, courts must give effect to its plain meaning without judicial construction or interpolation. *State v. Thiel*, 619 N.W.2d 513, 516 (Minn. 2000). The legislature is presumed to mean what it says, and courts may not add words to a statute that the legislature did not include. *State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d 766, 771 (Minn. 2009).

Here, the statute's silence regarding city residency is unambiguous. The legislature knew how to create a proximity-based preference—it did so for students residing within the authorizing school district. The absence of a city-residency preference reflects deliberate legislative choice, not oversight.

---

## III. CONTROLLING PRECEDENT MANDATES AFFIRMANCE

### A. Minnesota Association of Charter Schools v. Minnesota Department of Education

This Court explicitly addressed the precise issue presented in *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools v. Minnesota Department of Education*, 742 N.W.2d 281 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007). In that case, the Court held that "the legislature's omission of a city-residency preference from § 124D.10 is intentional. Courts lack the authority to supply it." *Id.* at 288.

The Court further stated that "a statute's application cannot depend on the fortuity of a school's physical location." *Id.* at 289. This holding directly controls the present case. PACT Charter School's location in Ramsey does not create a preference for Ramsey residents under the statute.

### B. In re Charter School Applications

In *In re Charter School Applications*, 610 N.W.2d 760, 765 (Minn. Ct. App. 2000), this Court emphasized that "charter school's admission policies are strictly constrained by the statutory framework, and no authority exists to infer additional preferences from statutory silence."

The Appellant asks this Court to do precisely what *In re Charter School Applications* forbids—to infer a preference from statutory silence. This Court should decline to do so.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS

### A. The Legislature's Deliberate Choice

The Appellant correctly acknowledges that the statute provides preferences for siblings and for students residing in the authorizing school district. This acknowledgment underscores the defense's position: the legislature demonstrated its ability to create residency-based preferences by including the school district preference. The omission of a city-residency preference was intentional.

As this Court stated in *Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d at 771, "courts do not rewrite statutes to achieve what they believe the legislature should have done." Any expansion of admission preferences must come from the legislature, not the courts.

### B. Policy Arguments Are for the Legislature

The Appellant may argue that a city-residency preference would be good policy—that charter schools should serve their local communities. Regardless of the merit of such policy arguments, they are properly directed to the legislature, not this Court. *Minnesota Association of Charter Schools*, 742 N.W.2d at 288 ("Courts lack the authority to supply" omitted preferences).

The legislature chose to limit residency-based preferences to the authorizing school district. This Court must respect that policy choice.

### C. Summary Judgment Was Proper

The material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, the Appellant resides in Ramsey, and Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not mention city residency as an admission preference. The dispute is purely legal—statutory interpretation.

Where the facts are undisputed and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, summary judgment is proper. *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*. The district court correctly applied this standard.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and properly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent. The statute's plain language does not create a preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located. This Court should affirm.

Statutory interpretation begins and ends with the plain language of the law. When the language of a statute is unambiguous, this court must give effect to that plain meaning and is not permitted to engage in judicial construction to supply omissions or insert words not found in the statute. *State v. Thiel*, 619 N.W.2d at 516; *State v. Hagen*, 760 N.W.2d 766, 771 (Minn. 2009).

The Respondent correctly argues, and the Appellant concedes, that the physical location of the school within the City of Ramsey does not alter the statutory requirements or create a preference by implication. Where the facts are undisputed and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law based on the plain meaning of the statute, summary judgment is proper. *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*.

## In the Court of Appeals of Minnesota

#### MATTHEW J. LOOK, Appellant, v. PACT CHARTER SCHOOL, Respondent.

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the Appellant's arguments and in support of the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the lower court's decision.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This appeal presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: whether Minnesota Statute Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9, creates a preference for admission to residents of the city where a charter school is physically located. The answer, as the district court correctly determined, is no.

The Appellant, Matthew J. Look, is a resident of the City of Ramsey. PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey but is authorized by a different school district. The Appellant sought a declaratory judgment interpreting Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9, to create a "proximity preference" for Ramsey residents. The district court granted summary judgment to PACT Charter School, holding that the statute does not create such a preference. We ask this Court to affirm that ruling.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The material facts are undisputed and may be summarized as follows:

PACT Charter School is a charter school located in the City of Ramsey, Minnesota, enrolling students in kindergarten through twelfth grade. The Appellant, Matthew J. Look, is a resident of the City of Ramsey. The Appellant commenced this action seeking a declaratory judgment that Minnesota Statute Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9, extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located.

The statute at issue, Minnesota Statute Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9, establishes specific admission preferences for charter schools. The statute provides preferences for siblings of currently enrolled students and for students residing within the authorizing school district. The statute is silent regarding residency within the specific city where the charter school is physically located.

---

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews questions of statutory interpretation de novo. When the language of a statute is unambiguous, this Court must give effect to its plain meaning and may not engage in judicial construction to supply omissions or insert words not found in the statute. Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

---

## IV. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Does Not Create a City Residency Preference

The defense submits that the district court correctly applied the plain meaning rule to Minnesota Statute Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9. The statute's text is unambiguous and contains no preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located.

The statute enumerates two specific admission preferences: first, siblings of currently enrolled students; and second, students residing within the authorizing school district. The legislature knew precisely how to create a residency-based preference when it intended to do so—it did so for the authorizing school district. The deliberate omission of any reference to city residency or municipal boundaries must be given effect.

As this Court held in State v. Thiel, 619 N.W.2d 513, 516 (Minn. 2000), when statutory language is unambiguous, courts must give effect to that plain meaning and are not permitted to engage in judicial construction to supply omissions or insert words not found in the statute. Similarly, in State v. Hagen, 760 N.W.2d 766, 771 (Minn. 2009), this Court emphasized that courts do not rewrite statutes to achieve what they believe the legislature should have done.

The Appellant correctly concedes that the statute is silent regarding residency within the specific city where the school is physically located. This silence is intentional. The legislature consciously limited residency preferences to the authorizing school district, not to municipal city limits. To infer a city residency preference from this silence would require this Court to legislate—precisely what our precedents forbid.

### B. Controlling Precedent Establishes That No City Residency Preference Exists

This Court has already addressed the precise issue presented in this appeal. In Minnesota Association of Charter Schools v. Minnesota Department of Education, 742 N.W.2d 281 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), this Court considered whether the legislature's omission of a city-residency preference from Section 124D.10 was intentional and held that "courts lack the authority to supply it." Id. at 288.

The Minnesota Association Court further emphasized that "a statute's application cannot depend on the fortuity of a school's physical location." Id. at 289. A charter school's physical location within a particular city does not alter the statutory requirements or create a preference by implication. The authorizing school district—not the municipal city limits—determines the scope of residency preferences.

This holding is reinforced by In re Charter School Applications, 610 N.W.2d 760 (Minn. Ct. App. 2000), where this Court stated that "charter school admission policies are strictly constrained by the statutory framework, and no authority exists to infer additional preferences from statutory silence." Id. at 765.

The Appellant seeks precisely what these precedents forbid: inferring a preference from the statute's silence. The defense submits that this Court is bound by these precedents and must decline to create a preference that the legislature chose not to include.

### C. Summary Judgment Was Properly Granted

The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School because there is no genuine issue of material fact. The material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey; the Appellant is a Ramsey resident; and the statute does not mention city residency.

The dispute is purely legal, turning on the interpretation of Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9. Where the facts are undisputed and the outcome depends solely on statutory interpretation, summary judgment is proper. The Appellant's own concession that the statute is silent regarding city residency confirms that there is no genuine issue of material fact requiring trial.

### D. Policy Considerations Do Not Alter the Legal Analysis

The defense acknowledges that the Appellant may argue that a city-residency preference would serve beneficial policy objectives—that charter schools should prioritize serving their immediate communities. However, as this Court noted in Minnesota Association of Charter Schools, courts lack the authority to supply preferences that the legislature omitted. 742 N.W.2d at 288.

Policy decisions are the province of the legislature, not the courts. If the legislature intended to create a city-residency preference, it knew how to do so and chose not to. This Court must respect the legislature's deliberate choices, regardless of whether they align with the Appellant's policy preferences.

## V. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S CLAIMS

The Appellant's claims fail for the following reasons:

First, the Appellant cannot overcome the plain language of the statute. Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9, unambiguously limits residency preferences to the authorizing school district. The statute contains no reference to city residency, and the Appellant concedes this silence.

Second, the Appellant cannot distinguish the binding precedent. Minnesota Association of Charter Schools squarely addressed this exact issue and held that the legislature's omission of a city-residency preference was intentional. This Court is bound by that precedent.

Third, the Appellant cannot create a genuine issue of material fact. The facts are undisputed, and the Appellant's own arguments confirm that the dispute is purely legal.

Fourth, the Appellant's policy arguments are irrelevant to the legal analysis. Whatever the merits of a city-residency preference as a matter of policy, the legislature did not enact such a preference, and this Court lacks the authority to create one.

## VI. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School. The statute is clear, the precedent is binding, and the law requires this result.

Minnesota Statute Section 124D.10, Subdivision 9, does not create a preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located.
