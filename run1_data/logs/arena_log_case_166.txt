## ARGUMENT

### I. STANDARD OF REVIEW

When reviewing a judgment entered after the superior court sustains a demurrer without leave to amend, this Court must determine whether the complaint states a viable cause of action. (Evans v. City of Berkeley (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241].) This Court treats the demurrer as admitting all well-pleaded allegations of material fact but does not admit contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. (Evans, supra, 38 Cal.4th at p. 6.)

---

### II. THE COMPASSIONATE USE ACT DOES NOT CREATE A PRIVATE RIGHT OF ACTION AGAINST EMPLOYERS

The central issue in this case is whether the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 creates a private right of action that allows employees to sue their employers for termination based on medical marijuana use. The answer is no.

The Compassionate Use Act, codified at Health and Safety Code section 11362.5, was added by initiative (Proposition 215) in the November 1996 general election. The statute provides that persons who use marijuana for medical purposes upon a physician's recommendation "shall not be subject to criminal prosecution" under certain state criminal provisions involving marijuana. (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (d).)

Critically, nothing in the text of the Compassionate Use Act suggests that the voters intended the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees. The statute by its terms provides a defense to certain *state criminal charges*—not a cause of action against private employers. The statute was designed to exempt qualifying patients from criminal prosecution under California's marijuana prohibition laws; it was not intended to regulate the employment relationship.

In Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], the California Supreme Court recognized that employers may require preemployment drug tests and may take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. This holding reinforces the principle that the Compassionate Use Act does not create workplace protections.

The legislative history of Proposition 215 contains no indication that the voters contemplated or intended the measure to affect employer-employee relationships. The ballot argument presented to the voters focused exclusively on medical use and criminal prosecution—it made no reference to employment, workplace rights, or accommodations. (See Ballot Argument, Prop. 215, Gen. Elec. (Nov. 5, 1996).)

Therefore, the trial court correctly concluded that the Compassionate Use Act does not provide Plaintiff with a cause of action against his employer.

---

### III. PLAINTIFF FAILS TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR DISCRIMINATION UNDER THE FEHA

Plaintiff's first cause of action alleges that Defendant violated the FEHA by discharging him because of his disability and by failing to make reasonable accommodation for his disability. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a).) This cause of action fails for two independent reasons.

#### A. The FEHA Does Not Require Employers to Accommodate the Use of Illegal Drugs

The FEHA prohibits employment discrimination based on disability. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a).) However, the FEHA's protections do not extend to individuals who use illegal drugs. Government Code section 12940, subdivision (c), provides that it is not unlawful for an employer to discriminate against an individual "who is currently engaging in the illegal use of drugs" when the employer acts based on that use.

Marijuana remains illegal under federal law (see 21 U.S.C. §§ 801 et seq.) and is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance. Although the Compassionate Use Act provides an affirmative defense to criminal prosecution under California law, it does not legalize marijuana or transform its use into lawful conduct. The use of marijuana—whether for medical purposes or otherwise—constitutes illegal drug use under both federal and state law.

In Loder v. City of Glendale, supra, 14 Cal.4th at pp. 882-883, the California Supreme Court held that an employer may require preemployment drug tests and may consider illegal drug use in employment decisions. The Court explicitly recognized that "the use of marijuana remains illegal under federal law" and that employers are not required to accommodate such use. (Id. at p. 883.)

Here, Plaintiff used marijuana in violation of federal law, regardless of his physician's recommendation. The FEHA does not require Defendant to accommodate this illegal drug use, and Defendant did not violate the FEHA by terminating Plaintiff based on his positive drug test.

#### B. Plaintiff Fails to Allege That He Was Qualified to Perform the Essential Functions of the Job

Even if the FEHA somehow applied to medical marijuana use, Plaintiff fails to state a viable discrimination claim because he has not alleged that he was qualified to perform the essential functions of his position. To state a cause of action for disability discrimination under the FEHA, a plaintiff must allege that he or she is a qualified individual with a disability—meaning the individual can perform the essential functions of the job with or without reasonable accommodation. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (c)(1).)

While Plaintiff alleges that his disability and marijuana use do not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of the job, this conclusory allegation is insufficient. More critically, Plaintiff has not alleged that he could perform these functions *at the time of his termination*. An employee who uses illegal drugs—even for medical purposes—may be disqualified from certain positions, particularly those requiring drug-free status as a condition of employment.

Defendant hired Plaintiff for a position subject to a drug-testing requirement. By using marijuana, Plaintiff could not comply with the conditions of his employment. Defendant was not required to create a special exception for Plaintiff or to accommodate his violation of the employer's lawful drug policy.

---

### IV. PLAINTIFF FAILS TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR WRONGFUL TERMINATION IN VIOLATION OF PUBLIC POLICY

Plaintiff's second cause of action alleges wrongful termination in violation of public policy, citing Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157] and Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

To state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must allege that the termination violated a policy that is (1) embodied in a constitutional or statutory provision, and (2) designed to protect the health, safety, or welfare of the public. (Tameny, supra, 27 Cal.3d at pp. 176-178.)

Plaintiff cannot satisfy this standard. The Compassionate Use Act does not embody a public policy that protects employers or the workplace from the consequences of employee drug use. To the contrary, the statute was designed to protect *patients* from criminal prosecution—not to regulate employment relationships.

Moreover, there is no constitutional or statutory provision that prohibits employers from terminating employees who use illegal drugs, even if used for medical purposes. The FEHA itself contemplates such terminations. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (c).)

In Tameny, the California Supreme Court recognized that a wrongful termination claim requires a violation of a public policy that serves the community as a whole—not merely a private interest between employer and employee. (Tameny, supra, 27 Cal.3d at pp. 176-178.) The Compassionate Use Act does not serve this purpose. It was not enacted to protect the public from workplace hazards or to regulate the employment relationship; it was enacted to provide an affirmative defense to criminal prosecution for patients who use marijuana for medical purposes.

Therefore, the trial court correctly concluded that Plaintiff failed to state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

---

### V. DEFENDANT'S DRUG TESTING POLICY IS LAWFUL AND CONSTITUTIONAL

Plaintiff's termination was based on a lawful preemployment drug test administered as a condition of employment. In Loder v. City of Glendale, supra, 14 Cal.4th at pp. 882-883, the California Supreme Court explicitly held that employers may require preemployment drug tests and may consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions.

Defendant's drug testing policy is a legitimate business practice designed to ensure a drug-free workplace. Defendant had no obligation to accommodate Plaintiff's use of marijuana, even if used for medical purposes, because such use constitutes illegal drug use under federal law. (See 21 U.S.C. §§ 801 et seq.)

---

### VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the superior court. Plaintiff's complaint fails to state viable causes of action for disability discrimination under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The Compassionate Use Act does not create a private right of action against employers, does not require employers to accommodate the use of marijuana, and does not embody a public policy that protects employees from termination based on drug use. Defendant's lawful drug testing policy and termination of Plaintiff were proper, and the trial court correctly sustained the demurrer without leave to amend.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. We have reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. Notably, plaintiff’s counsel has conceded that the trial court correctly sustained the demurrer, acknowledging that plaintiff failed to state viable causes of action under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. Defendant’s rebuttal provides additional grounds for affirmance, including procedural deficiencies in plaintiff’s complaint. For the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the judgment of the superior court must be affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

As previously stated, in reviewing a judgment entered after the superior court sustains a demurrer without leave to amend, we must determine whether the complaint states a viable cause of action. We treat the demurrer as admitting all well-pleaded allegations of material fact but do not admit contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. (Evans v. City of Berkeley (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241].)

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. Plaintiff Concedes that the Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

Plaintiff’s counsel correctly concedes that the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) was designed solely to provide a defense to criminal prosecution and does not create workplace protections. The statute’s text limits its scope to "a defense to certain state criminal charges" and contains no language suggesting voter intent to regulate employer-employee relationships. (Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200].) As acknowledged by plaintiff, the Act “by its terms provides a defense to certain state criminal charges—not a cause of action against private employers.” (Id.) Therefore, the Compassionate Use Act cannot support a FEHA claim against an employer.

**B. Plaintiff Concedes that the FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation for Illegal Drug Use**

Plaintiff further concedes that the FEHA explicitly permits employers to discriminate against individuals currently engaging in illegal drug use. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (c).) Plaintiff’s claim for failure to accommodation fails because marijuana use—regardless of medical recommendation—constitutes "illegal drug use" under California law for employment purposes. In Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], the California Supreme Court held that "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." This precedent controls the outcome of this claim.

Additionally, plaintiff’s complaint fails to allege facts showing he was a "qualified individual" under the FEHA. To state a disability discrimination claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate the ability to perform the essential functions of the job with or without accommodation. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (c)(1).) Plaintiff’s admission that he used marijuana in violation of defendant’s lawful drug policy—coupled with the absence of any allegation that he could perform his duties without violating that policy—confirms that he cannot satisfy the FEHA’s qualification requirement.

**C. Plaintiff Concedes Failure to State a Wrongful Termination Claim**

Plaintiff also concedes that his wrongful termination claim lacks legal foundation. To state such a claim, a plaintiff must show termination violated a public policy "embodied in a constitutional or statutory provision" designed to protect public health, safety, or welfare. (Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330]; Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157].) As plaintiff correctly notes, the Compassionate Use Act does not meet this standard because it was enacted "to protect patients from criminal prosecution—not to regulate employment relationships." (Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200].)

**D. Alternative Grounds for Affirmance**

Even in the absence of plaintiff’s concessions, defendant has identified independent grounds that require affirmance of the superior court’s judgment.

**1. Failure to Exhaust Administrative Remedies**

Before filing suit under the FEHA, a plaintiff must file a complaint with the California Department of Fair Employment and Housing (DFEH) and obtain a right-to-sue letter. (Gov. Code, § 12960; see also Thompson v. City of Monrovia (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 1055, 1063 [115 Cal.Rptr.3d 29].) Plaintiff’s complaint does not allege that he filed a DFEH complaint or obtained a right-to-sue letter. This procedural deficiency provides an independent ground for dismissal of the FEHA claims.

**2. Failure to Trigger the Interactive Process**

The FEHA imposes a duty on employers to engage in a timely, good faith, interactive process with the employee to determine effective reasonable accommodations. However, this duty is generally triggered by a request for accommodation from the employee. (See Zivkovic v. Southern California Edison Co. (2002) 302 F.3d 1080, 1089; Humphrey v. Memorial Hospitals Association (2003) 239 F.3d 1128, 1137.) Plaintiff requested no accommodation for his back condition; he only informed defendant of his marijuana use after testing positive. Because plaintiff never requested accommodation, defendant cannot be liable for failing to engage in the interactive process.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on plaintiff’s concessions and the additional grounds raised by defendant, it is clear that the trial court properly sustained the demurrer. The Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights, the FEHA does not require accommodation for marijuana use, and plaintiff failed to state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. Furthermore, the complaint is procedurally deficient due to the failure to exhaust administrative remedies and the failure to trigger the interactive process. The judgment of the superior court is affirmed.

Your Honor, the defendant-respondent RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the affirmance of the judgment of the superior court.

We thank the Court for the thorough analysis provided in the updated ruling, and we note with appreciation that plaintiff's counsel has made appropriate concessions regarding the legal deficiencies in their client's complaint. For the reasons set forth below, and as essentially conceded by plaintiff, the judgment of the superior court must be affirmed.

---

## I. THE COMPASSIONATE USE ACT DOES NOT CREATE EMPLOYMENT RIGHTS

The Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) was enacted by voter initiative to provide a defense to certain state criminal charges involving marijuana. The statute expressly provides that its purpose is to "ensure that seriously ill Californians have the right to obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes" upon a physician's recommendation. Critically, the Act contains no language suggesting voter intent to regulate employer-employee relationships or to create any affirmative duty on the part of employers to accommodate medical marijuana use.

As this Court recognized in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." The Act "by its terms provides a defense to certain state criminal charges—not a cause of action against private employers." (Id.) The plaintiff's complaint, which acknowledges that he used marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation solely to obtain a criminal defense, cannot state a cause of action against his employer based on the Compassionate Use Act.

---

## II. THE FEHA DOES NOT REQUIRE ACCOMMODATION FOR ILLEGAL DRUG USE

The California Fair Employment and Housing Act (Gov. Code, § 12940) explicitly permits employers to discriminate against individuals currently engaging in illegal drug use. Government Code section 12940, subdivision (c) provides that it is not unlawful for an employer to refuse to hire or discharge an employee "because of the individual's current illegal use of drugs."

The facts of this case establish that the plaintiff tested positive for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the primary psychoactive component of marijuana. Regardless of whether the plaintiff possessed a physician's recommendation for medical marijuana use, his use constituted "illegal drug use" under California law for employment purposes. The plaintiff has conceded this point in his closing statement.

To state a claim for failure to accommodate under the FEHA, a plaintiff must demonstrate that he is a "qualified individual" who can perform the essential functions of the job with or without reasonable accommodation. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (c)(1).) Here, the plaintiff's own allegations establish that he used marijuana in violation of the defendant's lawful drug policy. Because the FEHA does not require employers to accommodate illegal drug use—even when accompanied by a physician's recommendation—the plaintiff's failure-to-accommodate claim fails as a matter of law.

---

## III. THE WRONGFUL TERMINATION CLAIM FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW

To state a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the termination violated a public policy "embodied in a constitutional or statutory provision" designed to protect public health, safety, or welfare. (Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330]; Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157].)

The Compassionate Use Act was enacted to protect patients from criminal prosecution—not to regulate employment relationships. As such, it does not embody a public policy sufficient to support a wrongful termination claim. The plaintiff has correctly conceded this point.

---

## IV. ALTERNATIVE GROUNDS FOR AFFIRMANCE ARE INDEPENDENTLY VALID

Even absent the plaintiff's concessions, the defendant has identified multiple independent grounds requiring affirmance of the superior court's judgment.

### A. Failure to Exhaust Administrative Remedies

Before filing suit under the FEHA, a plaintiff must file a complaint with the California Department of Fair Employment and Housing (DFEH) and obtain a right-to-sue letter. (Gov. Code, § 12960.) As this Court held in *Thompson v. City of Monrovia* (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 1055, 1063 [115 Cal.Rptr.3d 29], failure to allege compliance with this prerequisite "is a fatal procedural defect that warrants dismissal."

The plaintiff's complaint contains no allegation that he filed a DFEH complaint or obtained a right-to-sue letter. This procedural deficiency provides an independent ground for dismissal of the FEHA claims.

### B. Failure to Trigger the Interactive Process

The FEHA imposes a duty on employers to engage in a timely, good faith, interactive process with the employee to determine effective reasonable accommodations. However, this duty is triggered only by a request for accommodation from the employee. (Zivkovic v. Southern California Edison Co. (2002) 302 F.3d 1080, 1089; Humphrey v. Memorial Hospitals Association (2003) 239 F.3d 1128, 1137.)

The plaintiff never requested an accommodation for his back condition. He only informed the defendant of his marijuana use after testing positive for THC. Because the plaintiff failed to request accommodation, the defendant cannot be held liable for failing to engage in the interactive process.

The defendant respectfully submits that the judgment of the superior court should be affirmed. The plaintiff's complaint fails to state viable causes of action for several independent reasons: (1) the Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights; (2) the FEHA does not require accommodation for illegal drug use; (3) the plaintiff failed to state a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy; (4) the plaintiff failed to exhaust administrative remedies; and (5) the plaintiff failed to trigger the interactive process.

The defendant acted lawfully in requiring preemployment drug testing and in terminating the plaintiff's employment when he tested positive for marijuana. The defendant's drug policy is a lawful, legitimate, and non-discriminatory workplace policy that applies to all employees regardless of disability.

For all the foregoing reasons, and as essentially conceded by the plaintiff, the judgment of the superior court must be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**IN THE COURT OF APPEAL OF THE STATE OF CALIFORNIA**

**GARY ROSS,**

**RAGINGWIRE TELECOMMUNICATIONS, INC.,**
Defendant and Respondent.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. We have reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both parties. In a notable turn of events, plaintiff’s counsel has conceded that the superior court correctly sustained the demurrer, acknowledging that plaintiff failed to state viable causes of action under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. Defendant’s closing argument supports this conclusion and raises additional procedural grounds for affirmance. For the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the judgment of the superior court must be affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In reviewing a judgment entered after the superior court sustains a demurrer without leave to amend, we must determine whether the complaint states a viable cause of action. We treat the demurrer as admitting all well-pleaded allegations of material fact but do not admit contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. (Evans v. City of Berkeley (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241].)

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

Plaintiff concedes, and we agree, that the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) was designed solely to provide a defense to criminal prosecution and does not create workplace protections. The statute’s text limits its scope to "a defense to certain state criminal charges" and contains no language suggesting voter intent to regulate employer-employee relationships. As plaintiff correctly notes, the Act “by its terms provides a defense to certain state criminal charges—not a cause of action against private employers.” (Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200].) Therefore, the Compassionate Use Act cannot support a FEHA claim against an employer.

**B. The FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation for Illegal Drug Use**

Plaintiff further concedes that the FEHA explicitly permits employers to discriminate against individuals currently engaging in illegal drug use. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (c).) Plaintiff’s claim for failure to accommodate fails because marijuana use—regardless of medical recommendation—constitutes "illegal drug use" under California law for employment purposes. In Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], the California Supreme Court held that "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." This precedent controls the outcome of this claim.

Additionally, plaintiff’s complaint fails to allege facts showing he was a "qualified individual" under the FEHA. To state a disability discrimination claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate the ability to perform the essential functions of the job with or without accommodation. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (c)(1).) Plaintiff’s admission that he used marijuana in violation of defendant’s lawful drug policy—coupled with the absence of any allegation that he could perform his duties without violating that policy—confirms that he cannot satisfy the FEHA’s qualification requirement.

**C. Plaintiff Concedes Failure to State a Wrongful Termination Claim**

Plaintiff also concedes that his wrongful termination claim lacks legal foundation. To state such a claim, a plaintiff must show termination violated a public policy "embodied in a constitutional or statutory provision" designed to protect public health, safety, or welfare. (Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330]; Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157].) As plaintiff correctly notes, the Compassionate Use Act does not meet this standard because it was enacted "to protect patients from criminal prosecution—not to regulate employment relationships." (Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200].)

**D. Alternative Grounds for Affirmance**

Even in the absence of plaintiff’s concessions, defendant has identified independent grounds that require affirmance of the superior court’s judgment.

**1. Failure to Exhaust Administrative Remedies**

Before filing suit under the FEHA, a plaintiff must file a complaint with the California Department of Fair Employment and Housing (DFEH) and obtain a right-to-sue letter. (Gov. Code, § 12960; see also Thompson v. City of Monrovia (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 1055, 1063 [115 Cal.Rptr.3d 29].) Plaintiff’s complaint does not allege that he filed a DFEH complaint or obtained a right-to-sue letter. This procedural deficiency provides an independent ground for dismissal of the FEHA claims.

**2. Failure to Trigger the Interactive Process**

The FEHA imposes a duty on employers to engage in a timely, good faith, interactive process with the employee to determine effective reasonable accommodations. However, this duty is generally triggered by a request for accommodation from the employee. (See Zivkovic v. Southern California Edison Co. (2002) 302 F.3d 1080, 1089; Humphrey v. Memorial Hospitals Association (2003) 239 F.3d 1128, 1137.) Plaintiff requested no accommodation for his back condition; he only informed defendant of his marijuana use after testing positive. Because plaintiff never requested accommodation, defendant cannot be liable for failing to engage in the interactive process.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on plaintiff’s concessions and the additional grounds raised by defendant, it is clear that the trial court properly sustained the demurrer. The Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights, the FEHA does not require accommodation for marijuana use, and plaintiff failed to state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. Furthermore, the complaint is procedurally deficient due to the failure to exhaust administrative remedies and the failure to trigger the interactive process. The judgment of the superior court is affirmed.
