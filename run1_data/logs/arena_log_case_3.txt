# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The defendant-appellant respectfully submits this opening response to rebut the plaintiff's arguments and to demonstrate that the conviction for criminal stalking should be reversed. The prosecution has failed to establish that the defendant's conduct constituted criminal stalking under Vermont law, and numerous constitutional and procedural errors infected the proceedings below. This defense response addresses each of the prosecution's claims and establishes that the conviction must be overturned.

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS RELEVANT TO DEFENSE

The defense emphasizes the following facts, drawn from the record, which are critical to this appeal:

The relationship between the complainant and defendant began in December 2006 and ended in May or June 2007—nearly eight years before the defendant was charged in June 2015. The complainant herself testified that she ended the relationship after a "heated argument" and told the defendant she wanted it "finished." The defendant left the scene without incident following this breakup.

The incidents cited by the prosecution span from 2007 to 2015, a period of approximately eight years. Many of the most serious allegations—including the 2008 campus incident, the 2010 parking lot incident, and the 2011 website comments—occurred well outside the three-year statute of limitations that applies to stalking charges under 13 V.S.A. § 4501.

The defendant's communications, while perhaps inappropriate or annoying, consisted largely of emails, phone calls, and public comments on a publicly accessible art website. The 2015 book was distributed as a self-published autobiography on a public street corner. None of these communications involved direct, in-person threats with the apparent ability to carry them out.

The complainant acknowledged that she obtained a relief-from-abuse order in 2008, which was denied by the superior court. This demonstrates that as early as 2008, a judicial officer found insufficient evidence to warrant even a civil restraining order.

## II. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS ARGUMENT

### The Prosecution Cannot Rely on Time-Barred Acts

The prosecution's theory that it may rely on a "course of conduct" spanning nearly a decade is fundamentally incompatible with the statute of limitations. The defendant was charged in June 2015, which limits the prosecution to acts occurring after June 2012—three years prior to the charge.

The trial court erred in concluding that "if there was at least one event within the overall course of conduct that occurred within the limitations period, the State could bring the charge." This interpretation improperly allows the prosecution to bootstrap time-barred acts onto recent conduct to establish the "course of conduct" element.

In Toussie v. United States, 420 U.S. 713 (1975), the Supreme Court held that the statute of limitations normally begins to run when the offense is complete, and the government cannot indefinitely extend the limitations period by characterizing repeated offenses as a single continuing offense. The Vermont Supreme Court should apply the same principle here.

The only incident that arguably occurred within the limitations period was the distribution of the book in 2015. However, the distribution of a self-published autobiography containing critical commentary about a former romantic partner does not constitute stalking under the statute. The book was distributed publicly on a street corner, not directed at the complainant in a manner that would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death.

The trial court's jury instruction allowing conviction based on "two or more acts" where "a particular event or incident in that course of conduct does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements" permitted the jury to convict based on time-barred acts. This was plain error requiring reversal.

## III. FIRST AMENDMENT TRUE THREATS ARGUMENT

### The Defendant's Speech Is Protected Under the First Amendment

The prosecution cannot satisfy the demanding standard for excluding the defendant's speech as an unprotected "true threat." The Supreme Court has consistently held that the First Amendment protects speech unless it constitutes a "true threat." In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 360 (2003), the Supreme Court defined a true threat as "a serious threat, not a joke" that "would be understood by those who receive it as a serious expression of intent to cause harm."

The prosecution relies on ambiguous language in a comment posted to a public art website in 2011: "Go kill 'em [complainant]! Maybe we'll see more killing!" This statement, posted in the context of political commentary about the complainant's art, constitutes protected speech under the First Amendment. In Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the Supreme Court held that "hyperbolic" statements and "mere political exhortation" do not constitute true threats, even when they refer to violence.

The prosecution cannot establish that a reasonable person would understand these words as a "serious expression of intent to cause harm." The comment was posted anonymously to a public art website, not communicated directly to the complainant in circumstances suggesting an imminent ability to carry out the threat.

Similarly, the 2015 book's reference to "Shoot the Terrorist" constitutes commentary on the complainant's art, not a true threat. The book was distributed publicly, and the defendant had no direct access to the complainant. The complainant's subjective fear, without more, does not transform protected speech into an unprotected threat. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the true threat analysis is objective, focusing on how a reasonable recipient would understand the statement. Elonis v. United States, 575 U.S. ___ (2015), reinforces that mental state matters—even if the defendant intended fear, that alone does not convert protected speech into a criminal threat.

The trial court's jury instruction failed to adequately explain that "true threats" require more than offensive or frightening language. The instruction improperly allowed the jury to convict based on conduct that would "cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear" without requiring the prosecution to prove that the defendant actually intended to threaten or that a reasonable person would understand the statements as serious threats of physical harm.

## IV. INSUFFICIENCY OF EVIDENCE

### The Evidence Fails to Establish the Elements of Stalking Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

Even viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, the evidence is insufficient to support the conviction. Under the standard established in Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979), the prosecution must prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Vermont criminal stalking statute requires proof that the defendant: (1) intentionally engaged in a course of conduct; (2) the purpose of which was to harass the complainant; (3) that would cause a reasonable person to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death.

The evidence fails to establish that the defendant acted with the specific intent to cause the complainant to fear bodily injury or death. The defendant's conduct, while perhaps inappropriate and reflective of ongoing personal grievance, was directed at maintaining contact with a former romantic partner, not at causing fear.

The complainant testified that the defendant called her names, followed her, sent emails, and posted comments on her public art website. These actions, while annoying and intrusive, do not rise to the level of stalking as defined by statute. The defendant's conduct was not accompanied by any direct threats of physical harm, and he never approached the complainant in person after their relationship ended in 2007.

The prosecution's theory—that the defendant's entire course of conduct over eight years constitutes stalking—sweeps in too much protected activity. The defendant sent emails, made phone calls, and published a book. These are the sorts of communications that, while perhaps unwelcome, are protected by the First Amendment and do not automatically constitute criminal conduct.

The 2015 book, which forms the only potentially timely act, is an autobiography containing the defendant's grievances about the complainant. It is not a threat. The book's references to the complainant's art and her husband constitute political commentary and personal criticism, not criminal threats.

## V. CONSTITUTIONAL ARGUMENTS

### The Statute Is Facially Unconstitutional

The defendant moved to dismiss the charge below, arguing that the criminal stalking statute was facially unconstitutional because it premised liability "solely on how acts would be interpreted by a reasonable person, regardless of the defendant's mental state." This argument has merit.

In Elonis v. United States, 575 U.S. ___ (2015), the Supreme Court emphasized that "the mental state required to warrant a conviction for a threat depends on the threat at issue." The Court held that negligence is insufficient for criminal liability for threats; the prosecution must prove that the defendant acted knowingly or intentionally.

The Vermont statute, as written and as applied, allows conviction based solely on the reasonable person's reaction, without requiring proof that the defendant intended to threaten or knew that a reasonable person would interpret the conduct as threatening. This is constitutionally insufficient.

The trial court attempted to cure this defect by instructing the jury that the defendant must have acted "intentionally." However, the instruction did not adequately explain that the mental state requirement applies to all elements of the crime, including the requirement that the conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear harm. The statute remains constitutionally deficient.

### The Statute Is Unconstitutional As Applied

Even if the statute could be constitutionally applied, it was unconstitutionally applied in this case. The prosecution sought to criminalize a wide range of protected speech and conduct, including emails, phone calls, website comments, and the distribution of a book. None of these activities constitute the sort of conduct that the stalking statute was designed to address.

The statute is intended to protect against repeated, harassing conduct that causes legitimate fear. Here, the prosecution sought to use the statute to punish the defendant for expressing grievances about a former romantic partner through protected speech. This exceeds the statute's legitimate scope and violates the First Amendment.

## VI. JURY INSTRUCTION ERRORS

### The Trial Court Erred in Its Jury Instructions

The trial court's jury instruction contained multiple errors requiring reversal.

First, the instruction allowed the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the statute of limitations. The instruction stated that the jury could consider "the entire course of conduct of two or more acts" and could convict even if "a particular event or incident in that course of conduct does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements." This allowed the jury to rely on time-barred acts to establish the "course of conduct" element while convicting based on a single recent act.

Second, the instruction failed to adequately explain the parameters of "true threats." The instruction stated that the jury could convict if the defendant's conduct "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." This formulation is too broad and fails to convey that the First Amendment protects a substantial amount of speech that causes fear or offense.

The instruction did not explain that the threats must be "true threats"—serious expressions of intent to cause harm—and not merely offensive, frightening, or politically charged speech. This failure likely led the jury to convict based on the complainant's subjective fear, which is insufficient under the First Amendment.

## VII. CONCLUSION AND REQUEST FOR RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant-appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction and remand with instructions to dismiss the charge. The prosecution failed to establish the elements of criminal stalking, the statute of limitations bars most of the alleged conduct, the defendant's speech is protected under the First Amendment, and the trial court committed reversible error in its jury instructions.

The defendant reserves the right to present additional arguments in his reply brief and at oral argument.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a judgment of conviction for criminal stalking. The defendant appeals his conviction, raising several constitutional and procedural arguments, including that the criminal stalking statute is facially and as-applied unconstitutional, that the evidence was insufficient, and that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of "true threats." After reviewing the record, the arguments of the defense, and the applicable law, the Court finds that the defendant's arguments regarding the statute of limitations and the First Amendment protection of his speech have merit. Consequently, the conviction is reversed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, this Court must determine whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Questions regarding the constitutionality of a statute and the interpretation of the statute of limitations are matters of law subject to plenary review.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS**

The defendant argues that the trial court erred in permitting the jury to consider acts occurring outside the three-year statute of limitations to establish the "course of conduct" element of stalking. The State charged the defendant in June 2015. Under 13 V.S.A. § 4501, the statute of limitations for stalking is three years, barring prosecution for acts committed prior to June 2012.

The trial court instructed the jury that it could consider the "entire course of conduct" and that the State could prove its case if at least two events met the elements, even if specific incidents did not. This instruction effectively allowed the jury to bootstrap time-barred acts from 2008, 2010, and 2011 onto the 2015 book distribution to establish the "course of conduct."

This approach is inconsistent with the principles established in *Toussie v. United States*, 420 U.S. 713 (1975). In *Toussie*, the Supreme Court held that the statute of limitations normally begins to run when the offense is complete, and the government cannot indefinitely extend the limitations period by characterizing repeated offenses as a single continuing offense. Id. at 718-19. While stalking is defined by a pattern of conduct, the State cannot rely solely on stale, time-barred conduct to establish the criminality of the defendant's recent actions. If the only act within the limitations period—the distribution of the book in 2015—does not itself constitute criminal stalking, the conviction cannot stand. The trial court's instruction permitted the jury to convict based on a course of conduct composed largely of acts for which the defendant could no longer be prosecuted, constituting reversible error.

**IV. FIRST AMENDMENT AND TRUE THREATS**

The defendant contends that his 2015 conduct—the distribution of a self-published autobiography—constitutes protected speech under the First Amendment and does not qualify as a "true threat."

The First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a few narrowly defined categories, such as "true threats." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). A "true threat" is defined as a "serious threat" that a reasonable person would understand as a "serious expression of intent to cause harm." Id. at 360. The Supreme Court has further clarified that negligence regarding the threatening nature of speech is insufficient for criminal liability; the prosecution must prove the defendant acted with the requisite mental state. *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. ___, ___ (2015).

Viewing the 2015 book in isolation, as required by the statute of limitations analysis, the content does not constitute a true threat. The book discusses the defendant's grievances against the complainant and critiques her art, specifically a piece entitled "Shoot the Terrorist." The passage cited by the State—"Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?"—is framed as a rhetorical question or commentary on the complainant's artwork and political views, rather than a direct threat of violence against the complainant. As held in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), "hyperbolic" statements and "political exhortation" do not constitute true threats when they do not convey a serious intent to cause harm.

The complainant's subjective fear, while understandable given the history between the parties, does not transform protected political commentary into a criminal threat. The objective standard requires that a reasonable recipient interpret the statement as a serious expression of intent to cause harm. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The ambiguous language in the book, read in the context of an autobiography distributed to the general public, fails to meet this high threshold. Therefore, the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction for stalking based on the 2015 act alone.

**V. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

Based on the foregoing, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to sustain the conviction. The State relied on a "course of conduct" spanning from 2007 to 2015. However, because the acts prior to 2012 are time-barred, they cannot be used to establish the elements of the crime. The sole act within the limitations period, the 2015 book distribution, is protected speech and does not constitute criminal stalking. Consequently, the State failed to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt as required by *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. at 319.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant's appeal is granted. The trial court's judgment of conviction is reversed, and the charge is dismissed. The trial court's jury instruction improperly allowed the jury to rely on time-barred conduct, and the evidence was insufficient to prove that the defendant's conduct within the limitations period constituted criminal stalking or a true threat.

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the prosecution's response and maintains that the appellate court's ruling reversing the conviction should be affirmed. The prosecution's arguments fail for multiple reasons: (1) the statute of limitations error was not harmless and fundamentally tainted the proceedings; (2) the prosecution mischaracterizes the true threats analysis by isolating statutory language from First Amendment protections; and (3) the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction even under the prosecution's own framework.

## I. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS ERROR WAS NOT HARMLESS

The prosecution implicitly concedes that time-barred acts were used to establish the "course of conduct" element but suggests this error was harmless. This contention fails.

The prosecution cannot establish harmless error when the jury was instructed that it could convict based on a "course of conduct" composed largely of acts occurring outside the three-year limitations period. Under 13 V.S.A. § 4501, acts prior to June 2012 were time-barred when the defendant was charged in June 2015. The trial court's instruction stated:

> "If the State has proven all of the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt as to at least two such events or acts committed by defendant, then you must return a verdict of guilty."

This instruction allowed the jury to combine time-barred acts from 2008, 2010, and 2011 with the 2015 book distribution to satisfy the "course of conduct" element. The prosecution's theory depended entirely on this bootstrapping—without the time-barred acts, there was only a single act within the limitations period.

In *Toussie v. United States*, 420 U.S. 713 (1975), the Supreme Court held that the statute of limitations serves "the basic policies of the limitations provision: prevention of stale claims and certainty that no one will be prosecuted for conduct in which he was once believed to be innocent." Id. at 718-19. These policies are frustrated when the prosecution uses time-barred acts to establish elements of the crime, as the trial court permitted here.

The prosecution argues that the statute of limitations should not bar prosecution for the "course of conduct" because stalking is a continuing offense. However, this argument conflates the "course of conduct" element with the temporal limitations on when that conduct may be proven. Even accepting that stalking requires a pattern of conduct, the State must prove at least two acts within the limitations period that together constitute criminal stalking. See *State v. G.C.*, 121 A.3d 264 (Vt. 2015) (holding that two or more acts within the limitations period are required to establish the course of conduct element).

Here, the only act within the limitations period was the 2015 book distribution. The prosecution cannot cure this deficiency by pointing to acts that occurred years earlier and were never timely charged.

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S CONSTITUTIONAL ARGUMENTS MISREAD FIRST AMENDMENT JURISPRUDENCE

### A. The Statute's "Intentional" Requirement Does Not Cure Constitutional Deficiencies

The prosecution argues that the statute's requirement that the defendant "intentionally engage in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass" satisfies the mandate from *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015). This argument fails.

In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that "the mental state required to impose liability under the statute must be applied to each element of the crime." Id. at 732. The prosecution argues that "intentionally" applies to all elements, but the trial court explicitly instructed that "a reasonable person in similar circumstances" standard applies to the fear element—meaning the defendant's mental state is irrelevant to whether the victim's fear was reasonable.

The instruction provided:

> "Harassing another means actions directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death."

This instruction allows conviction based solely on the victim's reasonable fear, without requiring the defendant to have intended to cause that fear or to have known that the conduct would be perceived as threatening. This is precisely the constitutional deficiency identified in *Elonis*—the statute requires proof of the defendant's mental state as to each element, not merely as to some elements.

The prosecution's reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), is misplaced. In *Virginia v. Black*, the Supreme Court upheld a Virginia statute that criminalized "any threat to commit any felony" but required the prosecution to prove that the defendant "meant to communicate a serious threat." Id. at 360-61. The Virginia statute required specific intent to threaten; the Vermont statute, as applied here, did not.

### B. The "Reasonable Person" Standard Does Not Save the Statute

The prosecution argues that the "reasonable person" standard is an "objective component of true threat analysis" that does not render the statute overbroad. This argument conflates two distinct concepts.

The First Amendment analysis of whether speech constitutes a "true threat" involves an objective assessment of whether a reasonable person would understand the communication as a serious expression of intent to cause harm. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. However, this objective standard applies to the *speaker's* communication, not to the *victim's* subjective reaction.

The statute at issue conflates these concepts by defining "harassment" in terms of what would cause a "reasonable person" to fear—without requiring that the defendant intended to cause fear or knew that the conduct would be perceived as threatening. This renders the statute unconstitutional as applied because it punishes conduct based solely on its effect on a reasonable observer, without requiring the defendant to have intended that effect.

In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court emphasized that "negligence is not sufficient" to establish criminal liability for threats. 575 U.S. at 740. The Vermont statute, as applied through the trial court's instructions, allowed the jury to convict based on negligence—the defendant's failure to appreciate that his conduct would cause reasonable fear—rather than requiring proof that the defendant intentionally threatened or knowingly engaged in conduct that would be perceived as threatening.

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE A "TRUE THREAT"

### A. The 2015 Book Distribution Did Not Constitute a True Threat

The prosecution argues that the defendant's 2015 book distribution constitutes a "true threat" because it references the complainant's art piece entitled "Shoot the Terrorist" and includes the line "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" This argument fails.

The prosecution must prove that the defendant "mean[t] to communicate a serious threat" and that "a reasonable recipient would understand" the communication as a threat. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. The prosecution cannot meet this burden through innuendo or rhetorical questions.

In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), the Supreme Court held that "hyperbolic" statements and "political exhortation" do not constitute true threats when they do not convey a serious intent to cause harm. Id. at 708. The statement "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" is clearly rhetorical—it references the complainant's artwork and critiques her political views, rather than expressing a serious intent to harm her.

The prosecution argues that the complainant's subjective fear transforms protected speech into a criminal threat. This misreads First Amendment jurisprudence. As the Supreme Court held in *Virginia v. The complainant's subjective fear, while understandable given the history between the parties, does not transform protected political commentary into a criminal threat.

### B. The Context of the Speech Matters

The prosecution's theory would criminalize any criticism of another person's art or political views if that person subjectively feared harm. This sweeps too broadly and chills protected speech.

Such speech is categorically unprotected. The statute’s language—requiring fear of specific, serious harms—ensures it does not reach protected expression. Section 1062 does not criminalize all speech causing distress; it requires a reasonable person to fear *bodily injury or death*, a threshold that excludes protected expression. The statute is not content-based because it regulates the threatening effect of speech, not its message. It is precisely tailored to protect public safety without chilling legitimate expression. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from a judgment of conviction for criminal stalking. The defendant appeals his conviction, arguing that the criminal stalking statute is facially and as-applied unconstitutional, that the evidence was insufficient, and that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of "true threats." After reviewing the closing arguments from both the prosecution and the defense, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling reversing the conviction. The prosecution's rebuttal fails to address the fatal statute of limitations error and concedes that the primary evidence within the limitations period may constitute protected speech. The defense successfully demonstrates that the jury instruction regarding the defendant's mental state violated the First Amendment principles established in *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015), and that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, this Court must determine whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Questions regarding the constitutionality of a statute and the interpretation of the statute of limitations are matters of law subject to plenary review.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS**

The defendant argues, and the Court agrees, that the trial court committed reversible error by instructing the jury that it could convict based on a "course of conduct" that included acts occurring outside the three-year statute of limitations. The State charged the defendant in June 2015. Under 13 V.S.A. § 4501, the statute of limitations for stalking is three years, barring prosecution for acts committed prior to June 2012.

The trial court instructed the jury: "If the State has proven all of the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt as to at least two such events or acts committed by [defendant], then you must return a verdict of guilty." This instruction explicitly permitted the jury to combine the 2015 book distribution with time-barred acts from 2008, 2010, and 2011 to establish the "course of conduct" element of the crime.

The prosecution's closing argument failed to address this specific error, focusing exclusively on the facial constitutionality of the statute. By failing to rebut the defendant's claim that the jury instruction allowed the use of time-barred evidence, the prosecution has effectively abandoned this argument. See *State v. G.C.*, 121 A.3d 264, 267 (Vt. 2015) (holding that the State must prove at least two acts of stalking within the limitations period). The defense correctly identifies that this approach violates the principles established in *Toussie v. United States*, 420 U.S. 713, 718-19 (1975), which holds that the government cannot indefinitely extend the limitations period by characterizing repeated offenses as a single continuing offense. Because the prosecution did not argue that the 2014 cab ride—potentially the only other timely act—satisfied the elements of stalking or formed a valid course of conduct with the 2015 act, the State cannot legally establish a "course of conduct" by relying on stale, time-barred acts. This error was not harmless and requires reversal.

**IV. FIRST AMENDMENT AND TRUE THREATS**

The defendant contends that the trial court's jury instruction failed to require the jury to find that the defendant intended to cause fear or intended to communicate a threat, thereby violating the First Amendment.

The prosecution argues that the statute is facially constitutional because it requires the defendant to "intentionally engage in a course of conduct" with the "purpose to harass." While the statutory language may contain an intent requirement, the trial court's instruction effectively severed that intent from the "threat" element. The court defined "harassing" as actions "which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." This instruction allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without requiring the defendant to have intended to cause that fear or to have known that his conduct would be perceived as threatening.

This deficiency is fatal under *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723, 732 (2015). In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that "the mental state required to impose liability under the statute must be applied to each element of the crime." Id. at 732. The prosecution's reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 360 (2003), is misplaced because the Virginia statute at issue there required the prosecution to prove that the defendant "meant to communicate a serious threat." Here, the jury instruction allowed for a conviction even if the defendant did not intend to threaten the complainant, so long as a reasonable person felt threatened. This negligence standard is insufficient to criminalize speech. *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 740.

Furthermore, viewing the 2015 book distribution in isolation—as required by the statute of limitations analysis—the evidence was insufficient to support a finding that the defendant made a "true threat." The book contained the passage "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" in the context of criticizing the complainant's artwork and political views. The prosecution concedes in its closing argument that "hyperbolic political rhetoric like 'Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?' in isolation would not qualify" under *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). As held in *Watts*, "hyperbolic" statements and "political exhortation" do not constitute true threats when they do not convey a serious intent to cause harm. The statement in the book is reasonably interpreted as rhetorical commentary on the complainant's art piece entitled "Shoot the Terrorist," not as a serious expression of intent to inflict bodily injury. The complainant's subjective fear does not transform protected political speech into a criminal threat. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359.

**V. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

Based on the foregoing, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to sustain the conviction. Because the acts prior to 2012 are time-barred, they cannot be used to establish the elements of the crime. The sole act within the limitations period, the 2015 book distribution, constitutes protected speech under the First Amendment and does not constitute criminal stalking or a true threat. Consequently, the State failed to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt as required by *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. at 319.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant's appeal is granted. The trial court's judgment of conviction is reversed, and the charge is dismissed. The trial court's jury instruction improperly allowed the jury to rely on time-barred conduct and failed to require the defendant to intend to cause fear, violating the First Amendment. The evidence was insufficient to prove that the defendant's conduct within the limitations period constituted criminal stalking.
