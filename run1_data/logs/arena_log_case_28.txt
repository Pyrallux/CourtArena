Your Honor, the State submits that 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as it existed in June 2015, was facially constitutional. A facial challenge to a statute is an "especially difficult" burden requiring the challenger to demonstrate "that no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745, 107 S. Ct. 2095, 95 L. Ed. 2d 697 (1987). The statute at issue here—defining stalking as "intentionally engaging in a course of conduct directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear for his or her safety or the safety of a member of his or her immediate family"—does not criminalize protected speech. It targets only unprotected "true threats," which the First Amendment has never shielded.  

The defendant’s central misconception—that the statute criminalized speech causing "substantial emotional distress"—is legally inaccurate. The statute explicitly requires a reasonable person to fear "for his or her safety or the safety of a member of his or her immediate family," meaning fear of physical harm, bodily injury, or death. This is not mere emotional distress; it is a well-established category of unprotected speech. As the Supreme Court clarified in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-360, 123 S. Ct. 1536, 155 L. Ed. 2d 535 (2003), "true threats" are those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group. Such threats "are not protected by the First Amendment" because they "place the victim in fear of bodily harm or death." The statute’s requirement that speech cause fear of physical harm—rather than general emotional distress—ensures it sweeps only within the realm of unprotected speech.  

The defendant’s argument that the statute is "overly broad" fails because it misstates the law. A statute is not facially invalid merely because it could be applied unconstitutionally in hypothetical scenarios; the challenger must show it is unconstitutional in *all* applications. *Salerno*, 481 U.S. at 745. Here, the statute’s text limits liability to conduct causing reasonable fear of physical harm—a category universally recognized as unprotected. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L. Ed. 2d 664 (1969), the Court held that political hyperbole, even if threatening, is protected unless it is "a true threat." The statute’s objective standard—whether a reasonable person would fear physical harm—mirrors this framework. It does not criminalize protected expression because it requires the speaker’s conduct to objectively instill fear of violence, not mere offense or emotional discomfort. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709, 717, 132 S. Ct. 2537, 183 L. Ed. 2d 574 (2012), "the Government may restrict speech that is not protected by the First Amendment," including true threats.  

Nor is the statute impermissibly content-based. A law is content-based only if it "regulates speech based on its message, ideas, subject matter, or content." *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155, 163, 135 S. Ct. 2218, 192 L. Ed. 2d 236 (2015). Here, the statute regulates conduct based on its *effect*—whether it causes reasonable fear of physical harm—not the content of the speech itself. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld such statutes as content-neutral. In *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 388, 112 S. Ct. 2538, 120 L. Ed. 2d 305 (1992), the Court explained that laws targeting "the threatening nature of speech" are not content-based because they "do not target the content of the speech" but rather the "act of threatening." The statute here focuses solely on whether speech causes fear of violence—regardless of the speaker’s viewpoint, ideology, or subject matter.  

Finally, the defendant’s reliance on *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 192 L. Ed. 2d 1 (2015), is misplaced. *Elonis* held that recklessness is insufficient for a federal true-threats statute, but it explicitly preserved the requirement that the government prove the defendant "intentionally" engaged in threatening conduct. The Vermont statute explicitly requires "intentionally engaging in a course of conduct," satisfying *Elonis*’s mens rea standard. The trial court correctly interpreted the statute to require intentional conduct toward the victim, aligning with *Elonis*’s core holding.  

In sum, 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is constitutional on its face because it targets only unprotected true threats—speech that causes reasonable fear of physical harm—and applies a content-neutral standard rooted in longstanding First Amendment precedent. It does not criminalize protected expression, nor does it impermissibly discriminate based on content. The facial challenge must fail.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

The defendant-appellant respectfully submits this opening response to the Court, addressing the prosecution's facial constitutional challenge to 13 V.S.A. § 1062 and the multiple grounds for appeal raised below. The prosecution's argument mischaracterizes both the statute at issue and the applicable constitutional framework. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the conviction and remand with instructions to dismiss the charge.

## I. The Statute Is Facially Unconstitutional

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Does Not Satisfy Elonis Requirements

The prosecution contends that the statute is constitutional because it requires "intentional" conduct and targets only "true threats." This argument fails because the statute's text, as written in 2015, did not require the prosecution to prove that the defendant intended his communications to be threatening.

In *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 340, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 192 L.Ed.2d 1 (2015), the United States Supreme Court held that "the mental state required to be guilty of the crime of posting threats" must be proven by the government, and that "negligence is not enough." The Court explicitly rejected the argument that a "reasonable person" standard could substitute for a intent requirement in true threats statutes. The prosecution in *Elonis* had argued that the defendant's mental state was irrelevant so long as a reasonable person would have perceived the communication as a threat.

The Vermont statute defined criminal stalking as "intentionally engaging in a course of conduct directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear for his or her safety." 13 V.S.A. § 1062(a)(1). The critical flaw in this statutory scheme is that the word "intentionally" modified only the "engaging in a course of conduct" element—not the element requiring that the conduct "would cause a reasonable person...to fear." This meant that the statute, as written, permitted conviction based on a negligence standard: the defendant intentionally engaged in conduct (e.g., sent emails, followed the complainant), and a reasonable person would fear for safety, but the prosecution was not required to prove that the defendant intended to threaten or that he knew his communications would be perceived as threatening.

This Court should hold that the statute is facially unconstitutional because it "premise[d] liability solely on how acts would be interpreted by a reasonable person, regardless of the defendant's mental state," which is precisely what *Elonis* prohibited. The prosecution's argument that the trial court "read" an intent requirement into the statute does not save the statute from facial invalidity. A court cannot rewrite a statute to save it from constitutional deficiency; rather, the statute must be struck as written.

### B. The Statute Is Overbroad

Even if this Court were to accept the prosecution's argument that the statute can be narrowed through judicial construction, the statute remains unconstitutionally overbroad. A statute is overbroad if "it sweeps within its prohibitions activities protected by the First Amendment." *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 612, 93 S. Ct. 2908, 37 L.Ed.2d 830 (1973). The statute at issue here criminalizes not only "true threats" but also protected speech that causes a reasonable person to fear for their safety. The prosecution conflates "true threats" with any speech that might cause fear. However, the Supreme Court has consistently held that "true threats" require the speaker to have meant to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359, 123 S. Ct. 1536, 155 L.Ed.2d 535 (2003).

The Vermont statute did not require proof that the defendant meant to communicate a threat. A defendant could be convicted for sending an email that the defendant did not intend as a threat but that a reasonable person might have interpreted as threatening. This captures protected speech. For example, the defendant in this case sent emails criticizing the complainant's art and posted comments on her website. While these communications were offensive and distressing to the complainant, they did not constitute "true threats" as defined by First Amendment jurisprudence because there was no evidence the defendant intended to communicate a serious threat of violence. The statute's overbreadth is demonstrated by the prosecution's ability to charge the defendant for these communications.

### C. The Statute Is Impermissibly Content-Based

The prosecution argues that the statute is content-neutral because it regulates conduct based on its effect rather than the content of speech. This argument fails under *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155, 135 S. Ct. 2218, 192 L.Ed.2d 236 (2015), which held that a law is content-based if it "regulates speech based on its message, ideas, subject matter, or content." The Vermont statute explicitly targets speech—specifically, speech that would cause a reasonable person to fear for safety. The determination of whether conduct constitutes stalking under the statute depends entirely on the content of the defendant's communications: whether they would cause a reasonable person to fear. This is a content-based regulation of speech.

Furthermore, the statute discriminates based on viewpoint. A statement expressing political criticism that causes a reasonable person to fear for safety is criminalized, while a statement expressing the same political criticism that does not cause such fear is protected. The statute thus grants the government power to suppress speech based on the government's determination of whether the speech is threatening—a determination that will inevitably reflect the government's view of which viewpoints are acceptable.

## II. The Statute of Limitations Violation

The prosecution introduced evidence of conduct spanning from 2006 to 2015. The defendant was charged in June 2015, meaning the statute of limitations barred evidence of conduct occurring before June 2012. The trial court held that "if there was at least one event within the overall course of conduct that occurred within the limitations period, the State could bring the charge" and concluded that the defendant's 2015 dissemination of his book met this requirement.

This holding was erroneous. The statute of limitations for criminal stalking begins running when the offense is complete. Each act of stalking is a separate offense. *See State v. McLaren*, 158 Vt. 194, 199, 608 A.2d 711, 714 (1992) (holding that each violation of a restraining order constitutes a separate offense). The prosecution cannot rely on acts outside the limitations period to establish the elements of the crime and then rely on a single act within the period to invoke jurisdiction.

The trial court's jury instruction compounded this error by allowing the jury to convict based on "two or more acts" even if some of those acts occurred outside the limitations period. The instruction stated: "it is the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by [defendant] which you must consider as meeting all of the requirements and stated elements of the crime, even if a particular event or incident in that course of conduct does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements." This instruction allowed the jury to convict based on time-barred acts, in violation of the defendant's constitutional right to be free from prosecution for acts outside the statute of limitations.

## III. The True Threats Jury Instruction Was Deficient

The trial court instructed the jury that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person." This instruction failed to adequately explain the parameters of "true threats" under the First Amendment.

Under *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359-60, and *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L.Ed.2d 664 (1969), the First Amendment protects political hyperbole, jest, and idle talk that would not be understood by a reasonable person as a serious expression of intent to commit violence. The trial court's instruction did not explain this distinction. By instructing the jury that it could convict based on conduct that "would cause" a reasonable person to fear, without requiring proof that the defendant intended to communicate a threat, the trial court allowed the jury to convict the defendant for protected speech.

The prosecution argued in its opening that the statute targets only "true threats." But the jury instruction did not require the jury to find that the defendant's communications constituted true threats. Instead, the instruction allowed conviction based on a reasonable person's fear, regardless of the defendant's intent. This was constitutional error.

## IV. Insufficient Evidence

Even if the statute were constitutional and the jury instruction correct, the evidence was insufficient to sustain the conviction. The prosecution was required to prove that the defendant "intentionally" engaged in conduct "the purpose of which was to harass" the complainant. The evidence showed that the defendant sent emails criticizing the complainant's art, posted comments on her website, distributed a book about their relationship, and followed her on one occasion in 2010.

None of this evidence established that the defendant acted with the purpose of causing the complainant to fear for her safety. The emails and website comments expressed criticism of the complainant's art and personal life. The book was a autobiographical account of the defendant's experiences. The following in 2010 occurred in a public parking lot. This evidence, at most, established that the defendant's communications were offensive to the complainant and that she felt frightened. It did not establish that the defendant intended to threaten her or that his purpose was to cause her to fear for her safety.

The prosecution relied heavily on the complainant's subjective fear. But the statute requires that a "reasonable person" would fear for safety, not that the particular complainant did fear. The evidence showed that the complainant was frightened, but a reasonable person in her circumstances—receiving critical emails about art and a book about a past relationship—would not fear for their physical safety. The defendant's communications did not contain explicit threats of violence. The comment stating "Go kill 'em [complainant]!" was ambiguous and could reasonably be understood as hyperbole rather than a serious threat. Without more, this evidence was insufficient to prove that the defendant committed criminal stalking.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction, hold that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional, and remand with instructions to dismiss the charge. In the alternative, the defendant requests that the Court reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial on the grounds that the jury instruction allowed conviction based on time-barred acts and failed to adequately explain true threats, and that the evidence was insufficient.

Respectfully submitted,

**STATE OF VERMONT v. DEFENDANT**

**Case No.** [REDACTED]

**Pursuant to** the review of the prosecution and defense opening arguments, and the facts provided in the case background, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 13 V.S.A. § 1062**

The Defendant argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it premises liability on how acts would be interpreted by a reasonable person, regardless of the defendant's mental state, purportedly violating *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 340, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 192 L.Ed.2d 1 (2015). The Court is not persuaded by this argument.

A facial challenge to a statute is an "especially difficult" burden, requiring the challenger to demonstrate that "no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745, 107 S. Ct. 2095, 95 L. Ed. 2d 697 (1987). The Defendant has failed to meet this high burden. The statute explicitly requires that a defendant "intentionally engag[e] in a course of conduct." While the Defendant argues that the term "intentionally" modifies only the act of engaging in conduct and not the result (causing fear), the statute can be constitutionally applied to a defendant who intentionally engages in a pattern of conduct with the purpose of causing another to fear for their safety. Such an application targets unprotected "true threats"—statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-360, 123 S. Ct. 1536, 155 L. Ed. 2d 535 (2003).

The Defendant's reliance on *Elonis* is misplaced in the context of a facial challenge. *Elonis* held that negligence is insufficient to convict for making true threats under a specific federal statute, but it did not hold that statutes requiring intent to engage in a course of conduct are facially invalid. *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 344. Because the Vermont statute can be validly applied to conduct involving intentional stalking and true threats, it is not facially unconstitutional. The trial court's construction of the statute to require intent for all elements further ensures its constitutionality in this application.

**II. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND JURY INSTRUCTION**

The Defendant contends that the trial court erred by allowing the jury to consider acts occurring outside the three-year statute of limitations and that the jury instruction improperly permitted conviction based on time-barred acts. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive.

Criminal stalking is defined by statute as a "course of conduct," meaning a pattern of conduct consisting of two or more acts over a period of time, showing a continuity of purpose. See 13 V.S.A. § 1062(1). The statute of limitations for a "course of conduct" offense typically begins to run from the date of the last act comprising the offense. The State presented evidence that the Defendant disseminated a book in 2015, which falls within the limitations period. This act, combined with prior acts, constituted the alleged course of conduct.

The Defendant's reliance on *State v. McLaren*, 158 Vt. 194, 199, 608 A.2d 711, 714 (1992), is misplaced. *McLane* involved discrete violations of a court order, where each violation was a separate offense. Here, the crime is defined by the continuity of the conduct. The trial court correctly instructed the jury that they must consider the "entire course of conduct" to determine if the elements of the crime were met. This instruction was proper because the 2015 act brought the entire course of conduct within the purview of the trial. The jury was not instructed to convict based solely on time-barred acts, but rather to evaluate the pattern of conduct which included a timely act.

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE AND TRUE THREATS**

The Defendant argues that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction because the State failed to prove he intended to threaten the complainant, and that the jury instruction failed to adequately describe the parameters of true threats.

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, the record reveals a sustained pattern of conduct spanning several years. This includes the Defendant following the complainant, waiting outside her workplace, staring at her in a remote parking lot, and sending communications referencing "killing" and "shoot[ing] the artist." Specifically, the 2015 book contained the passage "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" in the context of describing the complainant's "adversity" and the Defendant's "breaking point."

The jury instruction required the State to prove that the Defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass" the complainant, and defined harassment as actions that would cause a reasonable person to fear "bodily injury, or death." This instruction adequately conveyed the *mens rea* requirements set forth in *Elonis*. By requiring the jury to find that the Defendant acted with the "purpose" to harass—defined as causing fear of death or bodily injury—the instruction ensured that the jury could only convict if they found the Defendant intended the threatening nature of his conduct. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L. Ed. 2d 664 (1969) (distinguishing true threats from political hyperbole).

The evidence, including the explicit references to violence in the book and the physical acts of surveillance and pursuit, was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find that the Defendant intentionally engaged in a course of conduct directed at the complainant with the purpose of causing her to fear for her safety.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant's arguments regarding the facial constitutionality of the statute, the statute of limitations, the jury instructions, and the sufficiency of the evidence are rejected. The trial court's rulings are affirmed, and the conviction stands.

## II. THE STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL AS APPLIED TO PROTECTED SPEECH

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Nature of the Defendant's Speech

The prosecution contends that the statute targets only "unprotected true threats" and that the Defendant's conduct falls within this category. This characterization is fundamentally flawed. The Defendant's book, while critical of the complainant, constitutes protected political speech and commentary on the complainant's artistic work. The prosecution's attempt to characterize the phrase "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" as a "true threat" ignores the context in which this statement appeared.

As this Court is aware, the determination of whether speech constitutes a true threat requires consideration of the context in which the statement was made. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L.Ed.2d 664 (1969). In *Watts*, the Supreme Court reversed a conviction for threatening the President, holding that the statement at issue was "political hyperbole" not a true threat. *Id.* at 708. The Court emphasized that "the mere absence of an intent to threaten is not sufficient to enable the Government to criminalize speech" and that the government must prove "that the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence." *Id.*

The Defendant's book was a self-published autobiography containing the Defendant's grievances about his relationship with the complainant. The passage referencing "Shoot the terrorist? ### B. The Statute's "Reasonable Person" Standard Impermissibly Dispenses with Mens Rea

The prosecution argues that the "reasonable person" standard is merely "a tool to determine whether speech qualifies as a true threat" and that the statute requires intent. However, this interpretation contradicts the holding of *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 340, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 192 L.Ed.2d 1 (2015). In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that "negligence is not sufficient to warrant liability under" a criminal threat statute. *Id.* at 353. The Court emphasized that "the mental state required to be proved in a criminal case depends on the crime" and that "the absence of a mental state requirement would . . . expand the scope of criminal liability." *Id.* at 352.

The Vermont statute, as written and as applied, permits conviction based on a "reasonable person" standard without requiring proof that the defendant intended to cause fear. The trial court's jury instruction attempted to cure this deficiency by requiring that the defendant acted "with the purpose of causing" fear, but this instruction conflicted with the statutory language that defines harassment as conduct that "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear." 13 V.S.A. § 1062(3). The statute's plain language permits conviction based on the effect of the conduct on a reasonable person, regardless of the defendant's mental state. This is precisely the type of liability that *Elonis* rejected.

The prosecution's reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 123 S. Ct. 1536, 155 L.Ed.2d 535 (2003), is misplaced. In *Virginia v. Black*, the Supreme Court upheld a Virginia statute that criminalized cross burning "with intent to intimidate," holding that such conduct constituted a true threat unprotected by the First Amendment. *Id.* at 360. The Virginia statute explicitly required "intent to intimidate," which satisfies the mens rea requirement recognized in *Elonis*. The Vermont statute, by contrast, defines stalking in terms of a "course of conduct" that would cause a reasonable person to fear, without requiring proof that the defendant intended to cause such fear. The statute's failure to require intent to threaten distinguishes it from the statute upheld in *Virginia v. Black* and renders it unconstitutional as applied to the Defendant's protected speech.

### C. The Statute Is Overbroad

The prosecution argues that the statute is not overbroad under *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 93 S. Ct. 2908, 37 L.Ed.2d 830 (1973), because it only prohibits unprotected true threats. This interpretation permits conviction for protected speech that causes fear in a reasonable person, even when the speaker had no intent to threaten. Such a statute is overbroad and unconstitutional.

## III. THE JURY INSTRUCTION ON THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFICIENT

### A. The Prosecution's Argument Misappplies the "Course of Conduct" Doctrine

The prosecution contends that the trial court correctly instructed the jury to consider the "entire course of conduct" because the 2015 act (dissemination of the book) brought the entire course within the limitations period. This argument misapplies the doctrine of continuing offenses and violates the defendant's constitutional right to be free from prosecution for acts outside the limitations period.

The doctrine of continuing offenses applies to offenses that are inherently continuous in nature, such as conspiracy or ongoing violations of a court order. *See State v. McLaren*, 158 Vt. 194, 199, 608 A.2d 711, 714 (1992). However, criminal stalking, while defined as a "course of conduct," is not a continuing offense in the same sense as conspiracy. Each act of stalking is a discrete event that can form the basis of a separate charge. The statute of limitations begins to run on each act when that act is committed. The prosecution cannot revive liability for acts that occurred outside the limitations period by combining them with a timely act.

The trial court's instruction allowed the jury to convict based on two acts, even if one of those acts occurred outside the limitations period, so long as another act occurred within the period. This instruction is constitutionally deficient because it permits conviction based on time-barred acts. The Defendant has a constitutional right to be prosecuted only for acts that occurred within the limitations period. *See United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 97 S. The prosecution argues that the instruction was proper because "the 2015 act brought the entire course of conduct within the purview of the trial." This argument is unpersuasive. This instruction permitted the jury to convict based on two acts, one of which could have occurred outside the limitations period. For example, if the jury found that the defendant committed the acts in 2008 and 2010 (both outside the limitations period) but not the act in 2015 (within the limitations period), the jury could still convict based on the time-barred acts. This instruction is constitutionally impermissible.

The correct instruction would have required the jury to find that at least two acts constituting the course of conduct occurred within the limitations period. The trial court failed to give this instruction, and the Defendant's objection to the instruction was properly preserved. This Court should reverse the conviction.

The evidence presented at trial established that the Defendant published a book containing political commentary about the complainant's art. There is no evidence that the Defendant published the book with the purpose of causing the complainant to fear for her safety.

The complainant's subjective fear, while relevant to the reasonable person analysis, does not establish that the Defendant acted with the purpose of causing fear. The Defendant may have been aware that his conduct might cause fear, but awareness is insufficient under *Elonis*. The prosecution must prove that the Defendant acted with the purpose of causing fear, and the evidence was insufficient to establish this element.

**UPDATED RULING**

**STATE OF VERMONT v. DEFENDANT**

**Case No.** [REDACTED]

**Pursuant to** the review of the rebuttals of opening arguments from both the prosecution and defense, and the facts provided in the case background, the Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 13 V.S.A. § 1062**

The Defendant renews his argument that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it permits liability based on a reasonable person standard without a sufficient *mens rea* requirement, purportedly violating *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 340, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 192 L.Ed.2d 1 (2015). The Court adheres to its determination that the statute is not facially invalid.

As established in *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745, 107 S. Ct. 2095, 95 L. Ed. 2d 697 (1987), a party challenging a statute on its face must demonstrate that "no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid." The Defendant has not met this heavy burden. The statute explicitly requires that a defendant "intentionally engag[e] in a course of conduct." The trial court further construed the statute to require that the defendant act with the "purpose" to harass, defined as causing fear of bodily injury or death. This construction aligns the statute with the requirements of *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-360, 123 S. Ct. 1536, 155 L. Ed. 2d 535 (2003), which held that prohibitions on "true threats"—statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence—are consistent with the First Amendment. Because the statute can be validly applied to conduct involving intentional stalking and true threats, it is not facially unconstitutional.

**II. CONSTITUTIONALITY AS APPLIED AND *MENS REA* REQUIREMENTS**

The Defendant argues that the statute is unconstitutional as applied to his conduct because it criminalizes protected political speech and lacks the requisite *mens rea* regarding the result of the conduct. Specifically, the Defendant contends that the publication of his book constituted political commentary protected by *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L. Ed. 2d 664 (1969), and that the statute's "reasonable person" standard permits conviction without proof of intent to cause fear.

The Court is not persuaded. The trial court's jury instruction required the State to prove that the Defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass" the complainant. By requiring the jury to find that the Defendant acted with the specific purpose of causing fear, the instruction cured any potential constitutional deficiency in the statute's plain language. *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 344 (holding that negligence is insufficient for criminal liability). The instruction ensured that the Defendant could only be convicted if the jury found he intended the threatening nature of his conduct.

While the Defendant characterizes the book as political hyperbole, the evidence presented at trial, including the book's references to the complainant's "breaking point" and the question "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?", combined with the Defendant's history of physical surveillance and pursuit, provided a sufficient basis for a reasonable jury to conclude that the Defendant acted with the purpose of harassing the complainant, rather than engaging in protected political discourse. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. Therefore, the statute was not unconstitutionally applied in this instance.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND JURY INSTRUCTION**

The Defendant contends that the trial court erred in its jury instruction regarding the statute of limitations by allowing the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the three-year limitations period. Upon further review of the Defendant's rebuttal, the Court finds this argument to be meritorious.

Criminal stalking is defined as a "course of conduct," meaning a pattern of conduct consisting of two or more acts over a period of time, showing a continuity of purpose. 13 V.S.A. § 1062(1). However, the prosecution of such an offense is subject to the statute of limitations, which bars prosecution for acts committed more than three years prior to the filing of the charge. 13 V.S.A. § 4501.

The trial court instructed the jury that they must consider the "entire course of conduct" and that "[i]f the State has proven all of the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt as to at least two such events or acts committed by [defendant], then you must return a verdict of guilty." This instruction was legally deficient. It permitted the jury to convict the Defendant based on the finding of two acts that occurred outside the limitations period, so long as those acts formed a "course of conduct." This interpretation violates the Defendant's constitutional right to be prosecuted only for acts within the limitations period. *See United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 789, 97 S. Ct. 2044, 52 L.Ed.2d 752 (1977) (noting the statute of limitations' primary function of protecting defendants from prejudice).

The State argues that the 2015 dissemination of the book brought the entire course of conduct within the purview of the trial. While the State may use timely acts to provide context for a course of conduct, the jury must be instructed that at least one act constituting the offense must have occurred within the limitations period. *See State v. McLaren*, 158 Vt. 194, 199, 608 A.2d 711, 714 (1992) (distinguishing between discrete offenses and continuing courses of conduct). The instruction here failed to require this finding. Because the instruction allowed for a conviction based solely on time-barred acts, it constitutes reversible error.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The Defendant argues that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction because the State failed to prove he intended to threaten the complainant. Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, the record reveals a sustained pattern of conduct spanning several years, including following the complainant, waiting outside her workplace, staring at her in a remote parking lot, and sending communications referencing "killing" and "shoot[ing] the artist." This evidence was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find that the Defendant intentionally engaged in a course of conduct directed at the complainant with the purpose of causing her to fear for her safety. However, due to the erroneous jury instruction on the statute of limitations, the conviction cannot stand.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant's arguments regarding the facial and as-applied constitutionality of the statute are rejected. However, the trial court's jury instruction on the statute of limitations was erroneous. The judgment of the trial court is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

This test does not expand liability to protected speech; it merely identifies when speech crosses the line into unprotected territory. As *Virginia v. The statute’s language thus falls squarely within the government’s authority to prohibit true threats, leaving no substantial overbreadth.  

Third, the defense’s reliance on *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L. **FINAL RULING**

**STATE OF VERMONT v. DEFENDANT**

**Case No.** [REDACTED]

**Pursuant to** the review of the closing arguments from the prosecution and defense, and the facts provided in the case background, the Court issues its final ruling as follows.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 13 V.S.A. § 1062**

The Defendant argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it lacks a sufficient *mens rea* requirement and criminalizes protected speech. The Court rejects this contention.

As the prosecution correctly notes, a party challenging a statute on its face must demonstrate that "no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745, 107 S. Ct. 2095, 95 L. Ed. 2d 697 (1987). The Defendant has not met this heavy burden. The statute explicitly requires that a defendant "intentionally engag[e] in a course of conduct" with the "purpose of harassing" another person. The trial court further construed the statute to require that the defendant act with the specific purpose of causing fear. This construction aligns the statute with the requirements of *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-360, 123 S. Ct. 1536, 155 L. Ed. 2d 535 (2003), which held that prohibitions on "true threats"—statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence—are consistent with the First Amendment. Because the statute can be validly applied to conduct involving intentional stalking and true threats, it is not facially unconstitutional.

**II. CONSTITUTIONALITY AS APPLIED AND TRUE THREATS**

The Defendant argues that the statute was unconstitutionally applied to his conduct, specifically regarding the dissemination of his book in 2015. He characterizes the book as protected political hyperbole under *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708, 89 S. Ct. 1399, 22 L. Ed. 2d 664 (1969).

The Court is not persuaded. While *Watts* protects crude or hyperbolic political speech, it does not shield conduct that constitutes a "true threat." The prosecution correctly distinguishes *Watts*, noting that the Defendant's book was not an isolated statement at a political rally, but part of a multi-year pattern of stalking and surveillance. The book's content, including references to the complainant's "breaking point" and the rhetorical question "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?", when viewed in the context of the Defendant's prior conduct—following the complainant, waiting outside her workplace, and staring at her in a remote parking lot—provided a sufficient basis for a reasonable jury to conclude that the Defendant acted with the purpose of harassing the complainant. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. The statute was not unconstitutionally applied in this instance.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND JURY INSTRUCTION**

The Defendant contends that the trial court's jury instruction regarding the statute of limitations constituted reversible error requiring dismissal of the charges. The Court agrees that the instruction was erroneous but disagrees that dismissal is the appropriate remedy.

Criminal stalking is defined as a "course of conduct," meaning a pattern of conduct consisting of two or more acts over a period of time, showing a continuity of purpose. 13 V.S.A. § 1062(1). The prosecution of such an offense is subject to the statute of limitations, which bars prosecution for acts committed more than three years prior to the filing of the charge. 13 V.S.A. § 4501. The Defendant was charged in June 2015; thus, only acts occurring on or after June 2012 are timely.

The trial court instructed the jury that they must consider the "entire course of conduct" and that "[i]f the State has proven all of the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt as to at least two such events or acts committed by [defendant], then you must return a verdict of guilty." This instruction was legally deficient because it permitted the jury to convict the Defendant based on the finding of two acts that occurred outside the limitations period (e.g., the 2007 or 2008 incidents), without requiring that at least one act constituting the offense occurred within the limitations period. This violates the Defendant's constitutional right to be prosecuted only for acts within the limitations period. *See United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 789, 97 S. Ct. 2044, 52 L. Ed. 2d 752 (1977).

However, the defense's request for dismissal is denied. The State presented evidence of a timely act—the dissemination of the book in 2015. The defense argues that the book, standing alone, does not constitute stalking. This is a question of fact for the jury, not a legal basis for dismissal at this stage. The preliminary ruling found that the evidence, including the 2015 book dissemination combined with the history of the Defendant's conduct, was sufficient to support a conviction. *See State v. McLaren*, 158 Vt. 194, 199, 608 A.2d 711, 714 (1992) (distinguishing between discrete offenses and continuing courses of conduct). On remand, the State may use the timely 2015 act as the anchor for the charge, with the older acts providing the necessary context to establish the "course of conduct," provided the jury is properly instructed that at least one act must have occurred within the limitations period.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant's arguments regarding the facial and as-applied constitutionality of the statute are rejected. The trial court's jury instruction on the statute of limitations was erroneous. The judgment of the trial court is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for a new trial with instructions consistent with this ruling.
