## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The District Court Correctly Classified the Life-Insurance and Death-Gratuity Benefits as Nonmarital Property

Under Minnesota law, "marital property" is defined as "property, real or personal, including present rights of survivorship, acquired by the parties before or during the marriage or at the dissolution." Minn. Stat. § 518.58, subd. 1. However, the Minnesota Legislature has expressly provided that "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" is nonmarital property. **In re Estate of Cournoyer v. Cournoyer**, 288 Minn. 345, 180 N.W.2d 455 (1970).

The defense argues that the district court correctly classified these benefits as Loretta Angelí's nonmarital property because she acquired them through statutory entitlements that function equivalently to a bequest or devise. When Levi Angelí died during active military service, the law entitled his designated survivor—his mother Loretta—to receive these death benefits. This is not property acquired through the efforts of the marriage or through marital labor, but rather represents a statutory substitute for inheritance that would have passed to Loretta upon Levi's death.

As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in **Sand v. Sand**, 302 Minn. 329, 225 N.W.2d 66 (1975), property acquired by a spouse through inheritance or bequest constitutes nonmarital property regardless of when received. The death benefits here are functionally indistinguishable from an inheritance—they represent assets that passed to Loretta Angelí by operation of law upon her son's death, intended to provide for her financial security in the wake of her son's sacrifice.

Gordon Angelí's argument that these benefits should be classified as marital property because Loretta did not acquire them "as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" misconstrues the nature of these statutory payments. The death-gratuity and SGLI benefits are statutory entitlements that occupy the same legal position as bequests or inheritances. They represent payments made to a designated beneficiary upon the death of a servicemember, intended to provide financial support to the survivor. This Court should reject any argument that would expand the definition of marital property to include statutory death benefits that are functionally equivalent to inheritances.

Furthermore, the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property is rebuttable. **Keller v. Keller**, 365 Minn. 410, 311 N.W.2d 904 (1981). Loretta Angelí has clearly rebutted this presumption by establishing that these funds: (1) were derived from statutory benefits payable upon her son's death; (2) were deposited into a separate account in her name alone; (3) were never commingled with marital property; and (4) remained separate at the time of dissolution. ### A. The Death Benefits Do Not Constitute "Inheritance" or "Bequest" Under Minnesota Law

The critical distinction lies in the nature of the acquisition. Traditional inheritance under Minnesota law derives from the decedent's estate through testate or intestate succession. See *In re Estate of Cournoyer*, 288 Minn. 345, 180 N.W.2d 455 (1970). The statutory death benefits at issue here—paid pursuant to 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 and the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance program—represent compensation paid by the federal government, not property passing from Levi Angelí's estate.

As this Court observed in *Cournoyer*, nonmarital property must be acquired by "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance." 180 N.W.2d at 457. Death gratuity payments and SGLI proceeds are not bequests or inheritances in any legal sense; they are statutory entitlements triggered by a servicemember's death, paid directly by the government pursuant to congressional appropriation. The plaintiff did not receive these funds because Levi Angelí willed them to her or because she inherited them under the laws of intestate succession. Rather, she received them because federal statute designated her as the beneficiary of government-provided death benefits.

The analogy to inheritance fails because there is no decedent's estate, no probate proceeding, and no testamentary intent. The funds derive from the public treasury, not from the decedent's property. This Court should decline to extend the *Johnson* reasoning and should instead hold that statutory death benefits do not satisfy the statutory definition of "inheritance" or "bequest."

### B. The Presumption of Marital Property Was Not Adequately Rebutted

The plaintiff invokes *Keller v. Keller*, 365 Minn. 410, 311 N.W.2d 904 (1981), for the proposition that the presumption of marital property was rebutted. However, the defense argues that the plaintiff failed to meet her burden.

In *Keller*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that the presumption of marital property may be rebutted by demonstrating that property was acquired by one of the statutorily enumerated means. 311 N.W.2d at 906. Critically, the spouse seeking nonmarital classification bears the burden of proof. The plaintiff in this case cannot demonstrate that the death benefits were acquired by "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" as those terms are traditionally understood.

The plaintiff's argument that the funds were "derived solely from her son's death" conflates two distinct concepts: the source of the funds (Levi's death) and the legal mechanism of acquisition (statutory government payment). The death of a family member may trigger the payment, but the payment itself is not an inheritance. This distinction is dispositive.

Moreover, even assuming *Johnson* controls, the defense notes that the plaintiff in *Johnson* was the sole surviving parent and received the death gratuity directly. Here, Loretta Angelí received benefits that, while designated to her as parent, were part of a broader statutory scheme providing for military survivors. The marital partnership contributed to the family unit that existed at the time of Levi's death, and the marriage's dissolution should not strip Gordon Angelí of any interest in benefits that accrued during the marital unit.

### C. The Commingling Doctrine Does Not Resolve the Classification Question

The plaintiff's emphasis on the separate bank account and lack of commingling, while relevant to the *Taylor* analysis, 302 Minn. 339, 225 N.W.2d 82 (1975), does not address the threshold question: whether the property was properly classified as nonmarital at all.

The commingling doctrine addresses situations where nonmarital property has been combined with marital property, potentially losing its nonmarital character. *Taylor*, 225 N.W.2d at 67. But this doctrine presupposes that the property was nonmarital at the time of acquisition. Here, the threshold question remains unanswered: were these benefits ever properly classified as nonmarital property?

The defense does not dispute that the funds were deposited in a separate account. However, this factual circumstance cannot overcome the legal deficiency in the classification analysis. The separate deposit merely preserved whatever character the property possessed at inception—it did not transform marital funds into nonmarital funds.

### D. The Timing of Acquisition Remains Relevant to the Characterization Inquiry

While *Sand v. Sand* holds that property acquired by inheritance is nonmarital regardless of when received, 302 Minn. 329, 225 N.W.2d 66 (1975), this principle applies only to property that constitutes an inheritance in the first instance. As discussed above, statutory death benefits do not meet this definition.

The defense acknowledges that *Sand* stands for the proposition that timing alone does not convert nonmarital property to marital property. However, the inverse is equally true: timing does not convert marital property to nonmarital property. The plaintiff received these benefits during the marriage, while the marital partnership existed. The benefits should inure to both spouses.

### E. Policy Considerations Support Classification as Marital Property

The defense urges this Court to consider the equitable implications of treating all statutory death benefits as the sole property of the surviving parent. During a 27-year marriage, both spouses contribute to the family unit. The death of a child is a tragedy that affects both parents. To allow one spouse to retain all death benefits, simply because the other spouse did not die first, creates an arbitrary and inequitable result.

Gordon Angelí lost his son Levi just as Loretta Angelí did. The grief was shared. The marital partnership encompassed the entire family unit. The statutory benefits, while directed to the surviving parent, represent a form of compensation for the loss of the child—a loss borne by both parents. Classification as marital property would acknowledge this shared loss and the shared nature of the marital unit.

Alternatively, should this Court affirm the nonmarital classification, the defense maintains that the monetary award to Gordon Angelí should be upheld, as it represents an appropriate application of the unfair hardship provision under Minnesota law.

The defense submits that the classification analysis requires careful examination of the nature of the acquisition, not merely the source of the funds. Statutory death benefits are not inheritances, and the district court erred in classifying them as nonmarital property.

### INTRODUCTION

### I. THE DEFENSE'S POSITION ON CLASSIFICATION

The defense maintains that the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits should have been classified as marital property under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58. The defense's position rests on the following legal grounds.

#### A. The Definition of "Inheritance" Under Minnesota Law

Under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, property acquired by "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" constitutes nonmarital property. The defense contends that the benefits received by Loretta Angelí do not fall within this statutory category.

In *In re Estate of Cournoyer*, 288 Minn. 345, 180 N.W.2d 455 (1970), this Court held that "inheritance" strictly refers to property passing from a decedent's estate through probate proceedings. The Cournoyer Court specifically addressed the meaning of inheritance in the context of Minnesota's probate statutes and concluded that the term encompasses only those transfers effectuated through the decedent's estate.

The benefits received by Loretta Angelí derive entirely from federal statutory programs. The death-gratuity benefits were paid pursuant to 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 (2000 & Supp. IV 2004), which establishes a uniform system of death benefits for service members who die during active duty. The Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance benefits were paid under 38 U.S.C. §§ 1965-80, a federal life insurance program administered by the Veterans' Administration. Neither program involves any transfer from Levi Angelí's estate; rather, these are direct payments from the federal government to designated beneficiaries.

The defense submits that *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 392 N.W.2d 759 (Minn. Ct. App. 1986, incorrectly classified military death gratuities as nonmarital property by expansively interpreting "inheritance" beyond its statutory meaning. The Johnson Court failed to apply the holding of *Cournoyer* and instead created a new category of statutory benefits that it deemed equivalent to inheritance. This interpretation contradicts the plain language of section 518.58 and the precedent established in *Cournoyer*.

#### B. The Presumption of Marital Property

Minnesota law establishes a presumption that all property acquired during marriage is marital property. This presumption can be rebutted only by demonstrating that the property was acquired by one of the statutorily enumerated means: gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance. See *Keller v. Keller*, 365 Minn. 410, 311 N.W.2d 904 (1981).

The defense submits that Loretta Angelí failed to rebut this presumption. Because the benefits were not acquired through any of the statutorily specified methods—meaningful transfers from a donor or decedent's estate—the presumption of marital property remains intact. The fact that the benefits were received during the marriage supports their classification as marital property accumulated during the marital partnership.

In *Sand v. Sand*, 302 Minn. 329, 225 N.W.2d 66 (1975), this Court held that property acquired by inheritance is nonmarital regardless of when it is received. However, the Sand Court explicitly based its holding on the characterization of the property as inheritance in the first instance. The defense does not dispute that inheritance received during marriage remains nonmarital; rather, the defense contends that the benefits at issue here do not constitute inheritance under any reasonable interpretation of that term.

#### C. The Policy Behind the Statutory Framework

The defense submits that the policy rationale underlying Minnesota's marital property framework supports classification of these benefits as marital property. Marriage is a partnership in which both parties contribute to the accumulation of assets. When a spouse receives substantial funds during the marriage, those funds typically become part of the marital estate available for division upon dissolution.

Military death benefits are designed to provide financial support to surviving family members. However, the defense submits that this purpose is not served by excluding the benefits from the marital estate when the surviving spouse is the beneficiary. The benefits serve to support the family unit, which includes both spouses during an ongoing marriage. Classification as marital property does not defeat the purpose of the benefits; rather, it ensures that the benefits are distributed equitably between the parties who formed the partnership during the marriage.

The plaintiff has presented several arguments in support of the nonmarital classification. The defense responds as follows.

#### A. Response to the Plaintiff's Reliance on *Johnson*

The plaintiff heavily relies on *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 392 N.W.2d 759 (Minn. Ct. App. 1986), for the proposition that military death gratuities paid to a parent constitute nonmarital property. The defense submits that *Johnson* was wrongly decided and should not bind this Court.

The Johnson Court reasoned that military death gratuities "function as an inheritance" for classification purposes. However, this reasoning conflates the purpose of the benefit with its legal character. The benefits may serve a function similar to inheritance in providing financial support to survivors, but this does not transform them into inheritance within the meaning of section 518.58.

As the defense argued in *Cournoyer*, the term "inheritance" has a established legal meaning referring to property passing from a decedent's estate. The Johnson Court departed from this established meaning without justification. The defense respectfully requests that this Court decline to follow *Johnson* and instead apply the correct interpretation of "inheritance" as established in *Cournoyer*.

#### B. Response to the Plaintiff's Presumption Argument

The plaintiff argues that the presumption of marital property was rebutted because the benefits qualify as inheritance under *Johnson*. The defense submits that this argument is circular: it assumes the very proposition it seeks to prove.

The presumption of marital property can be rebutted only by demonstrating that property was acquired by one of the statutorily enumerated means. Because the defense contends that the benefits do not constitute inheritance, the presumption was never properly rebutted. The plaintiff's reliance on *Keller v. Keller* is misplaced; *Keller* held that the presumption is rebutted when property is acquired by inheritance, but it did not hold that any particular category of property constitutes inheritance for purposes of the statute.

#### C. Response to the Plaintiff's Timing Argument

The plaintiff argues that under *Sand v. Sand*, the timing of receipt is irrelevant because inheritance received during marriage remains nonmarital. The defense agrees with this statement of law but submits that it does not apply here because the benefits are not inheritance in the first instance.

The Sand Court explicitly held that "property acquired by inheritance is nonmarital property regardless of the time of its receipt." 225 N.W.2d at 68. However, this holding assumes that the property was acquired by inheritance. The defense does not dispute that inheritance received during marriage remains nonmarital; rather, the defense contends that the benefits at issue here do not satisfy the threshold requirement of being inheritance.

### III. REGARDING THE DIVISION OF BENEFITS

Although the defense has argued that the benefits should be classified as marital property, the defense acknowledges the court's recent ruling reversing the monetary award to Gordon Angelí based on federal preemption. The defense submits that this ruling is correct and should be affirmed.

Federal law contains extensive protections for military benefits. Title 10 U.S.C. § 1445 and title 42 U.S.C. § 659 establish anti-attachment provisions designed to protect military benefits from seizure or assignment. These provisions reflect a federal policy of ensuring that military personnel and their survivors receive the benefits they have earned through service.

The defense agrees that these federal protections preempt state law division of the benefits. Even if the benefits were properly classified as marital property under Minnesota law, federal law would prohibit their division between the parties. The district court therefore correctly refrained from dividing the benefits, and this Court should affirm the reversal of the monetary award.

### IV. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

The defense respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **Reverse the classification** of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angelí's nonmarital property and instead classify these benefits as marital property;

2. **Affirm the reversal** of the monetary award to Gordon Angelí based on federal preemption; and

3. **Remand the matter** for further proceedings consistent with this opinion, including an equitable division of the marital estate.

The defense submits that the classification of these benefits as marital property is required by the plain language of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58 and the precedent established in *In re Estate of Cournoyer*. The defense acknowledges that *In re Marriage of Johnson* reached a contrary result, but respectfully submits that *Johnson* was wrongly decided and should not be followed.

Alternatively, if this Court determines that it is bound by *Johnson*, the defense requests that the Court acknowledge the tension between *Johnson* and *Cournoyer* and clarify the correct interpretation of "inheritance" for purposes of Minnesota's marital property statutes.

The defense submits that an equitable result can be achieved through proper application of the law. ### Analysis

**I. Classification of the Death Benefits**

The primary issue on appeal is whether the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits are marital property or Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The defense argues that these benefits should be classified as marital property because they were paid directly by the federal government rather than through a probate estate. The defense relies on *In re Estate of Cournoyer*, 288 Minn. 345, 180 N.W.2d 455 (1970), to assert that "inheritance" is strictly limited to property passing through probate.

However, the prosecution correctly identifies that this court is bound by the precedent of *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 392 N.W.2d 759 (Minn. Ct. App. 1986). In *Johnson*, the Minnesota Court of Appeals specifically addressed the classification of military death gratuities paid to a parent. The court held unequivocally that "Military death gratuities paid to a parent are not marital property but are instead the parent’s separate property." 392 N.W.2d at 761. The defense’s invitation to disregard *Johnson* as wrongly decided is not persuasive. Under the doctrine of stare decisis, this court must follow the controlling precedent established by the Court of Appeals, which has already determined that statutory military benefits function as an inheritance for the purposes of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58.

The defense further argues that the presumption of marital property was not rebutted because the funds were not acquired by a statutorily enumerated means. Under *Keller v. Keller*, 365 Minn. 410, 311 N.W.2d 904 (1981), the presumption of marital property is rebutted when the property is acquired by gift or inheritance. Because *Johnson* classifies the benefits as nonmarital property, the presumption is rebutted. The defense’s reliance on *Sand v. Sand*, 302 Minn. 329, 225 N.W.2d 66 (1975), regarding the timing of the acquisition is inapposite; *Sand* holds that property acquired by inheritance is nonmarital regardless of when it is received, but it presupposes that the property qualifies as an inheritance. *Johnson* resolves that threshold question in the affirmative.

**II. Division of the Benefits**

Having affirmed the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property, the court must determine whether the district court could divide them to prevent unfair hardship. The district court awarded Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2.

Both the prosecution and the defense now agree that federal law prohibits the division of these benefits. Federal statutes, including 10 U.S.C. § 1445 and 42 U.S.C. § 659, contain anti-attachment provisions designed to protect military benefits from creditors and legal processes. The supremacy of federal law preempts the state court’s authority to divide these funds, even if state law would otherwise permit such a division to prevent unfair hardship. Therefore, the district court’s reliance on state law to apportion Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property was error.

### Conclusion

The district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The defense’s arguments to classify the benefits as marital property are rejected based on the binding precedent of *In re Marriage of Johnson*. However, federal law preempts the division of these benefits. Accordingly, the court affirms the classification of the property as nonmarital but reverses the monetary award to Gordon Angell.
