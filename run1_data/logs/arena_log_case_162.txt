### STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews a trial court's grant of exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action de novo. *Louisiana Safety Services, Inc. v. Berry*, 985 So. 2d 188, 191 (La. App. 4 Cir. 2008). The question before this Court is whether, on the face of the petition, the law provides no remedy for the claimed grievance. *Id.* at 192. Similarly, the question of no right of action is whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the subject matter of the suit. *LSA-C.C.P. art. 927; Chevron Chemical Co. v. Sonat, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 592, 597 (La. 1985).

---

### ARGUMENT

#### I. THE APPELLANT LACKS A RIGHT OF ACTION

The defense submits that the trial court correctly determined that the appellant lacks a right of action to bring this claim. A right of action is a legal interest in the subject matter of the suit, and a plaintiff must have such an interest to maintain a claim. *Chevron Chemical Co. v. Sonat, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 592, 597 (La. 1985).

The appellant, as a presumed father under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, has no legal interest in seeking reimbursement from an alleged biological father for child support payments he made pursuant to a court order. The Louisiana Supreme Court has been unequivocal that "the payment of child support by a presumed father is not a debt owed to him by the biological father; it is a fulfillment of his own legal duty to the child." *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 412 So. 2d 1098, 1101 (La. 1982).

The appellant cannot establish a legal interest in reimbursement because he has never availed himself of the statutory disavowal procedures. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 185, a presumed father who wishes to challenge paternity must do so within a specified time period and through proper judicial channels. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d 644, 648 (La. 2005). The appellant never initiated disavowal proceedings. Therefore, under Louisiana law, he remains the legal father of the child, with all the obligations attendant thereto—including the obligation to pay child support.

A presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a third party for child support payments he voluntarily made pursuant to a court order. *Smith v. Smith*, 650 So. 2d 755, 758 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1995). The appellant, having failed to disavow the child, cannot now attempt to shift his support obligation to another individual.

#### II. THE APPELLANT FAILS TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION

Beyond the lack of a right of action, the appellant fails to state a cause of action upon which relief can be granted. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 imposes a duty of support on parents toward their children, but it does not create a right of reimbursement between parents. The obligation to support is owed directly to the child, not to another parent. *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 412 So. 2d at 1101.

The appellant attempts to rely on Article 227 to support his claim, but this reliance is misplaced. That provision defines the duty of support but does not create a cause of action between parents for reimbursement. Louisiana law has consistently held that child support obligations are personal to the parent-child relationship and cannot be enforced as debts between adults. *Id.*

The appellant asks this Court to recognize a novel cause of action for reimbursement of child support payments from an alleged biological father. No such cause of action exists in Louisiana law. The appellant cites no authority—because none exists—authorizing a presumed father to recover child support payments from a third party who has never been judicially recognized as the biological parent.

The appellant also seeks future child support payments from both R.R. and A.F.Y. This claim fails for the same reasons. The appellant has never established that R.R. is the biological father through any judicial proceeding. More fundamentally, the appellant remains the legal father under the presumption of Article 184 and thus remains obligated to support the child. He cannot escape his own obligations by seeking to impose them on others.

#### III. THE PRESUMPTION OF LEGITIMACY CANNOT BE CIRCUMVENTED

The presumption of legitimacy under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 is a cornerstone of Louisiana family law, designed to protect the stability of family units and the best interests of children. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648. This presumption cannot be circumvented by a presumed father seeking reimbursement from a third party.

The appellant attempts to do indirectly what he could not do directly—challenge the paternity of the child—without following the statutory procedures. This Court should not countenance such an end-run around Louisiana's carefully crafted paternity statutes. To hold otherwise would transform a child's right to support into a private debt between adults and undermine the very purpose of the presumption of legitimacy.

The appellant had the statutory remedy available to him: disavowal under Article 185. He chose not to pursue it. He cannot now ask this Court to create a new remedy that bypasses the established statutory framework.

#### IV. THE APPELLANT'S CLAIM IS INCONSISTENT WITH LOUISIANA PUBLIC POLICY

The defense further submits that recognizing the claim sought by the appellant would be contrary to Louisiana public policy. The presumption of legitimacy exists to protect children from uncertain paternity disputes and to ensure their right to support. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648.

Allowing a presumed father to seek reimbursement from an alleged biological father would create perverse incentives. A presumed father could accept the benefits of the father-child relationship—companionship, inheritance rights, emotional bonds—while simultaneously seeking to avoid the financial responsibilities. This undermines the stability of family relationships and harms children.

Moreover, such a claim would invite chaotic litigation over family responsibilities. Every child support order could become the subject of a separate reimbursement action against an alleged biological father, disrupting the orderly administration of support and harming the children who depend on consistent support.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the appellant J.M.Y.'s suit. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**J.M.Y. v. R.R. and A.F.Y.**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE TRIAL COURT**

**DECIDED: [Date]

**BEFORE: The Court

**DECISION:**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y. is affirmed.

**OPINION:**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y. ("Appellant") from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. and A.F.Y. ("Appellees"). For the following reasons, we find that the trial court did not err in dismissing Appellant's claims.

**I. Standard of Review**

The appropriate standard of review for the granting of exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action is de novo. *Louisiana Safety Services, Inc. v. Berry*, 985 So. 2d 188, 191 (La. App. 4 Cir. 2008). The court must determine whether the plaintiff, on the face of the petition, has a legal interest in the subject matter of the suit (right of action) and whether the law affords a remedy for the grievance asserted (cause of action). *Chevron Chemical Co. v. Sonat, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 592, 597 (La. 1985).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The Presumption of Paternity and the Requirement of Disavowal**

The facts establish that J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, this creates a presumption of paternity in favor of J.M.Y. The Louisiana Supreme Court has consistently held that this presumption is "strong" and is designed to protect the stability of the family unit and the best interests of the child. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d 644, 648 (La. 2005).

To rebut this presumption, a presumed father must strictly comply with the statutory procedures for disavowal set forth in Louisiana Civil Code Article 185. The record indicates that J.M.Y. never initiated a disavowal action under Article 185. Instead, he filed a petition in 2008 seeking genetic testing and reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. As noted in *In re: A.C.*, the presumption of paternity "can only be rebutted through strict compliance with the disavowal procedures." *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648. Because J.M.Y. failed to avail himself of this statutory remedy, the presumption of paternity remains intact.

**B. No Right of Action**

A right of action requires that the plaintiff have a legal interest in the subject matter of the suit. *Chevron Chemical Co. v. Sonat, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d at 597. J.M.Y. seeks to recover child support payments from R.R., the alleged biological father. However, because J.M.Y. remains the legal father under the presumption of paternity, he lacks the legal interest necessary to seek reimbursement from a third party.

The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeal addressed a similar scenario in *Smith v. Smith*, holding that "a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a third party for child support payments he voluntarily made." *Smith v. Smith*, 650 So. 2d 755, 758 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1995). J.M.Y.'s failure to disavow the child precludes him from asserting a right of action against R.R. for the recovery of support payments.

**C. No Cause of Action**

Furthermore, J.M.Y. has failed to state a cause of action upon which relief can be granted. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 imposes a duty of support on parents toward their children, but it does not create a right of reimbursement between parents. The obligation to support is owed directly to the child, not to the other parent or a third party.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the notion that child support payments constitute a debt owed by the biological father to the presumed father. In *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, the Court stated, "[t]he payment of child support by a presumed father is not a debt owed to him by the biological father; it is a fulfillment of his own legal duty to the child." *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 412 So. 2d 1098, 1101 (La. 1982). J.M.Y.'s attempt to characterize his support obligations as a debt recoverable from R.R. is contrary to this established principle. Louisiana law recognizes no cause of action that allows a presumed father to retroactively shift the financial burden of child support to an alleged biological father without first having successfully disavowed his own paternity.

**III. Conclusion**

The arguments presented by the Appellees correctly identify that J.M.Y.'s claims are barred by the presumption of legitimacy and the absence of a statutory mechanism for reimbursement under these circumstances. Allowing such a claim would circumvent the strict disavowal procedures of Article 185 and undermine the public policy favoring the stability of family relationships. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648.

Accordingly, the trial court properly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**SO ORDERED.**

**J.M.Y. v. R.R. and A.F.Y.**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE TRIAL COURT**

**DECIDED: [Date]

**BEFORE: The Court

**DECISION:**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y. is affirmed.

**OPINION:**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y. ("Appellant") from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by R.R. and A.F.Y. ("Appellees"). For the following reasons, we find that the trial court did not err in dismissing Appellant's claims.

**I. Standard of Review**

The appropriate standard of review for the granting of exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action is de novo. *Louisiana Safety Services, Inc. v. Berry*, 985 So. 2d 188, 191 (La. App. 4 Cir. 2008). In reviewing an exception of no cause of action, the court must determine whether the law affords a remedy on the facts pleaded. *Chevron Chemical Co. v. Sonat, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 592, 597 (La. 1985). The court accepts all well-pleaded facts as true but determines whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any set of facts provable under the petition. *Id.*

**II. Analysis**

**A. The Presumption of Paternity and the Requirement of Disavowal**

The Appellant argues that he has a right of action to seek genetic testing to determine biological paternity before initiating disavowal proceedings. He contends that the trial court erred by dismissing his suit without allowing him to establish the factual basis for his claims. We find this argument unpersuasive.

The facts establish that J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, this creates a presumption of paternity in favor of J.M.Y. The Louisiana Supreme Court has consistently held that this presumption is "strong" and is designed to protect the stability of the family unit and the best interests of the child. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d 644, 648 (La. 2005).

To rebut this presumption, a presumed father must strictly comply with the statutory procedures for disavowal set forth in Louisiana Civil Code Article 185. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648. The Appellant's argument that he must obtain genetic testing *before* he can file a disavowal action misconstrues the statutory framework. The proper procedural vehicle for a presumed father to challenge paternity and seek genetic testing is the disavowal action itself. By filing a petition for reimbursement against third parties rather than a disavowal action under Article 185, the Appellant has attempted to circumvent the exclusive statutory remedy provided by the legislature.

The Appellant attempts to distinguish *Smith v. Smith*, 650 So. 2d 755 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1995), by noting that he has alleged he is not the biological father, whereas the father in *Smith* did not. However, the controlling principle in *Smith* is not the father's subjective belief, but his legal status. *Smith* held that "a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a third party for child support payments he voluntarily made." *Smith v. Smith*, 650 So. 2d at 758. Because the Appellant remains the presumed father under Article 184 and has not successfully navigated the disavowal procedures of Article 185, he lacks the legal interest necessary to maintain this action against R.R. or A.F.Y. for reimbursement.

**B. No Cause of Action: Unjust Enrichment and Fraud**

The Appellant further argues that he has stated a cause of action for unjust enrichment and fraudulent concealment against A.F.Y., citing *Walter v. McNeil*, 31 So. 2d 602 (La. 1953). He alleges that A.F.Y. accepted child support payments knowing he was not the biological father.

While the Appellant alleges fraud, the relief he seeks—the recovery of child support payments—is barred by the nature of the child support obligation. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 imposes a duty of support on parents toward their children. The Louisiana Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the notion that child support payments constitute a debt owed by the biological father (or the mother) to the presumed father. In *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, the Court stated, "[t]he payment of child support by a presumed father is not a debt owed to him by the biological father; it is a fulfillment of his own legal duty to the child." *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 412 So. 2d 1098, 1101 (La. 1982).

This principle applies equally to the Appellant's claim against A.F.Y. The child support payments paid by J.M.Y. were made pursuant to a legal obligation owed to the child, not to A.F.Y. As the legal father, J.M.Y. was responsible for the support of the child. The fact that A.F.Y. may have allegedly concealed the child's true paternity does not transform the Appellant's legal obligation to the child into a debt owed to him by A.F.Y. that can be recovered via a claim for unjust enrichment. To allow such a claim would effectively permit a presumed father to condition his duty of support on the mother's honesty, thereby undermining the child's right to support. The "unjust enrichment" cited in *Walter v. McNeil* cannot apply where the "benefit" received by the mother was the fulfillment of the child's legal right to support.

**C. Public Policy Considerations**

The Appellant argues that public policy is served by allowing him to establish the truth of his paternity. While the truth is a noble goal, it must be pursued within the confines of the law. The strict procedures of Article 185 exist to balance the interests of the child, the mother, and the presumed father. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648. Allowing the Appellant to proceed with a reimbursement action against third parties without first disavowing the child would disrupt the stability of the family unit and deprive the child of the security intended by the presumption of paternity.

**III. Conclusion**

The arguments presented by the Appellant do not overcome the presumption of paternity or the absence of a statutory mechanism for reimbursement under these circumstances. The trial court correctly found that the Appellant failed to state a cause of action or demonstrate a right of action because he remains the presumed father and has not complied with the mandatory disavowal procedures. The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action is affirmed.

**SO ORDERED.**

## II. THE APPELLANT LACKS A RIGHT OF ACTION

### A. The Presumption of Paternity Under Article 184

The cornerstone of Louisiana's paternity law is the presumption of paternity established by Civil Code Article 184. When a child is born during marriage, the husband is presumed to be the father of the child. This presumption is not merely a procedural formality; it is a "strong" presumption designed to protect the stability of the family unit and the best interests of the child. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d 644, 648 (La. 2005).

The appellant does not dispute that he was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. Therefore, the presumption of paternity under Article 184 applies with full force. The appellant is the legal father of the child.

### B. The Exclusive Statutory Remedy: Disavowal Under Article 185

To rebut the presumption of paternity, Louisiana law provides a specific statutory mechanism: the disavowal action under Civil Code Article 185. The Louisiana Supreme Court has consistently held that to rebut this presumption, a presumed father must strictly comply with the statutory procedures for disavowal set forth in Article 185. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d at 648.

The appellant argues that he should be permitted to obtain genetic testing *before* initiating disavowal proceedings. This argument misconstrues the statutory framework. The proper procedural vehicle for a presumed father to challenge paternity and seek genetic testing is the disavowal action itself. Article 185 already provides the mechanism for testing within the disavowal action.

The appellant attempts to circumvent the exclusive statutory remedy by filing a petition for reimbursement against third parties rather than a disavowal action under Article 185. This attempt must fail. As the Louisiana Fourth Circuit Court of Appeal held in *Smith v. Smith*, 650 So. 2d 755, 758 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1995), "a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a third party for child support payments he voluntarily made."

### C. The Appellant's Reliance on Smith is Misplaced

The appellant attempts to distinguish *Smith v. Smith* by noting that he has alleged he is not the biological father, whereas the father in *Smith* did not. However, this distinction is legally irrelevant. The controlling principle in *Smith* is not the father's subjective belief about biological paternity; it is his legal status as the presumed father. *Smith* held that a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a third party for child support payments he voluntarily made. *Id.* at 758.

Because the appellant remains the presumed father under Article 184 and has not successfully navigated the disavowal procedures of Article 185, he lacks the legal interest necessary to maintain this action against R.R. or A.F.Y. for reimbursement. The court accepts all well-pleaded facts as true but determines whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any set of facts provable under the petition. *Id.*

**II. Analysis**

**A. The Presumption of Paternity and the Requirement of Disavowal**

The Appellees argue, and the record confirms, that J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, this creates a "strong" presumption of paternity in favor of J.M.Y. *In re: A.C.*, 897 So. 2d 644, 648 (La. 2005). Smith*, 650 So. 2d at 758. The Appellant's allegation that he is not the biological father does not alter this legal principle. The right to seek reimbursement is contingent upon the father's legal status, not his subjective belief. Because the Appellant remains the presumed father under Article 184 and has not successfully navigated the disavowal procedures of Article 185, he lacks the legal interest necessary to maintain this action against R.R. or A.F.Y.

The Appellees further argue that the Appellant has failed to state a cause of action for unjust enrichment or fraudulent concealment. We agree. The Appellant alleges that A.F.Y. accepted child support payments knowing he was not the biological father. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 imposes a duty of support on parents toward their children. The Louisiana Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the notion that child support payments constitute a debt owed by the biological father (or the mother) to the presumed father. In *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, the Court stated, "[t]he payment of child support by a presumed father is not a debt owed to him by the biological father; it is a fulfillment of his own legal duty to the child." *Boudreaux v. The "unjust enrichment" doctrine cited in *Walter v.
