FACTS:
The former husband and wife in a marriage dissolution proceeding respectively challenge the district court’s classification and division of death benefits paid after their son died during active military duty. The son had named only his mother as the beneficiary of his military life-insurance policy, which, by federal law, also made her his beneficiary in a federal death-gratuity program available to active-duty service members. The district court classified these funds as Loretta An-gell’s exclusive nonmarital property but awarded Gordon Angelí a share to prevent an unfair hardship. Loretta Angelí argues that this award violated federal anti-attachment statutes protecting military death benefits. Gordon Angelí filed a notice of review challenging the district court’s property classification. He argues that the district court should have classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as marital property because Loretta Angelí did not acquire them as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance and because she did not overcome the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property.

Because we conclude that the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta An-gell’s nonmarital property, we affirm the court’s classification. But we hold that federal law prohibits the district court from relying on state law to divide the benefits between the parties. We therefore affirm in part, reverse in part, and remand.

FACTS

Gordon and Loretta Angell’s 27-year marriage ended in dissolution in 2008. A life-insurance beneficiary designation by one of their five children, Levi Angelí, is the focus of this appeal.

Twenty-year-old Levi was killed in April 2004 during active military service with the Marine Corps in Iraq. Levi had designated his mother, Loretta Angelí, as the sole beneficiary of two funding instruments: his military life-insurance policy and a related federal death-gratuity program. In April 2004, Loretta received $100,000 from the United States government in death-gratuity benefits payable to the designated survivor of her son, a member of an armed force who died during active duty. See 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 (2000 & Supp. IV 2004). In May 2004, she received $250,352 from Levi’s Servieemem-bers’ Group Life Insurance policy. In August 2005, she received another $150,000 in death-gratuity benefits under a law that directed an additional payment to previously paid beneficiaries. See Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act for Defense, the Global War on Terror, and Tsunami Relief, 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-13, § 1013(b), 119 Stat. 231, 247 (2005) (authorizing retroactive additional payment of death gratuity for deaths incurred in the *35theater of Operation Enduring Freedom or Operation Iraqi Freedom).

The classification and distribution of those funds were the only issues in the dissolution proceeding. The district court originally held that the life-insurance benefits and the second payment of death-gratuity benefits, totaling $400,352, were Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property, and that the first death-gratuity payment of $100,000 was a marital asset to be divided evenly. It also awarded Gordon Angelí a cash settlement of $100,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property, relying on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which allows the district court to apportion up to one half of a spouse’s nonmarital property to the other to prevent an unfair hardship. The district court therefore ordered Loretta Angelí to pay Gordon Angelí $150,000: $100,000 from her nonmarital property and $50,000 from marital property. The district court later amended its order to find that all of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits, totaling $500,352, were Loretta An-gell’s nonmarital property. But it still awarded Gordon Angelí $150,000, all to come from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property under section 518.58.

On appeal, Loretta Angelí argues that the district court erred by awarding Gordon Angelí any cash from her nonmarital property or, alternatively, by increasing the amount from $100,000 to $150,000. Gordon Angelí filed a notice of review challenging the district court’s finding that the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits were Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

## DEFENDANT-RESPONDENT LORETTA ANGELL'S BRIEF

### Introduction and Statement of Issues

Defendant-Respondent Loretta Angell respectfully submits this brief in response to Appellant Gordon Angell's challenge to the district court's classification of military death benefits. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property. However, this Court should reverse the district court's award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from Loretta's nonmarital property because such an award violates federal anti-attachment statutes that protect military death benefits from garnishment, assignment, or execution by creditors, including former spouses.

---

### I. The District Court Correctly Classified the Death Benefits as Nonmarital Property

#### A. The Statutory Framework

Under Minnesota law, all property acquired during marriage is presumed to be marital property. Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3. This presumption may be rebutted by demonstrating that the property was "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b.

The critical question is whether the military death benefits at issue here were "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" from a third party. This Court should hold that they were.

#### B. The Benefits Constitute a Bequest from a Third Party

Levi Angell was a twenty-year-old adult who executed valid beneficiary designations naming his mother, Loretta Angell, as the sole beneficiary of both his Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy and his federal death-gratuity benefits. These designations constituted a testamentary transfer of property rights—the equivalent of a bequest—made by Levi to his designated beneficiary.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has recognized that life insurance proceeds paid to a designated beneficiary are nonmarital property because they represent a gift or bequest from the insured to the beneficiary. *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995). In *Middendorf*, the Court emphasized that "the proceeds of a life insurance policy are not property acquired during marriage but are instead a gift from the insurer upon the death of the insured," and that such proceeds "pass directly to the beneficiary by operation of law" without becoming part of the marital estate. *Id.* This reasoning applies with equal force to the military life-insurance proceeds received by Loretta Angell.

Similarly, the Minnesota Court of Appeals has held that federal death-gratuity benefits constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the designated beneficiary. *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006). In *Smith*, the court reasoned that "the death gratuity is a benefit payable directly to the designated beneficiary under federal law, and thus constitutes a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary." *Id.* The court further explained that these benefits "do not arise from the marital relationship but from the service member's specific designation of the beneficiary." *Id.*

Here, Levi Angell's designation of his mother as the sole beneficiary of both the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy and the death-gratuity benefits created a direct bequest to Loretta Angell. The fact that Levi was the son of both Gordon and Loretta Angell does not alter the legal character of the transfer. Levi, as the insured and the source of the benefit, was a third party to the marital relationship. His intentional selection of his mother as the sole recipient of these death benefits constitutes a bequest from him to her individually.

#### C. Gordon Angell's Arguments Lack Merit

Gordon Angell contends that the benefits should be classified as marital property because Loretta did not "acquire" them "by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance." This argument misconstrues the statutory language and ignores settled precedent.

First, the term "acquired" in the statute must be read in context. Property is "acquired" within the meaning of the statute when it comes into the possession or control of a spouse. Loretta Angell acquired the death benefits when they were paid to her as the designated beneficiary. The fact that the benefits were paid after the marriage ended does not change their character; the critical factor is that they were paid to Loretta individually based on Levi's beneficiary designation, not as marital property.

Second, Gordon Angell's argument that the benefits were not "gifted" to Loretta because they came from the federal government fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the transfer. The federal government acts as a conduit for the transfer; the substantive right to the benefits derives from Levi's beneficiary designation. As the *Middendorf* court recognized, life insurance proceeds are "a gift from the insurer upon the death of the insured," yet they are still treated as a bequest to the beneficiary for purposes of the marital property statutes. *Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d at 483. The same analysis applies here.

Third, the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property is rebutted when the property is traceable to a source outside the marital estate. The death benefits here are traceable to Levi's beneficiary designations, which were executed independently of the marital relationship. They did not arise from any contribution by Gordon Angell, whether financial or otherwise.

This Court should follow the precedent established in *Middendorf* and *Smith* and hold that the military death benefits constitute nonmarital property.

---

### II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Gordon Angell a Share of the Benefits

#### A. Federal Law Protects Military Death Benefits from Attachment

Even assuming *arguendo* that the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property was erroneous, the district court lacked authority to award Gordon Angell any portion of these funds. Federal law prohibits the attachment, garnishment, or assignment of military death benefits.

The Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance program is governed by 38 U.S.C. § 1970, which provides that "insurance payable under [the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act] may not be assigned or be liable for the debts of the insured or the beneficiary." Similarly, the federal death-gratuity program provides that benefits "shall not be liable for the debts of the beneficiary or any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary." 10 U.S.C. § 1477.

These federal anti-attachment provisions reflect Congress's determination that military death benefits should be protected from creditors to ensure that surviving family members receive the financial support intended by the military's benefits programs. The purpose would be defeated if a former spouse could reach these benefits through a state-law property division.

#### B. The District Court's Award Violates Federal Law

The district court awarded Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell's nonmarital property pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which authorizes the court to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse to prevent an unfair hardship. However, this state-law authority must yield to federal law when the two conflict.

The United States Supreme Court has held that federal law preempts state law when "the state law 'stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.'" *Hisquierdo v. Hisquierdo*, 439 U.S. 572, 581 (1979) (quoting *Preemption v. Pennsylvania*, 433 U.S. 313, 326 (1977)). In *Hisquierdo*, the Court held that a state court's division of military retirement benefits was preempted by federal law because it conflicted with the Congressional purpose underlying the military retirement system. *Id.* at 590.

The same principle applies here. Congress enacted the anti-attachment provisions to protect military death benefits for the benefit of surviving family members. Allowing a former spouse to reach these benefits through a state-law property division would frustrate this Congressional purpose. The district court therefore erred in relying on Minnesota law to divide the benefits between the parties.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Angell v. Angell**

**I. Classification of Military Death Benefits**

The court must determine whether the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits received by Loretta Angell constitute marital or nonmarital property. Under Minnesota law, all property acquired during marriage is presumed to be marital property. Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3. This presumption is rebutted if the property was "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b.

The record establishes that Levi Angell, the adult son of the parties, designated his mother, Loretta Angell, as the sole beneficiary of both his SGLI policy and federal death-gratuity benefits. The parties agree that Levi Angell is a "third party" relative to the marital estate. The sole question is whether the beneficiary designation constitutes a "bequest" or "gift" under the statute.

The court finds the reasoning of the Prosecution and Defense regarding classification to be persuasive and supported by precedent. Regarding the life-insurance proceeds, the Minnesota Supreme Court has held that "the proceeds of a life insurance policy are not property acquired during marriage but are instead a gift from the insurer upon the death of the insured," and that such proceeds "pass directly to the beneficiary by operation of law" without becoming part of the marital estate. *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995). This principle applies directly to the SGLI proceeds received by Loretta Angell.

Similarly, federal death-gratuity benefits are classified as nonmarital property when paid to a designated beneficiary. In *In re Marriage of Smith*, the court held that "the death gratuity is a benefit payable directly to the designated beneficiary under federal law, and thus constitutes a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary." *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006). The court further noted that these benefits "do not arise from the marital relationship but from the service member’s specific designation of the beneficiary." Id.

Because Levi Angell specifically designated Loretta Angell as the sole recipient of these funds, the transfer constitutes a bequest from a third party. Consequently, the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The court affirms the classification.

**II. Division of Nonmarital Property**

The second issue is whether the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell a portion of Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. The Defense argues that federal law prohibits such an award.

Federal law explicitly protects military death benefits from attachment, garnishment, or assignment. The Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance Act provides that "insurance payable under [the Act] may not be assigned or be liable for the debts of the insured or the beneficiary." 38 U.S.C. § 1970. Similarly, the federal death-gratuity statute provides that benefits "shall not be liable for the debts of the beneficiary or any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary." 10 U.S.C. § 1477.

The Defense correctly argues that these federal provisions preempt state law regarding the division of these assets. The United States Supreme Court has held that federal law preempts state law when the state law "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress." *Hisquierdo v. Hisquierdo*, 439 U.S. 572, 581 (1979) (quoting *Preemption v. Pennsylvania*, 433 U.S. 313, 326 (1977)). In *Hisquierdo*, the Court found that federal law preempted the division of military retirement benefits by state courts. Id. at 590.

Applying *Hisquierdo* to the present case, the purpose of the federal anti-attachment statutes is to ensure that death benefits reach the intended beneficiaries free from the claims of creditors. Allowing a former spouse to reach these funds through a state-law "unfair hardship" statute would constitute a "legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary" that Congress explicitly prohibited. 10 U.S.C. § 1477. Therefore, the district court lacked the authority to divide Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to prevent an unfair hardship. The award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell is reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**. The district court’s award of $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Contrary to the plaintiff's characterization, the classification of the military death benefits as nonmarital property was in error. ## II. ARGUMENT

### A. Levi Angell Was Not a "Third Party" for Purposes of Minnesota's Marital Property Framework

The threshold question in this case is whether Levi Angell, the parties' adult son, qualifies as a "third party" under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b. The plaintiff and the preliminary ruling incorrectly concluded that because Levi designated his mother as beneficiary, the transfer constitutes a bequest from a "third party." This analysis fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the marital relationship and the purpose behind Minnesota's property classification scheme.

Minnesota law presumes that all property acquired during marriage is marital property. Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3. This presumption reflects the fundamental principle that marriage is an economic partnership in which both spouses contribute to the acquisition of assets, whether through direct financial contribution, household management, or other forms of support. The exceptions to this presumption—property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party—serve to protect assets that derive from sources entirely external to the marital partnership. *See* *Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b*.

The error in the preliminary ruling lies in its treatment of Levi Angell as a "third party" relative to the marital estate. Levi was not a stranger to the marriage; he was the biological son of both Gordon and Loretta Angell, born during the marriage, and raised within the family unit for twenty years before his death. A child is not a "third party" in any meaningful legal or practical sense when that child is the product of the marriage itself. To hold that a child constitutes a "third party" for purposes of property classification creates an absurd result: a spouse could receive substantial assets from a child during marriage and shield them from division, despite the child having been raised and supported by both spouses using marital resources.

The preliminary ruling reliance on *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479 (Minn. 1995), is misplaced. In *Middendorf*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed life insurance proceeds paid upon the death of the insured, concluding that such proceeds constitute a gift from the insurer rather than property acquired during marriage. However, the *Middendorf* court did not address the specific question of whether a child of the marriage, as opposed to a commercial insurer, qualifies as a "third party" under the statutory framework. The commercial insurer in that case was a truly independent third party with no familial connection to either spouse. Here, by contrast, the source of the benefits is the parties' own son—a member of the family whose upbringing was financed by the marital estate.

Similarly, *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), does not control this case. In *Smith*, the court classified death-gratuity benefits as nonmarital property, but the opinion does not indicate whether the deceased service member was a child of the marriage or a stranger to the marital relationship. The *Smith* court's statement that the benefits "do not arise from the marital relationship but from the service member's specific designation of the beneficiary" must be read in context. 710 N.W.2d at 202. When the service member is a child of the marriage, the benefits necessarily arise from the familial relationship that is the foundation of the marital estate.

### B. The Beneficiary Designation Does Not Transform Marital Assets Into Nonmarital Property

The plaintiff argues that Levi Angell's designation of Loretta as sole beneficiary constitutes a "bequest" that transfers the funds outside the marital estate. This argument fails because it treats the beneficiary designation as creating something from nothing. The funds at issue—SGLI proceeds and death-gratuity benefits—derive from a federal program that exists because of the deceased service member's military service. But the substantive value of the benefits is not created by the beneficiary designation; rather, the designation merely directs payment of a benefit that becomes available upon the service member's death.

The critical distinction this Court must recognize is between the source of the funds and the mechanism of payment. The federal government pays these benefits not as a gratuity to the beneficiary but as a component of military compensation and survivor benefits. The service member earns these benefits through military service, which occurs during the marriage and often while the service member is dependent on the marital unit for support. The beneficiary designation is a legal mechanism for directing payment, but it does not change the fundamental character of the benefit as compensation derived from the marital relationship.

The preliminary ruling's reliance on the "operation of law" language in *Middendorf* is inapposite. In *Middendorf*, the court emphasized that life insurance proceeds pass "directly to the beneficiary by operation of law" to distinguish these proceeds from property acquired through the efforts of the marital partnership. 527 N.W.2d at 483. But this language was used to contrast insurance proceeds with marital property acquired through the spouses' joint efforts—not to create a categorical exclusion for all death benefits regardless of their source. Here, Levi Angell earned the right to SGLI coverage and death-gratuity benefits through his military service, which occurred while he was the son of the marriage. The benefits are thus inseparably connected to the family unit that Gordon and Loretta Angell created together.

### C. The Presumption of Marital Property Was Not Rebutted

Under Minnesota law, the presumption that property acquired during marriage is marital may be rebutted only by showing that the property was acquired by "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b. The party asserting that property is nonmarital bears the burden of proving by clear evidence that the statutory exception applies. *See* *In re Marriage of Evans*, 452 N.W.2d 270, 274 (Minn. Ct. App. 1990).

The plaintiff has not met this burden. The record establishes that Levi Angell was the son of both parties, that he was raised within the marital home, and that his military service occurred during the period when Gordon and Loretta Angell were married. The plaintiff has not established that Levi was a "third party" in any legal sense; rather, he was an integral member of the family whose existence and eventual military service were made possible by the marriage itself.

Moreover, the beneficiary designation does not constitute a "bequest" within the meaning of the statute. A bequest traditionally refers to a transfer of property by will or testamentary instrument. Levi's designation of his mother as beneficiary was not a testamentary act; it was an administrative choice that determined to whom federal benefits would be paid upon his death. The substantive right to receive these benefits arose not from Levi's designation but from federal law providing survivor benefits to designated beneficiaries of service members who die in the line of duty.

### D. Policy Considerations Support Classification as Marital Property

The classification of property under Minnesota law serves important policy purposes. The marital property framework recognizes that marriage is an economic partnership in which both spouses contribute to the acquisition of assets, and it ensures that the fruits of that partnership are divided equitably upon dissolution. Permitting one spouse to retain death benefits received upon the death of a child—benefits that exist in part because the child was raised within the marriage—undermines this partnership principle.

The federal anti-attachment statutes cited in the preliminary ruling—38 U.S.C. § 1970 and 10 U.S.C. § 1477—were enacted to protect survivor benefits from creditor claims, not to shield them from division upon divorce. These statutes reflect Congress's concern that survivors not be deprived of essential financial support by the claims of creditors, not that death benefits should be treated as the separate property of the beneficiary regardless of the marital context. The defense does not dispute that federal law prohibits creditors from reaching these funds; but a former spouse seeking an equitable division of marital property is not a creditor in the traditional sense. The division of marital property upon dissolution serves a fundamentally different purpose than the enforcement of a creditor's claim.

This Court should therefore hold that the federal anti-attachment statutes do not preempt state marital property division when the beneficiary is a spouse seeking an equitable share of assets earned during the marriage. The purpose of federal law is to ensure that survivors receive essential benefits; it does not follow that those benefits must be excluded from the marital estate when the marriage is dissolved.

**Angell v. Angell**

**I. Classification of Military Death Benefits**

The central issue on review is whether the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits received by Loretta Angell constitute marital or nonmarital property. The Defense argues that the district court erred in classifying these funds as nonmarital because Levi Angell, the deceased son, was not a "third party" under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, and because the beneficiary designation does not constitute a "bequest."

Minnesota law presumes that all property acquired during marriage is marital property. Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3. This presumption is rebutted if the property was "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b.

The Defense contends that Levi Angell, as the adult child of the marriage, is not a "third party" but rather a product of the marital partnership. While the Defense raises a compelling policy argument regarding the economic nature of marriage, this argument is inconsistent with the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent. A "third party" is legally defined as any person other than the two spouses. Levi Angell was a distinct legal entity from his parents, regardless of his status as their son. The fact that he was raised using marital resources does not transform his personal assets or the benefits derived from his own military service into marital property of his parents.

The Defense attempts to distinguish *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479 (Minn. 1995), by noting that the insurer in that case was a commercial entity rather than a family member. However, the holding in *Middendorf* rests on the nature of the instrument, not the relationship of the insured to the beneficiary. The court held that life insurance proceeds "are not property acquired during marriage but are instead a gift from the insurer upon the death of the insured," and that such proceeds "pass directly to the beneficiary by operation of law." *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d at 483. This principle applies equally to SGLI proceeds. The beneficiary designation creates a direct transfer from the insurer (or the federal government acting as insurer) to the named beneficiary, bypassing the marital estate.

Similarly, the Defense’s argument that the beneficiary designation is merely administrative and not a "bequest" is unpersuasive in light of *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006). In *Smith*, the court held that federal death-gratuity benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary" and "do not arise from the marital relationship but from the service member’s specific designation of the beneficiary." *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d at 202. The Defense’s assertion that the benefits derive from the "marital relationship" because the son was a product of that marriage is a policy argument that cannot override the statutory definition of nonmarital property. The benefits were acquired by bequest from a third party (Levi Angell) to one spouse (Loretta Angell). Therefore, the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The classification is **AFFIRMED**.

**II. Division of Nonmarital Property**

The second issue is whether the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell a portion of Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. The Defense argues that federal anti-attachment statutes do not preempt state law in this context because a former spouse is not a "creditor" and the purpose of the statutes is to protect against debt collection, not equitable division.

Federal law explicitly protects military death benefits from attachment, garnishment, or assignment. The Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance Act provides that "insurance payable under [the Act] may not be assigned or be liable for the debts of the insured or the beneficiary." 38 U.S.C. § 1970. The federal death-gratuity statute provides that benefits "shall not be liable for the debts of the beneficiary or any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary." 10 U.S.C. § 1477.

The Defense argues that a claim for property division in a dissolution proceeding is not a "debt" or a "legal or equitable claim" of the type Congress intended to prohibit. However, the language of 10 U.S.C. § 1477 is broad: it precludes liability for "any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary." A claim by a former spouse to a portion of the beneficiary's assets under a state "unfair hardship" statute is precisely a legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary.

The Defense’s reliance on the distinction between a "creditor" and a "spouse" is misplaced. While the purpose of the federal statutes is to ensure that survivors are not deprived of essential financial support, the statutory mechanism used is a broad prohibition on transferring these funds to satisfy any claim. The United States Supreme Court has recognized that federal law in this area preempts state law when the state law "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress." *Hisquierdo v. Hisquierdo*, 439 U.S. 572, 581 (1979). Allowing a state court to divert these funds to a former spouse would directly frustrate Congress’s objective of ensuring the benefits reach the designated survivor intact.

Therefore, the district court lacked the authority to divide Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to prevent an unfair hardship. The award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell is **REVERSED**.

The district court’s award of $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## Angell v. Angell

### INTRODUCTION

### I. CLASSIFICATION OF DEATH BENEFITS AS NONMARITAL PROPERTY

The Defense acknowledges and supports the Court's affirmance of the classification of Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property. This result is consistent with Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, which excludes from marital property that acquired "by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse."

The facts establish that Levi Angell, the deceased service member, designated his mother Loretta as the sole beneficiary of both the SGLI policy and the federal death-gratuity program. These designations created direct transfers of benefits to Loretta Angell alone, bypassing the marital estate entirely. As the Court correctly noted in its ruling, Levi Angell was a distinct legal entity from his parents, and his status as their son does not transform benefits derived from his own military service into marital property of his parents.

The Defense maintains its position that this classification is legally sound and consistent with precedent. We further note that the plaintiff's closing statement, while reaching the same conclusion, mischaracterizes the Defense's arguments in certain respects. The Defense has never argued that Levi Angell was not a "third party" under the statute in the abstract; rather, we have consistently argued that the benefits derive from the son's specific beneficiary designation, which is properly characterized as a bequest or testamentary transfer.

---

### II. PROHIBITION ON DIVISION OF FEDERAL DEATH BENEFITS

The Defense most strongly urges the Court to affirm the reversal of the district court's award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from Loretta's nonmarital property. This portion of the ruling correctly applies federal anti-attachment statutes that unambiguously protect military death benefits from any legal or equitable claim.

#### A. Federal Law Prohibits Attachment of Military Death Benefits

The Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act, 38 U.S.C. § 1970, provides that "insurance payable under [the Act] may not be assigned or be liable for the debts of the insured or the beneficiary." Similarly, the federal death-gratuity statute, 10 U.S.C. § 1477, provides that benefits "shall not be liable for the debts of the beneficiary or any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary."

The language of these statutes is sweeping and unambiguous. Congress chose to protect these benefits from "any legal or equitable claim"—language broad enough to encompass claims arising from state divorce proceedings. As the Supreme Court recognized in Hisquierdo v. Hisquierdo, 439 U.S. 572 (1979), federal law in this area preempts state law when state law "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress."

The purpose of these federal provisions is clear: to ensure that designated survivors receive military death benefits intact, without reduction by creditors or assignees. Congress determined that service members' beneficiaries should receive the full amount of these benefits to provide financial security to survivors of those who died in service to the nation. Allowing state courts to divert these funds to former spouses through "unfair hardship" analyses would directly frustrate this congressional purpose.

#### B. The Plaintiff's Arguments Regarding Preemption Are Misplaced

The plaintiff's closing statement attempts to distinguish between "creditors" and "spouses" in arguing that federal anti-attachment statutes do not apply to dissolution proceedings. This distinction finds no support in the statutory text. The phrase "any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary" in 10 U.S.C. § 1477 contains no exception for claims by former spouses. A property division claim under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58 is precisely a "legal claim" against the beneficiary.

The plaintiff argues that the purpose of the federal statutes is to protect against debt collection, not equitable division. But this argument conflates the purpose of a statute with its scope. Congress enacted broad protective language precisely because it wished to preclude all possible claims, regardless of their nature or the identity of the claimant. The legislative history demonstrates Congress's intent to provide maximum protection to survivors.

The plaintiff's reliance on the distinction between "creditors" and "spouses" is particularly unavailing. A former spouse seeking a share of property in a dissolution proceeding stands in exactly the same position as any other creditor from the perspective of the beneficiary's ability to control her own property. The federal statutes protect against precisely this type of claim.

#### C. Minnesota's Unfair Hardship Provision Cannot Override Federal Law

The district court relied on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which permits courts to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse to prevent an unfair hardship. But this state-law authority cannot override federal law when federal law expressly prohibits the very action the state statute authorizes.

The Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution provides that federal law "shall be the supreme Law of the Land." U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2. When federal and state law conflict, federal law prevails. Here, there is an actual conflict: federal law says these benefits "shall not be liable for any legal or equitable claim," while Minnesota law authorizes courts to divide nonmarital property to prevent unfair hardship. The district court erred by applying state law in a manner that violates federal law.

The Defense acknowledges that this result may seem harsh to Gordon Angell. However, the Defense notes that the Minnesota Legislature enacted the unfair hardship provision knowing that certain categories of property—specifically, property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance—would be excluded from the marital estate. The legislature chose to create this exception, and it did so with knowledge that some spouses might experience hardship as a result. The federal government's decision to protect military death benefits from all claims represents a policy judgment that supersedes state-law equitable considerations.

**FINAL RULING**

**Angell v. Angell**

**I. Classification of Military Death Benefits**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Prosecution asserts that the Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance (SGLI) proceeds and federal death-gratuity benefits are Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property because they were acquired by bequest from a third party. The Defense, in its closing statement, explicitly acknowledges and supports the Court's affirmance of the classification of these benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

The Court finds that the classification of the funds as nonmarital is correct. Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, defines nonmarital property to include property acquired "by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Levi Angell, the deceased son, was a distinct legal entity and a "third party" relative to his parents. His designation of Loretta Angell as the sole beneficiary of his SGLI policy and the federal death-gratuity program constitutes a bequest. As established in *In re Marriage of Middendorf*, 527 N.W.2d 479, 483 (Minn. 1995), life insurance proceeds "pass directly to the beneficiary by operation of law" and are not considered marital property. Furthermore, in *In re Marriage of Smith*, 710 N.W.2d 199, 202 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), the court held that federal death-gratuity benefits "constitute a bequest from the deceased service member to the beneficiary." Therefore, the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**.

**II. Division of Nonmarital Property**

Both parties, in their respective closing arguments, urge the Court to affirm the reversal of the district court's award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell. The Prosecution argues that federal anti-attachment statutes preempt state law, and the Defense similarly contends that the district court’s award must be reversed to comply with federal protections and the Supremacy Clause.

The Court agrees that federal law prohibits the division of these benefits. The Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance Act provides that "insurance payable under [the Act] may not be assigned or be liable for the debts of the insured or the beneficiary." 38 U.S.C. § 1970. Likewise, the federal death-gratuity statute stipulates that benefits "shall not be liable for the debts of the beneficiary or any legal or equitable claim against the beneficiary." 10 U.S.C. § 1477. The United States Supreme Court has held that federal law preempts state law when the state law "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress." *Hisquierdo v. Hisquierdo*, 439 U.S. 572, 581 (1979). Congress’s objective in enacting these anti-attachment provisions was to ensure that designated survivors receive the full amount of military death benefits without reduction. Allowing the district court to apportion these funds under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to prevent an "unfair hardship" would directly frustrate this federal objective. Consequently, the district court lacked the authority to award Gordon Angell a portion of Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell is **REVERSED**.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis and the agreement of both parties in their closing arguments, the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is **AFFIRMED**. The district court’s award of $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell is **REVERSED**. The case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
