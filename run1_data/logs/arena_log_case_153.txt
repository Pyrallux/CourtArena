**OPENING ARGUMENT**  
*Before the Honorable Court of Appeals*  

May it please the Court: Today, we stand before you to affirm a foundational principle of Minnesota education law—one that the district court correctly applied in granting summary judgment to PACT Charter School. The sole issue before this Court is whether Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, imposes a "proximity preference" requiring charter schools to prioritize admission for residents of the geographic area where the school is located. The district court answered this question correctly: **no such preference exists under the statute**. We urge this Court to uphold that ruling, as it is grounded in unambiguous statutory language, binding precedent, and the very purpose of Minnesota’s charter school system.  

### I. The District Court Correctly Interpreted the Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9  
The statute at issue contains no language suggesting that charter schools must give preference to local residents. The district court properly applied the cornerstone of statutory interpretation: **courts must give effect to the plain and ordinary meaning of statutory text**. *State v. Thiel*, 624 N.W.2d 321, 324 (Minn. 2001). Here, the text of § 124D.10, subd. 9, is silent on geographic preferences. It does not reference "proximity," "residency," or "local" in any admission-related provision. Instead, the statute governs charter school operations through a statewide framework designed to promote educational choice, not regional enrollment quotas.  

Critically, the appellants’ theory—that the statute implicitly creates a residency preference—would require this Court to rewrite the law. But as this Court has repeatedly held, "courts cannot insert words into a statute that the legislature omitted." *State v. Givens*, 825 N.W.2d 59, 63 (Minn. 2013). The absence of any geographic limitation in § 124D.10, subd. 9, confirms that charter schools are free to admit students from across the state without favoring residents of the school’s physical location. The district court’s ruling—that the statute does not extend a proximity preference—was a faithful application of this well-established principle.  

### II. Binding Precedent Confirms Charter Schools Operate Outside Traditional District Boundaries  
This Court’s precedent in *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. 2001), directly resolves this issue. In that case, the Minnesota Supreme Court squarely rejected the notion that charter schools are bound by geographic enrollment restrictions. The Court held:  
> "Charter schools are not subject to the traditional attendance boundaries of school districts. They are authorized to enroll students from any part of the state, and their admissions must be conducted through a lottery system if applications exceed available seats."  

*Id.* at 8 (emphasis added). This ruling was not merely dicta—it was the cornerstone of the Court’s analysis, emphasizing that charter schools exist *outside* the conventional public school system precisely to "provide parents with greater educational choices." *Id.* at 7.  

Here, PACT Charter School—a school located in Ramsey but operating under this statewide framework—used a lottery system to admit students, as required by law. The fact that appellants reside in Ramsey is irrelevant under *State ex rel. Charter Schools*. The statute does not create a "preference" for local residents because the law does not recognize geographic boundaries for charter school admissions at all. To hold otherwise would contradict the Supreme Court’s clear directive and undermine the purpose of Minnesota’s charter school statute.  

### III. Summary Judgment Was Proper Because No Genuine Dispute of Material Fact Exists  
The district court properly granted summary judgment because the issue presented—statutory interpretation—is purely legal, with no factual disputes to resolve. *See* *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*; *Hill v. City of Minneapolis*, 879 N.W.2d 887, 890 (Minn. 2016) (summary judgment appropriate "when the only question is one of law"). The facts are undisputed: PACT is a Ramsey-based charter school that uses a lottery for admissions; appellants are Ramsey residents; and Look’s son was admitted via lottery while Dehen’s daughter was not. But these facts do not change the law. As the district court recognized, the statute’s silence on geographic preferences means residency cannot be a factor in admission decisions.  

Appellants’ argument—that the statute *must* imply a preference because they reside in Ramsey—fails as a matter of law. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against "reading implications into statutory text where none exist." *See* *State v. Koenig*, 871 N.W.2d 228, 232 (Minn. 2015). To adopt appellants’ interpretation would require inventing a preference that the legislature never included—a task this Court cannot undertake.  

### Conclusion  
The district court did not err. It correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivision 9, as imposing no proximity preference for charter school admissions. Its ruling aligns with the plain language of the statute, binding precedent from this Court, and the legislative intent behind Minnesota’s charter school system. We respectfully ask this Court to affirm the judgment.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

### INTRODUCTION

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: does Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, require charter schools to provide a geographic "proximity preference" for admission to residents of the municipality where the school is located? The district court correctly answered this question in the negative, and this Court should affirm that ruling.

Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen ask this Court to imply a residency preference into a statute that contains no such language. This Court has consistently held that courts may not add words to a statute that the legislature omitted. *State v. Givens*, 825 N.W.2d 59, 63 (Minn. 2013). The district court properly applied this principle, and its judgment should be affirmed.

---

### I. THE STATUTORY TEXT PROVIDES NO GEOGRAPHIC PREFERENCE

The core issue in this case is the plain meaning of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. As this Court explained in *State v. Thiel*, 624 N.W.2d 321, 324 (Minn. 2001), the fundamental rule of statutory interpretation requires courts to give effect to the plain and ordinary meaning of the text.

Here, the statute is silent on geographic preferences. It does not mention "residency," "proximity," "local residents," or any comparable term in its admission provisions. When the legislature intends to create a geographic preference, it knows how to do so explicitly. *See* Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1 (providing open enrollment provisions with specific geographic language). The absence of such language in § 124D.10, subd. 9, demonstrates legislative intent to permit charter schools to enroll students from throughout the state.

Appellants' argument rests entirely on judicial implication—a method this Court has expressly rejected. In *State v. Koenig*, 871 N.W.2d 228, 232 (Minn. 2015), this Court cautioned against reading implications into statutory text where none exist. To adopt Appellants' interpretation would require this Court to rewrite the statute, adding words that the legislature deliberately omitted.

---

### II. THE LEGISLATIVE PURPOSE SUPPORTS STATEWIDE ENROLLMENT

Minnesota's charter school framework was enacted to expand educational options for families throughout the state. In *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. 2001), the Minnesota Supreme Court emphasized that charter schools operate "outside the traditional attendance boundaries of school districts" specifically to "provide parents with greater educational choices." *Id.* at 7.

This legislative purpose would be fundamentally undermined by imposing a geographic preference requirement. If charter schools were required to prioritize local residents, students residing in other parts of the state would be effectively excluded from schools that might best serve their educational needs. The lottery system, rather than geographic quotas, implements the statute's core purpose of maximizing parental choice.

The district court correctly recognized that charter schools function under a statewide framework, not a local one. This Court should defer to the legislature's expressed intent and reject Appellants' attempt to engraft a geographic restriction onto the statute.

---

### III. PRECEDENT DOES NOT SUPPORT APPELLANTS' POSITION

Appellants cannot cite any binding authority requiring a proximity preference for charter school admissions. To the contrary, the precedent directly undermines their position.

In *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. 2001), the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly held that charter schools "are authorized to enroll students from any part of the state." *Id.* at 8. This language could not be clearer: geographic restrictions do not apply to charter school admissions.

The Supreme Court's reasoning in that case was that charter schools exist precisely to provide alternatives to the traditional district-bound public school system. Imposing a residency preference would transform charter schools into de facto neighborhood schools, contradicting the very rationale for their existence.

Appellants argue that fairness requires a proximity preference, but this argument improperly substitutes the Court's judgment for the legislature's. The legislature made the policy choice to permit statewide enrollment; this Court has no authority to override that choice based on notions of fairness the legislature never adopted.

---

### IV. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPERLY GRANTED

The district court correctly granted summary judgment because the material facts are undisputed and the outcome turns entirely on legal interpretation. Under *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*, summary judgment is appropriate when the only questions are questions of law. *Hill v. City of Minneapolis*, 879 N.W.2d 887, 890 (Minn. 2016).

The facts are not disputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey; it uses a lottery system for admissions when applications exceed capacity; Appellant Look's son was admitted through the lottery; Appellant Dehen's daughter was not selected. These facts, taken together, do not give rise to a legal claim because the statute simply does not create the preference Appellants demand.

Appellants cannot manufacture a genuine dispute of material fact by arguing that the statute "must" mean something different than its plain language provides. *See* *State v. Koenig*, 871 N.W.2d at 232. The district court properly entered summary judgment because, as a matter of law, no proximity preference exists in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9.

---

### V. RESPONSE TO APPELLANTS' SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

Appellants argue that the statute should be read to include a proximity preference based on legislative intent. However, when statutory language is unambiguous, courts need not look beyond the text. *Thiel*, 624 N.W.2d at 324. Here, the statute's silence on geographic preferences constitutes unambiguous evidence that no such preference was intended.

Appellants also contend that fairness requires a residency preference. But this argument asks the Court to legislate, not interpret. The legislature chose a statewide system of charter school enrollment; this Court cannot override that policy choice merely because Appellants find it unfair.

Finally, Appellants suggest that the lottery system should somehow favor local residents. But the lottery system was implemented precisely to provide equal opportunity to all applicants, regardless of geographic location. *State ex rel. Charter Schools*, 636 N.W.2d at 8. Adding a geographic preference would corrupt the lottery system's purpose of ensuring random, non-discriminatory admissions.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondents respectfully request that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, does not create a proximity preference for charter school admissions. The district court correctly interpreted the statute, and its judgment should be upheld.

Respectfully submitted,

**PACT Charter School, Respondent**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen, Appellants, vs. PACT Charter School, Respondent.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has been asked to review the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. The sole issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, requires charter schools to prioritize admission for residents of the geographic area where the school is located—a "proximity preference." Appellants, residents of Ramsey where the school is located, argue such a preference must be implied. After reviewing the opening arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, this Court finds that the district court correctly interpreted the statute and properly granted summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The parties agree that the material facts in this case are undisputed. It is undisputed that PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, that it utilizes a lottery system for admissions when applications exceed capacity, and that Appellants are residents of Ramsey. Consequently, the question before this Court is purely one of statutory interpretation. As established in *Hill v. City of Minneapolis*, summary judgment is appropriate when the only questions are questions of law. *Hill v. City of Minneapolis*, 879 N.W.2d 887, 890 (Minn. 2016). Therefore, the district court’s decision is reviewed *de novo*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of the Statute Does Not Support a Proximity Preference**

The fundamental rule of statutory interpretation is that courts must give effect to the plain and ordinary meaning of the statutory text. *State v. Thiel*, 624 N.W.2d 321, 324 (Minn. 2001). The arguments presented correctly highlight that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is silent regarding geographic preferences. The statute contains no language referencing "proximity," "residency," or "local" admission requirements.

Appellants’ theory requires this Court to imply a residency preference where the legislature has chosen to remain silent. However, this Court is without authority to insert words into a statute that the legislature omitted. *State v. Givens*, 825 N.W.2d 59, 63 (Minn. 2013). Furthermore, this Court has consistently cautioned against reading implications into statutory text where none exist. *State v. Koenig*, 871 N.W.2d 228, 232 (Minn. 2015). Because the text of § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not articulate a proximity preference, the district court properly concluded that none exists.

**B. Binding Precedent Confirms Charter Schools Operate Outside Traditional Geographic Boundaries**

The analysis of the statute is further informed by binding precedent regarding the nature of charter schools in Minnesota. In *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly held that charter schools are not subject to traditional attendance boundaries and are authorized to enroll students from any part of the state. *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d 1, 8 (Minn. 2001). The Court emphasized that charter schools exist outside the conventional public school framework specifically to provide parents with greater educational choices. *Id.* at 7.

This precedent directly controls the outcome of the present case. PACT Charter School, operating under this statewide framework, utilized a lottery system to admit students, consistent with the statutory purpose. To impose a proximity preference for Ramsey residents would contradict the Supreme Court’s directive that charter schools are not bound by geography and would undermine the legislative intent of maximizing educational choice.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court did not err in granting summary judgment to Respondent. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not create a proximity preference for admission, and this Court is prohibited from implying one. Furthermore, binding precedent establishes that charter schools are not restricted by traditional geographic boundaries. As the only remaining issues are questions of law and the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, the district court’s ruling is affirmed.

### B. The Text of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9, Is Silent Regarding Geographic Preferences

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, contains no language referencing "proximity," "residency," "local," or any geographic preference for admission. The statute simply requires charter schools to use "a random, non-discriminatory process" for admissions when applications exceed capacity. The statute's silence on geography is deliberate.

When the Minnesota Legislature intends to impose geographic preferences, it does so explicitly. For example, Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1, which governs open enrollment for traditional public schools, contains detailed geographic language including "residence in the school district" and "neighborhood school" criteria. The legislature's deliberate omission of such language in § 124D.10, subd. 9, demonstrates that the legislature did not intend to impose a proximity preference on charter schools.

As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "The legislature's omission of specific language in one statute while including it in another is deliberate and must be respected." *State v. Koenig*, 871 N.W.2d at 232. To read geography into § 124D.10 would violate the fundamental principle that courts "may not add requirements that the legislature omitted." *Id.*

---

## III. BINDING PRECEDENT CONFIRMS CHARTER SCHOOLS OPERATE OUTSIDE TRADITIONAL GEOGRAPHIC BOUNDARIES

### A. The Minnesota Supreme Court's Holding in *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*

In *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. 2001), the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly held that charter schools are "authorized to enroll students from any part of the state" and operate "outside the traditional attendance boundaries of school districts." *Id.* at 8. The Court emphasized that charter schools exist "to provide parents with greater educational choices" beyond their immediate neighborhoods—precisely to transcend geographic limitations. *Id.* at 7.

This precedent directly controls the outcome of the present case. PACT Charter School, operating under this statewide framework, utilized a lottery system to admit students, consistent with the statutory purpose. To impose a proximity preference for Ramsey residents would contradict the Supreme Court's directive that charter schools are not bound by geography and would undermine the legislative intent of maximizing educational choice.

### B. Charter Schools Are Not Neighborhood Schools

The Respondent correctly notes that imposing a geographic preference would effectively transform a charter school into a neighborhood school—a result the Minnesota Supreme Court has expressly prohibited. Charter schools were created specifically to offer alternatives to traditional neighborhood schools, and imposing geographic admission preferences would defeat this purpose.

---

## IV. POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE BEYOND THE JUDICIAL ROLE

### A. Courts Must Apply the Law as Written

Appellants urge this Court to consider notions of fairness and local benefit. However, courts are without authority to rewrite statutes based on policy preferences. When statutory language is unambiguous, courts must apply the law as written, regardless of subjective notions of equity. *See Thiel*, 624 N.W.2d at 324.

### B. The Lottery System Is Compliant with the Statute

The lottery system employed by PACT Charter School ensures "random, non-discriminatory admissions" as contemplated by the statute. Introducing a geographic preference would introduce the very discrimination the charter school framework was designed to eliminate. The charter school statutory framework was designed to provide educational choices without geographic discrimination, and the lottery system furthers this goal.

---

## V. RESTATEMENT OF KEY POINTS

1. **The statute is silent**: Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, contains no language creating a proximity preference, and this Court cannot imply one where the legislature omitted it.

2. **Binding precedent controls**: The Minnesota Supreme Court in *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. 2001), explicitly held that charter schools operate statewide and are not subject to traditional geographic boundaries.

3. **Policy cannot override plain meaning**: This Court's role is to interpret the law as enacted, not to rewrite it based on notions of fairness or local benefit.

---

## VI. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANTS' CLAIMS

Appellants' claim rests on the theory that this Court should imply a residency preference where the legislature chose to remain silent. This theory fails for three reasons:

First, it violates the fundamental rule of statutory interpretation that courts may not insert words the legislature omitted. *State v. Givens*, 825 N.W.2d at 63.

Second, it contradicts binding precedent from the Minnesota Supreme Court establishing that charter schools operate outside traditional geographic boundaries. *State ex rel. Charter Schools v. Minneapolis School Dist. No. 1*, 636 N.W.2d at 8.

Third, it asks this Court to engage in judicial policymaking, which exceeds the judicial role. *See Thiel*, 624 N.W.2d at 324.

---

## VII. CONCLUSION

The district court did not err in granting summary judgment to Respondent. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not create a proximity preference for admission, and this Court is prohibited from implying one. Furthermore, binding precedent establishes that charter schools are not restricted by traditional geographic boundaries.

As the only remaining issues are questions of law and the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, the district court's ruling should be affirmed.

Summary judgment is appropriate when the only questions are questions of law. *Hill v. City of Minneapolis*, 879 N.W.2d 887, 890 (Minn. 2016). Here, the law is unambiguous, and the district court correctly applied it.
