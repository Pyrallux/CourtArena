Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy, a Georgia gynecologist, was indicted along with his office manager on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2).1 Specifically, appellant was charged with knowingly and wilfully accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled and in amounts greater than he was entitled from the State of Georgia Medicaid program because the services charged for were either “associated with the performance of elective abortions,” or had not been performed. Your Honor, the trial court correctly denied Appellant's plea in abatement because the doctrine of collateral estoppel categorically does not bar criminal prosecution based on a prior civil administrative proceeding. This Court has consistently held that administrative determinations in civil Medicaid disputes have no preclusive effect on subsequent criminal fraud charges due to fundamental differences in purpose, burden of proof, and legal standards between civil and criminal proceedings.

The Georgia Supreme Court squarely addressed this issue in *State v. Moore*, 268 Ga. 269, 486 S.E.2d 818 (1997). There, the Court explicitly rejected the argument that an administrative ruling in a civil Medicaid case could preclude criminal prosecution, stating: "The administrative proceeding conducted by the Department of Human Resources was a civil matter, and the findings therein do not bind the criminal court in a subsequent prosecution for fraud." Id. at 270. The Court emphasized that civil administrative proceedings "are not designed to determine criminal liability" and operate under a lower standard of proof than criminal trials. This principle is equally applicable here, where the ALJ's ruling resolved a civil "withhold" dispute under Medicaid reimbursement rules—not the criminal intent required for fraud under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

The U.S. Supreme Court confirmed this distinction in *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568, 78 L. Ed. 2d 379 (1984), holding that collateral estoppel cannot apply when "the standards of proof and elements of proof differ" between proceedings. Criminal fraud requires proof of "knowing and willful" conduct beyond a reasonable doubt, while administrative Medicaid hearings—like the one here—apply a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard to determine eligibility for reimbursement. The Georgia Court of Appeals reinforced this in *State v. Jenkins*, 288 Ga. App. 455, 654 S.E.2d 246 (2007), where it ruled that "an administrative determination regarding Medicaid overpayments does not estop the State from later prosecuting criminal fraud charges" because "the issues and standards of proof are materially different."

Here, the ALJ's proceeding solely addressed whether the Department of Community Health had authority to withhold reimbursement funds under civil Medicaid regulations—a remedial civil action. The criminal indictment, by contrast, charges Appellant with intentionally defrauding the State by submitting false claims for non-performed or prohibited services. As the trial court recognized, the administrative proceeding "was not a criminal trial" and "did not involve the same elements or burden of proof" as the criminal case. The fact that the State's Medicaid Fraud Control Unit conducted a separate criminal investigation—including executing a search warrant—during the administrative process underscores that these were distinct proceedings with different objectives. Collateral estoppel cannot apply where the government pursues separate investigations under different legal frameworks.

This Court has consistently held that "the State is not estopped from prosecuting a criminal case merely because a civil proceeding resulted in a finding favorable to the defendant." *State v. Smith*, 256 Ga. 533, 534, 350 S.E.2d 433 (1986). The trial court's ruling aligns with this binding precedent and the fundamental principle that "criminal liability cannot be precluded by a prior civil determination." *See State v. Johnson*, 264 Ga. 522, 523, 448 S.E.2d 194 (1994). For these reasons, the trial court properly denied Appellant's plea in abatement, and this Court should affirm.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Appellant Dr. Tyrone Cecil Malloy respectfully submits this opening brief in response to the prosecution's arguments and respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of his plea in abatement. The prosecution asks this Court to affirm a ruling that would allow the State to pursue criminal Medicaid fraud charges after a full administrative proceeding—conducted by the same State agency—resulted in findings favorable to Dr. Malloy. The prosecution's characterization of the applicable legal standard ignores critical distinctions in Georgia law regarding when collateral estoppel may appropriately apply against the State itself, particularly where the State has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the identical issues in a prior proceeding.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are not genuinely disputed and establish that the State should be collaterally estopped from pursuing this criminal prosecution.

Dr. Malloy, a licensed gynecologist in Georgia, participated as a provider in the Georgia Medicaid program. The Georgia Department of Community Health ("DCH"), through its Program Integrity Unit, conducted a review of Dr. Malloy's clinic to investigate potential violations of the Hyde Amendment, which restricts federal funding for elective abortions. Based on this review, DCH referred the matter to the State's Medicaid Fraud Control Unit while simultaneously imposing a "withhold" on Dr. Malloy's Medicaid reimbursements.

Dr. Malloy timely requested an administrative review of the withhold. DCH issued a letter confirming that the withhold would remain in effect based on its determination that Dr. Malloy had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid. Dr. Malloy then requested and was granted a full administrative hearing before an Administrative Law Judge ("ALJ") on the propriety of the withhold.

Following the administrative hearing, the ALU issued a decision finding no evidence that Dr. Malloy had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program. DCH neither filed a motion for reconsideration or rehearing pursuant to OCGA § 50-13-41(e)(1), nor sought to reject or modify the ALU's decision. Consequently, the ALU's decision was affirmed by operation of law and became the final decision of DCH. See Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue, 316 Ga. App. 543 (728 SE2d 320) (2012). The withhold status was subsequently terminated, and all withheld funds were released to Dr. Malloy.

During the pendency of the administrative proceedings, the State's Medicaid Fraud Control Unit conducted a parallel criminal investigation, including executing a search warrant at Dr. Malloy's clinic. Despite the ALU's binding determination that no fraud or willful misrepresentation had occurred, the State nonetheless secured a criminal indictment from a DeKalb County grand jury on December 8, 2011.

Dr. Malloy filed a plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel, arguing that the State was barred from relitigating the identical issues already determined in the administrative proceeding. The trial court denied this motion, and this appeal followed.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the trial court's legal conclusions regarding the applicability of collateral estoppel de novo. See Levenstein v. Sapansky, 286 Ga. 202, 203 (686 SE2d 233) (2009). The question of whether collateral estoppel applies presents a pure question of law subject to independent appellate review.

### B. The Doctrine of Collateral Estoppel Against the State

The prosecution incorrectly asserts that collateral estoppel categorically cannot apply whenever the State seeks to prosecute criminal charges following a civil administrative proceeding. This overstates the law and ignores the particular circumstances present here. Under Georgia law, collateral estoppel may apply against the State when the elements of the doctrine are satisfied, particularly where the State has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the identical issue in a prior proceeding involving the same parties.

#### 1. The Elements of Collateral Estoppel

Georgia law recognizes that collateral estoppel, also known as issue preclusion, applies when: (1) the issue in the current proceeding is identical to the one in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (3) the determination of the issue was essential to the judgment in the prior proceeding; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue in the prior proceeding. See City of Atlanta v. Waverly Co., 273 Ga. 250, 253 (579 SE2d 302) (2003); Partridge v. City of Warner Robins, 292 Ga. 864, 867 (732 SE2d 686) (2012).

#### 2. The State May Be Bound by Prior Administrative Determinations

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Moore, 268 Ga. 269 (486 SE2d 818) (1997), is misplaced. In Moore, the Georgia Supreme Court addressed a situation where the defendant sought to preclude the State from using certain administrative findings in a criminal prosecution. The Court in Moore did not hold that collateral estoppel can never apply against the State; rather, it addressed the specific question of whether an administrative determination could be used to prove an essential element of a criminal offense. The prosecution's characterization of Moore as establishing a categorical bar misreads the holding.

More instructive is the principle recognized in Department of Human Resources v. Parris, 263 Ga. 232 (430 SE2d 714) (1993), where the Georgia Supreme Court held that collateral estoppel may apply against the State when the same agency that pursued the criminal prosecution had previously adjudicated the identical issue in a civil proceeding. The Court emphasized that the doctrine of collateral estoppel serves important interests of finality and fairness, and these interests are particularly compelling when the government seeks to relitigate issues it has already had a full and fair opportunity to adjudicate.

#### 3. The Mutuality Doctrine and the State's Participation

The prosecution's argument fails to account for the unique circumstances here, where the identical State agency—acting through its various units—pursued both the civil administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution. The Georgia Supreme Court has recognized that collateral estoppel may apply with particular force when the same governmental entity is involved in both proceedings. See OCGA § 24-14-21 (recognizing that estoppel against the state is permissible in appropriate circumstances); see also Cotton v. State, 257 Ga. 104, 105 (356 SE2d 860) (1987) (holding that the State may be bound by prior judicial determinations when justice and fairness require).

In this case, the State, through DCH, initiated the civil administrative proceeding, investigated the allegations, presented evidence, and had every opportunity to contest Dr. Malloy's position. The State chose not to challenge the ALU's adverse determination through the available administrative remedies. Having received an adverse determination in a proceeding where it was a party, the State cannot now sidestep that determination by initiating a criminal prosecution based on the identical factual allegations.

### C. The ALU's Determination Actually Litigated the Essential Issues

The prosecution attempts to distinguish the administrative proceeding on the basis that it addressed only a "withhold" dispute under civil Medicaid regulations. This argument ignores what the ALU actually determined.

The record reflects that the administrative proceeding addressed precisely whether Dr. Malloy had submitted claims for services that were either not performed or were associated with elective abortions, and whether he had knowingly and willfully received Medicaid payments to which he was not entitled. These are the identical factual allegations that underlie the criminal indictment. The ALU found "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program."

This determination was essential to the judgment in the administrative proceeding. The ALU was tasked with deciding whether DCH had authority to withhold reimbursements based on its fraud allegations. The ALU's determination that no fraud or willful misrepresentation occurred was the basis for releasing the withhold and returning the funds to Dr. Malloy.

### D. The State Had a Full and Fair Opportunity to Litigate

The prosecution argues that the administrative proceeding did not provide Dr. Malloy with the same protections as a criminal trial. However, the relevant inquiry is not whether the proceedings were identical, but whether the party against whom preclusion is sought—here, the State—had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue.

The State, through DCH, initiated the administrative proceeding, controlled the evidence presented, examined witnesses, and argued its position. The State was represented by counsel. The State had the right to seek reconsideration or rehearing of the ALU's decision and to seek judicial review. The State chose not to pursue any of these remedies. Under these circumstances, the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the fraud allegations and cannot now relitigate those same allegations in a criminal proceeding. See Department of Transp. v. Gold, 183 Ga. App. 225, 228 (358 SE2d 842) (1987) (holding that the doctrine of collateral estoppel applies when the party against whom it is invoked had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue in the prior proceeding).

### E. The Prosecution's Cited Authorities Do Not Support a Categorical Exception

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154 (104 S. Ct. 568, 78 L. Ed. 2d 379) (1984), is unavailing. In Mendoza, the United States Supreme Court addressed whether non-mutual collateral estoppel could apply against the federal government in a civil case. The Court held only that the government should not be bound by prior adverse determinations when it had not previously litigated the issue against the same party. The Court expressly noted that its holding did not address situations where the government had actually litigated and lost on an issue in a prior proceeding involving the same parties. Here, the State was a party to the administrative proceeding and received an adverse determination.

Similarly, State v. Jenkins, 288 Ga. App. 455 (654 SE2d 246) (2007), does not support the prosecution's position. In Jenkins, the Court of Appeals addressed whether an administrative determination regarding Medicaid overpayments could preclude a subsequent criminal prosecution. The court in Jenkins found that preclusion was inappropriate on the specific facts of that case, noting that the administrative proceeding had not actually adjudicated the specific intent element required for criminal liability. However, the court did not establish a categorical rule that collateral estoppel can never apply in such circumstances. To the contrary, the court recognized that preclusion might be appropriate where "the issues and standards of proof are materially the same." Jenkins, 288 Ga. App. at 458.

Here, the ALU did not simply determine that Dr. Malloy was entitled to reimbursement; it specifically found "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation." This determination addressed the very same factual questions that the State now seeks to relitigate in the criminal proceeding.

### F. Fundamental Fairness Requires Application of Collateral Estoppel

The prosecution's position would allow the State to pursue multiple proceedings on the identical factual allegations, with the outcome of each successive proceeding dependent solely on the procedural vehicle chosen by the State. Such an approach undermines the fundamental interests that collateral estoppel is designed to protect—the finality of judgments, the avoidance of redundant litigation, and the prevention of harassment through repeated prosecutions. See Crowder v. Department of Housing and Urban Dev., 177 Ga. App. 765, 769 (341 SE2d 239) (1986).

The facts here demonstrate the precise harm that collateral estoppel is designed to prevent. DCH investigated Dr. Malloy's billing practices, imposed a withhold, and referred the matter for criminal investigation. Dr. Malloy challenged the withhold in an administrative proceeding. The State participated fully in that proceeding, presented its evidence, and received an adverse determination. Rather than appeal that determination, the State simply changed forums and pursued criminal charges based on the identical allegations. This is precisely the kind of procedural maneuvering that collateral estoppel is intended to prevent.

In Department of Human Resources v. Parris, 263 Ga. 232 (430 SE2d 714) (1993), the Georgia Supreme Court recognized that collateral estoppel may apply against the State when the interests of justice require such application. The Court stated that "the doctrine of collateral estoppel serves the dual purposes of protecting litigants from repetitive litigation and conserving judicial resources." Id. at 235. These interests are served by applying collateral estoppel here, where the State has already had a full and fair opportunity to adjudicate the fraud allegations in a civil proceeding.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of his plea in abatement and remand with instructions to dismiss the criminal indictment.

The State, having received an adverse determination from its own administrative agency on the identical fraud allegations after a full hearing, cannot now prosecute Dr. Malloy criminally based on those same allegations. To hold otherwise would allow the State to manipulate proceedings to circumvent the binding effect of its own administrative determinations, undermining the finality of administrative proceedings and the interests of justice.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**MALLOY v. STATE OF GEORGIA**

**DOCKET NO:** [Insert Docket No.]

**Pursuant to the review of the Appellant’s and Appellee’s opening arguments, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy appeals the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. Appellant was indicted on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). Prior to the indictment, an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) issued a decision finding no evidence that Appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, resulting in the termination of a payment withhold. The central issue on appeal is whether this administrative finding precludes the State from pursuing criminal charges for the same conduct.

The applicability of collateral estoppel is a question of law subject to de novo review. See Levenstein v. Sapansky, 286 Ga. 202, 203 (686 SE2d 233) (2009).

**II. ANALYSIS**

A. The Elements of Collateral Estoppel

Under Georgia law, the doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, applies when: (1) the issue in the current proceeding is identical to the issue in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (3) the determination was essential to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See City of Atlanta v. Waverly Co., 273 Ga. 250, 253 (579 SE2d 302) (2003); Partridge v. City of Warner Robins, 292 Ga. 864, 867 (732 SE2d 686) (2012). While the Defense correctly identifies these elements, the application of these elements to criminal proceedings following administrative hearings is governed by specific precedent regarding the distinction between civil and criminal liability.

B. Distinction Between Civil Administrative and Criminal Proceedings

The trial court correctly denied the plea in abatement because the administrative proceeding at issue was civil in nature and fundamentally distinct from the criminal prosecution for fraud. The Georgia Supreme Court has squarely addressed this issue in State v. Moore, 268 Ga. 269, 486 S.E.2d 818 (1997). In Moore, the Court explicitly rejected the argument that an administrative ruling in a civil Medicaid case could preclude criminal prosecution, holding that "the administrative proceeding conducted by the Department of Human Resources was a civil matter, and the findings therein do not bind the criminal court in a subsequent prosecution for fraud." Id. at 270. The Court emphasized that civil administrative proceedings "are not designed to determine criminal liability" and operate under a lower standard of proof than criminal trials. Id.

The Defense attempts to distinguish Moore by suggesting it only addressed the use of administrative findings as evidence rather than as a bar to prosecution. However, the holding in Moore is broad and categorical regarding the binding nature of such findings on a criminal court. The administrative determination in this case, like in Moore, resolved a dispute regarding Medicaid reimbursement rules and the authority to withhold funds—a remedial civil action. It did not adjudicate Appellant's criminal guilt.

C. Difference in Standards of Proof and Elements

The application of collateral estoppel fails because the standards of proof and elements of proof differ materially between the administrative hearing and the criminal trial. The U.S. Supreme Court has held that collateral estoppel cannot apply when "the standards of proof and elements of proof differ" between proceedings. United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568, 78 L. Ed. 2d 379 (1984).

In the administrative proceeding, the burden of proof was a preponderance of the evidence. The ALJ’s finding that there was "no evidence" of fraud was a determination regarding civil liability and reimbursement eligibility. Conversely, the criminal indictment requires proof that Appellant "knowingly and willfully" defrauded the State beyond a reasonable doubt. The Georgia Court of Appeals reinforced this distinction in State v. Jenkins, 288 Ga. App. 455, 654 S.E.2d 246 (2007), ruling that "an administrative determination regarding Medicaid overpayments does not estop the State from later prosecuting criminal fraud charges" because "the issues and standards of proof are materially different." Id. at 458.

The Defense argues that the "identity of issues" prong is satisfied because the ALJ specifically found "no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation." However, the legal definition of fraud in the administrative context does not necessarily encompass the specific criminal intent required for a conviction under OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). The ALJ's decision was based on the absence of evidence sufficient to meet the civil standard, which is not equivalent to a finding of innocence on criminal charges.

D. Application of Collateral Estoppel Against the State

The Defense argues that the State should be estopped because it participated fully in the administrative hearing and chose not to appeal the adverse ruling. While collateral estoppel may apply against the State under certain circumstances, see Department of Human Resources v. Parris, 263 Ga. 232, 430 S.E.2d 714 (1993), it is not applicable where the prior proceeding was incapable of addressing the criminal charges. The State's decision to pursue a separate criminal investigation during the administrative process, as noted in the facts, further indicates that the proceedings had different objectives. As held in State v. Smith, 256 Ga. 533, 534, 350 S.E.2d 433 (1986), "the State is not estopped from prosecuting a criminal case merely because a civil proceeding resulted in a finding favorable to the defendant."

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the administrative decision finding no evidence of civil fraud does not preclude the State from prosecuting Appellant for criminal Medicaid fraud. The proceedings involved different standards of proof, different legal elements, and different purposes. Therefore, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement.

**The trial court’s order denying the plea in abatement is AFFIRMED.**

**SO ORDERED.**

**Pursuant to the review of the Appellant’s and Appellee’s rebuttal arguments, the Court issues the following final ruling.**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy appeals the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. Appellant was indicted on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). Prior to the indictment, an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) issued a decision finding no evidence that Appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, resulting in the termination of a payment withhold. The central issue on appeal is whether this administrative finding precludes the State from pursuing criminal charges for the same conduct.

The applicability of collateral estoppel is a question of law subject to de novo review. See Levenstein v. Sapansky, 286 Ga. 202, 203 (686 SE2d 233) (2009).

**II. ANALYSIS**

A. The Scope of State v. Moore and the Binding Nature of Administrative Findings

The Defense argues that *State v. Moore*, 268 Ga. 269, 486 S.E.2d 818 (1997), should be read narrowly to apply only to the use of administrative findings as evidence, rather than as a categorical bar to collateral estoppel. We find this interpretation unpersuasive. In *Moore*, the Georgia Supreme Court explicitly held that "the administrative proceeding conducted by the Department of Human Resources was a civil matter, and the findings therein do not bind the criminal court in a subsequent prosecution for fraud." Id. at 270. The language "do not bind" is a direct statement regarding the preclusive effect of such findings.

The Defense contends that *Moore* did not address a scenario where the State fully participated in the administrative hearing and chose not to appeal. However, the Court in *Moore* did not base its holding on the State's level of participation or the procedural history of the administrative appeal. Rather, the holding was rooted in the fundamental distinction between civil administrative proceedings and criminal prosecutions. The administrative determination in this case, like in *Moore*, resolved a dispute regarding Medicaid reimbursement rules—a remedial civil action. It did not, and could not, adjudicate Appellant's criminal guilt. Therefore, *Moore* remains controlling precedent that civil administrative findings cannot bind a criminal court.

B. Material Differences in Standards of Proof and Legal Elements

The Defense argues that the administrative proceeding in this case is distinguishable from *State v. Jenkins*, 288 Ga. App. 455, 654 S.E.2d 246 (2007), because the ALJ specifically found "no evidence of fraud," whereas *Jenkins* involved only a determination of overpayment. While we acknowledge this factual distinction, it does not overcome the legal barrier identified in *Jenkins*.

In *Jenkins*, the Court held that an administrative determination regarding Medicaid overpayments does not estop the State from later prosecuting criminal fraud charges because "the issues and standards of proof are materially different." Id. at 458. The Defense correctly notes that the ALJ found "no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation." However, this finding was made based on a preponderance of the evidence standard applicable to civil administrative disputes. The criminal prosecution requires proof that Appellant "knowingly and willfully" defrauded the State beyond a reasonable doubt.

The United States Supreme Court has held that collateral estoppel cannot apply when "the standards of proof and elements of proof differ" between proceedings. United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154, 159–60, 104 S. Ct. 568, 572, 78 L. Ed. 2d 379 (1984). The Defense argues that *Mendoza* permits estoppel where the government has "previously litigated and lost" involving the same parties. Even assuming the "same parties" requirement is met, the differing standards of proof—preponderance of the evidence in the administrative hearing versus beyond a reasonable doubt in the criminal trial—constitute a material difference that precludes the application of collateral estoppel. The ALJ's finding that the evidence was insufficient to meet the civil standard is not equivalent to a finding that the State cannot prove criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt.

C. The State’s Authority to Prosecute and Fundamental Fairness

The Defense relies on *State v. Smith*, 256 Ga. 533, 534, 350 S.E.2d 433 (1986), arguing that the State should be estopped because it fully litigated the issue and lost, rather than the civil proceeding resulting in a "merely" favorable outcome. The Defense posits that allowing the State to prosecute after losing the administrative hearing violates fundamental fairness and constitutes "forum shopping."

While the Defense's fairness arguments are intuitively appealing, they are contrary to established Georgia jurisprudence. *Smith* holds that "the State is not estopped from prosecuting a criminal case merely because a civil proceeding resulted in a finding favorable to the defendant." Id. The Court in *Smith* did not create an exception for cases where the State participated fully in the civil proceeding. The State's sovereign authority to enforce criminal laws is not waived by its participation in a parallel civil administrative process.

Furthermore, the administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution serve distinct societal interests. The administrative process is designed to ensure the fiscal integrity of the Medicaid program and determine provider eligibility. The criminal prosecution is designed to punish wrongdoing and deter criminal conduct. As noted in *Department of Human Resources v. Parris*, 263 Ga. 232, 235, 430 S.E.2d 714 (1993), collateral estoppel against the State is disfavored, particularly where the prior proceeding was civil in nature and the current proceeding is criminal. Permitting collateral estoppel in this context would allow the outcome of a civil administrative hearing, utilizing a lower standard of proof, to dictate the State's ability to enforce its criminal laws.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the administrative decision finding no evidence of civil fraud does not preclude the State from prosecuting Appellant for criminal Medicaid fraud. The proceedings involved different standards of proof, different legal elements, and different purposes. The binding precedent of *State v. Moore*, *State v. Jenkins*, and *State v. Smith* establishes that collateral estoppel cannot be applied to bar this prosecution. Therefore, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement.

**MALLOY v. STATE OF GEORGIA**

**DOCKET NO:** S12A1234 (Example)

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments presented by both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues the following final ruling.**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy appeals the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. Appellant was indicted on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). Prior to the indictment, an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) issued a decision finding no evidence that Appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, resulting in the termination of a payment withhold. The central issue on appeal is whether this administrative finding precludes the State from pursuing criminal charges for the same conduct.

The applicability of collateral estoppel is a question of law subject to de novo review. See Levenstein v. Sapansky, 286 Ga. 202, 203 (686 SE2d 233) (2009).

**II. ANALYSIS**

A. The Binding Nature of Administrative Findings in Criminal Proceedings

The Defense argues that the unique procedural posture of this case—specifically, the State’s active participation in the administrative hearing and its failure to appeal the adverse decision—distinguishes it from the holding of *State v. Moore*, 268 Ga. 269, 486 S.E.2d 818 (1997). We find this distinction unpersuasive.

In *Moore*, the Georgia Supreme Court held that "the administrative proceeding conducted by the Department of Human Resources was a civil matter, and the findings therein do not bind the criminal court in a subsequent prosecution for fraud." Id. at 270. The Defense contends that *Moore* should be read as establishing a general rule with exceptions for cases where the State fully litigates and loses. However, the language "do not bind" is categorical. The Court in *Moore* based its decision on the fundamental distinction between civil administrative proceedings and criminal prosecutions, not on the level of the State's participation or the procedural history of the administrative appeal. The administrative determination in this case, like in *Moore*, resolved a dispute regarding Medicaid reimbursement rules—a remedial civil action. It did not, and could not, adjudicate Appellant's criminal guilt. Therefore, *Moore* remains controlling precedent that civil administrative findings cannot bind a criminal court, regardless of the State's decision to forgo an administrative appeal.

B. Standards of Proof and the "No Evidence" Finding

The Defense places significant weight on the ALJ’s specific finding that there was "no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation," arguing this constitutes a substantive factual determination that transcends the civil standard of proof. While we acknowledge the specificity of the ALJ's language, this finding does not overcome the legal barrier identified in *State v. Jenkins*, 288 Ga. App. 455, 654 S.E.2d 246 (2007).

In *Jenkins*, the Court held that an administrative determination regarding Medicaid overpayments does not estop the State from later prosecuting criminal fraud charges because "the issues and standards of proof are materially different." Id. at 458. The Defense argues that *Jenkins* is distinguishable because it involved an overpayment determination rather than a finding of "no fraud." However, the principle of *Jenkins* applies with equal force here. The ALJ's finding of "no evidence" was a conclusion based on the "preponderance of the evidence" standard applicable to civil administrative disputes. The criminal prosecution requires proof that Appellant "knowingly and willfully" defrauded the State "beyond a reasonable doubt."

The United States Supreme Court has held that collateral estoppel cannot apply when "the standards of proof and elements of proof differ" between proceedings. United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154, 159–60, 104 S. Ct. 568, 572, 78 L. Ed. 2d 379 (1984). The Defense argues that a finding of "no evidence" implies the record is empty, rendering the standard of proof irrelevant. We disagree. The ALJ's finding that the evidence was insufficient to meet the civil standard is not equivalent to a finding that the State cannot prove criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The administrative record was developed for the purpose of determining reimbursement eligibility, not criminal culpability. Furthermore, the facts indicate that the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit continued its investigation, including executing a search warrant, during the administrative process. Thus, the evidence available to the criminal trier of fact may differ from that presented to the ALJ.

C. Fundamental Fairness and Sovereign Authority

The Defense argues that allowing the State to prosecute after losing the administrative hearing violates fundamental fairness and constitutes "forum shopping." While these arguments are intuitively appealing, they are contrary to established Georgia jurisprudence.

*State v. Smith*, 256 Ga. 533, 534, 350 S.E.2d 433 (1986), holds that "the State is not estopped from prosecuting a criminal case merely because a civil proceeding resulted in a finding favorable to the defendant." Id. The Defense argues that the outcome here was more than "merely favorable" because it directly addressed the issue of fraud. However, *Smith* does not limit its holding to cases where the civil proceeding is tangentially related; it addresses the State's sovereign authority to prosecute. The State's right to enforce criminal laws is not waived by its participation in a parallel civil administrative process, nor by its failure to appeal an administrative decision that addressed only civil liability.

As noted in *Department of Human Resources v. Parris*, 263 Ga. 232, 235, 430 S.E.2d 714 (1993), collateral estoppel against the State is disfavored, particularly where the prior proceeding was civil in nature and the current proceeding is criminal. Permitting collateral estoppel in this context would allow the outcome of a civil administrative hearing, utilizing a lower standard of proof and serving a remedial purpose, to dictate the State's ability to enforce its criminal laws. The administrative process ensures the fiscal integrity of the Medicaid program, while the criminal prosecution serves to punish wrongdoing and deter criminal conduct. These are distinct societal interests that permit parallel proceedings.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the administrative decision finding no evidence of civil fraud does not preclude the State from prosecuting Appellant for criminal Medicaid fraud. The proceedings involved different standards of proof, different legal elements, and different purposes. The binding precedent of *State v. Moore*, *State v. Jenkins*, and *State v. Smith* establishes that collateral estoppel cannot be applied to bar this prosecution. The State's participation in the administrative hearing and its choice not to appeal the decision do not constitute a waiver of its sovereign power to prosecute criminal offenses. Therefore, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement.

**The trial court’s order denying the plea in abatement is AFFIRMED.**
