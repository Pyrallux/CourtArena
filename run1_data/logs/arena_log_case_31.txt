FACTS:
Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. Judgment of conviction was entered upon his plea of guilty after the charge against him was reduced from aggravated battery, as origi*380nally alleged. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years in custody of the Board of Correction. He later filed a motion under I.C.R. 35, seeking reduction of the sentence so he could be released for psychotherapy that he claimed was unavailable in confinement. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

 However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

WALTERS, C.J., and SWANSTROM, J., concur.

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying the Rule 35 Motion

The prosecution correctly notes that a motion to reduce a sentence under Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); *State v. Russell*, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct. App. 1985); *State v. Araiza*, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct. App. 1985). However, the existence of discretion does not mean that its exercise can be arbitrary or that this Court cannot review its application. The question before this Court is whether the district court's denial of the Rule 35 motion was based upon a consideration of the proper factors and was not otherwise arbitrary or unreasonable.

**1. The Court Failed to Give Adequate Weight to the Changed Circumstances**

The Defense submits that the district court failed to give adequate consideration to the changed circumstances presented in the Rule 35 proceeding. At the original sentencing, the court had before it the initial psychological evaluation, which painted a pessimistic picture of Galbraith's therapeutic prospects. However, at the Rule 35 hearing, the court heard testimony from a different psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems and who was more optimistic about Galbraith's prospects for successful rehabilitation through therapy.

This Court has recognized that a Rule 35 motion presents a fresh opportunity for the court to consider the defendant's circumstances in light of new evidence or changed conditions. In *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984), this Court noted that a Rule 35 motion is essentially a plea for leniency, and the court has the discretion to grant such leniency when circumstances warrant.

The prosecution argues that the district court properly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation against the risk to society. However, the Defense submits that the court's weighing was flawed because it failed to give due consideration to the testimony of the second psychologist, who specifically recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible.

**2. The Court's Rationale Was Based on an Incorrect Assessment of Risk**

The prosecution argues that the district court properly prioritized societal protection over speculative therapeutic benefits. The Defense submits that this characterization misstates the nature of the evidence presented at the Rule 35 hearing.

The second psychologist testified that therapy was necessary and that the passage of time was a factor bearing on the likelihood of success. While he acknowledged that delaying therapy until parole would not substantially diminish the probability of success, he still recommended immediate therapy. This testimony was not speculative; it was the professional opinion of a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in the type of treatment Galbraith required.

Moreover, the court's assessment of risk failed to distinguish between the risk to Galbraith's child and the risk to the general public. The psychological evaluation specifically noted that Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public. The risk identified by the psychologist was specifically related to his having access to his child. Given that Galbraith's child is not in his custody and is not likely to be in his custody in the foreseeable future, the court's concern about risk to "other defenseless persons" was not adequately supported by the evidence.

**3. The Sentence Is Excessive in Light of the Purpose of Rehabilitation**

The prosecution argues that the sentence is appropriate because it provides for parole eligibility and the possibility of eventual rehabilitation. However, the Defense submits that the sentence, as applied, fails to serve the rehabilitative purpose because the therapy Galbraith needs is unavailable in confinement.

In *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982), this Court recognized that the primary objectives of sentencing are protecting society, deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement is treated as one-third of the facial length for purposes of appellate review.

Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years, which means he would be eligible for parole after approximately twenty months. However, the evidence presented at the Rule 35 hearing established that the therapy Galbraith needs is unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. This means that Galbraith will not receive the treatment he needs during his period of incarceration, and his rehabilitation will be delayed until after he is released on parole.

The Defense submits that a sentence that deprives a defendant of the opportunity for rehabilitation, when such rehabilitation is available and would serve the interests of society, is excessive and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

### B. The Sentence Constitutes Cruel and Unusual Punishment

The prosecution argues that Galbraith's constitutional challenge to the original sentence is untimely because he did not appeal the judgment of conviction. The Defense acknowledges that this Court has held that a defendant may not challenge the original sentence in a Rule 35 proceeding when the time for appeal has passed. *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984); *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983).

However, the Defense submits that the cruel and unusual punishment claim is properly before this Court in connection with the Rule 35 proceeding. The expanded record available at the Rule 35 hearing provides new evidence bearing on whether the sentence, as applied, constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. Specifically, the testimony of the second psychologist establishes that the therapy Galbraith needs is unavailable in confinement, and that the passage of time may affect the success of therapy.

**1. The Sentence Is Disproportionate to the Offense**

The Defense submits that the sentence of five years for aggravated assault is disproportionate to the offense committed, particularly in light of the mitigating circumstances. Galbraith was eighteen years old at the time of the offense, had no prior criminal record, and had been abused as a child. He expressed remorse for his actions and sought therapy.

The prosecution argues that the sentence was appropriate because of the seriousness of the offense. The Defense does not minimize the seriousness of the offense, but submits that the sentence must be proportionate to the offense and the offender. The United States Supreme Court has held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits sentences that are disproportionate to the crime. *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277 (1983).

**2. The Denial of Therapy Constitutes Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

The Defense further submits that the denial of therapy, when such therapy is necessary for rehabilitation and is unavailable in confinement, constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. The purpose of incarceration is not merely punishment but also rehabilitation. When a sentence deprives a defendant of the opportunity for rehabilitation, and when such deprivation is not necessary for the protection of society, the sentence becomes punitive in a manner that violates constitutional protections.

In this case, the evidence establishes that Galbraith is not a danger to the general public. The risk identified by the psychologist is specifically related to his access to his child. Given that Galbraith's child is not in his custody, the continued denial of therapy serves no legitimate penological purpose.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** [Redacted]
**Appeal from the District Court of the State of Idaho.**

**Pursuant to the review of the prosecution and defense opening arguments, and the facts provided, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling:**

The district court’s order denying Defendant-Appellant Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion for sentence reduction is affirmed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). On appellate review, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply not only to the review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). We will not overturn the district court’s decision absent an abuse of that discretion. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Timeliness of Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the state and federal constitutions. However, the record indicates that Galbraith did not appeal the judgment containing the original sentence; he appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. Under established precedent, a constitutional attack relating to the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely if not raised on direct appeal. See State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Therefore, we limit our review to whether the district court abused its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion based on the expanded record available at that time.

**B. Rule 35 Motion and Abuse of Discretion**

Galbraith contends the district court abused its discretion by denying his motion for sentence reduction to facilitate immediate psychotherapy. The defense emphasizes the testimony of a licensed clinical psychologist who recommended immediate placement in psychotherapy, noting that such therapy was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution.

However, the district court’s decision was grounded in a careful weighing of competing risks. The judge explicitly considered the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if he were released early. The district court found the latter risk to be greater. This finding is supported by the record. The presentence report and psychological evaluations indicated that Galbraith had a "poor social conscience" and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize," and posed a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child." The judge reasonably concluded that this risk extended to "other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry."

Crucially, the defense’s own expert witness admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—there would not be a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." This admission significantly undermines the argument that immediate release is strictly necessary for rehabilitation. Given that the indeterminate sentence imposed allows for parole eligibility after approximately twenty months, the denial of the motion does not foreclose the possibility of rehabilitation.

The objectives of sentencing include protecting society, deterrence, and rehabilitation. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The district court determined that the severity of the crime—striking an infant and causing serious injury—warranted a sentence consistent with the objective of protecting society. **STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** [Redacted]
**Appeal from the District Court of the State of Idaho.**

**Pursuant to the review of the rebuttals provided by the prosecution and defense, and the facts provided, this Court issues the following updated ruling:**

The district court’s order denying Defendant-Appellant Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion for sentence reduction is affirmed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). On appellate review, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply not only to the review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). We will not overturn the district court’s decision absent an abuse of that discretion. An abuse of discretion occurs when the district court "failed to consider the proper legal standards" or "based its decision on an erroneous view of the law." State v. Hedger, 115 Idaho 598, 768 P.2d 798 (Ct.App.1989).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Timeliness of Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the state and federal constitutions. The defense contends that because the Rule 35 motion introduced new evidence regarding the unavailability of treatment, the constitutional challenge is timely. We disagree. The record indicates that Galbraith did not appeal the judgment containing the original sentence; he appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. Under established precedent, a constitutional attack relating to the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely if not raised on direct appeal. See State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). The defense’s reliance on Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277 (1983), to argue that the sentence serves no penological purpose is unavailing. The sentence serves the legitimate penological goals of retribution and incapacitation. The fact that rehabilitation may be delayed does not render the sentence unconstitutional, particularly where the sentence imposed is within the statutory range for aggravated assault. Therefore, we limit our review to whether the district court abused its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion based on the expanded record available at that time.

**B. Rule 35 Motion and Abuse of Discretion**

Galbraith contends the district court abused its discretion by denying his motion for sentence reduction to facilitate immediate psychotherapy. The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to the primary goal of rehabilitation established in State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982), and that the court improperly expanded the risk assessment to include "other defenseless persons" without record support.

**1. Weighing of Sentencing Objectives**

While rehabilitation is a primary goal of sentencing, it is not the sole consideration. The district court must balance rehabilitation against the objective of protecting society. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The district court explicitly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if he were released early. The court found the latter risk to be greater.

The defense argues that the district court’s decision effectively sacrifices rehabilitation because the necessary therapy is unavailable in confinement. However, the indeterminate sentence imposed allows for parole eligibility after approximately twenty months. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The defense’s own expert witness admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—there would not be a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." The district court was entitled to rely on this admission to conclude that the marginal benefit of immediate release did not outweigh the risk to public safety. The defense’s distinction between "probability of success" and "necessity of treatment" does not render the court’s balancing of these factors an abuse of discretion.

**2. Risk Assessment**

The defense challenges the district court’s finding that Galbraith posed a risk to "other defenseless persons," arguing the psychological evaluation only identified a risk to his child and that he was "not considered a danger to the general public." We find this argument unpersuasive. The psychologist’s evaluation noted that Galbraith had a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize" and represented a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child." Given the nature of the offense—striking an infant and causing serious injury—it was not an abuse of discretion for the district court to infer that this lack of empathy and poor impulse control might extend to other vulnerable individuals, such as other children or defenseless persons to whom Galbraith might have access. This inference is supported by the record and does not constitute an erroneous factual finding under State v. Hedger, 115 Idaho 598, 768 P.2d 798 (Ct.App.1989).

**3. Availability of Treatment**

The defense argues that the sentence is flawed because the Idaho State Correctional Institution cannot provide the necessary therapy. While this is a relevant factor, it does not compel reduction of the sentence. The district court acknowledged the unavailability of treatment but determined that the severity of the crime and the risk to society warranted incarceration despite this fact. The court’s decision that the risk to society outweighed the therapeutic benefit of immediate release falls within the range of sound discretion. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**III. CONCLUSION**

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** [Redacted]
**Appeal from the District Court of the State of Idaho.**

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments provided by the prosecution and defense, and the facts provided, this Court issues the following final ruling:**

The district court’s order denying Defendant-Appellant Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion for sentence reduction is affirmed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). On appellate review, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). These criteria apply not only to the review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to the review of a judge’s refusal to reduce a sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). We will not overturn the district court’s decision absent an abuse of that discretion. An abuse of discretion occurs when the district court "failed to consider the proper legal standards" or "based its decision on an erroneous view of the law." State v. Hedger, 115 Idaho 598, 768 P.2d 798 (Ct.App.1989).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Timeliness of Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment in violation of the state and federal constitutions. The defense contends that because the Rule 35 motion introduced new evidence regarding the unavailability of treatment, the constitutional challenge is timely. We disagree.

The record indicates that Galbraith did not appeal the judgment containing the original sentence; he appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. Under established precedent, a constitutional attack relating to the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely if not raised on direct appeal. See State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983).

The defense attempts to distinguish these cases by arguing that the "expanded record" regarding the unavailability of psychotherapy renders the constitutional challenge timely. However, the unavailability of a specific form of therapy in confinement is a factor relevant to the *discretionary* reduction of a sentence under Rule 35, not a factor that retroactively invalidates the constitutionality of the sentence as originally imposed. The sentence of five years for aggravated assault is within the statutory range provided by I.C. §§ 18-905 and -906. The fact that rehabilitation may be delayed does not render the sentence unconstitutional, particularly where the sentence serves the legitimate penological goals of retribution and incapacitation. Therefore, we limit our review to whether the district court abused its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion based on the expanded record available at that time.

**B. Rule 35 Motion and Abuse of Discretion**

Galbraith contends the district court abused its discretion by denying his motion for sentence reduction to facilitate immediate psychotherapy. The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to the primary goal of rehabilitation and improperly expanded the risk assessment to include "other defenseless persons" without record support.

**1. Weighing of Sentencing Objectives**

The defense argues that the district court misapplied State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982), by treating rehabilitation as a secondary consideration that could be sacrificed. While rehabilitation is a related goal of sentencing, the protection of society remains the primary objective. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The district court explicitly weighed the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if he were released early. The court found the latter risk to be greater.

The defense asserts that the court effectively ignored rehabilitation because the necessary therapy is unavailable in confinement. However, the indeterminate sentence imposed allows for parole eligibility after approximately twenty months. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). Crucially, the defense’s own expert witness admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—there would not be a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." The district court was entitled to rely on this admission to conclude that the marginal benefit of immediate release did not outweigh the risk to public safety. The court’s consideration of this factor demonstrates that it did not ignore rehabilitation but rather balanced it against the need for public safety.

**2. Risk Assessment**

The defense challenges the district court’s finding that Galbraith posed a risk to "other defenseless persons," arguing the psychological evaluation only identified a risk to his child and that he was "not considered a danger to the general public." We find this argument unpersuasive.

The psychologist’s evaluation noted that Galbraith had a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize" and represented a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child." Given the nature of the offense—striking an infant and causing serious injury—it was not an abuse of discretion for the district court to infer that this lack of empathy and poor impulse control might extend to other vulnerable individuals, such as other children or defenseless persons to whom Galbraith might have access. The psychologist’s statement that Galbraith was not a danger to the "general public" is distinguishable from a risk to "defenseless persons" who may fall under his care or supervision. The court’s inference is supported by the record and does not constitute an erroneous factual finding under State v. Hedger, 115 Idaho 598, 768 P.2d 798 (Ct.App.1989).

**3. Availability of Treatment**

The defense argues that the sentence is flawed because the Idaho State Correctional Institution cannot provide the necessary therapy. While this is a relevant factor, it does not compel reduction of the sentence. The district court acknowledged the unavailability of treatment but determined that the severity of the crime and the risk to society warranted incarceration despite this fact. The court’s decision that the risk to society outweighed the therapeutic benefit of immediate release falls within the range of sound discretion. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. The court properly balanced the sentencing objectives, giving significant weight to the protection of society. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society and allows for rehabilitation through parole and future therapy. Because Galbraith failed to demonstrate that the district court’s refusal to reduce the sentence was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an erroneous view of the law, we conclude there was no abuse of discretion.

**ORDER**

The order of the district court denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.
