COURT ARENA LOG - Case 86
FACTS:
This appeal arises from a class action lawsuit filed by the plaintiff, Hong Pham, individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, against the defendant, Michael P. Starkowski, the commissioner of social services, challenging the constitutionality of Public Acts, Spec. Sess., September, 2009, No. 09-5, §§ 551 and 642 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5), which effectively terminated certain state funded medical assistance for members of the class who are all legal immigrants residing in the state, who are in need of publicly funded *416medical assistance and who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years. The defendant appeals3 from the judgment of the trial court, which granted the plaintiffs request for class certification and concluded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the equal protection clause of the fourteenth amendment to the United States constitution4 because those sections impermissibly discriminate against the class members5 on the basis of their status as legal aliens. The trial court permanently enjoined the defendant from enforcing the challenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5. On appeal, the defendant claims that the trial court incorrectly concluded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminate against the class members on the basis of alienage. Alternatively, the defendant claims that, if §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do discriminate on the basis of alienage, the trial court improperly applied strict scrutiny review in reaching its conclusions because the unique circumstances of this case demonstrate that such discrimination is subject only to a more deferential rational basis standard of review rather than the traditional, more searching strict scrutiny review normally applied to state classifications based on alie-nage. The plaintiff responds that the trial court correctly concluded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the equal protection clause of the fourteenth amendment and claims, as an alternative ground for affirming the judgment of the trial court, that the chai-*417lenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 also violate article first, § 20, of the constitution of Connecticut.6 We reverse the judgment of the trial court.

I

FACTS, LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY

The plaintiff and all class members are legal aliens who claim to be in need of publicly funded, nonemer-gency medical assistance (medical assistance)7 because they are indigent but are ineligible for such assistance through the federal Medicaid program, which bars aliens who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years from participating. Prior to December 1, 2009, the state provided medical assistance to these individuals through the state medical assistance for noncitizens program (SMANC).8 See Public Acts, Spec. Sess., June, 1997, No. 97-2, § 146 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 97-2), codified as amended at General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-257b. In response to budgetary concerns, however, the legislature, in 2009, passed Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, which substantially repealed SMANC and altered the statutory eligibility requirements for the state administered general assistance medical program (SAGA-medical), effectively eliminating all state funded medical assistance for the class members as of December 1, 2009.9 The plaintiff claims that this action by the *418state discriminates against her and the class members on the basis of their status as aliens, in violation of the federal and state constitutions.

The issues presented in this appeal require an examination of a number of state and federal medical assistance programs and a number of state and federal statutory provisions affecting those programs. For this reason, we first set forth an overview of the statutory programs and relevant legislation at issue before turning to the trial court proceedings and our analysis of the specific claims of the parties.

A

Federal Medicaid Program

The federal government created the federal Medicaid program through the enactment of Title XIX of the Social Security Amendments of 1965, Pub. L. No. 89-97, § 121, 79 Stat. 286, 343-52, codified as amended at 42 U.S.C. § 1396 et seq. (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). Federal Medicaid is an optional federal and state cooperative medical assistance program pursuant to which a state that elects to participate receives federal funding to assist it in providing publicly funded medical assistance to certain groups of indigent individuals who meet eligibility criteria and possess at least one of the categorical eligibility characteristics generally required for coverage.10 The phrase “categorical eligibility” generally refers to those who are disabled, blind, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or an individual under twenty-one years of age or sixty-five years of age or older. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 1396a (a) (10) and 1396d (a) (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009); see also 42 C.F.R. § 435.100 *419et seq. (2010). The members of the class all meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid.

Federal law governs the federal Medicaid program and determines the eligibility requirements for that program. See, e.g., K & A Radiologic Technology Services, Inc. v. Commissioner of the Dept. of Health, 189 F.3d 273, 277 (2d Cir. 1999). States that adopt federal Medicaid agree to administer the program in accordance with federal law and regulations in order to receive a substantial reimbursement from the federal government to subsidize the cost of the program. See id.; see also 42 U.S.C. § 1396a (a) (2006). Federal Medicaid provides the states certain options for coverage, and each state is required to submit its own plan that outlines the terms of the state’s participation in the federal Medicaid program. See 42 U.S.C. § 1396a (a) (2006). The federal government must approve that plan before the state may participate. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 1396 and 1396a (b) (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). Although the amount of federal funding varies by state, Connecticut, which, in 1966, authorized the state to participate in the federal Medicaid program; see Public Acts 1965, No. 357, § 1; currently receives a reimbursement from the federal government at a rate of approximately 50 percent of the cost of Connecticut’s federal Medicaid program.11 Cf. 42 U.S.C. § 1396d (b) (2006). The General Assembly has charged the defendant with administering federal Medicaid, in accordance with federal laws and regulations. See General Statutes § 17b-260. Until 1996, federal Medicaid provided assistance to many indigent individuals meeting the categorical eligibility requirements without regard to citizenship status or durational residency requirements.

*420On August 22, 1996, Congress enacted the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (Welfare Reform Act), Pub. L. No. 104-193, 110 Stat. 2106, which, as President William J. Clinton put it, was designed to “end welfare as we know it . . . .” (Internal quotation marks omitted.) P. Kilbom & S. Verhovek, “Clinton’s Welfare Shift Ends Tortuous Journey,” N.Y. Times, August 2, 1996, p. Al. Title IV of the Welfare Reform Act substantially impacted the provision of public welfare assistance to certain immigrants. See generally 8 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq. (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). In enacting the Welfare Reform Act, Congress included several statements regarding national immigration policy indicating that Congress favored self-sufficiency by immigrants, immigrants were applying for and receiving public assistance at greater rates and, through the Welfare Reform Act, Congress intended to discourage immigrants from relying on publicly funded assistance. 8 U.S.C. § 1601 (2006).12

*421Whether an alien is eligible for public assistance depends on a number of factors, including whether the alien entered the country lawfully, the particular legal status under which the alien entered, the date that the alien entered, and the amount of time that the alien has resided in the United States. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1612,1613, 1621, 1622 and 1641 (b) (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). The Welfare Reform Act divides aliens into two groups: qualified and nonqualified aliens. See 8 U.S.C. § 1641 (b) (2006) (defining “qualified alien”). Qualified aliens are generally those aliens lawfully admitted to the United States for permanent residence and those admitted pursuant to certain statutes. 8 U.S.C. § 1641 (b) (2006). Any alien not considered a qualified alien is a nonqualified alien, which includes illegal aliens. See 8 U.S.C. § 1641 (b) (2006). All nonqualified aliens are ineligible for federal public assistance, including federal Medicaid, subject to certain exceptions.13 8 U.S.C. § 1611 (a) and (b) (2006). The class members in the present case are all qualified aliens.

The Welfare Reform Act further distinguishes qualified aliens depending on their length of residency in the United States. Any qualified alien who has resided in the United States for five or more years is eligible for federal public assistance. See 8 U.S.C. § 1613 (a) (2006). Aliens who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years, however, generally are barred from receiving federal public assistance until they have resided in the United States for five years (five year *422rule).14 See 8 U.S.C. § 1613 (a) (2006); but see 8 U.S.C. § 1613 (b) (2006) (providing exceptions to five year rule). Federal law thus requires states to deny federal Medicaid coverage to qualified aliens who are barred from participating by the five year rule.15 Additionally, the Welfare Reform Act similarly authorizes states to deny state funded public assistance to certain qualified aliens. 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622 (a) and 1624 (2006). The class members meet the categorical eligibility requirements for participation in federal Medicaid but are barred from receiving federal Medicaid assistance because they have resided in the United States for fewer than five years.

B

State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens Program (SMANC)

In response to the federal government’s adoption of the five year rule in the Welfare Reform Act, this state, in 1997, created SMANC, a state funded program that afforded medical coverage exclusively to qualified aliens who otherwise were categorically eligible for federal Medicaid but who were barred from participating in federal Medicaid because of the federal five year rule. Spec. Sess. P.A. 97-2, § 146. Essentially, SMANC covered those indigent aliens who otherwise met the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid because they were blind, disabled, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or under the age of twenty-one *423or sixty-five years of age or older, but who lost their federal Medicaid coverage as a result of the federal five year rule. Because SMANC covered only this subgroup of aliens, no citizens and no aliens who had resided in the United States for five or more years could participate in SMANC. See General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-257b. Prior to the adoption of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, SMANC provided coverage similar to that provided by federal Medicaid but was administered and funded exclusively by the state. In response to budget concerns in 2009, the legislature enacted Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, which substantially eliminated SMANC and effectively terminated publicly funded medical assistance for most recipients, including all of the class members in the present case. Following the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, SMANC continues to be funded and administered entirely by the state but currently provides assistance to only those recipients who either have received or applied for nursing facility or home care prior to the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64.16 See General Statutes § 17b-257b. There are no individuals in the class who presently qualify for SMANC.

C

State Administered General Assistance Medical Program (SAGA-medicai)

In addition to federal Medicaid, state law provides for a separate, state medical assistance program, commonly referred to as SAGA-medical, which provides coverage for certain categories of indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements *424for federal Medicaid. See General Statutes § 17b-192 (a). Before the legislature enacted Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, the SAGA-medical statute provided that coverage under that program would be available to “persons ineligible for [federal] Medicaid.” General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-192 (a). Through the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 55, the legislature altered the statutory eligibility requirements for SAGA-medical such that it currently provides coverage to only those indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. General Statutes § 17b-192 (a). Unlike federal Medicaid, SAGA-medical has no citizenship or United States residency requirements. See generally General Statutes § 17b-192. Thus, SAGA-medical provides coverage to citizens and indigent qualified aliens who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements of federal Medicaid.17 Because the class members are categorically eligible for federal *425Medicaid, they are ineligible for coverage under SAGA-medical.

The class members currently are ineligible for any of these publicly funded medical assistance programs. The class members are barred from receiving federal Medicaid because of the five year rule in the Welfare Reform Act. They are ineligible for assistance under SMANC because Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, substantially repealed SMANC, thereby eliminating its coverage for the class members. Furthermore, the class members are ineligible for SAGA-medical because they are categorically eligible for federal Medicaid notwithstanding the federal five year rule, which bars them from participating in federal Medicaid. In the present case, the plaintiff challenges only the latter two eligibility limitations and not the federal five year rule in the Welfare Reform Act.

D

Proceedings in the Trial Court

On December 1, 2009, the plaintiff filed a lawsuit, which subsequently was certified as a class action, seeking to enjoin the defendant from enforcing §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5. The plaintiff claimed that those sections unlawfully discriminate against her and others similarly situated because of their status as aliens, in violation of the equal protection clauses of the federal and state constitutions.

The plaintiff claimed that Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§55 and 64, discriminated against her on the basis of her alienage because similarly situated citizens continued to receive publicly funded medical assistance through the federal Medicaid program, whereas she no longer received such assistance as a result of the implementation of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§55 and 64. The plaintiff further claimed that case law required courts to apply *426strict scrutiny to state classifications based on alienage and that the state could not establish a sufficient justification for the challenged classification. For this reason, the plaintiff sought a declaration that the challenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 were unconstitutional and an injunction preventing their enforcement.

The defendant responded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do not discriminate on the basis of alie-nage. Specifically, the defendant argued that § 64, which substantially eliminated SMANC, an alien-only medical assistance program, did not discriminate against aliens in favor of similarly situated citizens because only aliens, and not citizens, could participate in the program, and, consequently, the state did not provide assistance to any citizens through that program. The defendant also argued that § 55, which amended the statutory eligibility requirements for SAGA-medical in a way that barred participation by the plaintiff, did not discriminate on the basis of alienage because the eligibility requirements for that program are not based on citizenship status and apply equally to citizens and aliens alike. The defendant further contended that, even if it was assumed that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 did discriminate against aliens, a court should review those sections to determine only whether the legislature had a rational basis for enacting those sections rather than use the strict scrutiny analysis traditionally applied to state classifications based on alienage. The defendant, citing recent case law from other jurisdictions, argued that rational basis review was proper because the federal government, under its broad constitutional authority to regulate immigration, had validly authorized any discriminatory treatment by the states in the provision of state funded medical assistance. It is undisputed that, if a court subjected the challenged provisions to rational basis review, they would survive such review, and, conversely, if a court applied strict scrutiny, those provi*427sions would not survive such scrutiny. Thus, resolution of the dispute turned on whether §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminated on the basis of alienage and, if they did, whether a court should subject the purported classification that they create to rational basis review or strict scrutiny.

The case was tried to the court in December, 2009, with the parties offering two joint stipulations of fact describing the relevant characteristics of the class members and the legislative background, as well as extensive oral and written arguments. On December 18, 2009, the trial court issued a memorandum of decision, in which it certified the plaintiffs action as a class action and concluded that the challenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violated the federal constitution. The trial court certified the class to include those legal aliens who (1) have resided in the United States for fewer than five years, (2) otherwise would be eligible for federal Medicaid but for the federal five year rule, and (3) have lost their state funded medical care as a result of the implementation of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§ 55 and 64.18

The trial court then concluded that Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§55 and 64, discriminated against the class members on the basis of their status as aliens because the challenged provisions eliminated state funded medical assistance to the class whereas similarly situated citizens continued to receive publicly funded medical assistance through federal Medicaid. Having concluded that *428§§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminated on the basis of alienage, the trial court further concluded that the federal government’s authorization to the states to discriminate against aliens in determining eligibility for state funded medical assistance could not shield the state’s classification from strict scrutiny review. Upon applying strict scrutiny, the trial court concluded that the state had not established a compelling government interest in drawing the classification between aliens and citizens and that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violated the equal protection clause of the federal constitution. The trial court did not address the plaintiffs claims under the state constitution. Thereafter, the trial court rendered judgment in favor of the plaintiff and permanently enjoined the defendant from enforcing the challenged provisions.19 This appeal by the defendant followed.

Your Honor, this Court is presented with a fundamental question: Did the trial court correctly strike down two state provisions that, on their face, impose no discrimination based on alienage? Consider § 64 first. This section eliminated the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens program, or SMANC. Crucially, *citizens were never eligible for SMANC*. When the state terminated SMANC, it did not deny benefits to aliens while granting them to citizens. It ended a program that only aliens could access. There is no similarly situated citizen who lost coverage. How can there be discrimination based on alienage when citizens were never part of the equation? Now turn to § 55. This section amended the State Administered General Assistance Medical program, or SAGA-medical, to cover only those *not categorically eligible for federal Medicaid*. So too can an alien who lacks federal Medicaid eligibility for the same reasons. The class members here are ineligible for SAGA-medical not because they are aliens, but because they meet the federal categorical eligibility requirements for Medicaid. The trial court’s error was in mischaracterizing the provisions as "alienage-based" discrimination. Strict scrutiny applies only when a law facially singles out aliens for disadvantage. The federal government has long held broad authority over immigration, and it has made clear that states may restrict public assistance for recent immigrants. When a state acts in harmony with that federal framework, it does not trigger strict scrutiny.  

Under rational basis review—which is the only standard that applies here—the provisions easily survive constitutional scrutiny. The legislature had legitimate, rational reasons for these changes: severe budget constraints and a desire to align state programs with federal goals of promoting immigrant self-sufficiency. These are quintessential policy choices within the legislature’s authority.  

Your Honor, this is not a case of discrimination. It is a case of the state responding rationally to federal law and fiscal realities. The trial court’s conclusion that these neutral provisions violate equal protection is legally unsound. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of Position

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court committed reversible error in granting the plaintiff's request for class certification and in concluding that §§ 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution. The defense requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and render judgment in favor of the defendant.

The trial court erred in two fundamental respects: (1) it incorrectly concluded that the challenged provisions discriminate against the class members on the basis of alienage, and (2) even assuming arguendo that discrimination exists, it improperly applied strict scrutiny review when a deferential rational basis standard is the appropriate level of constitutional scrutiny. This defense opening response will address each of these errors in turn, demonstrating that the trial court's judgment must be reversed.

---

## I. The Challenged Provisions Do Not Discriminate on the Basis of Alienage

### A. Legal Standard for Alienage Discrimination

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment provides that no state shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const., amend. XIV, § 1. To establish an equal protection violation based on alienage, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the state has discriminated against a particular group on account of their status as aliens. See Graham v. Richardson, 403 U.S. 365, 371-72 (1971). Not every statutory classification that incidentally affects aliens constitutes discrimination based on alienage. Rather, the classification must "rest[] on the fact of noncitizenship." Id. at 371.

As the United States Supreme Court has consistently held, "the central distinction between lawful discrimination involving alienage and that which is forbidden is that the former reflects a legitimate legislative concern that the State not be required to share its resources with persons who, by virtue of their alienage, may be viewed as owing their primary allegiance to a foreign sovereign." Cabell v. Chavez-Salomon, 474 U.S. 200, 208 (1986). A law does not discriminate on the basis of alienage merely because it affects some aliens differently than some citizens; rather, the law must be designed to treat aliens worse than citizens who are similarly situated.

### B. Analysis of § 64: Elimination of SMANC

The trial court erred in concluding that § 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminates on the basis of alienage. This conclusion fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the SMANC program and the effect of its modification.

SMANC was created in 1997 in direct response to the federal Welfare Reform Act's five-year rule. See Spec. Sess. P.A. 97-2, § 146. The program was designed exclusively for qualified aliens who otherwise met the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid but who were barred from receiving federal Medicaid benefits because they had resided in the United States for fewer than five years. Critically, **no citizen was ever eligible for SMANC**. The program was, by its very design, an alien-only program.

The defense submits that when a state terminates a program that provided benefits exclusively to aliens—and to no citizens—the termination does not constitute discrimination based on alienage. The principle underlying this conclusion is straightforward: equal protection requires that similarly situated individuals be treated equally, but it does not require that the state maintain a program that benefits only one group. See Railway Express Agency v. New York, 336 U.S. 106, 109-10 (1949) (holding that the Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit all inequalities, only inequalities without rational basis).

The plaintiff cannot identify a single citizen who lost benefits as a result of § 64 because no citizen ever received benefits through SMANC. The trial court's reasoning—that the termination of SMANC discriminates against aliens because citizens continue to receive medical assistance through federal Medicaid—flaws the analytical error of comparing apples to oranges. Citizens who received federal Medicaid were never eligible for SMANC, and aliens who received SMANC were not similarly situated to citizens receiving federal Medicaid. The appropriate comparison is between aliens who received SMANC and aliens who did not receive SMANC, or between citizens who received federal Medicaid and citizens who did not receive federal Medicaid. Under either comparison, the termination of SMANC does not create an inequality based on alienage.

The case of In re Griffiths, 413 U.S. 717 (1973), is instructive. In Griffiths, the Supreme Court struck down a state law that barred aliens from obtaining licenses to practice law. The Court emphasized that the law discriminated on its face by explicitly barring noncitizens from a profession. Here, by contrast, § 64 does not mention aliens, citizenship, or immigration status. It simply modifies the scope of a state-funded program that happened to benefit only aliens. This distinction is constitutionally significant.

The defense further notes that the termination of SMANC was not an act of discrimination but rather a rational response to budgetary constraints. The legislature found that the state could no longer afford to provide state-funded medical assistance to this population. This is a legitimate governmental interest that supports the classification.

### C. Analysis of § 55: Modification of SAGA-Medical Eligibility

The trial court similarly erred in concluding that § 55 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminates on the basis of alienage. The text of the amended SAGA-medical statute demonstrates that the eligibility requirements are facially neutral and apply equally to citizens and aliens alike.

Prior to the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, the SAGA-medical statute provided coverage to "persons ineligible for [federal] Medicaid." General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-192 (a). Through § 55, the legislature altered this provision such that SAGA-medical now provides coverage only to those indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. General Statutes § 17b-192 (a). This modification does not reference citizenship, alienage, or immigration status. It applies equally to all individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid.

The class members are ineligible for SAGA-medical not because they are aliens, but because they meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. A citizen who is blind, disabled, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or under the age of twenty-one or sixty-five years of age or older is categorically eligible for federal Medicaid and therefore would also be ineligible for SAGA-medical under the amended statute. The same objective criteria apply to citizens and aliens alike.

The plaintiff attempts to circumvent this facially neutral language by arguing that the practical effect of § 55 is to discriminate against aliens because aliens who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years are disproportionately affected. However, the Equal Protection Clause does not condemn statutes merely because they produce disparate impacts. See Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976). To establish an equal protection violation, the plaintiff must demonstrate discriminatory intent, not merely discriminatory effect. See Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp., 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977). The plaintiff has not alleged, let alone proven, that the legislature enacted § 55 with a discriminatory intent toward aliens.

The case of Dandridge v. Jefferson County, 417 U.S. 527 (1974), is instructive. In Dandridge, the Supreme Court upheld a state welfare scheme that provided maximum grant amounts regardless of family size, resulting in larger families receiving less per capita assistance than smaller families. The Court applied rational basis review and rejected the argument that the scheme violated equal protection merely because it produced disparate impacts. Similarly, here, § 55 produces a disparate impact on certain aliens, but this does not establish an equal protection violation.

The defense submits that the trial court erred in relying on the disparate impact on aliens to find discrimination based on alienage. The correct analysis focuses on whether the statute on its face discriminates based on alienage or was enacted with discriminatory intent. Neither requirement is met here.

---

## II. Even If Discrimination Exists, Rational Basis Review Applies

### A. The Federal Authorization Doctrine

Assuming arguendo that §§ 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do discriminate on the basis of alienage, the trial court erred in applying strict scrutiny review. The defense submits that rational basis review is the appropriate standard because the federal government has authorized the discriminatory treatment at issue.

The federal Welfare Reform Act expressly authorizes states to deny state-funded public assistance to certain qualified aliens. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622(a) and 1624 (2006). Specifically, Congress provided that a state "may deny" benefits to qualified aliens who are otherwise subject to the five-year rule. 8 U.S.C. § 1622(a)(1). This authorization reflects Congress's determination that states should have flexibility in determining eligibility for state-funded programs affecting aliens.

The United States Supreme Court has recognized that when the federal government authorizes certain discriminatory treatment by the states, the states may act in conformity with that authorization without triggering strict scrutiny. In the context of alienage classifications, the Court has distinguished between classifications that reflect "a legitimate legislative concern that the State not be required to share its resources with persons who, by virtue of their alienage, may be viewed as owing their primary allegiance to a foreign sovereign," Cabell v. Chavez-Salomon, 474 U.S. at 208, and classifications that are not similarly justified.

The defense submits that when a state acts pursuant to explicit federal authorization to restrict benefits to aliens, the state's classification reflects the federal policy judgment rather than an independent state discrimination. In such circumstances, the rational basis standard applies because the state's action is not truly discriminatory in the constitutional sense; rather, it is implementing federal policy.

### B. Federal Immigration Policy Preempts State Autonomy in This Area

The regulation of immigration is a fundamental federal function. See Arizona v. United States, 567 U.S. 387, 392 (2012) ("The federal Government has broad constitutional power to determine national policy and control its relations with foreign nationals."). Congress enacted the Welfare Reform Act to further the policy of immigrant self-sufficiency and to discourage reliance on public benefits. See 8 U.S.C. § 1601 (2006). The state's decision to align its programs with this federal policy is a rational exercise of its authority to administer programs that complement federal initiatives.

The case of Mathews v. Diaz, 426 U.S. 67 (1976), is instructive. In Mathews, the Supreme Court upheld a federal statute that denied Medicare benefits to aliens who had not resided in the United States for a specified period. The Court recognized that Congress has broad authority to distinguish between citizens and aliens in the distribution of federal benefits, and that such distinctions are subject to rational basis review. The same principle applies to state distinctions that implement federal policy.

The defense submits that the state's implementation of the federal five-year rule through §§ 55 and 64 is analogous to the federal classification upheld in Mathews. The state is not acting with animus toward aliens; it is implementing a policy that Congress explicitly authorized and that reflects a legitimate governmental interest in conserving scarce resources and promoting immigrant self-sufficiency.

### C. Application of Rational Basis Review

Under rational basis review, the challenged provisions must be upheld if they are rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest. See FCC v. The legislature may be aware that a facially neutral classification will have disproportionate effects on certain groups without thereby engaging in unconstitutional discrimination.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Dandridge v. Jefferson County*, 417 U.S. 527 (1974), on the ground that the classification there was based on family size rather than alienage. This distinction is unavailing. The principle underlying *Dandridge*—that disparate impact alone does not violate equal protection—applies regardless of the characteristic that produces the disparate impact. The plaintiff cannot escape the holding of *Dandridge* by simply identifying the characteristic that produces the disparate impact.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO REBUT THE DEFENSE'S DEMONSTRATION THAT RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW APPLIES

### A. The Plaintiff Misreads Mathews v. Diaz

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67 (1976), on the ground that it involved federal rather than state action. This reading of *Mathews* is incorrect and contradicts the Supreme Court's subsequent interpretation of the case.

In *Mathews v. Diaz*, the Supreme Court upheld a federal statute denying Medicare benefits to aliens unless they had resided in the United States for a specified period. The Court held that "the federal government has broad constitutional authority over immigration" and that Congress may "distinguish between aliens and citizens" in the provision of benefits. The Court applied rational basis review, concluding that Congress could rationally determine that "the justification for the requirement must be found in the congressional judgment" about the appropriate conditions for participation in the program.

The principle underlying *Mathews v. Diaz* extends beyond the federal context. When the federal government explicitly authorizes states to take certain action, the state's implementation of that federal policy does not trigger the same scrutiny as independent state discrimination. The Welfare Reform Act expressly authorizes states to "deny" state-funded benefits to qualified aliens subject to the five-year rule. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622(a), 1624 (2006). This explicit authorization distinguishes the present case from cases involving unguided state discrimination against aliens.

### B. The Plaintiff Misunderstands the Preemptive Effect of Federal Authorization

The plaintiff argues that federal authorization does not "preempt" state autonomy and that the state remains free to discriminate or not discriminate as it sees fit. This argument misunderstands the nature of the defense's position.

The defense does not argue that federal law preempts the state's authority to enact the challenged provisions. Rather, the defense argues that when the federal government explicitly authorizes states to deny benefits to qualified aliens, the state's exercise of that authorization is not the kind of "discrimination" that triggers strict scrutiny under the Equal Protection Clause. The distinction is subtle but important: strict scrutiny applies to state classifications based on alienage when the state acts on its own authority, without federal guidance. When the state acts pursuant to explicit federal authorization, the classification reflects federal policy judgments rather than independent state discrimination, and rational basis review applies.

The plaintiff argues that the state's actions are "more discriminatory" than what federal law requires, suggesting that the state went beyond what was authorized. This argument is without merit. The Welfare Reform Act explicitly authorizes states to deny benefits to qualified aliens subject to the five-year rule. The state's decision to exercise this authorization is not "more discriminatory" than what federal law permits; it is simply an implementation of the federal authorization. The plaintiff has not identified any provision of federal law that prohibits the state from denying benefits to this population.

### C. The Plaintiff Cannot Overcome the Clear Statement of Federal Authorization

The Welfare Reform Act contains two provisions directly authorizing state discrimination against qualified aliens in the provision of state-funded benefits. Section 1622(a) provides that a "state may provide that an alien who is a qualified alien is not eligible for any [state-funded] public benefit" for the five-year period following entry. Section 1624 provides that nothing in the federal public benefits title "shall be construed to make an alien ineligible for any [state-funded] public benefit" unless the state "expressly provides" for such ineligibility.

These provisions unambiguously authorize the states to deny state-funded benefits to qualified aliens subject to the five-year rule. The state's exercise of this authorization is not an independent act of discrimination subject to strict scrutiny; it is an implementation of federal policy subject to rational basis review. The plaintiff cannot overcome this clear statement of federal authorization by invoking general principles of equal protection.

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO REBUT THE DEFENSE'S DEMONSTRATION THAT THE PROVISIONS SATISFY RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW

### A. Budgetary Concerns Are Legitimate Governmental Interests

The plaintiff attempts to dismiss the state's budgetary concerns as insufficient to justify the challenged provisions. This argument contradicts established precedent.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that a state may make rational decisions about the allocation of its finite resources. In *Jefferson v. Hackney*, 406 U.S. 535 (1972), the Court upheld a Texas welfare scheme that distinguished between categories of recipients, noting that "the Constitution does not require states to equalize the conditions of all welfare recipients." The Court recognized that states have "broad discretion" in allocating public benefits and that legislative judgments about the distribution of scarce resources are entitled to deference.

Connecticut faced severe budgetary constraints in 2009. The legislature rationally determined that eliminating state-funded medical assistance for a population that was already ineligible for federal Medicaid was a reasonable response to these constraints. The plaintiff may disagree with this policy judgment, but the Constitution does not permit courts to second-guess legislative judgments about resource allocation when they are rationally related to legitimate governmental interests.

### B. Alignment with Federal Immigration Policy Is a Legitimate Interest

The plaintiff argues that the state's alignment with federal immigration policy cannot justify discrimination. This argument is incorrect. The federal government has broad authority over immigration and immigration-related benefits. See *Arizona v. United States*, 567 U.S. 387, 392 (2012). When the federal government explicitly authorizes states to align their benefit programs with federal immigration policy, the state's decision to do so is a rational exercise of its authority.

The Welfare Reform Act was enacted to "promote immigrant self-sufficiency" and "discourage immigrants from relying on publicly funded assistance." 8 U.S.C. § 1601 (2006). The state's decision to terminate SMANC and modify SAGA-medical eligibility advances these federal objectives. The plaintiff may believe that the state should not align its programs with federal immigration policy, but this belief does not render the state's actions irrational or unconstitutional.

### C. The Plaintiff Cannot Negate Every Conceivable Basis for the Legislation

Under rational basis review, the plaintiff bears the burden of negating "every conceivable basis" that might support the legislation. See *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). The plaintiff has failed to carry this burden.

The defendant has identified multiple legitimate interests served by the challenged provisions: addressing budgetary constraints, aligning state programs with federal immigration policy, avoiding duplication of benefits, and prioritizing resources for citizens and aliens who are eligible for federal programs. The plaintiff has not addressed, let alone negated, each of these conceivable bases. Her argument focuses on her preferred policy alternatives, not on the constitutional standard that governs this Court's review.

## V. CONCLUSION

The plaintiff has failed to rebut the defendant's demonstration that the trial court's judgment should be reversed. Neither § 55 nor § 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminates on the basis of alienage within the meaning of the Equal Protection Clause. Section 64 terminated a program that was exclusively available to aliens and from which no citizen was ever eligible. Section 55 amended eligibility requirements for SAGA-medical in a facially neutral manner that applies equally to citizens and aliens. Even if any discrimination exists, rational basis review applies because the state acted pursuant to explicit federal authorization under the Welfare Reform Act. Under rational basis review, the provisions are constitutional because they are rationally related to legitimate governmental interests, including budgetary constraints and alignment with federal immigration policy.

The trial court committed reversible error by applying strict scrutiny to the challenged provisions. This Court should reverse the trial court's judgment and render judgment in favor of the defendant.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

This appeal presents a question of first impression regarding the proper application of equal protection principles when a state, acting pursuant to explicit federal authorization, modifies its eligibility criteria for state-funded medical assistance programs. The plaintiff-appellee, Hong Pham, individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, challenges Sections 55 and 64 of Special Session Public Act 09-5 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5), which effectively terminated certain state-funded medical assistance for legal immigrants who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years.

The trial court erred in granting the plaintiff's request for a permanent injunction. The challenged provisions do not discriminate on the basis of alienage in violation of the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution. Even assuming *arguendo* that discrimination exists, the proper standard of review is rational basis review, not strict scrutiny, because the state acted pursuant to explicit federal authorization under the Welfare Reform Act. Under either standard of review, the provisions are constitutional.

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING DISCRIMINATION BASED ON ALIENAGE

### A. Section 64 Terminated an Alien-Only Program—It Did Not Discriminate Against Aliens Relative to Citizens

The trial court's conclusion that Section 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminates against aliens rests upon a fundamental analytical error. The court compared aliens who lost benefits under the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens (SMANC) program to citizens who continue to receive federal Medicaid. This comparison is legally impermissible because it conflates two entirely distinct programs serving different populations.

SMANC was created in 1997 *exclusively* for qualified aliens who were barred from federal Medicaid by the federal five-year rule. As explicitly provided in General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) Section 17b-257b, SMANC covered only those indigent aliens who met the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid but lost their federal coverage due to the durational residency requirement. Critically, no citizen was ever eligible for SMANC because citizens meeting categorical eligibility requirements received federal Medicaid directly—not state-funded assistance through SMANC.

As the Supreme Court held in *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 109-10 (1949), the Equal Protection Clause does not require a state to maintain a benefit program for one group simply because another group receives a different benefit. The Constitution does not compel the state to preserve an alien-only benefit to avoid differential treatment. When a program serves only one group—aliens—and no citizens were ever eligible for that program, terminating that program cannot constitute discrimination against aliens *in relation to citizens*. The groups were never similarly situated with respect to SMANC, and the trial court's comparison was analytically unsound.

### B. Section 55 Is Facially Neutral—Its Disparate Impact Does Not Establish Discrimination

Section 55 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 amended the State Administered General Assistance Medical (SAGA-medical) program to cover only individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. This eligibility standard applies equally to all individuals regardless of citizenship status. A citizen who is blind, disabled, pregnant, or elderly—like an alien in the same categorical position—is equally ineligible for SAGA-medical if they meet federal Medicaid's categorical criteria.

The plaintiff's ineligibility stems not from her alienage, but from federal law's categorical eligibility rules, which bar all individuals (citizen or alien) from SAGA-medical if they qualify for federal Medicaid. The Supreme Court's teaching in *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976), is unambiguous: "The constitutional inquiry is not into the abstract 'effect' of the law, but into the intent behind the action." Disparate impact alone cannot establish an Equal Protection violation.

The plaintiff has presented no evidence of discriminatory intent. The legislature's motivation in enacting Section 55 was budgetary—addressing severe fiscal constraints—not animus toward aliens. As the Court held in *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977), awareness of disparate impact is insufficient to prove unconstitutional discrimination. Section 55's eligibility criteria are citizenship-neutral on their face, and the plaintiff has failed to meet her burden of proving discriminatory intent.

## III. RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW—NOT STRICT SCRUTINY—IS THE APPLICABLE STANDARD

The trial court applied strict scrutiny to the challenged provisions, concluding that they discriminated on the basis of alienage. This was reversible error. Even assuming *arguendo* that the provisions discriminate based on alienage, rational basis review—rather than strict scrutiny—is the proper standard of review because the state acted pursuant to explicit federal authorization.

### A. Federal Law Explicitly Authorizes the State's Actions

The Welfare Reform Act of 1996, codified at 8 U.S.C. Sections 1601 et seq., explicitly addresses the authority of states to deny state-funded benefits to qualified aliens. Specifically, 8 U.S.C. Section 1622(a) provides that states may deny state-funded public assistance to qualified aliens who have not resided in the United States for five years or more. Additionally, 8 U.S.C. Section 1624 grants states broad discretion to deny benefits to aliens.

Congress included explicit findings in the Welfare Reform Act indicating that the legislation was designed to "promote immigrant self-sufficiency" and "discourage immigrants from relying on publicly funded assistance." 8 U.S.C. Section 1601 (2006). The legislative history demonstrates that Congress intended to grant states significant latitude in determining eligibility for state-funded benefits.

### B. The Standard of Review Applicable When States Act Pursuant to Federal Authorization

As the Supreme Court held in *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67 (1976), Congress has "broad constitutional authority over immigration" and may "authorize states to distinguish between aliens and citizens" in the provision of benefits. When Congress has explicitly authorized states to take certain actions with respect to alien classifications, such classifications do not trigger strict scrutiny. Instead, they are subject to the more deferential rational basis review.

The principle underlying *Mathews v. Diaz* is that when a state implements federal policy that has been explicitly authorized by Congress, its actions reflect federal policy judgments rather than independent state discrimination. Strict scrutiny applies only when a state acts on its own, without federal guidance, to discriminate against aliens. Here, the state's choices are squarely within the bounds of federal authorization under the Welfare Reform Act.

## IV. THE CHALLENGED PROVISIONS SURVIVE RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW

Under rational basis review, a statute need only be "rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest." *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). The state's reasons for enacting Sections 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 are both legitimate and rational, and the provisions easily satisfy this deferential standard.

### A. Legitimate Governmental Interests

**Budgetary Constraints**: Connecticut faced severe fiscal pressures in 2009. The legislature reasonably determined that the state could no longer afford to provide state-funded medical assistance to individuals who were already barred from federal Medicaid by federal law. As this Court recognized in *Jefferson v. Hackney*, 406 U.S. 535 (1972), states have "broad discretion" to allocate finite resources, and legislative decisions about "the distribution of scarce resources are entitled to deference."

**Alignment with Federal Immigration Policy**: The Welfare Reform Act explicitly articulates Congress's intent to "promote immigrant self-sufficiency" and "discourage immigrants from relying on publicly funded assistance." 8 U.S.C. Section 1601 (2006). By terminating SMANC and refining SAGA-medical eligibility, Connecticut advanced these legitimate federal policy goals. As the Court held in *Dandridge v. Jefferson County*, 417 U.S. 527 (1974), "the Constitution does not require states to equalize the conditions of all welfare recipients."

### B. Rational Relationship Between the Provisions and Legitimate Interests

The legislature's decision to prioritize state funds for individuals who are eligible for federal programs—rather than those already excluded by federal law—is a rational response to budgetary crises. The provisions directly advance the legitimate interest in conserving finite state resources while aligning state policy with federal immigration objectives.

The plaintiff has not—and cannot—negate "every conceivable basis" supporting these provisions. *FCC v. Beach Communications*, 508 U.S. at 313. Under rational basis review, this Court must uphold the law if any reasonable justification exists, and here, multiple rational justifications do.

---

## V. RESPONSE TO THE PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff argues that the challenged provisions create an impermissible classification based on alienage. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the plaintiff confuses the source of the classification. The five-year rule that bars the plaintiff from federal Medicaid is a federal classification, not a state one. The state's termination of state-funded programs for individuals already excluded from federal programs does not create a new classification; it merely declines to provide a state substitute for a federal benefit that the federal government has determined these individuals should not receive.

Second, the plaintiff incorrectly assumes that the Equal Protection Clause requires the state to provide benefits to all individuals who are similarly situated with respect to the underlying need. This is not the law. As the Supreme Court held in *Dandridge v. Jefferson County*, 417 U.S. 527 (1974), the Constitution does not require states to equalize the conditions of all welfare recipients. The state may make rational distinctions in allocating its finite resources.

Third, the plaintiff's reliance on strict scrutiny is misplaced. Because the state acted pursuant to explicit federal authorization, rational basis review applies. Under this standard, the plaintiff's policy disagreements with the legislature's choices do not constitute constitutional defects.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and render judgment in favor of the defendant.

The trial court's judgment was legally unsound. Sections 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do not facially discriminate based on alienage. Section 64 terminated an alien-only program with no citizen counterpart, and Section 55 applied neutral eligibility criteria that affected citizens and aliens equally. Federal law explicitly authorized these actions, so rational basis review—not strict scrutiny—applies. And under that deferential standard, the provisions are plainly constitutional.

The Equal Protection Clause does not require the state to maintain benefits for a group it never extended to citizens, nor does it condemn facially neutral laws that incidentally affect aliens due to federal rules. To uphold the trial court's ruling would disrupt the delicate balance between state and federal authority and impose judicial second-guessing of rational legislative choices.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the judgment of the trial court, which granted the plaintiff's request for a permanent injunction and held that Public Acts, Spec. Sess., September, 2009, No. 09-5, §§ 55 and 64 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5) violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution. The defendant, Michael P. Starkowski, Commissioner of Social Services, appeals the trial court's judgment. Having reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the defendant, the Court finds that the trial court committed reversible error in its application of the Equal Protection Clause and its selection of the appropriate standard of review.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Challenged Provisions Do Not Discriminate on the Basis of Alienage**

The trial court concluded that §§ 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminate against the class members on the basis of alienage. The Court disagrees with this conclusion.

**1. Section 64 and the SMANC Program**

The trial court found discrimination by comparing aliens who lost benefits under the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens (SMANC) program to citizens who continued to receive benefits through the federal Medicaid program. This comparison is legally flawed. SMANC was created exclusively for qualified aliens who were barred from federal Medicaid due to the federal five-year residency rule. No citizen was ever eligible for SMANC because citizens meeting the categorical eligibility requirements received federal Medicaid directly.

As established in *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 109-10 (1949), the Equal Protection Clause does not require a state to maintain a benefit program for one group simply because another group receives a different benefit. Because citizens were never eligible for SMANC, the termination of that program cannot constitute discrimination against aliens relative to citizens. The groups were not similarly situated with respect to the state program at issue. The trial court’s conflation of the federal Medicaid program with the state-funded SMANC program was error.

**2. Section 55 and the SAGA-Medical Program**

Section 55 amended the eligibility requirements for the State Administered General Assistance Medical (SAGA-medical) program to cover only individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. This eligibility standard is facially neutral; it applies equally to citizens and aliens. A citizen who meets the federal Medicaid categorical requirements is just as ineligible for SAGA-medical as an alien who meets those requirements.

The plaintiff’s ineligibility stems from the federal five-year rule, not from the text or intent of Section 55. The Supreme Court has held that disparate impact alone, absent proof of discriminatory intent, does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976). Furthermore, awareness of disparate impact is insufficient to prove unconstitutional discrimination. *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977). The plaintiff has presented no evidence that the legislature enacted Section 55 with a discriminatory purpose. Therefore, the trial court erred in finding that Section 55 discriminates on the basis of alienage.

**B. Rational Basis Review Applies**

Even assuming, *arguendo*, that the challenged provisions discriminate on the basis of alienage, the trial court erred by applying strict scrutiny. The proper standard of review is rational basis.

The Welfare Reform Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622(a) and 1624, explicitly authorizes states to deny state-funded public assistance to qualified aliens who have not resided in the United States for five years. When Congress exercises its broad constitutional authority over immigration and explicitly authorizes states to distinguish between aliens and citizens in the provision of benefits, such classifications are not subject to strict scrutiny. *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67, 89-90 (1976). Instead, they are subject to the more deferential rational basis review. The trial court’s failure to accord deference to the federal authorization in this case was reversible error.

**C. The Provisions Survive Rational Basis Review**

Under rational basis review, a statute need only be rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). The state has identified several legitimate interests, including addressing severe budgetary constraints and aligning state policy with federal immigration policy goals of promoting self-sufficiency.

States have broad discretion to allocate finite resources, and legislative decisions regarding the distribution of scarce resources are entitled to deference. *Jefferson v. Hackney*, 406 U.S. 535, 544 (1972). Furthermore, the Constitution does not require states to equalize the conditions of all welfare recipients. *Dandridge v. Jefferson County*, 417 U.S. 527, 531 (1974). The legislature’s decision to prioritize state funds for individuals eligible for federal programs, rather than those excluded by federal law, is a rational response to budgetary limitations and a valid exercise of legislative judgment.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court’s judgment was legally unsound. Sections 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do not facially discriminate based on alienage, and even if they did, the provisions are subject to rational basis review pursuant to federal authorization. The provisions easily survive this deferential standard. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and judgment is rendered in favor of the defendant.
