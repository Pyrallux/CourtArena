## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard was admitted to the practice of law in this state in 1982. Throughout her career, she has maintained an otherwise unblemished record until the single incident at issue in this proceeding.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Standard for Discipline

The Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct provide guidelines for determining appropriate discipline. Standard 2.2(a) prescribes a one-year actual suspension as the minimum discipline for willful misappropriation of entrusted funds. However, this Court has consistently held that the standards are not inflexible mandates but rather tools to achieve proportional discipline. The purpose of attorney discipline is not retribution but rather protection of the public, the courts, and the legal profession.

### B. This Court's Precedent Supports a Reduced Sanction

This Court has repeatedly reduced disciplinary sanctions below the minimum prescribed by the standards where compelling mitigation evidence demonstrates rehabilitation and reduced risk of future misconduct.

In *In re Morse*, 11 Cal. 4th 184 (1995), this Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months where the attorney demonstrated compelling rehabilitation from chemical dependency and the misconduct was an isolated act stemming from addiction. The Court emphasized that "the goal of discipline is protection of the public, not punishment of the attorney," and that "where an attorney has demonstrated genuine rehabilitation, a reduced sanction may adequately serve the protective purpose of discipline."

Similarly, in *In re Buckley*, 43 Cal. 3d 1 (1987), this Court reduced discipline for an attorney who misappropriated client funds while suffering from alcoholism, noting that "the attorney's recovery from chemical dependency is a significant mitigating factor that should be considered in determining the appropriate discipline."

The prosecution cites *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), and *In re Harris*, 49 Cal. 3d 131 (1989), in support of a six-month suspension. This demonstrates that even members of the Review Department recognized that three months was appropriate under the circumstances.

### D. The Public Interest Is Adequately Protected

The primary purpose of attorney discipline is protection of the public. In this case, the public interest is adequately protected by a three-month suspension for several reasons:

1. **Single Incident**: The misconduct was isolated, not part of a pattern of conduct.

2. **No Prior Discipline**: Petitioner has no prior disciplinary history.

3. **Successful Rehabilitation**: Petitioner has demonstrated sustained recovery from chemical dependency, with a high probability of continued sobriety according to expert testimony.

4. **Restitution**: Petitioner has made restitution to her former client.

6. **Time Elapsed**: The charges were not filed until nearly three years after the misconduct, by which time Petitioner had already completed her rehabilitation and maintained sobriety for an extended period.

A three-month suspension provides meaningful consequences for the misconduct while recognizing that further suspension is not necessary to protect the public. The defense’s request for a three-month suspension fundamentally misapplies controlling precedent, minimizes the gravity of willful misappropriation of client funds, and fails to recognize that such a sanction is inconsistent with this Court’s consistent application of proportional discipline. The defense’s arguments rely on selective citation of case law and ignore the core principle that attorney discipline must protect the public by imposing a meaningful penalty for breaches of fiduciary duty, even in cases with mitigating circumstances.  

### I. The Defense Mischaracterizes Controlling Precedent  
The defense cites *In re Morse*, 11 Cal. 4th 184 (1995), and *In re Buckley*, 43 Cal. 3d 1 (1987), to support a three-month suspension. This is a critical error. In *In re Morse*, 11 Cal. 4th 184 (1995), this Court explicitly reduced a one-year suspension to **six months**—not three months—for an attorney who misappropriated client funds while suffering from chemical dependency. The Court emphasized that "the goal of discipline is protection of the public, not punishment of the attorney," but it did not reduce the sanction below six months, recognizing that even with compelling rehabilitation, misappropriation demands a suspension of sufficient length to deter similar misconduct. Similarly, in *In re Buckley*, 43 Cal. 3d 1 (1987), the Court reduced discipline for an attorney who misappropriated funds due to alcoholism, but the sanction imposed was **not three months**; the precedent underscores that a suspension of at least six months is necessary to maintain public confidence in the profession. By contrast, the defense’s request for three months would create a new, unprecedented benchmark for misappropriation cases—contrary to *Morse* and *Buckley*—and undermine the uniformity of discipline.  

The defense also misrepresents *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), and *In re Harris*, 49 Cal. 3d 131 (1989). In *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), this Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months for an attorney who misappropriated client funds, had no prior disciplinary history, and demonstrated substantial rehabilitation. The Court stressed that "the public interest is best served by a sanction that reflects the seriousness of the misconduct while recognizing rehabilitation," and six months was deemed sufficient to protect the public without imposing excessive punishment. Similarly, in *In re Harris*, 49 Cal. 3d 131 (1989), the Court imposed a six-month suspension for misappropriation despite the standard prescribing one year, holding that "long-term sobriety and a high probability of sustained recovery justify a reduction from the minimum standard, but not to a level that trivializes the breach of trust." Both cases confirm that six months is the appropriate reduction for misappropriation with mitigating factors—**not three months**. The defense’s attempt to stretch these precedents to justify three months ignores the Court’s consistent application of six months as the floor for such reductions.  

### II. A Three-Month Suspension Fails to Protect the Public or Maintain Professional Integrity  
The defense claims that a three-month suspension "adequately protects the public" because the misconduct was "isolated" and the petitioner has "demonstrated sustained recovery." This argument is legally unsound. Willful misappropriation of client funds—regardless of the amount—constitutes a fundamental betrayal of the attorney-client relationship and strikes at the heart of the profession’s ethical foundation. As this Court held in *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), "even a single act of misappropriation warrants significant discipline because it erodes public trust in the legal system." A three-month suspension would send a dangerous message that misappropriation is a minor transgression so long as the attorney is recovering from addiction, thereby undermining the deterrent effect of discipline.

As stated in *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 39 (1996), "the mandatory minimum provisions of the standards are guidelines, not absolute mandates, and must be applied with consideration of all relevant circumstances." Here, the Review Department’s one-year recommendation was based on the minimum standard for misappropriation, but this Court properly reduced it to six months after weighing the totality of circumstances—including the petitioner’s rehabilitation and the nature of the misconduct. A three-month suspension would disregard this independent review and improperly defer to the referee’s opinion, which the Review Department itself rejected with two dissenting votes.  

The defense’s argument that "the prosecution ignores the referee’s credibility findings" is baseless. This Court did not "second-guess" the referee’s findings; it conducted a full review of the record and concluded that six months—consistent with *Duncan* and *Harris*—is the appropriate sanction. The referee’s observation of the petitioner’s demeanor does not override the legal principle that discipline must be proportionate to the misconduct’s severity. As this Court stated in *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), "mitigating factors do not excuse the violation; they merely inform the scope of discipline." Three months is too lenient to serve that purpose.  

### IV. The Defense’s Request for Three Months Is Contrary to the Purpose of Attorney Discipline  
The defense’s argument that discipline must "avoid unnecessary harm to attorneys who have demonstrated genuine rehabilitation" misstates the law. The primary purpose of attorney discipline is not to protect attorneys but to protect the public, the courts, and the integrity of the profession. In *In re Harris*, 49 Cal. 3d 131 (1989), this Court made clear that "a sanction must be sufficient to deter others from similar misconduct and to reassure the public that the profession enforces its ethical standards." A three-month suspension would fail this test. It would imply that misappropriation—no matter how serious—is a minor offense if the attorney is in recovery, thereby encouraging clients to distrust attorneys who handle their funds.

In *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), this Court explicitly rejected a three-month suspension for misappropriation, holding that "any suspension below six months would not adequately reflect the gravity of the violation." Similarly, in *In re Harris*, 49 Cal. 3d 131 (1989), the Court found that a three-month suspension would be "inconsistent with the need to maintain public confidence in the profession." A three-month sanction trivializes the violation and fails to fulfill the protective purpose of discipline.  

### CONCLUSION  
The defense’s request for a three-month suspension is legally unsupportable. A three-month suspension would set a dangerous precedent, undermine public confidence, and fail to uphold the ethical standards of the profession. Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving $1,300 from a $3,500 settlement. This was not a pattern of misconduct nor a large-scale theft. As this Court observed in *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 39 (1996), the nature and extent of the misconduct are critical factors in determining appropriate discipline. A single, isolated act involving a relatively small sum warrants less severe discipline than systematic misappropriation.

### B. Full Restitution Was Made

Petitioner made complete restitution to her client. The client suffered no financial loss. This factor distinguishes her case from those where clients have been left uncompensated and demonstrates petitioner's commitment to making her client whole.

### C. No Prior Disciplinary History

Petitioner was admitted to practice in 1982. The misconduct occurred in 1985, and charges were not brought until 1988. Throughout her career, petitioner has had no prior record of discipline. This absence of prior misconduct weighs significantly in mitigation, as recognized in *In re Buckley*, 43 Cal. 3d 1 (1987).

Petitioner began drinking as a teenager and abused alcohol and drugs virtually all of her adult life. She is the child of alcoholics who deserted her in childhood. This Court has consistently recognized that chemical dependency, when proven as the cause of misconduct, constitutes significant mitigation. In *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), the Court reduced a suspension to six months where the attorney demonstrated that his misconduct was directly attributable to chemical dependency.

### E. Compelling Evidence of Successful Rehabilitation

The most significant mitigating factor in this case is petitioner's extraordinary rehabilitation. At the time of the referee's hearing in late 1988, petitioner had been sober for approximately two and one-half years. She has remained an active participant in these programs and assists others who suffer from chemical dependency through The Other Bar.

The referee credited expert testimony presented by petitioner to the effect that she "fell in the highest percentile of those whose recovery programs were likely to be successful." The referee found that petitioner's chemical dependency was well under control and predicted that, under appropriate supervision, it would continue to be so. The referee also noted that petitioner had performed well as a lawyer since 1986 when she stopped drinking and using drugs.

This evidence of sustained recovery distinguishes this case from those where rehabilitation remains speculative. Petitioner has demonstrated not merely the potential for recovery but its actual accomplishment.

## III. Six Months Adequately Serves the Purposes of Discipline

The purposes of attorney discipline are threefold: protecting the public, deterring future misconduct, and maintaining professional integrity. *In re Buckley*, 43 Cal. 3d 1 (1987). A six-month suspension achieves all three purposes without over-punishing petitioner.

The public is adequately protected by a six-month suspension. Petitioner has demonstrated through her sustained recovery that she no longer poses a risk to clients. Her chemical dependency is under control, and she has shown no propensity for repeating her misconduct. ### B. Deterrence

A six-month suspension provides meaningful deterrence to other attorneys. It communicates that misappropriation of client funds, even in small amounts, carries serious consequences. It also demonstrates that the profession takes this violation seriously while still recognizing genuine rehabilitation.

### C. Maintaining Professional Integrity

**IT IS ORDERED** that the petition for review is granted in part. The decision of the Review Department is modified to impose an actual suspension of six months. The preliminary ruling of this Court is affirmed. The prosecution's argument that a three-month suspension is insufficient, and the defense's acceptance of the six-month sanction, confirm that this penalty is the appropriate discipline under the circumstances.

**STATEMENT OF DECISION**

The issue before the Court is whether the mitigating factors present in this case warrant a departure from the standard one-year suspension for wilful misappropriation of client funds, and if so, the extent of that departure. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties, the Court finds that a six-month actual suspension strikes the proper balance between the need to protect the public and the need to recognize the petitioner's extraordinary rehabilitation.

The prosecution correctly argues that the defense's initial request for a three-month suspension is inconsistent with controlling precedent. The prosecution points out that in *In re Morse*, 11 Cal. 4th 184 (1995), and *In re Buckley*, 43 Cal. 3d 1 (1987), the courts imposed suspensions of six months, not three, even where compelling mitigation existed. The prosecution further asserts, relying on *In re Duncan*, 48 Cal. 3d 757 (1989), and *In re Harris*, 49 Cal. 3d 131 (1989), that six months represents the "floor" for reductions in misappropriation cases involving chemical dependency, as anything lower would trivialize the breach of fiduciary duty and fail to maintain public confidence in the profession. The Court agrees with the prosecution's analysis that a three-month suspension would be insufficient to reflect the gravity of misappropriating client funds, regardless of the amount involved.

The defense, in its rebuttal, acknowledges the prosecution's characterization of the precedent and accepts the Court's preliminary ruling of a six-month suspension. The defense concedes that a three-month suspension would be unprecedented and that the six-month sanction adequately serves the purposes of discipline by protecting the public, providing deterrence, and maintaining professional integrity. The defense emphasizes that the petitioner's misconduct was an isolated act involving a relatively small sum and that the petitioner has demonstrated a high likelihood of sustained recovery from chemical dependency.

The Court finds that the positions of both parties, as articulated in their rebuttals, converge on the conclusion that a six-month suspension is the appropriate sanction. The prosecution's arguments establish that the misconduct warrants a significant penalty, while the defense's acceptance of the six-month term acknowledges the severity of the offense. The petitioner's single act of misappropriation, coupled with her full restitution and compelling evidence of rehabilitation, supports a reduction from the standard one-year suspension. However, as the prosecution argues, and the defense now agrees, the reduction should not extend below six months.

Accordingly, the Court concludes that the six-month actual suspension ordered in the preliminary ruling is consistent with the principles of proportional discipline articulated in *In re Miller*, 14 Cal. 4th 39 (1996), and is fully supported by the parties' final arguments.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling imposing an actual suspension of six months. This sanction adequately protects the public and the profession while giving due recognition to the petitioner's significant mitigation and successful rehabilitation.
