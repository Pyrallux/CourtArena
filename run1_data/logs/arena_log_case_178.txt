**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT**  

Today, this Court confronts a critical legal error in the Director’s denial of Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim: the misapplication of *Flor v. Holguin* to the proximate cause analysis for the aggravation of a pre-existing disease. The Director incorrectly applied *Flor*’s test for whether a disease "results from the nature of the employment" to determine proximate cause—when *Flor* explicitly limits its scope to that narrow question and does not govern whether employment proximately caused the aggravation. As this Court has repeatedly held, proximate cause for all injuries, including disease aggravations, is determined solely by the unitary test. The record unequivocally satisfies this test.  

### I. *Flor v. Holguin* Does Not Apply to Proximate Cause Determination  
*Flor v. Holguin* established a specialized test for compensability of injuries *by disease*—specifically, whether the disease "is characteristic of or peculiar to the employment." *Flor v. Holguin*, 107 Haw. 198, 204 (2005). This test addresses only whether the disease itself arises from the nature of the employment. It does not, and cannot, govern proximate cause. As the *Flor* court expressly stated: "The test for compensability of an injury by disease is whether the disease is characteristic of or peculiar to the employment." *Id.* (emphasis added). The Court’s holding is confined to this threshold question of disease compensability—it says nothing about proximate cause.  

This distinction is fundamental. Proximate cause—whether employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury—is a separate analytical step governed by the unitary test applicable to *all* injuries, regardless of whether they involve disease. *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280, 288 (2008) ("[T]he unitary test for proximate cause applies to all workers' compensation claims, including those involving pre-existing conditions and diseases."). In *Kaua'i Concrete*, this Court rejected the notion that any special rule applies to proximate cause for disease-related injuries, holding instead that the "substantial contributing factor" standard governs universally. To conflate *Flor*’s disease-specific test with proximate cause—as the Director did here—is to ignore binding precedent and distort the law.  

### II. The Director Erred by Misapplying *Flor* to Proximate Cause  
The Director denied Van Ness’s claim based on the erroneous premise that *Flor* required proving Vog exposure was "unique" to his workplace. The Director stated: "[I]t was claimant’s exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition, and there was insufficient evidence to support [his] claim that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only." *Director’s Decision*, p. 551–52 (Apr. 21, 2008). This reasoning conflates *Flor*’s "characteristic of employment" test (which asks whether the disease itself is peculiar to the job) with proximate cause (which asks whether employment was a substantial factor in the aggravation).  

*Flor* does not demand that the hazard be exclusive to the workplace to establish proximate cause. It merely requires that the disease be characteristic of the employment to satisfy the compensability threshold for diseases. But once that threshold is met—as it is here—the unitary test controls proximate cause. The Director’s error is plain: by insisting Vog exposure must be "only" at Lahainaluna to satisfy proximate cause, the Director applied *Flor*’s disease test where it has no bearing.  

### III. The Unitary Test for Proximate Cause Is Satisfied  
Under the unitary test, proximate cause is established if employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Kaua'i Concrete*, 118 Haw. at 288. The record compels this conclusion:  

- Van Ness’s pre-existing asthma was aggravated by Vog exposure *specifically at work*. Dr. Mathison’s report confirms Vog is a "well-recognized risk factor" for asthmatics like Van Ness, and he diagnosed "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." *Dr. Mathison Consultation Report*, p. 550–51. Crucially, Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness’s symptoms "essentially resolved" while in Pennsylvania (a Vog-free environment) but "started to have wheezing and shortness of breath" immediately upon returning to Maui. *Id.* at 550.  

- The Director’s own findings acknowledge Van Ness’s exposure to severe Vog at work: "Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that 'unusually severe vog-polluted days' occurred during the weeks preceding December 23, 2005," and "the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that [they] could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School." *Director’s Decision*, p. 551. Van Ness testified he spent less than 5% of his workday in air-conditioned spaces, worked in "non-air conditioned environments all over campus" with open louvered windows, and frequently cleaned dust-clogged computer filters due to poor ventilation. *Record*, pp. 547–48.  

- The Director’s assertion that Vog was "present on the entire island" is irrelevant to proximate cause. The unitary test does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace; it requires only that employment was a substantial factor. *Kaua'i Concrete*, 118 Haw. at 288. Here, Van Ness’s work environment subjected him to Vog exposure he would not have encountered elsewhere—specifically, he was required to be present at Lahainaluna during peak Vog season, with no ability to avoid it while working. Dr. Mathison’s conclusion that Van Ness "can be considered disabled by virtue of his asthmatic tendency and susceptibility to volcanic pollutant effects" directly ties his worsening condition to workplace exposure. *Dr. Mathison Report*, p. 551.  

- The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s report—which explicitly stated "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" and refused to opine on causation without medical records—does not negate proximate cause. Dr. Arora’s opinion was speculative and incomplete; it did not address whether employment was a *substantial contributing factor* under the unitary test. *See Dr. Arora IME Report*, p. 550 (Feb. 5, 2008).  

### IV. Conclusion  
The Director’s denial rests on a legal misstep: applying *Flor v. Holguin*’s disease-specific test to proximate cause when binding precedent mandates the unitary test. *Flor* governs only whether a disease is compensable under the "characteristic of employment" standard—it does not dictate proximate cause. Under the correct standard, the record establishes Vog exposure at work was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s asthma aggravation. This Court should reverse the Director’s decision and remand for an award of benefits.

Counsel for the Plaintiff/Prosecution

## I. THE DIRECTOR CORRECTLY DETERMINED THAT VAN NESS'S INJURY DID NOT AROSE OUT OF AND IN THE COURSE OF EMPLOYMENT

The Director's denial rested not on a misapplication of *Flor v. Holguin*, but on the fundamental lack of evidence establishing that Van Ness's employment—was a substantial contributing factor to his injury. The record demonstrates that vog was present island-wide on Maui and was not peculiar to Lahainaluna High School.

In *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280, 288 (2008), this Court held that the "substantial contributing factor" test applies to all workers' compensation claims. The Director applied this standard and found insufficient evidence that Van Ness's exposure to vog at Lahainaluna specifically—and not elsewhere on Maui—was a substantial contributing factor to his asthma exacerbation.

The evidence fully supports this finding:

**A. Vog Was Present Island-Wide, Not Just at the Workplace**

The record establishes that vog during the relevant period was a general environmental condition affecting all of Maui, not a workplace-specific hazard. Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. Van Ness himself testified that volcanic ash would accumulate on his car and windshield while driving, and he could not see the administrative building "halfway down the campus" from the library on severe vog days. This widespread environmental contamination is precisely what the Director identified as insufficient to establish a workplace nexus.

**B. Van Ness's Home Environment Was Air-Conditioned and Filtered**

Critically, Van Ness lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei with a built-in filter, multiple "Bionic Breeze" HEPA filter units throughout his home, and a HEPA filter in his car. While at home—where he spent the majority of his non-working hours—Van Ness was protected from vog exposure. This undermines any claim that the workplace was the primary source of his exposure.

**C. Van Ness's Pre-Existing Condition Was the Dominant Factor**

The record demonstrates that Van Ness had a long-standing history of "mild persistent asthma" dating back to 1989. His asthma had required systemic corticosteroid treatment even before his Maui assignment. Dr. Mathison acknowledged that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis has largely been controlled with avoidances and the several-year course of immunotherapy during the early 90s." The exacerbation of a pre-existing condition does not automatically establish compensability; the employment must be a substantial contributing factor. Here, the evidence shows that Van Ness's asthma was susceptible to volcanic pollutants regardless of location—a characteristic of his pre-existing condition, not a new injury arising from employment.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON THE "UNITARY TEST" IS MISPLACED

The Plaintiff argues that the Director erred by not applying the unitary test for proximate cause. However, the Director did apply this standard—the Director simply found that the evidence failed to satisfy it. The Plaintiff cannot convert a factual deficiency into a legal error.

The unitary test requires that employment be a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Kaua'i Concrete*, 118 Haw. at 288. The Director made a factual finding that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition" and that "there was insufficient evidence to support Van Ness's claim that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that his exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only."

This factual finding is supported by substantial evidence:

- Vice Principal Dennis testified that vog was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived
- Van Ness admitted he spent little time outside when not at work
- Van Ness's home was equipped with multiple HEPA filters
- Dr. Arora noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely"

The Plaintiff argues that Dr. Mathison's report establishes causation. However, Dr. Mathison's report actually supports the Director's finding. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's symptoms "essentially resolved" while in Pennsylvania—a vog-free environment—but returned "as soon as he returned to Maui." This establishes that exposure to Maui generally, not specifically to Lahainaluna, triggered Van Ness's symptoms. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The central issue on appeal is whether the Director erred as a matter of law in determining that Van Ness’s injury did not arise out of and in the course of his employment. Specifically, the Court must determine whether the Director misapplied the legal standard for proximate cause by requiring Van Ness to prove that his exposure to volcanic smog ("vog") was unique to his workplace, rather than applying the correct "substantial contributing factor" test.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

In Hawai‘i, the standard for determining compensability in workers’ compensation cases is governed by the "unitary test." Under *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280, 288 (2008), the "unitary test for proximate cause applies to all workers' compensation claims, including those involving pre-existing conditions and diseases." To establish compensability, a claimant must demonstrate that their employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Id.*

This standard is distinct from the test established in *Flor v. Holguin*, 107 Haw. 198, 204 (2005). *Flor* addresses whether a disease itself is compensable by requiring the claimant to show that the disease is "characteristic of or peculiar to the employment." *Id.* This "characteristic of" inquiry is a threshold question regarding the nature of the disease, not a substitute for the proximate cause analysis required by *Kaua'i Concrete*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Director denied Van Ness’s claim, finding that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director concluded that there was "insufficient evidence to support [Van Ness’s] claim that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only."

The Court finds that the Director misapplied the law by conflating the "characteristic of" test from *Flor v. Holguin* with the "substantial contributing factor" test from *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*. The Director’s reasoning implies that because vog is a general environmental hazard present island-wide, it cannot be the basis for a workers’ compensation claim unless the exposure is exclusive to the workplace. This is an incorrect application of the law.

Under *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. at 288, the relevant inquiry is not whether the hazard was unique to the workplace, but whether the employment conditions were a substantial contributing factor to the injury. The record contains substantial evidence that the specific conditions of Van Ness’s employment at Lahainaluna High School significantly exacerbated his exposure to vog compared to his environment off-duty.

Van Ness testified that while at work, he spent less than five percent of his time in air-conditioned spaces, working instead in non-air conditioned buildings with louvered windows and fans that circulated air. He was required to perform physical labor, such as walking uphill and cleaning dust-clogged computer filters, which aggravated his respiratory condition. In contrast, Van Ness’s home in Kihei was air-conditioned with a built-in filter, and he utilized HEPA filter units in his home and car. This distinction between the filtered, air-conditioned home environment and the dusty, non-air conditioned work environment is material to the "substantial contributing factor" analysis.

The Director’s reliance on the island-wide presence of vog to deny the claim ignores these specific workplace conditions. The fact that vog existed generally on Maui does not preclude a finding that the employment conditions—which required Van Ness to be physically active in a non-filtered environment for seven to eight hours a day—were a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma. By focusing on the ubiquity of the hazard rather than the relative contribution of the employment, the Director applied an incorrect legal standard.

Furthermore, the medical evidence supports a finding of a nexus between the employment and the injury. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noted that his symptoms improved when he was not on Maui. Dr. Tom’s clinical notes also referenced "vog contributing" to Van Ness’s symptoms. While the Director credited Dr. Arora’s report, Dr. Arora explicitly stated he could not address causation without medical records and noted the legal implications were "not clear." The Director’s dismissal of the contrary medical evidence based on a misapprehension of the legal standard constitutes reversible error.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Director committed legal error by applying the "characteristic of or peculiar to the employment" standard from *Flor v. Holguin*, 107 Haw. 198 (2005), rather than the "substantial contributing factor" standard from *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280 (2008). The Director’s conclusion that the claim was non-compensable because vog was present island-wide is contrary to the established proximate cause analysis for workers’ compensation claims.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision is hereby VACATED, and the matter is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must determine whether Van Ness’s employment at Lahainaluna High School was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma, considering the specific conditions of the workplace and the home environment, without requiring the exposure to be unique to the workplace.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Court has reviewed the arguments presented and the factual record provided. The issue remains whether the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations erred in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim and, specifically, whether such error was harmless given the evidence in the record.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

As established in the Court's preliminary analysis, the standard for determining compensability in workers’ compensation cases involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition is governed by the "unitary test" for proximate cause. Under *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280, 288 (2008), a claimant must demonstrate that their employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. This standard is distinct from the "characteristic of or peculiar to the employment" test articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 107 Haw. 198, 204 (2005), which applies only to the threshold question of whether a disease itself is compensable.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Defense concedes that the Director misapplied the law by relying on the "characteristic of" standard from *Flor* rather than the "substantial contributing factor" standard from *Kaua'i Concrete*. However, the Defense argues that this error was harmless because the record, when viewed under the correct standard, does not support a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to his injury. The Court disagrees.

The Director’s decision to deny the claim was predicated entirely on the finding that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director concluded that because the vog was not unique to the workplace, there was "insufficient evidence" to support the claim. This reasoning constitutes a legal error that precludes the Court from affirming the Director's decision on the existing record.

Under *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. at 288, the inquiry is not whether the hazard (vog) was unique to the workplace, but whether the conditions of employment were a substantial contributing factor to the injury. The Director failed to conduct this analysis. By focusing solely on the geographic ubiquity of the vog, the Director ignored evidence regarding the specific nature of Van Ness’s exposure at work compared to his exposure elsewhere.

The record contains substantial evidence that the conditions at Lahainaluna High School differed materially from the conditions in Van Ness’s home environment. Van Ness testified that he spent seven to eight hours a day at work in non-air conditioned buildings with louvered windows and circulating fans, where he was required to perform physical labor such as walking uphill and cleaning dust-clogged computer filters. In contrast, his home in Kihei was air-conditioned with a built-in filter and equipped with HEPA filter units. While the Defense correctly notes that vog was present island-wide and that Van Ness’s symptoms resolved when he left Maui entirely, this evidence does not, as a matter of law, negate the possibility that the specific workplace conditions—unfiltered air, dust, and physical exertion—were a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma.

The Defense argues that the medical evidence, particularly Dr. Mathison’s report, links the exacerbation solely to general volcanic exposure rather than workplace-specific conditions. However, Dr. Mathison’s report does not exclude the workplace as a substantial factor; it merely identifies volcanic products as the risk factor. The Director was required to weigh the evidence regarding the *intensity* and *nature* of the exposure at work against the exposure at home. Because the Director’s decision was based on the erroneous legal premise that island-wide exposure precludes compensability, the Director failed to make the necessary factual findings regarding whether the dusty, non-air conditioned work environment substantially contributed to Van Ness’s condition compared to his filtered home environment.

An appellate court cannot affirm an agency decision based on a rationale the agency did not adopt, particularly where the agency failed to consider material evidence due to a legal error. See *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. at 288 (emphasizing the necessity of applying the unitary test to all claims). Because the Director did not evaluate the comparative exposure evidence under the "substantial contributing factor" standard, the Court cannot conclude that the Director would have reached the same result had the correct legal standard been applied.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s "harmless error" argument fails because the Director’s legal error infected the entire factual analysis. The Director did not weigh the specific workplace conditions against the home environment; instead, the Director dismissed the claim based on the impermissible requirement that the exposure be unique to the workplace. Therefore, the error is not harmless, and the Director’s decision cannot stand.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision is VACATED. The matter is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must determine whether Van Ness’s employment at Lahainaluna High School was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma, applying the unitary test from *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280 (2008), and considering all relevant evidence regarding the conditions of exposure at work and at home.

The Prosecution argues that the unitary test does not require medical evidence to isolate workplace exposure as the *sole* cause. However, the test does require a showing that workplace conditions "materially increased exposure relative to non-employment settings." The medical evidence here identifies volcanic products generally as the risk factor, without distinguishing between workplace and home exposure.

### C. The Physical Exertion Factor

The Prosecution notes that Van Ness performed physical labor at work, including walking uphill and cleaning dust-clogged computer filters. However, the record does not establish that these activities substantially contributed to his asthma exacerbation beyond the baseline exposure to vog.

The unitary test requires that employment conditions be a substantial contributing factor; the mere presence of physical labor, without medical evidence linking that labor to the exacerbation, is insufficient.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon the closing arguments of the Prosecution and the Defense following the Court’s preliminary ruling. The central issue for determination is whether the legal error committed by the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) was harmless. Specifically, the Court must decide if the Director’s misapplication of the "characteristic of or peculiar to the employment" test, rather than the "substantial contributing factor" test, can be overlooked given the factual record presented.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The standard for reviewing a workers’ compensation claim involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition is the "unitary test" for proximate cause established in *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280, 288 (2008). Under this test, a claimant must demonstrate that their employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. This requires a comparative analysis of the risks and exposures inherent in the employment versus those encountered in the claimant's non-employment life. *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. at 288.

The "characteristic of or peculiar to the employment" test articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 107 Haw. 198, 204 (2005), applies only to the threshold question of whether a disease is compensable as an occupational disease, not to the determination of proximate cause for an aggravated condition.

An appellate court may affirm an agency decision on grounds other than those relied upon by the agency only if the alternative grounds are supported by the record and the agency's findings. However, where the agency failed to consider material evidence due to a legal error, the error is generally not harmless, and the case must be remanded. *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. at 288.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Defense concedes that the Director applied the incorrect legal standard by relying on *Flor* rather than *Kaua'i Concrete*. The Defense argues, however, that this error was harmless because the record, even when viewed through the lens of the "substantial contributing factor" test, does not support a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to his injury. The Court disagrees.

The Director’s decision to deny the claim was based exclusively on the finding that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director concluded that this ubiquity of the hazard precluded a finding of compensability. This reasoning is legally flawed under *Kaua'i Concrete*, which explicitly rejects the notion that a hazard must be unique to the workplace to be compensable. *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. at 288.

The Defense now asks this Court to affirm the denial on the alternative ground that the evidence shows Van Ness’s exposure at home was equal to or greater than his exposure at work. The Defense points to testimony that vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, and that his symptoms resolved only when he left the island entirely.

The Court cannot adopt this rationale for two reasons. First, the Director did not make findings regarding the comparative intensity of vog exposure in Kihei versus Lahainaluna. The Director found only that vog was present on the entire island. The Director did not weigh the testimony of Vice Principal Dennis regarding the severity of vog in Kihei against the evidence of Van Ness’s home environment. Second, and more critically, the Director failed to consider the evidence regarding the specific conditions of Van Ness’s workplace compared to his home.

The record contains unrefuted evidence that Van Ness’s workplace at Lahainaluna High School was non-air conditioned, utilized louvered windows and circulating fans, and required him to perform physical exertion in dusty environments where computer filters were frequently clogged. In contrast, Van Ness’s home in Kihei was air conditioned with a built-in filter and equipped with multiple HEPA filter units. While the Defense argues that the vog was worse in Kihei generally, the Director never analyzed whether the specific *mitigation measures* at home (filtration) versus the lack thereof at work resulted in a materially higher exposure at the workplace.

Under *Kaua'i Concrete*, the Director was required to determine if the conditions of employment were a substantial contributing factor to the injury. This necessitated a factual comparison of the filtered home environment against the unfiltered, dusty work environment. Because the Director dismissed the claim based on the erroneous legal premise that island-wide exposure was fatal, the Director never made the necessary factual findings regarding whether the workplace conditions substantially contributed to the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma relative to his home conditions.

The Defense’s argument that the medical evidence fails to establish workplace-specific causation does not render the error harmless. Dr. Mathison’s report identifies volcanic products as the risk factor but does not exclude the workplace as a substantial contributor. It was the Director’s responsibility to weigh the medical evidence alongside the evidence of environmental conditions. The Director’s failure to do so—due to the reliance on the wrong legal standard—precludes the Court from affirming the decision on the basis that the Defense now advances.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s "harmless error" argument fails because it requires the Court to make factual findings and credibility determinations that the Director did not make. The Director’s legal error infected the entire analysis, causing the Director to ignore material evidence regarding the differential exposure between Van Ness’s workplace and his home. Therefore, the error is not harmless.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision is VACATED. The matter is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director must apply the "substantial contributing factor" test from *Kaua'i Concrete v. LIRAB*, 118 Haw. 280 (2008), and make specific findings of fact regarding the comparative exposure at Lahainaluna High School and Van Ness’s home in Kihei, including the impact of the air conditioning and filtration systems present in the home environment.
