# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

Appellant respectfully submits that this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand for further proceedings. The trial court erred in denying Appellant's motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant because the informant's identity was directly relevant to the motion to suppress—the very issue before the court under the dispositive motion agreement. The prosecution's characterization that Appellant sought disclosure only for "guilt or innocence" purposes mischaracterizes the nature of the suppression hearing and improperly conflates the issues. When a defendant challenges the existence of probable cause based upon an informant's tip, the reliability of that informant is the central question, and the defense is entitled to probe that reliability, including through disclosure of the informant's identity when necessary.

---

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

On the date in question, Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine). Appellant filed a motion to suppress the evidence on the basis that it was illegally obtained. He also filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant, citing two grounds: (1) that the informant's identity was needed to show that the officers who conducted the arrest and search did not have probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant, and (2) that the informant's identity was necessary because the informant was a material witness to the Appellant's guilt or innocence and upon the possible defense of entrapment.

The parties agreed that the motion to suppress would be "dispositional," meaning that if the trial court ruled in the State's favor on the suppression issue, Appellant would plead guilty, and the court would determine sentencing. If the ruling favored the Defendant, the case would be dismissed.

During the hearing on the motion to suppress, defense counsel sought to re-urge the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity based upon new information. Defense counsel represented that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day of the arrest, that the informant's belongings were found in Appellant's car when it was released from evidence, and that this information was critical to the defense of entrapment. Defense counsel argued that this information went to the reliability of the informant, which was directly relevant to the probable cause determination underlying the suppression motion.

The trial judge denied both the motion to suppress and the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. Appellant pled guilty and was sentenced to sixteen years in prison.

---

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Confidential Informant's Identity Was Relevant to the Suppression Issue, Not Merely to Guilt or Innocence

The prosecution erroneously contends that Appellant sought disclosure of the confidential informant's identity solely for purposes related to guilt or innocence, such as entrapment. This characterization ignores the first basis for the disclosure motion, which was directly tied to the suppression hearing: demonstrating that the officers did not have probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant.

When a defendant moves to suppress evidence obtained through a search warrant or arrest supported by an informant's tip, the reliability of that informant is the threshold question. As the Supreme Court recognized in Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), the totality of the circumstances must be assessed to determine whether an informant's tip establishes probable cause. The reliability and credibility of the informant are central to this analysis. Indeed, in Gates, the Court emphasized that the informant's veracity, reliability, and basis of knowledge are crucial factors in the probable cause determination.

Here, the defense specifically argued that the confidential informant's reliability was at issue in connection with the motion to suppress. Defense counsel stated: "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress, even on a Motion to Suppress. That does not resolve the fact that he thinks she set him up is important even on a Motion to Suppress. Guilt or innocence or not, if she set him up, it goes to her reliability." This statement makes clear that the defense sought disclosure to challenge the informant's reliability as it related to probable cause, not merely to establish a defense at trial.

The prosecution cites United States v. Rivera, 649 F.3d 120, 125 (2d Cir. 2011), for the proposition that disclosure is required only when the informant's testimony is relevant to guilt or innocence or to the legality of the search. However, the prosecution's reliance on Rivera is misplaced. The Rivera court explicitly acknowledged that disclosure may be required "to the legality of the search." Here, the Appellant sought disclosure precisely to challenge the legality of the search by attacking the probable cause determination, which rests upon the informant's reliability. Thus, under the framework acknowledged in Rivera, disclosure was warranted.

#### B. The Trial Court Erred in Denying Disclosure When the Informant's Reliability Was Central to the Probable Cause Determination

The trial court's refusal to disclose the confidential informant's identity, even in the context of a dispositive suppression motion, violated Appellant's constitutional rights. The Fourth Amendment guarantees the right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures, and the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to present a complete defense. When a defendant challenges the existence of probable cause based upon an informant's tip, the defendant must have a meaningful opportunity to test the informant's reliability.

In Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), the Supreme Court held that the privilege of the government to withhold the identity of a confidential informant must give way when the informant's testimony is relevant to a fair determination of the case. While the prosecution correctly notes that Roviaro addressed situations where the informant was a material witness to guilt or innocence, the principle underlying Roviaro—that the defendant's right to prepare a defense must be balanced against the government's privilege—applies with equal force when the informant's identity is relevant to the suppression issue.

The prosecution argues that United States v. Harris, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971), establishes that an informant's reliability may be established through testimony about prior accurate tips without disclosing the informant's identity. However, Harris does not stand for the proposition that a defendant is never entitled to disclosure when reliability is at issue. Rather, Harris held that in that particular case, the informant's reliability was sufficiently established. The Court emphasized that the totality of the circumstances must be evaluated. Here, defense counsel represented that there was "new information" regarding the informant's role—specifically, that the informant was with Appellant on the day of the arrest and that the informant's belongings were found in Appellant's car. This information directly bears on the informant's reliability and credibility. Under the totality of the circumstances, disclosure may have been necessary to allow the defense to meaningfully challenge the probable cause determination.

#### C. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Suppression Motion Did Not Waive Appellant's Right to Disclosure

The prosecution argues that because the motion to suppress was dispositive, the trial court properly limited the hearing to suppression issues alone and properly excluded evidence related to guilt or innocence. The prosecution cites State v. Smith, 694 N.W.2d 409, 415 (Iowa 2005), for this proposition. However, this argument misapprehends the nature of the agreement and the scope of the issues properly before the court.

The agreement that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive" addressed the consequences of the ruling—it determined whether the case would proceed to trial or end in a guilty plea. It did not, and could not, strip the court of its authority to consider all evidence relevant to the suppression issue itself. The suppression hearing is not a truncated proceeding in which the court considers only one side's evidence; it is a full hearing on the legality of the search, at which both parties must be permitted to present evidence relevant to the issue.

Here, the suppression issue was whether the officers had probable cause to arrest and search Appellant. The existence of probable cause depends upon the reliability of the informant. The defense sought to present evidence bearing on the informant's reliability—including the informant's identity and the circumstances of her involvement—to demonstrate that probable cause was lacking. This evidence was directly relevant to the suppression issue, not merely to guilt or innocence. The trial court erred in treating the "dispositive" nature of the motion as a limitation on the evidence the defense could present.

Moreover, the agreement contemplated that the motion to suppress would be heard by "Judge Hill" or another judge, and the defense represented that "at the time when Judge Hill was here, we did not have the new information regarding the CI." The defense sought to present this new information—which was directly relevant to the informant's reliability—to the court hearing the suppression motion. The trial court should have considered this evidence rather than summarily denying the disclosure motion.

#### D. The Defense Was Entitled to Investigate the Informant's Role in the Alleged Offense to Challenge Her Reliability

Defense counsel represented that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day of the arrest, that her belongings were found in his car, and that she may have "set him up." This evidence is relevant not only to the defense of entrapment but also to the informant's reliability and credibility. An informant who is involved in the offense, or who has a relationship with the defendant that might motivate false or unreliable information, may not be a credible source for probable cause purposes.

In Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 331 (2006), the Supreme Court held that a state rule that excludes evidence of third-party guilt when the defendant has been convicted of a prior offense violates the defendant's constitutional right to present a complete defense. While Holmes addressed a different issue, its reasoning underscores the importance of allowing defendants to present evidence that is relevant to their defense. Here, the evidence regarding the informant's involvement was relevant to the suppression issue because it bore on her reliability.

The trial court's suggestion that these matters were "defense matters for trial" improperly minimized the significance of the informant's reliability to the probable cause determination. The court should have recognized that the informant's potential involvement in the offense was relevant to assessing her credibility and, consequently, the existence of probable cause.

---

### IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand for further proceedings. The trial court erred in denying Appellant's motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity because the informant's identity was directly relevant to the suppression issue—whether the officers had probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant. The prosecution's characterization of the disclosure motion as seeking only guilt-related evidence mischaracterizes the motion and ignores its relevance to the suppression hearing. Under the Fourth and Sixth Amendments, Appellant was entitled to a meaningful opportunity to challenge the informant's reliability, and the trial court erred in denying this opportunity.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the arguments presented by the Appellant and the Appellee regarding the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. Based on the provided facts and the applicable legal standards, the Court affirms the decision of the lower court.

**I. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Motion to Suppress**

The central procedural fact in this case is the agreement between the parties that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." As confirmed by the trial transcript, both the prosecution and defense counsel agreed that if the court ruled in favor of the State on the motion to suppress, the Appellant would plead guilty, thereby foregoing a trial on the merits. The prosecution correctly argues that this agreement limited the scope of the hearing to the single issue of the legality of the search and seizure.

In *State v. Smith*, 694 N.W.2d 409, 415 (Iowa 2005), the court held that "when a suppression motion is dispositive, the trial court properly limits the hearing to the suppression issue and does not consider defenses that relate to guilt or innocence." Here, the defense counsel explicitly acknowledged the dispositive nature of the motion, stating, "You are clear in your understanding of the Motion to Suppress." By entering into this agreement, the defense accepted the risk that issues pertaining to guilt or innocence—such as entrapment or whether the informant "set him up"—would not be litigated if the suppression motion failed. The trial court acted within its discretion in enforcing the parameters of this agreement and refusing to expand the hearing into a broader inquiry regarding the informant's potential culpability or the defendant's intent.

**II. Disclosure of the Confidential Informant’s Identity**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the informant’s identity because the informant was a material witness to the probable cause determination. The general rule regarding informant privilege is established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), which states that "the privilege must give way if the disclosure of an informer's identity... is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." However, this privilege is not absolute.

The trial court determined that the reliability of the informant had been sufficiently established through testimony regarding the "effectiveness of her past tips" and the "accuracy of them." This finding is consistent with the holding of the Supreme Court in *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971), which established that "an informant's reliability may be established by evidence of prior accurate tips without disclosing the informant's identity." The Appellant’s claim of "new information"—that the informant’s belongings were found in the car and that she was with him on the day of the arrest—does not automatically override the established reliability of her prior tips. While this information might be relevant to a defense of entrapment at trial, it does not negate the probable cause possessed by the officers at the time of the arrest, which was based on the informant's historical reliability.

Furthermore, as the prosecution notes, the standard for disclosure in this context requires that the informant's testimony be relevant to the legality of the search, not merely to the defendant's guilt or innocence. *United States v. Rivera*, 649 F.3d 120, 125 (2d Cir. 2011). The Appellant’s arguments that the informant "set him up" and that her presence was "critical to his defense" are fundamentally arguments regarding guilt or innocence, specifically entrapment. Because the hearing was limited to the dispositive issue of the motion to suppress, the trial court properly excluded these arguments.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The "dispositive" agreement properly limited the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search, and the reliability of the informant was sufficiently established by evidence of past tips under *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573 (1971). The Appellant’s attempts to introduce "new information" regarding the informant’s involvement pertained to issues of guilt and innocence which were waived by the dispositive nature of the proceeding. Therefore, the ruling of the court of appeals is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the court of appeals be, and the same hereby is, affirmed.

## I. INTRODUCTION

## II. THE "DISPOSITIVE" AGREEMENT DID NOT WAIVE CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO CHALLENGE PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution incorrectly argues that by agreeing to make the motion to suppress "dispositive," the defense waived any right to disclosure of the informant's identity. This argument conflates two distinct concepts: waiving issues related to *guilt or innocence* versus preserving constitutional challenges to the *legality of the search*.

The defense never agreed that issues pertaining to the validity of the search itself would be waived. To the contrary, the very purpose of the motion to suppress was to challenge the legality of the search and seizure. The informant's identity was sought specifically to challenge the *probable cause* determination—the central issue before the court on the suppression motion. As defense counsel explicitly stated during the hearing: "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress... The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Smith*, 694 N.W.2d 409 (Iowa 2005), is misplaced. That case stands for the proposition that a dispositive suppression motion limits the hearing to suppression issues—it does not stand for the proposition that a defendant waives the right to present evidence relevant to the suppression issue itself. The defense did not seek to litigate entrapment or ultimate guilt; it sought to disclose the informant's identity to demonstrate that the officers lacked probable cause because the informant was unreliable, biased, or potentially involved in orchestrating the crime.

## III. THE INFORMANT'S IDENTITY WAS RELEVANT TO THE SUPPRESSION ISSUE UNDER ROVIARO

The prosecution attempts to restrict *Roviaro* to cases involving guilt or innocence, but this reading contradicts the Supreme Court's explicit language. In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), the Court held that the informant privilege "must give way if the disclosure of an informer's identity... is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." The phrase "fair determination of a cause" encompasses suppression hearings, which are "causes" where the defendant seeks to vindicate Fourth Amendment rights.

The prosecution cites *United States v. However, the defense's argument *does* bear directly on the legality of the search. If the informant "set up" the defendant, this directly impacts the informant's reliability and the reasonableness of the officers' belief that criminal activity was afoot.

The defense presented "new information" that the informant was present during the transaction and that her belongings were found in the defendant's car. This evidence is not merely relevant to entrapment at trial—it is directly relevant to whether the informant was a credible and reliable source for probable cause purposes. As the Court stated in *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 64, "the desirability of informing the jury of the identity of the informant... may be greater than the desire to preserve the state's privilege for its own informant."

---

## IV. THE "NEW INFORMATION" UNDERMINED THE RELIABILITY FINDING UNDER HARRIS

The prosecution defends the trial court's finding that the informant was reliable based on "past tips," but this ignores the defense's central argument: the "new information" demonstrated that the informant's past reliability was compromised by her personal involvement in the crime.

In *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971), the Court held that an informant's reliability may be established through prior accurate tips. However, this presumption of reliability is not absolute. When new evidence emerges that the informant has a personal stake in the outcome—such as being present during the crime and having belongings in the defendant's car—the prior tips cannot sustain a finding of reliability without further inquiry.

The defense's argument that the informant "set him up" goes to the heart of the probable cause determination. If the informant manufactured or induced the crime, her tip was not a report of criminal activity but an orchestration of it. This directly undermines the basis for probable cause. The trial court erred in characterizing this as a "trial matter" when it is precisely the type of evidence that must be considered in determining whether the officers had a reasonable basis for their belief.

The prosecution argues that probable cause is judged based on information available to officers "at the time of the search." This is correct. Rather, the defense argues that the informant's identity was necessary to *challenge* the sufficiency of the information the officers relied upon. Without disclosure, the defendant could not demonstrate that the informant's tip was unreliable because the informant was personally involved in the crime.

---

## V. THE DEFENSE HAD A RIGHT TO PRESENT A DEFENSE UNDER HOLMES V. SOUTH CAROLINA

The prosecution dismisses the defense's citation to *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), as "inapposite." This characterization is incorrect. In *Holmes*, the Supreme Court held that a state rule excluding third-party guilt evidence violated the defendant's Sixth Amendment right to present a complete defense. The Court emphasized that "the Constitution prohibits the exclusion of defense evidence under rules that serve no legitimate purpose or that are arbitrary in all cases." *Holmes*, 547 U.S. at 331.

While *Holmes* addressed third-party guilt evidence at trial, its reasoning applies with equal force here. The defense was seeking to present evidence that directly challenged the probable cause basis for the search—the very issue before the court. Excluding this evidence because the proceeding was "dispositive" serves no legitimate purpose and was arbitrary. The defendant had a constitutional right to present a defense to the charge that the search was unlawful, and the informant's identity was essential to that defense.

The prosecution's argument that the Sixth Amendment right to present a defense "does not extend to disclosure of an informant's identity" conflates two distinct issues. The right to present a defense encompasses the right to present evidence relevant to the charged offense—in this case, the legality of the search. Disclosure of the informant's identity was the means by which the defense could present that relevant evidence.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand for an evidentiary hearing on the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The informant's identity was directly relevant to that challenge under *Roviaro*, and the "new information" presented by the defense was sufficient to undermine the reliability finding under *Harris*. The Appellant's attempt to use this new information to challenge the informant's reliability effectively introduced a factual dispute regarding the informant's role in the offense—an issue typically reserved for trial—thereby violating the spirit of the dispositive agreement.

The Appellant contends that the "new information" undermined the trial court's finding of reliability under *United States v. The defense's need to explore the informant's potential bias in this specific instance relates more to the defendant's guilt or innocence than to the legality of the officers' conduct. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 331 (2006), is misplaced. *Holmes* addresses the exclusion of third-party guilt evidence at trial, where the defendant's right to present a complete defense is at its zenith. **FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Appellant and the Appellee. Upon careful consideration of the record, the legal standards governing the disclosure of confidential informants, and the scope of dispositive motions to suppress, the Court affirms the judgment of the court of appeals.

**I. The Scope of the "Dispositive" Motion and the Admission of New Evidence**

The Appellant argues that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress governed only the *outcome* of the hearing (i.e., a plea if the State prevailed, dismissal if the defense prevailed) and did not constitute a waiver of the right to present new evidence relevant to the suppression issue. Specifically, the Appellant contends that because the "new information" regarding the confidential informant's presence and belongings was received only the day before the hearing, the trial court was obligated to consider it.

The Court is not persuaded. The record clearly establishes that the defense agreed to a "dispositive Motion to Suppress" where the State would abandon the enhancement and the court would determine sentencing based on a specific range if the motion was denied. As the prosecution correctly noted, such an agreement implies that the court is to litigate a specific issue—the legality of the search—and if the State prevails, the defendant pleads. The Appellant’s attempt to introduce "new information" that the informant "set him up" and that her belongings were found in the car fundamentally altered the nature of the hearing. This evidence does not merely challenge the legality of the search; it introduces a factual dispute regarding the informant's role in the offense and the defendant's intent—issues that are quintessentially matters for trial on the merits, not a suppression hearing.

As recognized in *State v. Smith*, 694 N.W.2d 409, 415 (Iowa 2005), when parties agree to a dispositive motion, the trial court acts within its discretion to limit the hearing to the agreed-upon parameters. The defense cannot agree to a streamlined proceeding to determine probable cause and then, after the evidence is presented, seek to expand the scope into a full-blown inquiry into entrapment or agency based on newly discovered facts. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to allow the defense to transform the suppression hearing into a trial on the defense of entrapment under the guise of a probable cause challenge.

**II. Reliability of the Informant and Probable Cause**

The Appellant contends that the "new information" undermined the trial court's finding of reliability under *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971). The Appellant argues that if the informant "set him up," this fact goes to her reliability and thus to the probable cause determination. This argument misinterprets the legal standard for informant reliability.

Under *Harris*, an informant's reliability may be established by evidence of the informant's past track record of providing accurate information. The trial court specifically found the informant reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This finding is sufficient to support probable cause. The Appellant's theory that the informant "set him up" implies that the informant was involved in the crime or instigated it. However, this does not render the information provided to the police unreliable; rather, it suggests the informant had a personal motive or was a participant. If the informant set the Appellant up, the information that the Appellant possessed drugs was likely factually accurate. Probable cause deals with the factual basis for the search, not the moral character or ultimate culpability of the informant.

Furthermore, under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), probable cause is based on the "totality of the circumstances" known to the officers at the time of the search. The officers relied on the informant's established history of accuracy. They were not aware of the alleged "set up" or the presence of the informant's belongings at the time they initiated the search. The Appellant cannot retroactively invalidate the officers' probable cause based on facts discovered later. The discovery of the cocaine confirmed the accuracy of the tip, reinforcing the probable cause determination.

**III. Disclosure of the Informant's Identity**

The Appellant relies on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), arguing that the informant's identity was essential to a fair determination of the motion to suppress. *Roviaro* establishes that disclosure is required when the informant's identity is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." However, this standard is not met in the context of a suppression hearing limited to probable cause.

The defense sought disclosure to prove that the informant "set him up" and that her belongings were in the car. As discussed, this evidence relates to the defense of entrapment or the agency of the informant—issues of guilt or innocence. It does not bear on the legality of the search. As held in *United States v. Rivera*, 649 F.3d 120, 125 (2d Cir. 2011), "disclosure of an informant’s identity is required only when it directly bears on the legality of the search or seizure; it is not required for guilt-related defenses such as entrapment, which are reserved for trial." Because the informant's reliability for probable cause purposes was independently established by her past tips, and the "new information" pertained solely to the informant's potential involvement in the crime, the trial court properly denied the motion to disclose.

**IV. The Right to Present a Defense**

Finally, the Appellant's reliance on *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 331 (2006), is misplaced. *Holmes* addresses a defendant's Sixth Amendment right to present a defense at trial, specifically regarding the exclusion of third-party guilt evidence. A suppression hearing is a distinct procedural mechanism focused on the reasonableness of the officer's conduct, not the defendant's culpability. The trial court's refusal to allow the defense to expand the dispositive hearing to include the informant's motives did not violate the Appellant's constitutional rights. The Appellant retained the right to raise these defenses at trial, but by agreeing to the dispositive motion, he waived that opportunity in exchange for the State's concession on sentencing range.

**V. Conclusion**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The "dispositive" agreement properly limited the scope of the hearing to the issue of probable cause. The reliability of the informant was adequately supported by evidence of past tips under *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573 (1971). The Appellant's "new information" regarding the informant's belongings and presence at the scene pertained to issues of entrapment and guilt, which were outside the scope of the agreed-upon suppression hearing. Therefore, the judgment of the court of appeals is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the court of appeals be, and the same hereby is, affirmed.
