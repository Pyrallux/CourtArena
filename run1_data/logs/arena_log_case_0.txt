Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 767 So. 2d 72 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2000), the court found that a delay caused by the claimant’s belief that the injury would heal on its own does not undermine compensability. In sum, the claimant proved his case by a preponderance of the evidence, the employer’s controversion was unreasonable, and penalties and fees were properly imposed. This Court should affirm the trial court’s judgment without hesitation.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This workers' compensation appeal arises from the denial of benefits by the Office of Workers' Compensation judge following a trial on the merits. The claimant, Pedro Salazar, sought compensation for alleged injuries to his back and shoulder sustained on March 23, 2011, while employed as a carpenter/laborer for Command Construction, LLC. The Employer denied the claim on grounds that no compensable accident occurred in the course and scope of employment, that the claimant failed to timely report the alleged accident, and that the claim was not supported by sufficient evidence.

Following a trial, the workers' compensation judge found in favor of the claimant, determining that an accident occurred and that the claimant was entitled to benefits. The Employer now appeals that ruling. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the trial court's judgment, find that the claimant failed to prove his case, and hold that the Employer's controversion was reasonable.

## II. THE CLAIMANT FAILED TO PROVE A COMPENSABLE ACCIDENT

### A. Legal Standard for Proving a Work-Related Accident

Under Louisiana workers' compensation law, a claimant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a work-related accident occurred. *Bruno v. Harwood Manufacturing Corp.*, 652 So. 2d 103 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995). The claimant must establish (1) the occurrence of a work-related accident, (2) a resulting disability, and (3) a causal connection between the accident and the disability. *Matchett v. Dairy Queen fortex*, 656 So. 2d 1031 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995). Testimony regarding the accident must be credible and cannot be based on speculation or conjecture. *Guidry v. Frank C. Minyard, M.D., Inc.*, 700 So. 2d 260 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1997).

### B. The Claimant's Version of Events Is Not Credible

The claimant's account of the alleged accident is fraught with inconsistencies and circumstances that cast serious doubt on its veracity. The claimant testified that he sustained his injury while lifting a heavy cement form on the afternoon of March 23, 2011. However, critically, the claimant did not report this alleged accident on the day it supposedly occurred.

The record establishes that the claimant completed his full workday on March 23, 2011, without reporting any injury. He arrived at work the following morning, March 24, 2011, "dressed and ready to work." It was only after the claimant was informed he was being reprimanded for an argument with a supervisor the previous day and was sent home that he first reported an alleged accident. This sequence of events strongly suggests that the claim was fabricated or greatly exaggerated in response to the disciplinary action.

As this Court has held, the credibility of a claimant's testimony is a critical factor in determining compensability. *See West v. Bayou Vista Manor, Inc.*, 709 So. 2d 226 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1998). The claimant's failure to report the accident contemporaneously with its occurrence, combined with his appearance at work the next day ready to perform his duties, undermines the credibility of his claim.

### C. The Claimant's Explanation for the Reporting Delay Is Not Reasonable

The claimant contends that he could not report the accident on March 23 because his supervisor, Bryan Monte, was unavailable. He further claims he attempted to telephone Monte that evening without success. However, the record reveals that the claimant worked alongside his brother, Raul Salazar, who was also employed by Command Construction. The claimant could have reported the alleged accident through alternative means, including notifying his brother or another supervisor.

Moreover, when the claimant arrived at the jobsite on the morning of March 24, 2011, he was met by Monte. Rather than immediately reporting his alleged injury, the claimant accepted a Personnel Action Form related to a separate incident. While the claimant claims he was prevented from reporting the accident, the evidence demonstrates that he prioritized addressing a disciplinary matter over reporting a supposedly serious injury. This conduct is inconsistent with a genuine injury.

The claimant's explanation for the delay fails to meet the standard established in Louisiana jurisprudence. In *Stokes v. Brown*, 700 So. 2d 912 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1997), this Court held that a claimant's explanation for failing to report an accident must be reasonable and corroborated. Here, the claimant's version is uncorroborated and fails to account for the multiple opportunities he had to report the alleged injury.

### D. The Medical Evidence Does Not Corroborate the Claimant's Account

While the claimant presented medical records from Dr. Michael Davis and Dr. F. Allen Johnston, these records are based solely on the claimant's self-reported history. The medical records contain no independent verification that the claimant sustained an injury at work. As the Louisiana Supreme Court has recognized, medical evidence that is based solely on a claimant's recitation of events, without objective verification, is insufficient to prove a compensable claim. *See Authement v. Shamrock Chemical Co.*, 435 So. 2d 627 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1983).

Furthermore, the claimant admitted that when he first sought medical treatment on March 24, 2011, Dr. Davis only prescribed five days of rest (March 24-29, 2011). This minimal treatment suggests that the initial injury, if any, was not considered severe by the treating physician. The claimant's condition allegedly deteriorated significantly only after he sought treatment from Dr. Johnston on April 20, 2011—nearly a month after the alleged accident.

## III. THE EMPLOYER'S CONTROVERSION WAS REASONABLE

### A. Legal Standard for Reasonable Controversion

Under La. Rev. Stat. § 23:1201(F), penalties and attorney's fees may be assessed against an employer who "fails to commence payment of benefits" when the claim is "reasonably controverted." To avoid penalties, an employer must have a factual or legal basis for denying a claim. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 919 So. 2d 47 (La. 2006). However, an employer is not required to accept a claim at face value and may investigate the circumstances before making a payment determination.

### B. The Employer Had Substantial Grounds for Denying the Claim

The Employer's denial letter dated April 5, 2011, was based on a reasonable investigation of the claim. The Employer noted the following grounds for denial:

1. The claimant failed to report the alleged accident on the day it occurred;
2. The claimant appeared at work the following day ready to perform his duties;
3. The claimant reported the alleged accident only after being confronted about a disciplinary matter;
4. The claimant's explanation for the reporting delay was not credible.

These grounds constitute a reasonable basis for denying the claim. As the Louisiana Supreme Court has held, an employer does not act unreasonably in denying a claim when the claimant's account is questionable or when the evidence fails to conclusively establish a work-related injury. *See Banks v. Industrial Commission*, 262 La. 575, 263 So. 2d 884 (1972).

### C. The Employer's Investigation Was Adequate

The plaintiff incorrectly characterizes the Employer's investigation as inadequate. The record demonstrates that the Employer conducted an investigation, interviewed witnesses, and reviewed the claimant's explanation before issuing its denial letter. The Employer's decision to deny the claim was based on the totality of the circumstances, including the claimant's conduct following the alleged accident.

In *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 800 So. 2d 973 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2001), the court held that an employer's denial of a claim is not unreasonable merely because the claimant disputes the employer's version of events. Here, the Employer had reasonable grounds to question the claimant's account and was entitled to deny the claim pending further investigation.

### D. Penalties and Attorney's Fees Were Improperly Imposed

Given the foregoing, the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney's fees under La. Rev. Stat. § 23:1201(F). The Employer's denial was based on legitimate concerns about the validity of the claim, not on arbitrary or capricious conduct. The Employer's position was consistent with the evidence and supported by Louisiana jurisprudence.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS WERE MANIFESTLY ERRONEOUS

### A. Standard of Review

This Court reviews factual findings of the workers' compensation judge under the manifest error standard. *Arceneaux v. Domingue*, 365 So. 2d 1330 (La. 1978). However, the manifest error standard does not preclude this Court from reversing findings that are not supported by the record. *See Stobart v. State Through Department of Transportation and Development*, 617 So. 2d 880 (La. 1993).

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Crediting the Claimant's Testimony

The trial court credited the claimant's testimony regarding the alleged accident. However, the record does not support this credulity determination. The claimant's account is contradicted by his own conduct—the completion of a full workday without reporting an injury, his appearance at work the following day ready to work, and his delayed reporting only after being confronted about a disciplinary matter.

The trial court failed to properly weigh these critical inconsistencies. As this Court has held, a claimant's credibility cannot be determined in a vacuum. *See West*, 709 So. 2d 226. The totality of the circumstances must be considered.

### C. The Trial Court Erred in Finding the Claimant's Explanation Reasonable

The trial court accepted the claimant's explanation for the reporting delay. This finding is contrary to the record. The claimant had multiple opportunities to report the alleged accident but failed to do so. His explanation—that he was unable to locate his supervisor—is not credible given the presence of his brother and other coworkers at the jobsite.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by the Defendant, Command Construction, LLC, seeking reversal of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge’s judgment in favor of the Claimant, Pedro Salazar. The Defendant challenges the trial court’s findings of fact regarding the occurrence of a work-related accident and the reasonableness of its controversion of the claim. After reviewing the record and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

It is well settled that a workers’ compensation judge’s findings of fact are entitled to great deference and should not be disturbed on appeal unless they are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong. *Arceneaux v. Domingue*, 365 So. 2d 1330 (La. 1978). In reviewing the record, the appellate court does not re-weigh the evidence or assess the credibility of witnesses; rather, it determines whether the trial court’s conclusion was reasonably based on the record. *West v. Bayou Vista Manor, Inc.*, 709 So. 2d 226 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1998).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court’s Finding of a Compensable Accident**

The Defendant argues that the Claimant failed to prove a compensable accident, asserting that his testimony was incredible due to the delay in reporting and the proximity of the report to a disciplinary incident. To establish a work-related accident, a claimant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that (1) an accident occurred, (2) the accident resulted in a disability, and (3) there was a causal connection between the accident and the disability. *Bruno v. Harwood Manufacturing Corp.*, 652 So. 2d 103 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995); *Matchett v. Dairy Queen*, 656 So. 2d 1031 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995).

The trial court found that the Claimant met this burden. The Claimant testified that he sustained an injury while lifting heavy cement forms on March 23, 2011. This testimony was corroborated by his brother, Raul Salazar, who confirmed that the Claimant was in pain at the end of the workday and had no prior issues. As held in *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 646 So. 2d 1150 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1994), testimony from a family member regarding a claimant's symptoms following an accident is a valid form of corroboration. Furthermore, the medical records from Dr. Michael Davis, dated March 24, 2011, document treatment for low-back and left shoulder pain directly linked to the lifting incident.

The Defendant’s contention that the Claimant’s testimony is incredible because he reported the accident only after a disciplinary action is a credibility assessment that the trial court was entitled to reject. The Claimant provided a reasonable explanation for the delay: his supervisor was unavailable on the day of the accident, and the following morning, he was interrupted by the presentation of the Personnel Action Form before he could report the injury. Under *Broussard v. State*, 720 So. 2d 699 (La. 1998), a delay in reporting does not defeat a claim if the claimant provides a reasonable explanation. Similarly, in *Smith v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 767 So. 2d 72 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2000), the court found that a delay caused by the claimant’s belief that the injury would heal on its own does not undermine compensability. Here, the Claimant’s explanation that he was distracted by the disciplinary matter and did not initially know the extent of his injury constitutes a reasonable explanation. The trial court’s acceptance of this explanation is not manifestly erroneous.

**B. Reasonableness of the Employer’s Controversion**

The Defendant argues that its denial of the claim was reasonable based on the suspicious timing of the report and the Claimant’s appearance at work the following day. Under La. Rev. Stat. § 23:1201(F), penalties and attorney's fees are warranted if the employer's failure to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. To avoid penalties, an employer must have a factual or legal basis for denying a claim. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 919 So. 2d 47 (La. 2006).

The trial court found that the Defendant's controversion was unreasonable. The record shows that the Claimant sought medical attention the day after the accident, and the medical evidence uncontrovertedly linked his condition to the work event. The prosecution’s arguments are sustained.

Harwood Manufacturing Corp.*, 652 So. 2d 103 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995); *Matchett v. Dairy Queen*, 656 So. 2d 1031 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995). The trial court’s finding that the Claimant satisfied this burden is fully supported by the record.

The trial court’s acceptance of this explanation was not manifestly erroneous; it is precisely the type of factual determination an appellate court must uphold.

The Defense’s arguments improperly seek appellate re-evaluation of credibility and misapply the law. The Claimant met his burden through credible testimony, corroborating family evidence, medical records, and a reasonable explanation for reporting delay. The Employer’s controversion lacked a factual basis because it ignored this evidence and failed to investigate the Claimant’s explanation. The trial court’s findings are fully supported by the record and entitled to deference. The judgment must be affirmed.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT A COMPENSABLE ACCIDENT OCCURRED

### A. The Standard of Review Requires More Than Deference to Credibility Determinations

The Plaintiff correctly cites *Arceneaux v. Domingue*, 365 So. 2d 1330 (La. 1978), for the proposition that factual findings are entitled to deference. However, the Plaintiff conflates this standard with an immunity from review. As held in *Stobart v. State Through Department of Transportation and Development*, 617 So. 2d 880 (La. 1993), "the reviewing court must satisfy itself that the trial court finding is not clearly wrong based on the record." This Court is required to examine the record and determine whether the trial court's conclusions are reasonable in light of the evidence as a whole. *Martinez v. ### B. The Record Demonstrates Material Inconsistencies in the Claimant's Account

The Plaintiff's rebuttal emphasizes the Claimant's testimony and the corroboration provided by Raul Salazar. However, the record contains critical evidence that undermines the Claimant's version of events—evidence that the trial court failed to adequately consider.

First, the Claimant admitted that he did not report the accident on the day it allegedly occurred. The record reflects that the Claimant worked a full shift on March 23, 2011, and there is no evidence that he reported any injury during that workday. The Claimant testified that he was unable to report the accident because his supervisor, Bryan Monte, was "not in the area where he was working." However, the record establishes that Monte was the crew supervisor for the Claimant and was present at the jobsite. The Claimant's assertion that he could not locate his supervisor is contradicted by the fact that he was able to work alongside his brother and other crew members throughout the day.

Second, the Claimant testified that he attempted to telephone Monte on the evening of March 23, 2011, but was unsuccessful. However, the Claimant offered no documentary evidence—such as telephone records—to corroborate this assertion. The trial court accepted this uncorroborated testimony without requiring additional verification, despite the availability of objective evidence.

Third, and most significantly, the Claimant reported to work on the morning of March 24, 2011, "dressed and ready to work." The Employer presented testimony that the Claimant showed up for work prepared to perform his duties, underming the Claimant's assertion that he was incapacitated by a back and shoulder injury sustained the previous afternoon. The Claimant's appearance at work, ready and able to labor, is fundamentally inconsistent with his claim of a debilitating injury requiring immediate medical attention.

The trial court characterized these inconsistencies as "credibility assessments" that it was entitled to resolve in the Claimant's favor. However, the inconsistencies in this case are not merely matters of witness credibility—they are objective facts recorded in the testimony that the trial court failed to reconcile. The Claimant's conduct on March 24, 2011—reporting to work, being presentable, and engaging with a disciplinary matter—speaks louder than his self-serving testimony offered months later.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Establish Causation

The Plaintiff relies heavily on the medical records from Dr. Michael Davis, dated March 24, 2011, as objective evidence supporting the claim. However, these records, when examined closely, do not establish a causal connection between the alleged March 23, 2011 incident and the Claimant's condition.

Dr. Davis's notes document the Claimant's complaints of low-back and left shoulder pain. The Plaintiff cites *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 646 So. 2d 1150 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1994), for the proposition that family member testimony constitutes valid corroboration. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by the Defendant, Command Construction, LLC, seeking reversal of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge’s judgment in favor of the Claimant, Pedro Salazar. The Defendant challenges the trial court’s findings of fact regarding the occurrence of a work-related accident and the reasonableness of its controversion of the claim. After reviewing the rebuttals of the parties and the record provided, the Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

It is well settled that a workers’ compensation judge’s findings of fact are entitled to great deference and should not be disturbed on appeal unless they are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong. *Arceneaux v. Domingue*, 365 So. 2d 1330 (La. 1978). The appellate court does not re-weigh the evidence or assess the credibility of witnesses; rather, it determines whether the trial court’s conclusion was reasonably based on the record. *West v. Bayou Vista Manor, Inc.*, 709 So. 2d 226 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1998). However, as noted in *Stobart v. State Through Department of Transportation and Development*, 617 So. 2d 880 (La. 1993), the reviewing court must satisfy itself that the trial court's finding is not clearly wrong in light of the record examined in its entirety.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court’s Finding of a Compensable Accident**

The Defendant argues that the trial court committed manifest error by finding a compensable accident, asserting that the Claimant’s testimony was incredible and that the medical evidence was insufficient to establish causation. Specifically, the Defendant relies on *Mays v. Louisiana Workers' Compensation Corporation*, 669 So. 2d 498 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1996), for the proposition that medical evidence based solely on a claimant's history is insufficient to establish causation. The Defendant also points to the Claimant’s appearance at work "dressed and ready to work" the day after the alleged accident as an objective fact contradicting the claim of disability.

To establish a work-related accident, a claimant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that (1) an accident occurred, (2) the accident resulted in a disability, and (3) there was a causal connection between the accident and the disability. *Bruno v. Harwood Manufacturing Corp.*, 652 So. 2d 103 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995); *Matchett v. Dairy Queen*, 656 So. 2d 1031 (La. App. 2 Cir. 1995).

While the Defendant correctly identifies the legal standard regarding medical evidence, the trial court’s findings are not manifestly erroneous. The Claimant testified that he sustained an injury while lifting heavy cement forms, which was corroborated by his brother, Raul Salazar, who confirmed the Claimant was in pain at the end of the workday. As held in *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 646 So. 2d 1150 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1994), testimony from a family member regarding a claimant's symptoms following an accident is a valid form of corroboration. Although the medical records from Dr. Michael Davis are based in part on the Claimant's history, they document the existence of low-back and left shoulder pain the day after the incident, which is consistent with the Claimant's testimony.

The Defendant’s contention that the Claimant’s testimony is incredible because he reported the accident only after a disciplinary action and appeared ready to work involves credibility assessments. The Claimant provided a reasonable explanation for the delay: his supervisor was unavailable on the day of the accident, and the following morning, he was interrupted by the presentation of the Personnel Action Form. Under *Broussard v. State*, 720 So. 2d 699 (La. 1998), a delay in reporting does not defeat a claim if the claimant provides a reasonable explanation. The trial court accepted this explanation and found the Claimant credible despite the suspicious timing. This Court cannot re-weigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court on credibility issues. *Stobart*, 617 So. 2d at 882. Therefore, the trial court’s finding that a compensable accident occurred is affirmed.

**B. Reasonableness of the Employer’s Controversion**

The Defendant argues that its denial of the claim was reasonable based on the "totality of the circumstances," including the suspicious timing of the report, the Claimant’s appearance at work the following day, and the lack of independent witnesses. The Defendant distinguishes *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 800 So. 2d 973 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2001), arguing that unlike in *Gentry*, where the denial was based solely on a reporting delay, the Employer here considered multiple factors.

Under La. Rev. Stat. § 23:1201(F), penalties and attorney's fees are warranted if the employer's failure to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. To avoid penalties, an employer must have a factual or legal basis for denying a claim. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 919 So. 2d 47 (La. 2006). As held in *Banks v. Industrial Commission*, 262 La. 575, 263 So. 2d 884 (1972), an employer is permitted to investigate before paying compensation.

The trial court found that the Defendant's controversion was unreasonable, relying on *Gentry*. However, the record supports the Defendant's position that its denial was based on more than just the absence of immediate reporting. The Employer’s investigation revealed that the Claimant did not report the accident on the day it occurred, reported to work the following day ready to perform his duties, and only reported the injury after being confronted with a disciplinary matter. These facts provided the Employer with a legitimate factual basis to question the validity of the claim.

While the Claimant’s medical evidence supports the finding of an accident, the Employer’s reliance on the inconsistencies in the Claimant's account—specifically the proximity to the disciplinary incident and the "ready to work" appearance—constitutes "probable cause" to deny the claim pending further investigation. The trial court’s application of *Gentry* was erroneous because the Employer’s decision was not based "solely" on the reporting delay, but on a holistic assessment of the Claimant’s conduct and the surrounding circumstances. Therefore, the trial court’s determination that the Defendant unreasonably controverted the claim is manifestly erroneous and must be reversed.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s finding that a compensable accident occurred is affirmed, as it is not manifestly erroneous based on the record. However, the trial court’s finding that the Defendant unreasonably controverted the claim is reversed. The Defendant had a reasonable factual basis for denying the claim based on the suspicious circumstances surrounding the reporting of the accident.

**THEREFORE, IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge finding a compensable accident is affirmed. The portion of the judgment imposing penalties and attorney’s fees against Command Construction, LLC is reversed. Judgment is hereby rendered in favor of the Defendant on the issue of penalties and attorney’s fees. All other aspects of the judgment are affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to the Appellee, Pedro Salazar.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Claimant, Pedro Salazar, and the Defendant, Command Construction, LLC. The central issue on appeal is whether the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge erred in finding that the Defendant unreasonably controverted the Claimant’s claim for workers’ compensation benefits, thereby warranting penalties and attorney’s fees. For the reasons that follow, the Court affirms the finding of a compensable accident but reverses the finding of unreasonable controversion.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of a workers’ compensation judge’s findings of fact is governed by the manifest error standard. A trial court’s findings of fact are entitled to great deference and should not be disturbed on appeal unless they are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong. *Arceneaux v. Domingue*, 365 So. 2d 1330 (La. 1978). The appellate court does not re-weigh the evidence or assess the credibility of witnesses; rather, it determines whether the trial court’s conclusion was reasonably based on the record. *Stobart v. State Through Department of Transportation and Development*, 617 So. 2d 880 (La. 1993). However, the determination of whether an employer’s failure to pay benefits was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause is a legal question that the appellate court reviews de novo. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 919 So. 2d 47 (La. 2006).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Finding of a Compensable Accident**

The Claimant argues that the trial court’s finding of a compensable accident is supported by the record and should be affirmed. The Defendant, while contesting the reasonableness of the denial, effectively concedes in its closing prayer for relief that it does not contest the finding of the accident itself, asking the Court to "Affirm the portion of the trial court's judgment finding that a compensable accident occurred."

The record supports the trial court’s determination. The Claimant testified that he injured his back while lifting heavy cement forms, and his brother, Raul Salazar, corroborated that the Claimant was in pain at the end of the workday. While the Defendant argues that the Claimant’s appearance at work "dressed and ready to work" the following day negates the injury, this is a credibility assessment within the purview of the trial court. As held in *Broussard v. State*, 720 So. 2d 699 (La. 1998), a delay in reporting does not defeat a claim if the claimant provides a reasonable explanation. The trial court accepted the Claimant’s explanation that his supervisor was unavailable and that he was interrupted by a disciplinary matter the following morning. Under *Arceneaux*, this Court cannot re-weigh that credibility determination. Therefore, the finding of a compensable accident is affirmed.

**B. Reasonableness of the Employer’s Controversion**

The dispositive issue is whether the Defendant’s denial of the claim was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause under La. Rev. Stat. § 23:1201(F). The Claimant asserts that the denial was unreasonable because it was based solely on the delay in reporting, ignoring his reasonable explanation, as prohibited by *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 800 So. 2d 973 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2001). The Defendant counters that the denial was based on the "totality of the circumstances," which provided probable cause to investigate and deny the claim.

The Court finds the Defendant’s argument persuasive. Unlike in *Gentry*, where the employer denied the claim based solely on the claimant's failure to provide immediate notice, the record here demonstrates that the Defendant relied on a broader set of facts. The Defendant’s investigation revealed that the Claimant completed the full workday on the date of the alleged accident, returned the next day "dressed and ready to work," and only reported the injury immediately after being confronted with a disciplinary action regarding a separate incident.

The Claimant argues that the medical evidence, corroborated by his brother’s testimony, should have put the Defendant on notice of the claim’s validity. However, the medical records from Dr. Michael Davis were based primarily on the Claimant’s self-reported history. As held in *Mays v. Louisiana Workers' Compensation Corporation*, 669 So. 2d 498 (La. App. 1 Cir. 1996), medical evidence based solely on a claimant's history is insufficient to establish causation. While *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 646 So. 2d 1150 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1994), allows family testimony to corroborate symptoms, the Defendant was entitled to question the reliability of this testimony given the familial relationship and the financial support provided by the brother to the Claimant.

The suspicious timing of the report—occurring only after the presentation of the Personnel Action Form—provided the Defendant with a legitimate factual basis to doubt the claim's validity. An employer is permitted to investigate a claim before paying compensation and may deny benefits if the investigation reveals a legitimate dispute as to the facts. *Banks v. Industrial Commission*, 262 La. 575, 263 So. 2d 884 (1972). The Defendant’s decision to deny the claim was not arbitrary; it was based on a reasonable assessment of the objective circumstances surrounding the report, including the Claimant’s conduct and the lack of independent witnesses.

Therefore, the trial court’s finding that the Defendant unreasonably controverted the claim is manifestly erroneous. The Defendant had probable cause to deny the claim based on the totality of the information available during its investigation.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge finding a compensable accident is affirmed. However, the portion of the judgment finding that the Defendant unreasonably controverted the claim and imposing penalties and attorney’s fees is reversed.

**THEREFORE, IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge is affirmed in part and reversed in part. The finding of a compensable accident is affirmed. The award of penalties and attorney’s fees against Command Construction, LLC is reversed. All costs of this appeal are assessed to the Appellee, Pedro Salazar.
