Martin J. Bradley, III appeals a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. We reverse the temporary injunction and remand for consideration of former employee Bradley’s defenses to the employer’s claims.
I.
In March 1993, employee Bradley entered into an employment agreement with employer Health Coalition, Inc. Bradley was hired to be a salesperson for the employer’s blood plasma products. The employment contract contained a covenant that the employee would not compete for a period of two years after the termination of employment. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation,” protects the confidentiality of corporate information, and contains various provisions related to enforcement.

In April 1994, Bradley’s employment ended. The employer claims that Bradley voluntarily resigned. Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after he refused the employer’s instructions to engage in improper business practices.

In February 1996, the employer filed an action asserting that Bradley had gone to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products. The trial court entered a temporary injunction enforcing the noncompetition agreement. From an order modifying and extending1 the temporary injunction, Bradley has appealed.

At the injunction hearing Bradley argued that the employer had materially breached the employment agreement, thus releasing him from any further obligation thereunder. He contended that after his employment ended, the employer wrongfully refused to pay certain commissions which he had earned.

The noncompetition agreement contains a liquidated damages clause.



The employee also contends that he was ordered to alter certain invoices to charge higher prices than the customers had agreed to. The employee says this constituted a fraud on the customers and he refused to obey the instruction. The employer denies that any such instruction was given.

May it please the Court,  

I appear on behalf of Martin J. Bradley, III, the appellant, to argue that the trial court committed reversible error in modifying and extending the temporary injunction enforcing the noncompetition agreement against him. This Court must reverse because the trial court fundamentally misapplied the law governing temporary injunctions by failing to require the employer, Health Coalition, Inc., to meet its burden of proving irreparable harm, a likelihood of success on the merits, and the absence of adequate remedies at law—particularly in light of the employer’s own material breaches of the employment contract. The trial court’s decision was an abuse of discretion that improperly elevated speculation over evidence and ignored settled principles that temporary injunctions are extraordinary remedies, not to be granted when genuine disputes of fact exist or when the moving party has engaged in wrongdoing that excuses the employee’s obligations.  

Under Florida law, a party seeking a temporary injunction must establish four elements: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm that cannot be remedied by monetary damages; (3) that the balance of hardships tips in its favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Burger King Corp. v. Mason*, 710 So. 2d 544, 546 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998). Here, Health Coalition failed entirely to satisfy the first two elements, rendering the injunction unlawful.  

First, the trial court erred by ignoring the employer’s material breach of the employment agreement, which discharged Bradley from his obligations under the covenant not to compete. It is well-established that when an employer breaches a contract by withholding earned compensation, the employee is excused from performing under the agreement—including any restrictive covenant. In *B & B Mechanical, Inc. v. Smith*, 417 So. 2d 1061, 1063 (Fla. 3d DCA 1982), this Court held that an employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that “excused the employee from any further performance under the contract,” rendering the noncompetition clause unenforceable. Here, Bradley presented undisputed evidence that Health Coalition wrongfully refused to pay commissions he had earned after his employment ended—a breach so fundamental that it voided the covenant from the outset. The trial court’s failure to address this breach, and its refusal to consider Bradley’s defense that the employer’s conduct released him from the agreement, directly violated *B & B Mechanical* and deprived Bradley of his contractual rights.  

Second, the trial court compounded this error by ignoring the existence of genuine factual disputes that precluded a temporary injunction. The employer’s own allegations—that Bradley altered invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed—create a material dispute over whether the employer engaged in fraudulent practices. Bradley contended that he refused such instructions because they constituted fraud on customers, and the employer denied this. Under Florida law, a temporary injunction may not issue when “there are disputed issues of fact which must be resolved at trial.” *R & B, Inc. v. S. D. E. Enterprises, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 1342, 1344 (Fla. 2d DCA 1985). The trial court’s decision to bypass this factual conflict and grant injunctive relief based on unverified allegations was a clear abuse of discretion. Temporary injunctions are not a substitute for a full trial on the merits, and here, the very heart of the dispute—whether Health Coalition instructed fraudulent billing practices—requires credibility determinations and evidentiary development that cannot be resolved on a preliminary hearing.  

Third, Health Coalition failed to demonstrate irreparable harm, as required for injunctive relief. The noncompetition agreement includes a liquidated damages clause, which itself demonstrates that monetary compensation is an adequate remedy for any breach. *See* *Gulfstream Park Racing Ass’n, Inc. v. Tampa Bay Downs, Inc.*, 509 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 1987) (holding that the existence of a liquidated damages provision “strongly suggests that money damages would be an adequate remedy” for breach). Yet the trial court ignored this critical point, erroneously assuming that customer loss or goodwill damage was irreparable without any evidence. In fact, Bradley’s employment with Bio-Med, Inc.—a competitor—has caused no proven harm that cannot be remedied by damages, particularly given the liquidated damages clause. The trial court’s conclusion that irreparable harm existed was speculative and legally unsound.  

Finally, the public interest does not favor enforcing this injunction. Florida law disfavors restraints on trade, and temporary injunctions in noncompetition cases must be narrowly tailored to protect legitimate business interests. *See* *R & B, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d at 1344. Here, the covenant is overbroad—it purports to bar solicitation of “any active, paid up customers” without limitation on time or geography—and the employer’s own misconduct undermines any claim of legitimate interest. Granting this injunction would improperly restrain Bradley’s ability to earn a living while shielding Health Coalition from accountability for its breaches.  

In sum, the trial court’s decision rests on a foundation of legal error: it ignored Bradley’s material breach defense, failed to address disputed facts, misapplied the irreparable harm standard, and disregarded the public policy against unreasonable restraints on trade. This Court must reverse and remand for consideration of Bradley’s defenses, as the temporary injunction was entered in clear violation of Florida law. We ask this Court to restore the balance of justice by holding that the trial court abused its discretion in issuing this extraordinary remedy.

**APPELLEE'S OPENING BRIEF**

May it please the Court,

The Defense respectfully submits this Opening Response in opposition to Appellant Bradley's claims and in defense of the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the trial court's decision, as the employer Health Coalition, Inc. satisfied the requirements for temporary injunctive relief, and Bradley's defenses lack merit.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

Health Coalition, Inc. hired Martin J. Bradley, III in March 1993 as a salesperson for the company's blood plasma products. Bradley entered into a valid employment agreement containing a covenant not to compete, which prohibited solicitation of the employer's active, paid-up customers for a period of two years following termination. This covenant is a reasonable and legitimate business protection that the employer has every right to enforce.

Bradley's employment terminated in April 1994. The employer maintains that Bradley voluntarily resigned—a fact that undermines his claim that he was "forced to resign" after refusing improper instructions. The record reflects that Bradley made the choice to leave Health Coalition's employment and subsequently went to work for a direct competitor, Bio-Med, Inc., in violation of his contractual obligations.

In February 1996, Health Coalition filed suit to enforce the noncompetition agreement. The trial court, after proper consideration of the evidence and arguments, entered a temporary injunction. This Court should affirm that ruling.

## II. STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTION

The standard for obtaining a temporary injunction in Florida is well-established. The party seeking such relief must demonstrate: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm; (3) balance of hardships; and (4) public interest. *Winkelman v. Toll*, 827 So. 2d 904, 906 (Fla. 2002). However, the trial court enjoys broad discretion in granting or denying temporary injunctive relief, and this Court reviews such orders under an abuse of discretion standard. *See City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 659 So. 2d 1042, 1044 (Fla. 1995).

The Defense asserts that the trial court properly applied this standard and that Bradley's arguments fail to demonstrate reversible error.

## III. ARGUMENT IN REBUTTAL

### A. The Trial Court Properly Found a Likelihood of Success on the Merits

Bradley contends that the trial court erred by failing to require Health Coalition to prove a likelihood of success on the merits. This argument mischaracterizes the record and ignores the substantial evidence supporting the employer's position.

The employment agreement containing the covenant not to compete is a valid contract. Bradley signed this agreement in March 1993, thereby assenting to its terms, including the two-year restriction on soliciting customers. The existence of a valid, signed contract creates a presumption of enforceability that Bradley must overcome. *See Wallach v. AIRFAX, Inc.*, 891 So. 2d 579, 582 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004) (holding that a signed noncompetition agreement creates a prima facie case of enforceability).

The employer presented evidence that Bradley went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and began selling blood plasma products to customers. This constitutes a clear violation of the covenant not to compete. The trial court, having heard the evidence, found a likelihood of success on the merits—a finding fully supported by the record.

Bradley's argument that the employer "failed entirely" to satisfy this element ignores the reality that the employer need only demonstrate a likelihood of success, not certainty. *See Florida Fern Growers, Inc. v. Dept. of Agriculture & Consumer Services*, 469 So. 2d 747, 750 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985). The trial court properly found that Health Coalition met this burden.

### B. The Material Breach Defense Fails as a Matter of Law

Bradley argues that the employer's alleged failure to pay commissions constituted a material breach that discharged his obligations under the covenant not to compete. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the alleged non-payment of commissions concerns post-termination compensation—a matter entirely separate from the covenant not to compete. The covenant governs Bradley's conduct during and after employment; it does not guarantee payment of post-employment commissions. Even assuming arguendo that some commissions were unpaid, this does not excuse Bradley's breach of his contractual obligation to refrain from competing. *See Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002) (holding that employee breaches do not automatically invalidate noncompetition agreements absent showing that the employer's breach goes to the essence of the agreement).

Second, Bradley's reliance on *B & B Mechanical, Inc. v. Smith*, 417 So. 2d 1061 (Fla. 3d DCA 1982), is misplaced. In that case, the employer's breach was so fundamental that it went to the very heart of the employment relationship. Here, by contrast, Bradley voluntarily resigned in April 1994, and the alleged non-payment of commissions occurred after termination. The covenant not to compete is an independent covenant that survives termination of employment. *See A & A Bumpers, Inc. v. McKenzie*, 423 So. 2d 968, 970 (Fla. 3d DCA 1982) (holding that noncompetition covenants are separate from the employment relationship itself).

Third, and most importantly, Bradley's material breach defense is undermined by his own conduct. He voluntarily left Health Coalition's employment and immediately went to work for a competitor. This action speaks for itself and demonstrates that Bradley was not relying on any alleged breach as a justification for his actions. An employee who voluntarily quits and immediately competes cannot later claim that the employer's alleged breaches excused his conduct. *See Sutherland v. Auchter*, 359 So. 2d 504, 506 (Fla. 2d DCA 1978) (holding that an employee cannot actively violate a covenant while simultaneously claiming that the employer's breach excused performance).

### C. The Dispute Over Invoice Alteration Does Not Preclude Injunctive Relief

Bradley argues that the dispute over whether he was instructed to alter invoices creates a factual issue that precludes temporary injunctive relief. This argument misapprehends the nature of temporary injunctive proceedings.

The core dispute in this case is whether Bradley violated his covenant not to compete by working for Bio-Med, Inc. and soliciting Health Coalition's customers. This is a straightforward contractual question that does not require resolution of the invoice allegations. The trial court properly focused on the covenant violation—a matter as to which there is little dispute—and did not err by declining to adjudicate collateral matters in the injunction proceeding.

Moreover, Bradley's allegations regarding invoice alterations are self-serving and unsupported by the evidence. The employer denies that any such instruction was given. The trial court was entitled to credit the employer's denials and to find that Bradley failed to demonstrate that this alleged dispute precluded injunctive relief. *See Plaza v. Fleming*, 360 So. 2d 454, 456 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978) (holding that the trial court, in injunction proceedings, is not required to resolve every disputed fact but must determine whether the movant has demonstrated entitlement to relief).

### D. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm

Bradley contends that the trial court erred by finding irreparable harm because the noncompetition agreement contains a liquidated damages clause. This argument misreads the law.

Liquidated damages provisions and injunctive relief are not mutually exclusive. Florida law recognizes that an employer may seek both injunctive relief and liquidated damages for breach of a noncompetition covenant. *See Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 715. The existence of a liquidated damages clause does not preclude injunctive relief; it merely provides an alternative remedy. *See Gulfstream Park Racing Ass'n, Inc. v. Tampa Bay Downs, Inc.*, 509 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 1987) (recognizing that liquidated damages provisions and injunctive relief may coexist).

Furthermore, the nature of harm in noncompetition cases is inherently difficult to quantify. Customer relationships, goodwill, and confidential business information cannot be adequately measured in monetary terms. The trial court properly found that irreparable harm existed because monetary damages would be insufficient to compensate Health Coalition for the loss of its customers and confidential business relationships. *See Wallach v. AIRFAX, Inc.*, 891 So. 2d at 583 (holding that loss of customer goodwill constitutes irreparable harm that cannot be remedied by monetary damages).

### E. The Balance of Hardships Favors the Employer

The trial court properly found that the balance of hardships favors Health Coalition. Bradley voluntarily entered into the covenant not to compete, received consideration for that covenant, and is bound by its terms. He cannot now claim hardship when he is simply being held to the terms of his agreement.

By contrast, Health Coalition faces ongoing harm from Bradley's competitive activities. The employer invested in training Bradley, provided him with access to confidential customer information, and entrusted him with customer relationships. Bradley's violation of the covenant threatens these legitimate business interests. The trial court properly balanced these competing interests and found in favor of the employer.

### F. The Public Interest Supports Enforcement

Bradley argues that the public interest disfavors enforcement of the covenant. This argument fails. Florida law recognizes that employers have legitimate interests in protecting their customer relationships and confidential business information. *See Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 713. Noncompetition agreements, when reasonable, serve important business purposes and do not violate public policy.

The covenant in this case is reasonable in scope. It is limited to two years and protects against solicitation of active, paid-up customers. These are legitimate protections that Florida law recognizes as enforceable. *See Plaza v. Fleming*, 360 So. 2d at 457 (holding that reasonable noncompetition covenants are enforceable).

Bradley's characterization of the covenant as "overbroad" is without merit. The covenant is tailored to protect Health Coalition's legitimate business interests and does not impose an undue burden on Bradley's ability to earn a living. Bradley agreed to these terms as part of his employment, and he should be held to his commitment.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. The trial court properly applied the law, did not abuse its discretion, and reached a result supported by the evidence. Bradley's arguments are without merit and should be rejected.

The Defense requests that this Court affirm the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**Preliminary Ruling**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**Case No.** [Insert Case Number]
**Date:** [Insert Date]

**OPINION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, argues that the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction because Appellee Health Coalition, Inc., failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits, failed to prove irreparable harm, and ignored genuine disputes of material fact regarding the employer's alleged misconduct. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties and the applicable law, we find that the trial court committed reversible error. Accordingly, we reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s decision to grant or deny a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 659 So. 2d 1042, 1044 (Fla. 1995). A trial court abuses its discretion when its ruling is based on a misapplication of the law or a conclusion that no reasonable person would reach based on the evidence presented. *See id.*

To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the moving party will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships tips in the moving party's favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Burger King Corp. v. Mason*, 710 So. 2d 544, 546 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998); *Winkelman v. Toll*, 827 So. 2d 904, 906 (Fla. 2002).

**II. Analysis**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Material Breach**

The trial court erred in finding that Health Coalition established a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. While a signed noncompetition agreement creates a presumption of enforceability, *see Wallach v. AIRFAX, Inc.*, 891 So. 2d 579, 582 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004), this presumption is rebutted where the employer has materially breached the employment contract.

Bradley presented evidence that Health Coalition wrongfully refused to pay commissions he had earned after his employment ended. Under Florida law, an employer’s failure to pay earned compensation constitutes a material breach that excuses the employee from further performance under the contract, including restrictive covenants. *B & B Mechanical, Inc. v. Smith*, 417 So. 2d 1061, 1063 (Fla. 3d DCA 1982). The defense argues that the commission dispute was post-termination and did not go to the essence of the agreement, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). However, the fact that the commissions were "earned" suggests the obligation arose during the employment relationship. The trial court failed to adequately address whether this alleged breach was sufficient to void the covenant under *B & B Mechanical*. Because the existence of a valid material breach defense creates a substantial question regarding the enforceability of the agreement, Health Coalition did not conclusively establish a likelihood of success on the merits.

**B. Genuine Disputes of Material Fact**

The trial court further erred by granting the injunction in the face of genuine disputes of material fact. Bradley alleged that he was forced to resign after refusing to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed—a claim of fraudulent business practices. Health Coalition denies these allegations.

Florida law dictates that temporary injunctions should not be granted when there are disputed issues of fact that must be resolved at trial. *R & B, Inc. v. S. D. E. Enterprises, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 1342, 1344 (Fla. 2d DCA 1985). The defense argues that the trial court is not required to resolve every disputed fact, *see Plaza v. Fleming*, 360 So. 2d 454, 456 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), but the allegations of fraud and the circumstances of Bradley's termination are central to the equity of enforcing the restrictive covenant. These are not merely collateral matters; they go to the heart of whether the employer is entitled to equitable relief. The trial court’s reliance on the existence of the contract without resolving these fundamental factual conflicts was an abuse of discretion.

**C. Irreparable Harm**

Finally, the trial court’s finding of irreparable harm is not supported by the record. The noncompetition agreement contains a liquidated damages clause. The existence of such a clause strongly suggests that the parties contemplated money damages as an adequate remedy for a breach. *Gulfstream Park Racing Ass’n, Inc. v. Tampa Bay Downs, Inc.*, 509 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 1987).

While the defense correctly notes that liquidated damages and injunctive relief are not mutually exclusive, *see Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 715, and that loss of goodwill can constitute irreparable harm, *see Wallach*, 891 So. 2d at 583, the presence of the liquidated damages provision shifts the burden to the employer to prove why damages are inadequate in this specific instance. The trial court failed to make specific findings explaining why the liquidated damages clause was insufficient to protect Health Coalition's interests. Without such findings, the conclusion of irreparable harm is speculative.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court abused its discretion in modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Health Coalition failed to conclusively demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits given the material breach defense, failed to show that irreparable harm would occur absent the injunction in light of the liquidated damages clause, and ignored genuine disputes of material fact regarding the employer's conduct.

**REVERSED and REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.**

**PER CURIAM.**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**Case No.** [Insert Case Number]
**Date:** [Insert Date]

**OPINION ON REBUTTALS**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, appeals the trial court’s order, arguing that the court abused its discretion by granting the injunction. Appellee Health Coalition, Inc., defends the order. Having reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by both parties, we reaffirm our preliminary ruling. We find that the trial court committed reversible error by granting the injunction where Appellee failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits, failed to prove irreparable harm in light of a liquidated damages clause, and ignored genuine disputes of material fact regarding Appellee’s conduct. Accordingly, we reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s decision to grant or deny a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 659 So. 2d 1042, 1044 (Fla. 1995). A trial court abuses its discretion when its ruling is based on a misapplication of the law or a conclusion that no reasonable person would reach based on the evidence presented. *See id.* To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the moving party will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships tips in the moving party's favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Burger King Corp. v. Mason*, 710 So. 2d 544, 546 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998).

**II. Analysis**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Material Breach**

The trial court erred in finding that Health Coalition established a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. While a signed noncompetition agreement creates a presumption of enforceability, this presumption is rebutted where the employer has materially breached the employment contract. *Wallach v. AIRFAX, Inc.*, 891 So. 2d 579, 582 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004).

Bradley presented evidence that Health Coalition wrongfully refused to pay commissions he had earned after his employment ended. Under Florida law, an employer’s failure to pay earned compensation constitutes a material breach that excuses the employee from further performance under the contract, including restrictive covenants. *B & B Mechanical, Inc. v. Smith*, 417 So. 2d 1061, 1063 (Fla. 3d DCA 1982). The defense argues that the commission dispute was post-termination and did not go to the essence of the agreement, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). However, the fact that the commissions were "earned" suggests the obligation arose during the employment relationship and is inseparable from the contract's essence. *See B & B Mechanical*, 417 So. 2d at 1063.

Furthermore, unlike in *A & A Bumpers, Inc. v. McKenzie*, 423 So. 2d 968, 970 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982), where the covenant was expressly severable from compensation disputes, the agreement here contains no language indicating the parties intended the covenant to survive independent of the compensation obligations. The trial court failed to adequately address whether this alleged breach was sufficient to void the covenant under *B & B Mechanical*. Because the existence of a valid material breach defense creates a substantial question regarding the enforceability of the agreement, Health Coalition did not conclusively establish a likelihood of success on the merits.

**B. Genuine Disputes of Material Fact**

The trial court further erred by granting the injunction in the face of genuine disputes of material fact. Bradley alleged that he was forced to resign after refusing to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed—a claim of fraudulent business practices. Health Coalition denies these allegations.

Florida law dictates that temporary injunctions should not be granted when there are disputed issues of fact that must be resolved at trial. *R & B, Inc. v. S. D. E. Enterprises, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 1342, 1344 (Fla. 2d DCA 1985). The defense argues that the trial court is not required to resolve every disputed fact, *see Plaza v. Fleming*, 360 So. 2d 454, 456 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), but the allegations of fraud and the circumstances of Bradley's termination are central to the equity of enforcing the restrictive covenant.

These are not merely collateral matters; they go to the heart of whether the employer is entitled to equitable relief. Florida law bars enforcement where the employer has engaged in misconduct, as equity requires that the moving party come to court with clean hands. *H. L. v. State*, 828 So. 2d 482, 485 (Fla. 2d DCA 2002). The trial court’s reliance on the existence of the contract without resolving these fundamental factual conflicts was an abuse of discretion.

**C. Irreparable Harm**

Finally, the trial court’s finding of irreparable harm is not supported by the record. The noncompetition agreement contains a liquidated damages clause. The existence of such a clause strongly suggests that the parties contemplated money damages as an adequate remedy for a breach. *Gulfstream Park Racing Ass’n, Inc. v. Tampa Bay Downs, Inc.*, 509 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 1987).

While the defense correctly notes that liquidated damages and injunctive relief are not mutually exclusive, *see Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 715, and that loss of goodwill can constitute irreparable harm, *see Wallach*, 891 So. 2d at 583, the presence of the liquidated damages provision shifts the burden to the employer to prove why damages are inadequate in this specific instance. *Gulfstream*, 509 So. 2d at 301. The trial court failed to make specific findings explaining why the liquidated damages clause was insufficient to protect Health Coalition's interests. Without such findings, the conclusion of irreparable harm is speculative.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court abused its discretion in modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Health Coalition failed to conclusively demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits given the material breach defense, failed to show that irreparable harm would occur absent the injunction in light of the liquidated damages clause, and ignored genuine disputes of material fact regarding the employer's conduct.

**REVERSED and REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.**

**OPINION ON FINAL RULING**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, appeals the trial court’s order, arguing that the court abused its discretion by granting the injunction. Appellee Health Coalition, Inc., defends the order. **I. Standard of Review**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s decision to grant or deny a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 659 So. 2d 1042, 1044 (Fla. 1995). A trial court abuses its discretion when its ruling is based on a misapplication of the law or a conclusion that no reasonable person would reach based on the evidence presented. *See id.* To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the moving party will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships tips in the moving party's favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Burger King Corp. v. Mason*, 710 So. 2d 544, 546 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998).

**II. Analysis**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Material Breach**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding that Health Coalition established a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. While Appellant argues that Health Coalition’s failure to pay post-termination commissions constituted a material breach, the Appellee correctly points out that under *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002), a breach must "go to the essence of the agreement" to excuse performance under a restrictive covenant.

The Appellant relies heavily on *B & B Mechanical, Inc. v. Smith*, 417 So. 2d 1061 (Fla. 3d DCA 1982), to argue that the failure to pay earned commissions voids the agreement. However, the Appellee distinguishes *B & B Mechanical* by noting that the commissions at issue here were contingent upon post-termination events. As the Florida Supreme Court held in *Davis*, the essential purpose of the employment agreement is the provision of services during the term, not post-termination payouts. 832 So. 2d at 714. The trial court could reasonably conclude that the dispute over post-termination commissions does not go to the essence of the employment bargain. Therefore, the Appellee demonstrated a likelihood of success on the enforceability of the covenant.

**B. Genuine Disputes of Material Fact**

The trial court did not err in granting the injunction despite the Appellant’s allegations of fraud regarding invoice alterations. The Appellee correctly cites *Plaza v. Fleming*, 360 So. 2d 454, 456 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), for the proposition that a trial court is not required to resolve every disputed fact before granting a temporary injunction.

The Appellant argues that *R & B, Inc. v. S. D. E. Enterprises, Inc.*, 464 So. 2d 1342 (Fla. 2d DCA 1985), requires reversal where there are genuine issues of material fact. However, the Appellee successfully argues that the fraud allegations are collateral to the existence and validity of the written contract. No party disputes that the employment agreement was executed or that Bradley went to work for a direct competitor. The Appellant’s claims of invoice alteration remain unproven assertions that do not automatically bar injunctive relief where the moving party has established a clear right to enforcement under the contract. The trial court was within its discretion to maintain the status quo pending a full trial on these collateral equitable defenses.

**C. Irreparable Harm**

The trial court’s finding of irreparable harm is supported by the record and controlling law. The Appellant relies on *Gulfstream Park Racing Ass’n, Inc. v. Tampa Bay Downs, Inc.*, 509 So. 2d 298 (Fla. 1987), to argue that the liquidated damages clause shifts the burden to the employer to prove inadequacy. However, the Appellee correctly identifies *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 715, as the controlling authority on restrictive covenants.

In *Davis*, the Florida Supreme Court explicitly held that "the existence of a liquidated damages provision does not preclude the entry of an injunction." *Id.* The Court noted that liquidated damages and injunctive relief are not mutually exclusive remedies. *Id.* Furthermore, as established in *Wallach v. AIRFAX, Inc.*, 891 So. 2d 579, 583 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004), the loss of customer goodwill and confidential business relationships constitutes irreparable harm for which monetary damages are inadequate. The trial court specifically found that Bradley’s solicitation of Health Coalition’s customers caused ongoing harm to these relationships. Given the explicit language in *Davis*, the trial court did not abuse its discretion by finding irreparable harm despite the presence of a liquidated damages clause.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, established irreparable harm through the loss of customer goodwill, and showed that the balance of hardships and public interest favored enforcement. The Appellee’s reliance on *Davis v. Monahan* successfully counters the Appellant’s arguments regarding material breach and the effect of the liquidated damages clause.

**AFFIRMED.**
