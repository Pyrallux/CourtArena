Your Honor, the trial court correctly found that Mr. Salazar proved his work-related injury by a preponderance of the evidence, and the employer’s challenges on appeal lack legal merit. The record contains ample credible testimony, corroborated medical evidence, and objective circumstances establishing the accident occurred during the course and scope of employment. Furthermore, the employer’s denial of the claim was arbitrary and capricious, warranting penalties and attorney’s fees under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). We urge this Court to affirm the trial court’s judgment in full.

### I. The Claimant Met His Burden of Proof Through Credible Testimony and Medical Evidence
Louisiana law requires a claimant to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that an accident occurred in the course and scope of employment. *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840, 844 (La. 1989). Here, Mr. Salazar’s testimony was fully corroborated by his brother Raul Salazar’s account of his immediate post-accident pain, the supervisor Bryan Monte’s confirmation of the physically demanding work (lifting 250-pound forms requiring three to four men), and the contemporaneous medical records. The trial court properly credited this evidence, and appellate review "must not reweigh the facts or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court unless manifest error is found." *Id.* at 845. The employer’s attempt to relitigate factual credibility is precisely the error this Court must reject.

Crucially, Louisiana courts consistently hold that lay testimony regarding physical pain and limitations—whether from family members or coworkers—is competent evidence to establish a work-related injury. *Williams v. Southern University*, 2005-0435 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/22/05), 929 So.2d 1252, 1258. Here, Raul Salazar’s testimony that his brother was "in a lot of pain" on the evening of March 23, 2011, and unable to work the next day, is precisely the type of lay testimony courts have repeatedly deemed sufficient. The fact that Raul did not witness the lifting incident itself is irrelevant; Louisiana law does not require eyewitnesses to the exact moment of injury. *Id.* Instead, the focus is on whether the claimant’s account of pain and functional limitations is credible and consistent with the circumstances. Mr. Salazar’s immediate visit to Dr. Davis on March 24, 2011—where he reported back and shoulder pain directly resulting from the lifting incident—further corroborated his testimony. Dr. Davis’s contemporaneous medical records, which were uncontroverted at trial, documented the injuries and ordered immediate work restrictions. This chain of evidence—testimony of pain, immediate medical attention, and documented injuries—satisfies the claimant’s burden under *Rosell*.

### II. The Employer’s "Delayed Reporting" Argument Ignores Established Precedent
The employer’s reliance on the claimant’s failure to report the accident on the exact day of injury is legally baseless. Louisiana law recognizes that delays in reporting are excused when there is a reasonable explanation. *Guidry v. Avondale Shipyards, Inc.*, 95-1506 (La. App. 4 Cir. 4/17/96), 673 So.2d 1235, 1238. Here, Mr. Salazar testified—and the trial court credited—that he could not report the accident on March 23 because his supervisor, Bryan Monte, was not present at the work site. He then attempted to contact Monte by phone that evening but was unsuccessful. The following morning, before he could report the injury, Monte handed him a personnel form regarding a separate incident with another supervisor, which distracted him from disclosing the accident. This sequence of events constitutes a reasonable explanation for the delay, as the claimant was actively trying to communicate the issue but was thwarted by operational circumstances. *See id.* at 1239 (finding a 48-hour delay reasonable where claimant was "confused about the extent of his injury and attempting to work through pain"). The employer’s insistence that "immediate reporting" is required ignores this settled law and the trial court’s factual finding that the delay was justified.

### III. The Employer’s "Reasonable Controversion" Defense Fails as a Matter of Law
The employer’s denial of the claim on April 5, 2011—stating it had "completed [its] investigation" and found "no compensable work-related claim"—was arbitrary and capricious, triggering penalties and attorney’s fees under La. R.S. 23:1201(F). *Johnson v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 2004-2069 (La. 12/15/04), 891 So.2d 1264, 1271. Reasonable controversion requires "objective evidence contradicting the claim," not merely the employer’s disbelief or speculation. *Id.* at 1272. Here, the employer had no medical evidence contradicting Dr. Davis’s or Dr. Johnston’s diagnoses; instead, its denial relied solely on the claimant’s delayed reporting and unsupported assertions about his credibility. The employer’s own documents—specifically, the April 5 denial letter—were entered into evidence as joint exhibits, yet they reveal a complete absence of investigation into the medical records or the physical demands of the job. Monte’s testimony confirmed that the work involved lifting 250-pound forms requiring multiple workers, yet the employer ignored this objective fact when denying the claim. This failure to review available evidence renders the denial arbitrary. *See Sandoz v. City of New Orleans*, 2001-1149 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/6/02), 814 So.2d 533, 540 (holding that denial without medical or factual basis constitutes "arbitrary and capricious" behavior warranting penalties).

Moreover, the parties stipulated to Mr. Salazar’s employment status and average weekly wage, which the employer accepted as fact. This stipulation directly undermines the employer’s argument that no accident occurred; if the employer genuinely doubted the injury, it would not have acknowledged the claimant’s employment and wage. The employer’s conduct—denying the claim while accepting the wage stipulation—demonstrates a lack of good faith and confirms the denial was not based on reasonable grounds.

### IV. The Trial Court’s Findings Are Ample and Should Be Affirmed
The trial court properly weighed the evidence, credited Mr. Salazar’s testimony, and found the injury work-related. Its factual determinations are entitled to deference on appeal under *Rosell*. The employer’s arguments—focusing on "absence of eyewitnesses" (which is legally irrelevant), reliance on family testimony (which is permissible), and delayed reporting (which was excused)—are red herrings that ignore the record. The medical evidence, the supervisor’s confirmation of heavy lifting, and the claimant’s immediate medical response collectively prove the accident occurred. The employer’s denial was not reasonable, and penalties are mandated. This Court should uphold the trial court’s judgment in full.

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a classic example of a workers' compensation claim where the claimant has failed to carry his burden of proving that a work-related accident actually occurred. The plaintiff presented only self-serving testimony from himself and his brother, Raul Salazar, neither of whom witnessed the alleged lifting accident. The employer's witnesses—the crew supervisor and other employees—provided testimony that contradicted the plaintiff's version of events. The trial court erred in crediting the plaintiff's testimony over that of the employer's witnesses, and in finding that a compensable injury was established. This Court should reverse the judgment below.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO MEET HIS BURDEN OF PROOF

### A. Legal Standard

Under Louisiana law, a claimant in a workers' compensation case bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a compensable accident occurred. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 631, 635 (La. 1992). This burden requires the claimant to show that an accident happened, that it occurred in the course and scope of employment, and that it had a causal connection with the employment. *Id.* The claimant must establish these elements by competent evidence, not merely by his own testimony or that of interested parties. *See Lacy v. McGinnis Construction Management Corp.*, 94-2092 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/28/95), 657 So.2d 592, 596.

### B. No Competent Evidence of an Accident

The plaintiff presented no eyewitness to the alleged lifting accident. Both he and his brother, Raul Salazar, admitted that Raul was not present when the accident allegedly occurred. Raul testified only that his brother was "in pain" at the end of the workday—a fact that is insufficient to establish that a work-related accident actually occurred. The plaintiff himself admitted that no one saw him lift the forms and sustain the injury.

Louisiana courts have consistently held that a claimant's own testimony, standing alone, is insufficient to prove the occurrence of an accident when that testimony is uncorroborated by objective evidence or credible witnesses. *See Chaisson v. Cajun Marine Corp.*, 97-1221 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/29/98), 716 So.2d 485, 488 (holding that claimant's testimony alone was insufficient where no other evidence corroborated the occurrence of an accident). Here, the only "corroboration" came from Raul Salazar—the plaintiff's brother—who had a clear interest in the outcome of the case and who admitted he did not witness the accident.

### C. The Supervisor's Testimony Does Not Corroborate the Plaintiff's Account

The plaintiff argues that Bryan Monte's testimony corroborated his claim because Monte confirmed that the crew was "working wrecking forms" and that the work involved lifting forms weighing up to 250 pounds. However, Monte's testimony does not establish that the plaintiff actually sustained an injury while performing this work. Monte testified that the work required three to four men to lift the forms, meaning the plaintiff was not lifting the forms alone. More significantly, Monte never witnessed the plaintiff sustain any injury, nor did he observe the plaintiff in any apparent distress during the workday.

The fact that heavy lifting occurred at the worksite does not prove that the plaintiff sustained a specific injury at a specific time. This argument conflates the general nature of the work with the occurrence of a specific compensable accident. The plaintiff must prove that he, specifically, sustained an injury while performing his job duties—not merely that heavy lifting was occurring at the worksite. *See Broussard v. Capitol Building Materials*, 98-1513 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/25/99), 740 So.2d 762, 767 (holding that general evidence of hazardous work conditions is insufficient to establish that a specific accident occurred).

### D. The Medical Evidence Does Not Establish a Work-Related Injury

The plaintiff relies on Dr. Davis's and Dr. Johnston's medical records to establish causation. However, these medical records are based entirely on the plaintiff's self-reported history—that he sustained a back and shoulder injury while lifting at work. A medical record that records a patient's subjective history is not objective evidence corroborating the occurrence of an accident. *See Green v. B & B Electrical Services*, 2003-1576 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/24/04), 871 So.2d 671, 677 (holding that medical records based solely on claimant's self-reported history do not constitute independent corroboration of the accident).

Dr. Davis's notes reflect that the plaintiff reported back pain "as a result of lifting at work the day before." However, this is simply a restatement of the plaintiff's subjective complaint, not an independent medical opinion establishing that a work-related accident occurred. The same is true of Dr. Johnston's records. Neither doctor performed any diagnostic testing that would establish a specific injury causally related to the alleged March 23, 2011 incident.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S DELAYED REPORTING IS NOT EXCUSED

### A. The Failure to Report on the Day of the Alleged Accident

The plaintiff did not report the alleged accident on March 23, 2011, the day it supposedly occurred. He testified that he completed the workday despite allegedly sustaining a back and shoulder injury while lifting a heavy cement form. This fact alone undermines the credibility of his claim. A worker who genuinely sustains a significant injury that prevents continued work would reasonably be expected to report the injury promptly, particularly when the supervisor is present at the worksite.

The plaintiff claims he could not report the accident because his supervisor, Bryan Monte, was "not in the area where he was working." However, the evidence shows that Monte was the crew supervisor and was present at the worksite on March 23, 2011. The plaintiff testified that Monte was "not in the area," but this vague statement does not establish that Monte was unavailable. The plaintiff also claimed he attempted to call Monte by phone that evening but was unsuccessful. Yet, the plaintiff presented no phone records to corroborate this claim, and Monte himself testified that he was never contacted by the plaintiff on the evening of March 23, 2011.

### B. The Following Morning

Even more damaging to the plaintiff's claim is what occurred on the morning of March 24, 2011. The plaintiff arrived at the jobsite and was met by Monte, who handed him a Personnel Action Form regarding an incident that occurred the day before between the plaintiff and another supervisor, Patrick Jackson. The plaintiff testified that he "did not sign the form because there was no place on the form for him to sign."

Critically, the plaintiff did not immediately inform Monte about the alleged accident when presented with this form. Instead, he waited until after the personnel matter was resolved before mentioning his injury. This delay is inexplicable if the plaintiff had genuinely sustained a significant work-related injury that caused him "a lot of pain," as he and his brother claimed. The plaintiff had multiple opportunities to report the accident—the end of the workday on March 23, the phone call that evening, and the meeting with Monte on the morning of March 24—but failed to do so until later that day.

Louisiana courts have consistently held that a claimant's failure to report an accident promptly, without a reasonable explanation, raises serious questions about the validity of the claim. *See Butler v. Union Pacific Railroad*, 2003-1765 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/2/04), 880 So.2d 140, 146 (holding that unexplained delay in reporting an accident supports a finding that no accident occurred). The plaintiff's explanations for the delay—the absence of his supervisor and the distraction of the personnel form—are not credible and do not constitute the kind of "reasonable explanation" contemplated by Louisiana law.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT'S CREDIBILITY DETERMINATIONS WERE MANIFESTLY ERRONEOUS

### A. The Trial Court Improperly Credited Interested Testimony

The trial court's judgment rests largely on its assessment of the plaintiff's credibility. However, the only witnesses who testified that an accident occurred were the plaintiff himself and his brother, Raul Salazar. Both witnesses have a direct financial interest in the outcome of this case. Raul Salazar testified that he had been "paying the living expenses of the claimant because the claimant had not worked since March 23, 2011." This testimony reveals that Raul has been supporting the plaintiff financially, creating a strong incentive to corroborate the plaintiff's account.

The employer's witnesses—Bryan Monte, the crew supervisor, and other employees—provided testimony that contradicted the plaintiff's version of events. These witnesses had no financial interest in the outcome of the case. The trial court erred in implicitly crediting the plaintiff's testimony over that of the disinterested witnesses.

### B. The Circumstantial Evidence Contradicts the Plaintiff's Account

The employer's evidence established several circumstances that cast serious doubt on the plaintiff's claim:

1. **No report on the day of the alleged accident**: The plaintiff completed a full workday and showed up for work the next day "dressed and ready to work."

2. **No immediate medical attention**: Despite allegedly sustaining a significant injury, the plaintiff waited until the following day to see a doctor.

3. **Prior disciplinary incident**: The plaintiff was involved in an argument with a supervisor on March 23, 2011, the same day as the alleged accident. He was being reprimanded for this incident on March 24, 2011.

4. **Desire to return to work**: The plaintiff called the jobsite foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the alleged accident "indicating that he wanted to return to work." This call suggests that the plaintiff did not believe he was seriously injured.

These circumstances, taken together, demonstrate that the plaintiff's claim is not credible. The trial court erred in ignoring this circumstantial evidence.

---

## V. THE EMPLOYER'S DENIAL WAS REASONABLE AND NOT ARBITRARY AND CAPRICIOUS

### A. The Employer Had Substantial Grounds to Deny the Claim

Even if this Court finds that the plaintiff proved a compensable injury—which the Employer respectfully submits he did not—the employer should not be penalized for denying the claim. Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F) provides for penalties only when the employer's denial is "arbitrary and capricious." This standard requires the employer to have no reasonable grounds for denying the claim. *See Brown v. Texas-LA Cartage, Inc.*, 98-1064 (La. 12/1/98), 721 So.2d 885, 890.

Here, the employer had substantial grounds to deny the claim:

1. **No eyewitness to the accident**: The employer investigated and found no one who witnessed the plaintiff sustain an injury.

2. **Delayed reporting**: The plaintiff did not report the accident until the day after it allegedly occurred, and his explanation for the delay was not credible.

3. **Inconsistent testimony**: The employer's witnesses contradicted the plaintiff's account of events.

4. **No objective evidence**: The medical records were based on the plaintiff's self-reported history and did not independently establish a work-related injury.

An employer who denies a claim based on these factors has not acted arbitrarily and capriciously. *See Williams v. General Motors Corp.*, 99-1227 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/30/00), 762 So.2d 1222, 1229 (holding that employer's denial was reasonable where claimant failed to report accident promptly and presented no corroborating evidence).

### B. The Employer's Investigation Was Reasonable

The plaintiff argues that the employer's denial was arbitrary because the employer "ignored" Monte's testimony about the heavy lifting. This argument is without merit. Monte's testimony established only that heavy lifting occurred at the worksite—it did not establish that the plaintiff sustained a specific injury at a specific time. The employer's investigation was reasonable, and its denial was based on the lack of corroborating evidence for the plaintiff's claim.

The plaintiff also argues that the employer's denial was improper because the employer stipulated to the plaintiff's employment status and average weekly wage. This stipulation, however, says nothing about whether a compensable accident occurred. The employer stipulated to these facts to narrow the issues for trial, not to concede the occurrence of an injury. The plaintiff's argument mischaracterifies the nature of the stipulation.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation awarding benefits to Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”) and assessing penalties and attorney’s fees against Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”). The central issue on appeal is whether the Claimant met his burden of proving a compensable accident by a preponderance of the evidence. After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, the Court finds that the Claimant failed to carry his burden of proof. Furthermore, even if the Claimant had met his burden, the Employer’s denial of the claim was based on reasonable grounds and does not support an award of penalties and attorney’s fees. Accordingly, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation is reversed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

It is well settled that a claimant in a workers’ compensation case bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that an accident occurred and that the injury arose out of and in the course and scope of employment. *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840, 844 (La. 1989). When reviewing a workers’ compensation case, the appellate court must not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court unless the trial court’s findings are manifestly erroneous. *Id.* However, where the trial court’s findings are based on an erroneous application of the law regarding the sufficiency of the evidence, manifest error is present.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Claimant Failed to Prove the Occurrence of an Accident by a Preponderance of the Evidence**

The trial court erred in finding that the Claimant proved a compensable accident. Louisiana law requires that a claimant establish the occurrence of an accident by competent evidence. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 631, 635 (La. 1992). Here, the Claimant’s evidence is legally insufficient to meet this burden.

First, the Claimant relies heavily on medical records to establish the accident. However, as the Employer correctly argues, medical records that merely document a patient’s subjective complaints do not constitute independent corroboration of the accident itself. In *Green v. B & B Electrical Services*, 2003-1576 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/24/04), 871 So.2d 671, 677, the court held that medical records based solely on a claimant’s self-reported history do not constitute independent corroboration of the accident. The records of Dr. Davis and Dr. Johnston in this case reflect only the Claimant’s subjective report that he was injured while lifting at work. They do not contain objective findings or diagnostic evidence confirming that a specific traumatic event occurred on March 23, 2011. Therefore, these records cannot serve as the substantial evidence required to prove the claim.

Second, the Claimant relies on the testimony of his brother, Raul Salazar. Louisiana courts have consistently held that a claimant’s own testimony, particularly when corroborated only by interested family members, is insufficient to prove an accident. *Chaisson v. Cajun Marine Corp.*, 97-1221 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/29/98), 716 So.2d 485, 488. Raul Salazar admitted he did not witness the accident and testified only that his brother was in pain at the end of the day. Given that Raul Salazar pays the Claimant’s living expenses, he is an interested party with a significant financial stake in the outcome of this litigation. His testimony regarding the Claimant’s pain, without more, does not elevate the Claimant’s uncorroborated version of events to a preponderance of the evidence.

Third, the Employer correctly distinguishes between evidence of general working conditions and evidence of a specific accident. In *Broussard v. Capitol Building Materials*, 98-1513 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/25/99), 740 So.2d 762, 767, the court held that general evidence of hazardous work conditions is insufficient to establish that a specific accident occurred. While Supervisor Bryan Monte testified that the crew lifted heavy forms, this merely establishes the nature of the work. It does not establish that the Claimant sustained a specific injury at a specific time. No witness testified to seeing the Claimant injure himself. Without an eyewitness or objective medical evidence, the Claimant’s testimony alone is insufficient to carry his burden of proof. *See Lacy v. McGinnis Construction Management Corp.*, 94-2092 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/28/95), 657 So.2d 592, 596.

**B. The Claimant’s Delayed Reporting Undermines His Credibility**

The circumstances surrounding the Claimant’s reporting of the accident further erode the credibility of his claim. The Claimant did not report the accident on the day it allegedly occurred, despite completing the full workday. He reported it only the next morning, after being confronted with a disciplinary form regarding an argument with another supervisor. Louisiana courts have recognized that unexplained delays in reporting, particularly where the claimant had the opportunity to report, support a finding that no accident occurred. *Butler v. Union Pacific Railroad*, 2003-1765 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/2/04), 880 So.2d 140, 146.

The Claimant’s explanation for the delay—that his supervisor was unavailable and he was distracted by the personnel form—is not supported by the record. The Claimant admitted he attempted to call the supervisor but offered no proof of the call. More importantly, when he met the supervisor face-to-face on the morning of March 24, 2011, he failed to mention the accident immediately. This sequence of events supports the Employer’s contention that the claim was fabricated in response to the disciplinary action. The trial court’s finding to the contrary disregarded these significant inconsistencies and constitutes manifest error.

**C. The Employer’s Denial Was Not Arbitrary and Capricious**

Even if this Court were to find that the Claimant proved a compensable injury, the award of penalties and attorney’s fees must be reversed. Penalties under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F) are warranted only when the employer’s refusal to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. *Brown v. Texas-LA Cartage, Inc.*, 98-1064 (La. 12/1/98), 721 So.2d 885, 890. An employer has reasonable grounds to deny a claim when the claimant fails to provide corroborating evidence and the circumstances are suspicious.

Here, the Employer had substantial evidence to support its denial. The Claimant provided no eyewitness testimony; his medical records were based solely on his self-reporting; and he reported the injury only after being disciplined. Under these circumstances, the Employer’s investigation and subsequent denial were reasonable. *See Williams v. General Motors Corp.*, 99-1227 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/30/00), 762 So.2d 1222, 1229. The Employer’s reliance on the lack of objective evidence and the suspicious timing of the report constituted "reasonable controversion" of the claim. Therefore, the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney’s fees.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Claimant failed to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he sustained a compensable work-related accident. The trial court’s judgment in favor of the Claimant is manifestly erroneous. Furthermore, the Employer’s denial of the claim was reasonable and not arbitrary and capricious.

**IT IS ORDERED, ADJUDGED, AND DECREED** that the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation is reversed in its entirety, and the Claimant’s petition is dismissed. Judgment is hereby entered in favor of Command Construction, LLC.

May it please the Court:  

The defendant’s opening statement mischaracterizes the law, misapplies precedent, and ignores the record evidence that conclusively establishes the claimant met his burden of proof. The trial court’s ruling—that the claimant suffered a compensable work-related accident—is amply supported by competent evidence and should be affirmed. The employer’s denial was unreasonable, warranting penalties and attorney’s fees.  

### I. THE CLAIMANT PROVED THE ACCIDENT BY A PREPONDERANCE OF THE EVIDENCE  
Louisiana law requires only that a claimant establish the occurrence of an accident by competent evidence, which may include the claimant’s testimony supported by circumstantial or corroborative evidence. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 631, 635 (La. 1992). Here, the claimant’s testimony was not "self-serving" or uncorroborated, as the defense contends. It was corroborated by:  
- **The supervisor’s testimony confirming the hazardous work conditions**. Bryan Monte testified that the crew lifted forms weighing up to 250 pounds requiring three to four men, directly supporting the claimant’s account of the lifting incident. This is not "general evidence of working conditions" but specific evidence of the task performed on the date of injury. *See Hargrove v. Town of Opelousas*, 98-1104 (La. App. 3 Cir. 3/3/99), 729 So.2d 549, 554 (holding that testimony about job duties and physical demands corroborates claimant’s account of injury during those duties).  
- **Medical records consistent with the claimant’s history**. Dr. Davis’s records from March 24, 2011, documented acute back and shoulder pain "as a result of lifting at work the day before," with immediate treatment orders. These records were not merely "subjective complaints" but reflected a contemporaneous medical evaluation tied to the claimed incident. *Sawyer v. State, Dept. of Transp. & Dev.*, 94-1659 (La. App. 1 Cir. 11/2/95), 664 So.2d 754, 758 (finding medical records sufficient to corroborate accident when diagnosis aligns with claimant’s description of injury and job duties).  
- **The brother’s testimony of post-accident pain**, which was consistent with the claimant’s account and the medical timeline. Raul Salazar’s observation of his brother’s pain at the end of the workday is not "mere interested-party testimony" but corroborative evidence of the injury’s onset. *Gautreaux v. Avondale Indus.*, 98-2215 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/19/00), 755 So.2d 840, 846 (upholding award where claimant’s testimony was supported by family member’s observation of pain and medical treatment).  

The defense erroneously equates "no eyewitness" with "no proof," but Louisiana law explicitly rejects this. *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840, 844 (La. 1989) (stating "a claimant’s testimony alone may be sufficient to prove an accident if credible and uncontradicted by other evidence"). Here, the claimant’s testimony was credible, uncontradicted by objective evidence, and supported by multiple corroborative strands. The trial court’s finding was not manifestly erroneous.

The defense’s argument that the claimant’s reporting delay "raises serious questions" is legally unsupported. Louisiana courts routinely accept reasonable explanations for delayed reporting, particularly where the claimant was unaware of the injury’s severity or encountered procedural barriers. *See Brown v. Texas-LA Cartage, Inc.*, 98-1064 (La. 12/1/98), 721 So.2d 885, 891 (holding that failure to report immediately does not bar claim if claimant "reasonably believed the injury was minor").  

The record shows:  
- The claimant completed his shift because he did not realize the extent of his injury until after work.  
- He attempted to contact his supervisor that evening but was unsuccessful, as confirmed by the lack of contradiction in the employer’s evidence.  
- On March 24, he was immediately confronted with a disciplinary form unrelated to his injury, which delayed his report until after that matter was resolved. This is a classic example of a "reasonable explanation" for delay. *Gautreaux*, 755 So.2d at 846 (finding delay excused where claimant was distracted by workplace conflict and unaware of injury severity).  

The defense’s attempt to frame this as "fabrication" ignores the claimant’s immediate medical visit and consistent reporting timeline. The trial court properly credited these explanations, and the defense’s reliance on *Butler v. Union Pacific Railroad* is misplaced—*Butler* involved a claimant who never reported the injury at all, whereas here the claimant reported within 24 hours with a plausible explanation. 2003-1765 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/2/04), 880 So.2d 140, 146 (distinguishing cases with "no report" from those with timely explanation).  

### III. THE EMPLOYER’S DENIAL WAS ARBITRARY AND CAPRICIOUS, WARRANTING PENALTIES  
Penalties under La. R.S. 23:1201(F) are proper when an employer denies a claim without "reasonable controversion." *Miles v. New Orleans Public Service Inc.*, 97-0965 (La. 1/21/98), 705 So.2d 1116, 1120 (defining "reasonable controversion" as requiring "some objective evidence or factual basis" for denial). Here, the employer’s denial was arbitrary because:  
- It ignored the stipulated facts and uncontroverted medical evidence. The parties stipulated to the claimant’s employment status and wage rate, yet the employer denied all benefits without challenging these fundamentals.  
- It disregarded the medical records, which were consistent with the claimant’s testimony and job duties. The defense’s claim that "medical records based on self-reporting cannot corroborate an accident" is contradicted by *Sawyer*, which held such records are admissible and corroborative when they align with the claimant’s narrative.

The employer presented no proof that the claimant’s injury was pre-existing or unrelated to work; instead, it merely "questioned credibility" without substantive evidence. *Miles*, 705 So.2d at 1120 (denial arbitrary where employer "offered no factual basis to controvert the claim").  

The defense’s assertion that its denial was "reasonable" because of "no eyewitness" or "delayed reporting" is legally flawed. Louisiana law does not require eyewitnesses for a valid claim, and delayed reporting was reasonably explained. *See Hargrove*, 729 So.2d at 554 (penalties imposed where employer denied claim based on "unsupported speculation" despite corroborative evidence).  

### IV. CONCLUSION  
The claimant met his burden of proof through credible testimony, corroborative medical records, workplace evidence, and a reasonable explanation for delayed reporting. The trial court’s ruling was correct and not manifestly erroneous. The employer’s denial was arbitrary and capricious, as it lacked any objective basis to controvert the claim. This Court should affirm the judgment and uphold the award of penalties and attorney’s fees.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for consideration of the rebuttals to the opening arguments filed by Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”) and Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”). The Court has reviewed the Claimant’s arguments seeking to reverse the preliminary ruling, as well as the Employer’s arguments in support of it. After careful consideration of the record, the applicable law, and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the Claimant has failed to demonstrate that the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge’s findings of fact were manifestly erroneous or that the law was misapplied. Accordingly, the preliminary ruling reversing the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

As previously stated, a claimant in a workers’ compensation case bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that an accident occurred and that the injury arose out of and in the course and scope of employment. *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840, 844 (La. 1989). The appellate court may not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court unless the trial court’s findings are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong. *Id.* Where the trial court’s findings are based on an erroneous application of the law regarding the sufficiency of the evidence, manifest error is present.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Medical Records Based on Self-Reporting Do Not Constitute Independent Corroboration**

The Claimant argues that the medical records of Dr. Michael Davis and Dr. F. Allen Johnston constitute independent corroboration of the accident, citing *Sawyer v. State, Dept. of Transp. & Dev.*, 94-1659 (La. App. 1 Cir. 11/2/95), 664 So.2d 754. The Court is not persuaded. In *Green v. B & B Electrical Services*, 2003-1576 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/24/04), 871 So.2d 671, 677, the court held that medical records based solely on a claimant’s self-reported history do not constitute independent corroboration of the accident. The records in this case reflect only the Claimant’s subjective report that he was injured while lifting at work. They do not contain objective diagnostic evidence confirming that a specific traumatic event occurred on March 23, 2011. While the Claimant argues that the diagnosis aligns with his job duties, the absence of objective findings distinguishing these records from mere documentation of the Claimant’s complaints renders them insufficient to meet his burden of proof. *See Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 631, 635 (La. 1992).

**B. The Brother’s Testimony is Insufficient to Establish the Accident**

The Claimant contends that the testimony of Raul Salazar corroborates his account. However, Louisiana law is clear that a claimant’s own testimony, particularly when corroborated only by interested family members, is insufficient to prove an accident. *Chaisson v. Cajun Marine Corp.*, 97-1221 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/29/98), 716 So.2d 485, 488. The record establishes that Raul Salazar pays the Claimant’s living expenses because the Claimant has not worked since the alleged accident. This creates a significant financial stake in the outcome of the litigation. Furthermore, Raul Salazar admitted he did not witness the accident and testified only that his brother was in pain at the end of the day. This testimony, without more, does not elevate the Claimant’s uncorroborated version of events to a preponderance of the evidence. The Claimant’s reliance on *Gautreaux v. Avondale Indus.*, 98-2215 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/19/00), 755 So.2d 840, is misplaced, as that case involved additional corroborative factors beyond the observation of a family member.

**C. Evidence of General Working Conditions is Insufficient**

The Claimant argues that the testimony of Supervisor Bryan Monte regarding the weight of the forms (up to 250 pounds) corroborates the accident. This argument conflates evidence of general working conditions with evidence of a specific accident. In *Broussard v. Capitol Building Materials*, 98-1513 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/25/99), 740 So.2d 762, 767, the court held that general evidence of hazardous work conditions is insufficient to establish that a specific accident occurred. Supervisor Monte’s testimony establishes the nature of the work but does not establish that the Claimant sustained a specific injury at a specific time. No witness testified to seeing the Claimant injure himself. Therefore, the Claimant’s testimony alone is insufficient to carry his burden of proof. *See Lacy v. McGinnis Construction Management Corp.*, 94-2092 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/28/95), 657 So.2d 592, 596.

**D. The Claimant’s Credibility is Undermined by Delayed Reporting and Contradictory Circumstances**

The Claimant suggests that his delay in reporting was reasonable because he was unaware of the severity of his injury and was distracted by a disciplinary matter. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive. The Claimant testified he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of the accident, which contradicts the assertion that he believed the injury was minor. *Brown v. Texas-LA Cartage, Inc.*, 98-1064 (La. 12/1/98), 721 So.2d 885, 891. More critically, the Claimant failed to report the accident when he met his supervisor face-to-face on the morning of March 24, 2011. He only reported the accident after being confronted with a disciplinary form regarding an unrelated incident. This sequence of events supports the Employer’s contention that the claim was fabricated in response to disciplinary action. *Butler v. Union Pacific Railroad*, 2003-1765 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/2/04), 880 So.2d 140, 146. The Claimant’s explanation for the delay does not overcome these significant inconsistencies.

**E. The Employer’s Denial Was Not Arbitrary and Capricious**

The Claimant argues that the Employer’s denial was arbitrary and capricious, warranting penalties under La. R.S. 23:1201(F). However, penalties are warranted only when the employer’s refusal to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. *Brown*, 721 So.2d at 890. An employer has reasonable grounds to deny a claim when the claimant fails to provide corroborating evidence and the circumstances are suspicious. *Williams v. General Motors Corp.*, 99-1227 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/30/00), 762 So.2d 1222, 1229. Here, the Employer had substantial evidence to support its denial: the Claimant provided no eyewitness testimony; his medical records were based solely on his self-reporting; and he reported the injury only after being disciplined. The Employer’s reliance on the lack of objective evidence and the suspicious timing of the report constituted "reasonable controversion" of the claim. Therefore, the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney’s fees.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Claimant failed to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he sustained a compensable work-related accident. The trial court’s judgment in favor of the Claimant was manifestly erroneous. Furthermore, the Employer’s denial of the claim was reasonable and not arbitrary and capricious. The rebuttal arguments presented by the Claimant do not overcome the deficiencies in the evidence supporting his claim.

**IT IS ORDERED, ADJUDGED, AND DECREED** that the preliminary ruling of this Court is affirmed. The judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation is reversed in its entirety, and the Claimant’s petition is dismissed. Judgment is hereby entered in favor of Command Construction, LLC.

App. 1 Cir. 6/25/99), 740 So.2d 762, 767, the court held that general evidence of hazardous work conditions is insufficient to establish that a specific accident occurred. Supervisor Monte's testimony establishes the nature of the work but does not establish that the Plaintiff sustained a specific injury at a specific time. No witness testified to seeing the Plaintiff injure himself.

The Plaintiff cites *Lacy v. McGinnis Construction Management Corp.*, 94-2092 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/28/95), 657 So.2d 592, 596, for the proposition that testimony alone is insufficient. Yet this is precisely the point—the Plaintiff's testimony alone, unsupported by objective evidence, cannot carry his burden.

---

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S CREDIBILITY IS FUNDAMENTALLY FLAWED

### A. The Delayed Reporting Undermines the Claim

The Plaintiff-Appellant argues that his 24-hour delay in reporting was reasonable. The record contradicts this assertion.

The Plaintiff testified he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of the accident, March 23, 2011. This contradicts the assertion that he believed the injury was minor. *Brown v. Texas-LA Cartage, Inc.*, 98-1064 (La. 12/1/98), 721 So.2d 885, 891.

More critically, the Plaintiff failed to report the accident when he met his supervisor face-to-face on the morning of March 24, 2011. He was handed a Personnel Action Form involving an unrelated incident and chose not to report the injury at that time. He only reported the accident after being confronted with the disciplinary form. This sequence of events supports the Employer's contention that the claim was fabricated in response to disciplinary action. *Butler v. Union Pacific Railroad*, 2003-1765 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/2/04), 880 So.2d 140, 146.

The Plaintiff's explanation for the delay does not overcome these significant inconsistencies. The fact that he was "distracted" by a disciplinary matter does not explain why he failed to report the injury during the entire workday of March 24, 2011, before being confronted with the disciplinary form.

### B. The Circumstantial Evidence Supports the Employer's Position

The Employer established numerous circumstances that discredit the Plaintiff's version of events:

1. The Plaintiff did not report the accident on the day it allegedly occurred;
2. The Plaintiff showed up for work the day following the supposed accident dressed and ready to work;
3. It was only after the Plaintiff was told he was being reprimanded for an argument with a supervisor and sent home that he first reported an accident;
4. The Plaintiff called the jobsite foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the accident indicating he wanted to return to work—not to report an injury.

These circumstances, combined with the lack of objective evidence, demonstrate that the Plaintiff failed to meet his burden of proof.

---

## IV. THE EMPLOYER'S DENIAL WAS NOT ARBITRARY AND CAPRICIOUS

The Plaintiff-Appellant argues that the Employer owed penalties under La. R.S. 23:1201(F). However, penalties are warranted only when the employer's refusal to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. *Brown*, 721 So.2d at 890.

An employer has reasonable grounds to deny a claim when the claimant fails to provide corroborating evidence and the circumstances are suspicious. *Williams v. General Motors Corp.*, 99-1227 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/30/00), 762 So.2d 1222, 1229. Here, the Employer had substantial evidence to support its denial:

1. The Plaintiff provided no eyewitness testimony to the accident;
2. His medical records were based solely on his self-reporting;
3. He reported the injury only after being disciplined;
4. The timing of the report was suspicious.

The Plaintiff's reliance on *Miles v. New Orleans Public Service Inc.*, 97-0965 (La. 1/21/98), 705 So.2d 1116, 1120, is unavailing. In *Miles*, the Louisiana Supreme Court found arbitrary denial where the employer ignored objective evidence. Here, the Employer did not ignore evidence—it reasonably concluded that the Plaintiff's self-reported history, without objective corroboration, was insufficient to establish a compensable claim.

The Employer's reliance on the lack of objective evidence and the suspicious timing of the report constituted "reasonable controversion" of the claim. The appellate court correctly found that the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney's fees.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Command Construction, LLC respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **AFFIRM** the appellate court's reversal of the trial court judgment;

2. **AFFIRM** the finding that the Plaintiff failed to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he sustained a compensable work-related accident;

3. **AFFIRM** the finding that the Employer's denial of the claim was reasonable and not arbitrary and capricious;

4. **DENY** the Plaintiff's request to reinstate the trial court judgment;

5. **AWARD** to the Employer any additional relief this Court deems just and proper.

The Plaintiff-Appellant has failed to demonstrate that the appellate court's findings were manifestly erroneous. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the Plaintiff, still fails to establish the occurrence of a compensable workplace accident. The appellate court correctly applied the law, and its judgment should be affirmed in all respects.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”) and Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”). The Court has meticulously reviewed the arguments presented by both parties, the applicable law, and the factual record provided. The Claimant seeks reversal of the appellate court’s preliminary ruling, which reversed the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation (“OWC”) judge. The Employer seeks affirmation of that reversal. For the reasons that follow, the Court finds that the Claimant has failed to carry his burden of proof. The arguments presented by the Claimant are unpersuasive in light of the controlling jurisprudence and the factual record. Accordingly, the preliminary ruling is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

It is well settled that a claimant in a workers’ compensation case bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that an accident occurred and that the injury arose out of and in the course and scope of employment. *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840, 844 (La. 1989). An appellate court may not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court unless the trial court’s findings are manifestly erroneous or clearly wrong based on the record. *Id.* However, where the trial court’s findings are based on an erroneous application of the law regarding the sufficiency of the evidence, manifest error is present. *Stobart v. State through Dept. of Transp. and Dev.*, 617 So.2d 880, 882 (La. 1993).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Medical Records Based on Self-Reporting Do Not Constitute Independent Corroboration**

The Claimant argues that the medical records of Dr. Michael Davis and Dr. F. Allen Johnston constitute substantial corroboration of the accident, relying on *Lirette v. State*, 2001-1424 (La. App. 1 Cir. 4/3/02), 818 So.2d 126. The Court is not persuaded. While *Lirette* suggests that medical records consistent with job duties can provide corroboration, the jurisprudence is clear that medical records based solely on a claimant’s self-reported history do not constitute independent corroboration of the accident itself. *Green v. B & B Electrical Services*, 2003-1576 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/24/04), 871 So.2d 671, 677.

In this case, the records of Dr. Davis and Dr. Johnston reflect only the Claimant’s subjective report that he was injured while lifting at work. They do not contain objective diagnostic evidence confirming that a specific traumatic event occurred on March 23, 2011. The diagnosis of low-back and left shoulder pain, without more, is consistent with the Claimant’s job duties but is also consistent with countless other non-work-related activities. As held in *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 593 So.2d 631, 635 (La. 1992), a claimant’s uncorroborated testimony is insufficient to prove an accident, and medical records that merely document the claimant’s complaints cannot supply the necessary corroboration. The Claimant’s reliance on *Lirette* is misplaced because that case involved a broader evidentiary context; here, the medical evidence stands alone as a derivative of the Claimant’s own unverified statements.

**B. The Brother’s Testimony is Insufficient to Establish the Accident**

The Claimant contends that the testimony of Raul Salazar corroborates his account, citing *Smith v. Louisiana Dep’t of Transp. & Dev.*, 94-0073 (La. 9/5/94), 642 So.2d 1286. However, Louisiana law is clear that a claimant’s own testimony, particularly when corroborated only by interested family members, is insufficient to prove an accident. *Chaisson v. Cajun Marine Corp.*, 97-1221 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/29/98), 716 So.2d 485, 488.

The record establishes that Raul Salazar pays the Claimant’s living expenses because the Claimant has not worked since the alleged accident. This creates a significant financial stake in the outcome of the litigation, directly impacting his credibility. Furthermore, Raul Salazar admitted he did not witness the accident and testified only that his brother was in pain at the end of the day. This testimony, without more, does not elevate the Claimant’s uncorroborated version of events to a preponderance of the evidence. *Smith* is distinguishable because it involved additional corroborative factors beyond the observation of a family member. Here, lacking such additional evidence, Raul Salazar’s testimony cannot sustain the Claimant’s burden.

**C. Evidence of General Working Conditions is Insufficient**

The Claimant argues that the testimony of Supervisor Bryan Monte regarding the weight of the forms (up to 250 pounds) corroborates the accident. This argument conflates evidence of general working conditions with evidence of a specific accident. In *Broussard v. Capitol Building Materials*, 98-1513 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/25/99), 740 So.2d 762, 767, the court held that general evidence of hazardous work conditions is insufficient to establish that a specific accident occurred.

Supervisor Monte’s testimony establishes the nature of the work but does not establish that the Claimant sustained a specific injury at a specific time. No witness testified to seeing the Claimant injure himself. Therefore, the Claimant’s testimony alone, unsupported by objective evidence of the incident, cannot carry his burden of proof. *See Lacy v. McGinnis Construction Management Corp.*, 94-2092 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/28/95), 657 So.2d 592, 596.

**D. The Claimant’s Credibility is Undermined by Delayed Reporting and Contradictory Circumstances**

The Claimant suggests that his delay in reporting was reasonable because he was unaware of the severity of his injury and was distracted by a disciplinary matter. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive. The Claimant testified he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of the accident, which contradicts the assertion that he believed the injury was minor. *Brown v. Texas-LA Cartage, Inc.*, 98-1064 (La. 12/1/98), 721 So.2d 885, 891.

More critically, the Claimant failed to report the accident when he met his supervisor face-to-face on the morning of March 24, 2011. He was handed a Personnel Action Form involving an unrelated incident and chose not to report the injury at that time. He only reported the accident after being confronted with the disciplinary form. This sequence of events supports the Employer’s contention that the claim was fabricated in response to disciplinary action. *Butler v. Union Pacific Railroad*, 2003-1765 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/2/04), 880 So.2d 140, 146. The Claimant’s explanation that he was "distracted" does not explain why he failed to report the injury during the entire workday of March 24, 2011, prior to the disciplinary confrontation. These inconsistencies significantly undermine the Claimant’s credibility.

**E. The Employer’s Denial Was Not Arbitrary and Capricious**

The Claimant argues that the Employer’s denial was arbitrary and capricious, warranting penalties under La. R.S. 23:1201(F). However, penalties are warranted only when the employer's refusal to pay benefits is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. *Brown*, 721 So.2d at 890. An employer has reasonable grounds to deny a claim when the claimant fails to provide corroborating evidence and the circumstances are suspicious. *Williams v. General Motors Corp.*, 99-1227 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/30/00), 762 So.2d 1222, 1229.

Here, the Employer had substantial evidence to support its denial: the Claimant provided no eyewitness testimony to the accident; his medical records were based solely on his self-reporting; and he reported the injury only after being disciplined. The Employer’s reliance on the lack of objective evidence and the suspicious timing of the report constituted "reasonable controversion" of the claim. The trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney’s fees, and the appellate court correctly reversed that portion of the judgment.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Claimant failed to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he sustained a compensable work-related accident. The trial court’s judgment in favor of the Claimant was manifestly erroneous. Furthermore, the Employer’s denial of the claim was reasonable and not arbitrary and capricious. The rebuttal arguments presented by the Claimant do not overcome the deficiencies in the evidence supporting his claim.

**IT IS ORDERED, ADJUDGED, AND DECREED** that the preliminary ruling of this Court is affirmed. The judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation is reversed in its entirety, and the Claimant’s petition is dismissed. Judgment is hereby entered in favor of Command Construction, LLC.
