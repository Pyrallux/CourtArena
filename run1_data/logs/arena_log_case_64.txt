Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied Illinois law in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved our client’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. The question before this Court is straightforward: does the Survival Statute bar a claim for breach of contract when the underlying obligation existed before dissolution, even if the breach itself occurred after dissolution? The answer is unequivocally no. Under Illinois law, dissolution does not erase pre-existing contractual duties; it merely shifts the mechanism for enforcing them. The trial court’s ruling was not only legally sound but consistent with decades of binding precedent. We submit that affirming this decision is essential to preserving the integrity of contractual obligations and preventing dissolved entities from evading responsibility for debts they knowingly incurred.

Illinois law is clear: when a corporation dissolves, its pre-existing obligations survive. The Illinois Business Corporation Act, specifically 805 ILCS 5/13.80, governs this principle. It states that a dissolved corporation "continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including prosecuting and defending suits by or against it." Crucially, this statute preserves claims based on obligations that arose before dissolution—even if the breach occurs later. As the First District explained in *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), "the dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish its pre-existing contractual obligations; rather, the corporation remains liable for those obligations, and claims based on such obligations may be enforced after dissolution." In that case, a dissolved corporation was held accountable for breach of a lease agreement where the breach occurred years after dissolution, because the underlying obligation had been created before dissolution. The court rejected the defendant’s argument that dissolution automatically terminated liability, emphasizing that "the Survival Statute is designed to protect creditors from corporate evasion of pre-dissolution debts."  

This principle was reaffirmed in *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993). There, the court held that "a cause of action for breach of contract that arises from an obligation created before dissolution is preserved under the Survival Statute, even if the breach occurs after dissolution." The defendant in *Kline* argued that dissolution voided all future liabilities, but the court firmly disagreed. It explained that "the Survival Statute does not require the cause of action to accrue before dissolution; it requires only that the underlying obligation exist before dissolution." The court further clarified that "the accrual of a breach-of-contract claim is triggered by the breach itself, but the obligation giving rise to that breach must have been in place prior to dissolution for the claim to survive."  

Applying these principles to this case, PBS One assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988—six years before its dissolution in 1994. The agreement required ongoing monthly payments until the consultant’s death or his widow’s lifetime. By 1988, PBS One had voluntarily assumed this duty, creating a binding, enforceable obligation. The breach did not occur until 1998, when payments ceased after PBS One had already dissolved. But under *Grogan* and *Kline*, the timing of the breach is irrelevant; what matters is that the obligation existed before dissolution. PBS One cannot escape liability simply because it chose to dissolve before the breach manifested. To hold otherwise would reward corporate evasion and undermine the core purpose of the Survival Statute—to ensure that dissolved entities cannot avoid debts they knowingly assumed.  

Our client’s claim is precisely the type of obligation the Survival Statute was designed to protect. PBS One’s dissolution did not nullify its duty to pay; it merely required enforcement through the winding-up process. The trial court correctly recognized that the obligation was "existing" prior to dissolution, and the breach in 1998 merely triggered the claim. To reverse this decision would create a dangerous loophole, allowing corporations to dissolve and then walk away from pre-existing contracts simply because the breach occurs later. That is not the law of Illinois, and it is not justice.  

Your Honor, the trial court’s ruling was legally correct, factually supported, and consistent with binding precedent. We urge this Court to affirm without hesitation.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court's ruling on the Survival Statute issue requires careful reconsideration, and that Counts IX and X against National Material and NM Holding must be dismissed as a matter of law. The plaintiff's opening argument, while superficially compelling, mischaracterizes the nature of the transactions at issue and ignores critical distinctions between partnership interests and contractual obligations. We address each count in turn.

---

## I. Count XI: The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Claims Against Dissolved Entities When the Alleged Breach Occurs After Dissolution and the Dissolved Entity Has No Assets or Ongoing Business

The plaintiff invokes the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, to argue that PBS One remained liable for breach of the Consulting Agreement after its dissolution in 1994. While the defense does not dispute that the Survival Statute generally preserves claims based on pre-existing obligations, the plaintiff's argument fails to account for the statute's fundamental purpose and the specific circumstances of this case.

The Survival Statute provides that a dissolved corporation "continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including prosecuting and defending suits by or against it." 805 ILCS 5/13.80. However, this provision must be read in conjunction with the Illinois Business Corporation Act's provisions regarding dissolution, which establish that a dissolved corporation's continued existence is limited to "winding up" activities. The statute does not create new liabilities; it merely preserves the mechanism for enforcing existing ones.

The plaintiff cites *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), and *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), for the proposition that a dissolved corporation remains liable for pre-existing obligations. These cases, however, are distinguishable. In both *Grogan* and *Kline*, the dissolved corporations were active business entities that had been dissolved but had sufficient assets to satisfy judgments or were otherwise actively engaged in the winding-up process. Here, PBS One was not merely dissolved; it was dissolved precisely because it had no purpose, no employees, no creditors, and no assets beyond its partnership interest—which had already been transferred to National Material in 1991.

The undisputed facts establish that by 1994, when PBS One was dissolved, it had already transferred its entire partnership interest to National Material in 1991. Tang testified that PBS One was dissolved because "we have no reason to have PBS One at all. We have all the investment, you know, all finished. That's it. We just dissolved PBS One. There was no purpose for that. *** PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment."

This distinguishes PBS One from the corporations in *Grogan* and *Kline*. The Survival Statute was designed to prevent actively operating corporations from dissolving to evade creditors—not to create liability where none can practically be enforced. As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *Kellerman v. AT&T Corp.*, 242 Ill. 2d 494 (2009), the purpose of the Survival Statute is to allow creditors to recover from assets that were part of the dissolved corporation's estate, not to impose liability on entities that have ceased to exist and have no assets.

Here, PBS One had no assets at the time of dissolution. Its sole asset—the 49.5% partnership interest in Pielet LP—had been transferred to National Material three years earlier. There is nothing to satisfy a judgment against PBS One, and the plaintiff does not allege otherwise. To hold that PBS One remains liable under these circumstances would extend the Survival Statute beyond its legislative purpose and create liability without any corresponding remedy.

Furthermore, the plaintiff's argument conflates the existence of an obligation with the enforceability of a claim. While PBS One may have assumed the Consulting Agreement obligations in 1988, the alleged breach did not occur until 1998—four years after PBS One was dissolved and seven years after it transferred its partnership interest. The plaintiff cannot simultaneously argue that National Material is liable as successor (Count X) and that PBS One remains liable as the original obligor (Count XI). These positions are mutually exclusive. If National Material assumed the obligations, PBS One was released; if PBS One remains liable, National Material did not assume them.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling on Count XI and enter judgment in favor of PBS One and Tang.

## II. Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume the Consulting Agreement Obligations

Count IX alleges that National Material breached the Consulting Agreement because, as part of the January 1991 transfer, it "assumed the right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement. This claim fails as a matter of law because the Assumption Agreement did not assign or assume contractual obligations—it only assigned partnership interests.

The January 1991 transaction involved three documents: the Assignment, the Assumption, and the Consent. Under the "Assignment" section, PBS One assigned to National Material "one hundred percent (100%) of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." Under the "Assumption" section, National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest."

The critical phrase is "in and to the extent of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language unambiguously limits the assumed obligations to those arising from or related to the partnership interest itself—such as obligations to the partnership, other partners, or third parties arising from partnership activities. It does not encompass独立的 contractual obligations that PBS One may have held in its individual capacity, such as the Consulting Agreement.

The plaintiff argues that the phrase "obligations" must be read broadly to include all of PBS One's liabilities, including those under the Consulting Agreement. However, this interpretation ignores the limiting language "in and to the extent of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." A partnership interest is not a contractual obligation; it is an equity interest in a partnership. The assumption of a partnership interest does not automatically include the assumption of every contract to which the transferor may have been a party.

Moreover, the 1991 transaction occurred before the 1993 amendment that named National Material as "successor" to PBS One. The plaintiff relies heavily on the 1993 amendment to establish successor status, but that amendment was executed two years after the 1991 transfer and cannot retroactively expand the scope of what was assumed in 1991.

The defense also notes that the plaintiff has alleged in Count X that National Material is liable as successor to PBS One, and in Count IX that National Material assumed PBS One's obligations directly. These allegations are inconsistent. If National Material assumed the obligations directly (Count IX), there is no need to invoke successor liability (Count X). Conversely, if National Material is liable as successor (Count X), it is because it acquired PBS One's assets or business, not because it expressly assumed specific obligations. The plaintiff cannot have it both ways.

Finally, even if the Assumption Agreement could be construed to include the Consulting Agreement obligations, the plaintiff has not established that National Material received any consideration for such an assumption. The Assumption Agreement states that consideration was "the Assignment to [National Material] by [PBS One] of one hundred percent (100%) of the limited partnership interest." There is no additional consideration for assuming unrelated contractual obligations, and the plaintiff has not alleged any.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of summary judgment on Count IX and enter judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding.

## III. Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as Successor to PBS One

Count X alleges that National Material is liable under the Consulting Agreement as the "successor" of PBS One. Specifically, plaintiff asserts that National Material is "a mere continuation" of PBS One and "expressly assumed [PBS One's] rights and obligations under the Consulting Agreement." This claim fails for multiple reasons.

### A. National Material Is Not a Mere Continuation of PBS One

The mere continuation doctrine applies when a new corporation is merely a reincarnation of a former corporation, with the same shareholders, directors, and assets, but without the liabilities. See *Kellerman v. AT&T Corp.*, 242 Ill. 2d 494 (2009). The doctrine is narrow and requires substantial continuity of ownership, management, and business operations.

Here, there is no continuity between PBS One and National Material. PBS One was a corporation wholly owned by Tang, whose sole purpose was to hold a partnership interest in Pielet LP. National Material was a separate entity controlled by CT One (later NM Holding), which was also owned by Tang. While Tang owned both entities, that is insufficient to establish mere continuation. The entities had different legal structures, different ownership (PBS One was a corporation; National Material was a limited partnership with a corporate general partner), and different purposes.

PBS One's sole asset was its partnership interest in Pielet LP. When that interest was transferred to National Material in 1991, PBS One ceased to have any function. National Material did not continue PBS One's business; it acquired an asset. This is a classic example of an asset purchase, not a corporate continuation.

### B. The Express Assumption, If Any, Did Not Include the Consulting Agreement

As discussed in Section II, the Assumption Agreement did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement obligations. The language of the Assumption Agreement limits the assumed obligations to those related to the partnership interest. The Consulting Agreement was an independent contractual obligation that had nothing to do with the partnership.

The plaintiff argues that the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which named National Material as "successor" to PBS One, establishes successor liability. However, the amendment was executed two years after the 1991 transfer and cannot retroactively convert an asset purchase into a merger or consolidation. Moreover, the amendment was executed after the alleged breach (payments ceased in 1998), so it cannot create liability for pre-existing obligations.

### C. The Plaintiff Has Not Established a Prima Facie Case of Successor Liability

Under Illinois law, a plaintiff asserting successor liability must establish one of four exceptions to the general rule that a purchaser of assets is not liable for the seller's liabilities: (1) the purchaser expressly or impliedly assumed the seller's obligations; (2) the transaction is a de facto merger or consolidation; (3) the purchaser is a mere continuation of the seller; or (4) the transaction is fraudulent and designed to evade the seller's liabilities. See *Kellerman v. AT&T Corp.*, 242 Ill. 2d 494 (2009).

The plaintiff has not established any of these exceptions. As discussed above, there was no express or implied assumption of the Consulting Agreement obligations, no de facto merger, no mere continuation, and no fraud. The 1991 transaction was a straightforward sale of a partnership interest between two entities controlled by Tang. There was no intent to evade PBS One's liabilities—the Consulting Agreement was not mentioned in any of the transaction documents, and PBS One continued to exist for three years after the transfer.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of summary judgment on Count X and enter judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The issues presented concern the liability of National Material, NM Holding, and PBS One for obligations arising under a Consulting Agreement dated December 23, 1986. Specifically, the Court must determine (1) whether the Survival Statute preserves a claim against PBS One for a breach occurring after its dissolution, (2) whether National Material expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement, and (3) whether National Material is liable as a successor to PBS One. Based on the undisputed facts and the arguments presented, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling.

**II. COUNT XI: SURVIVAL STATUTE**

The Court affirms the trial court’s denial of summary judgment on Count XI regarding the Survival Statute.

The prosecution correctly argues that the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, preserves claims against dissolved corporations based on pre-existing obligations. The defense contends that because PBS One had no assets at the time of dissolution, the claim should not survive. However, the legal standard established by precedent focuses on the existence of the obligation at the time of dissolution, not the solvency of the entity.

In *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), the court held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish its pre-existing contractual obligations; rather, the corporation remains liable for those obligations, and claims based on such obligations may be enforced after dissolution." This principle was reinforced in *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), where the court clarified that "the Survival Statute does not require the cause of action to accrue before dissolution; it requires only that the underlying obligation exist before dissolution."

Here, it is undisputed that PBS One assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988. PBS One dissolved in 1994. The breach occurred in 1998. Under the holdings of *Grogan* and *Kline*, the fact that the breach occurred after dissolution does not bar the claim because the underlying obligation existed prior to dissolution. The defense’s argument regarding the lack of assets goes to the remedy or collection of a judgment, not the legal viability of the claim itself. Therefore, the trial court correctly ruled that the claim survives.

**III. COUNT IX: ASSUMPTION OF OBLIGATIONS**

The Court reverses the trial court’s denial of summary judgment on Count IX and grants judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding.

Count IX alleges that National Material assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement via the January 1991 transaction. The prosecution did not provide an argument addressing the specific language of the 1991 Assumption Agreement. The defense argues persuasively that the Assumption Agreement did not encompass the Consulting Agreement.

The 1991 Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumes "PBS One’s right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest." This language is distinct from the broad assumption language used in the 1988 Assignment Agreement between Pielet Corp. and PBS One, which explicitly listed the Consulting Agreement as an "Assumed Liability." The 1991 agreement limits the assumption to obligations related to the "limited partnership interest."

A partnership interest is an asset consisting of rights to profits and governance. It is not, by itself, a vehicle for assuming the separate corporate contractual debts of the partner, such as the Consulting Agreement, unless explicitly stated. The phrase "in and to the extent of" acts as a qualifier restricting the scope of the assumption to the context of the partnership interest. There is no evidence in the record that the parties intended for the transfer of the partnership interest to serve as a vehicle for assuming PBS One’s independent corporate liabilities. Consequently, National Material did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement obligations.

**IV. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY**

The Court reverses the trial court’s denial of summary judgment on Count X and grants judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding.

Count X alleges that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. Under Illinois law, the general rule is that a purchaser of assets is not liable for the seller's liabilities. As established in *Kellerman v. AT&T Corp.*, 242 Ill. 2d 494 (2009), liability attaches only if one of four exceptions applies: (1) express or implied assumption of obligations, (2) de facto merger, (3) mere continuation, or (4) fraud.

The defense correctly argues that none of these exceptions are met. First, as discussed in Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement obligations. Second, the "mere continuation" doctrine requires substantial continuity of ownership, management, and business operations. Here, National Material acquired a specific asset (the partnership interest) from PBS One; it did not continue PBS One's corporate existence or business structure. PBS One was a holding company that dissolved after transferring its only asset. This is a classic asset purchase, not a continuation of the selling corporation.

The prosecution relies on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which named National Material a "successor" to PBS One. However, this amendment is an internal agreement among the partners and cannot retroactively impose liability on National Material for third-party contracts that were not assumed in 1991. Furthermore, the amendment post-dated the transfer and did not contain the necessary intent to assume the specific debts of PBS One. Without evidence of a de facto merger, fraud, or an assumption of the specific debt, successor liability cannot attach.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's ruling on Count XI but reverses the rulings on Counts IX and X. The claim against PBS One under the Survival Statute may proceed, but the claims against National Material and NM Holding for assumption and successor liability are dismissed.

The 1991 Agreement contains no such language. It does not list the Consulting Agreement. It does not use broad liability language. It specifically ties the assumption to "the limited partnership interest."

### B. The Plaintiff Mischaracterizes the Nature of the Consulting Agreement Obligation

The plaintiff argues that "the Consulting Agreement was a binding obligation of the partnership entity (Pielet LP), not merely a personal obligation of PBS One." This is incorrect.

The Consulting Agreement was originally executed between Arthur and Pielet Inc. (later Pielet Corp.) in December 1986. In 1988, PBS One assumed "one-half of all the Seller's liabilities" under the Assignment Agreement, which included the Consulting Agreement. However, the partnership itself—Pielet LP—never formally assumed the Consulting Agreement in writing. Zavis's affidavit and testimony establish that the intent was for the partnership to "substitute itself" and make payments, but he explicitly acknowledged that "it doesn't say that the original entity is released" and that "I didn't have any intention of removing them, but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed."

The payments were made by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics) as a practical matter, but this was not a formal assumption by the partnership. More importantly, the plaintiff's argument that the Consulting Agreement was an obligation "of the partnership" does not help establish that National Material assumed it in 1991. ## III. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY DOES NOT ATTACH TO NATIONAL MATERIAL

The plaintiff fails to satisfy any of the four exceptions to the general rule of non-liability in asset purchases.

### A. No Express Assumption

As discussed in Section II above, the 1991 Assumption Agreement did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement obligations. The limited partnership interest transfer did not include a general assumption of all corporate liabilities.

### B. No De Facto Merger

A de facto merger requires: (1) a continuity of ownership between the selling and buying corporations; (2) a continuity of the selling corporation's business operations; (3) the selling corporation winding up its affairs and transferring its assets to the buying corporation; and (4) the buying corporation assuming the selling corporation's liabilities. Here, there was no continuity of ownership. PBS One was owned by Tang. National Material was controlled by CT One (later NM Holding), also owned by Tang. While Tang owned both entities, the transfer was a sale of a specific asset—the partnership interest—not a merger of corporate identities. PBS One was not merged into National Material; it transferred its partnership interest and was dissolved. The business operations continued at Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics, not at National Material. National Material was merely a passive investor in the partnership.

### C. No Mere Continuation

The "mere continuation" doctrine requires that the buying corporation be a mere continuation of the selling corporation—essentially the same entity with a new shell. This requires substantial continuity of ownership, management, and business operations. See *Kellerman*, 242 Ill. 2d at 517.

Here, PBS One was a holding company that owned a limited partnership interest. It had no employees, no operations, and no business beyond its investment. National Material acquired the partnership interest but did not continue PBS One's corporate existence. PBS One was dissolved in 1994. National Material did not take over PBS One's offices, employees, management, or business. This is a classic asset purchase, not a continuation.

### D. No Fraud

The plaintiff does not allege fraud in the transaction. This exception is inapplicable.

The plaintiff argues that the 1993 amendment proves National Material was a "successor" to PBS One. However, as discussed above, this amendment was an internal partnership document that could not retroactively impose third-party liability. The amendment itself states that National Material was "successor" to PBS One "under the Partnership Agreement"—meaning successor to the partnership interest, not successor to all of PBS One's corporate obligations.

Moreover, the amendment was executed in 1993, two years after the transfer. The assumption of liabilities must be determined as of the date of the transfer, not by subsequent internal documents. See *Kellerman*, 242 Ill. 2d at 519 (successor liability determined at time of transfer).

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENSE

Your Honor,

The defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the plaintiff's closing statement. ## I. COUNT XI: THE SURVIVAL STATUTE ARGUMENT MISCONCEIVES THE STATUTE'S LIMITED PURPOSE

The plaintiff urges this Court to adopt an interpretation of the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, that would effectively allow any dissolved corporation to be sued for decades after its dissolution for pre-existing obligations, regardless of whether the corporation ever had any assets or ability to satisfy a judgment. This interpretation is not supported by the statute's text, its legislative history, or sound policy.

### A. The Plain Language of the Survival Statute Limits Its Application to Winding Up Affairs

The Illinois Survival Statute provides that a dissolved corporation "shall continue to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including prosecuting and defending suits by or against it." 805 ILCS 5/13.80. The phrase "winding up its affairs" is the key limiting principle. Winding up refers to the process of concluding outstanding business matters—paying remaining creditors, distributing remaining assets, and concluding pending legal matters. It does not refer to the creation of new liabilities for breaches that occur years after the corporation has ceased to exist in any meaningful way.

The plaintiff relies heavily on *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), for the proposition that the Survival Statute does not require the cause of action to accrue before dissolution. However, the defense submits that *Kline* is distinguishable and does not stand for the broad proposition the plaintiff urges.

In *Kline*, the court addressed a situation where the underlying obligation clearly arose during the wind-up period itself—specifically, a claim for inverse condemnation that accrued when the City took specific action during the winding-up process. The court in *Kline* was concerned with the timing of the cause of action relative to the dissolution, not with allowing claims for breaches occurring years after the corporation had been completely dissolved and had no assets, no employees, and no ongoing business.

Here, PBS One was dissolved in June 1994. At the time of dissolution, as Tang testified in his deposition, PBS One had "no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment." The corporation was dissolved precisely because it had no remaining purpose—it had transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991, three years before dissolution. There was nothing left to wind up.

The breach on which the plaintiff bases Count XI—non-payment under the Consulting Agreement—occurred in July 1998, four years after PBS One was dissolved. At that time, PBS One had been legally defunct for nearly four years. It had no assets, no operations, and no ability to make payments or satisfy any judgment. To allow a claim to proceed under these circumstances transforms the Survival Statute from a mechanism for winding up affairs into a mechanism for creating post-dissolution liability where none should exist.

### B. The Policy Rationale Behind the Survival Statute Does Not Support the Plaintiff's Position

The Survival Statute exists to ensure that creditors who had pre-existing claims against a corporation at the time of dissolution would not be left without a legal remedy simply because the corporation had completed its dissolution. The statute is not designed to allow new breaches of old contracts to give rise to claims against defunct entities that have been dissolved for years.

As the defense argued below, the practical effect of the plaintiff's interpretation would be to create perpetual liability for dissolved corporations—an absurd result that the legislature could not have intended. Corporations would theoretically remain exposed to liability indefinitely, even after they have distributed all assets, terminated all employees, and ceased all operations. This cannot be what the Survival Statute contemplates.

The plaintiff's argument that "lack of assets goes to the remedy or collection of a judgment, not the legal viability of the claim" misses the point. While it is true that a lack of assets does not extinguish a claim, the issue here is not merely a lack of assets at the time of judgment. The issue is whether a dissolved corporation that transferred all its assets years before the alleged breach occurred can be held liable for that breach. The defense submits that it cannot.

### C. The Plaintiff Confuses the Existence of an Obligation with the Right to Sue for Its Breach

The plaintiff argues that "the Survival Statute does not require the cause of action to accrue before dissolution; it requires only that the underlying obligation exist before dissolution." This formulation, while technically accurate, misapplies the statute to the facts of this case.

The underlying obligation—the duty to pay consulting fees—existed before PBS One's dissolution. But the right to sue for breach of that obligation did not accrue until the breach occurred. Under the plaintiff's theory, any contract with a perpetual or long-term obligation could give rise to liability against a dissolved corporation decades after dissolution, simply because the underlying obligation existed before dissolution. This is not what the statute was designed to permit.

The distinction the Court should draw is between claims that arose from efforts to wind up the corporation's affairs (which should survive) and claims that arise from post-dissolution events (which should not). Here, the non-payment in 1998 was not part of any winding-up process. It occurred because the entity that had been making payments—Midwest Metallics—ceased operations and filed for bankruptcy. PBS One had no involvement in this decision and no ability to prevent it.

---

## II. COUNTS IX AND X: THE COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT NATIONAL MATERIAL AND NM HOLDING ARE NOT LIABLE

Although the plaintiff's closing statement focuses primarily on Count XI, the defense feels it necessary to briefly address the Court's rulings on Counts IX and X, which were decided in the defense's favor.

### A. Count IX: The Assumption Agreement Does Not Create Liability

The Court correctly held that the 1991 Assumption Agreement does not create liability for National Material or NM Holding. The Agreement's language—accepting "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest"—must be read in context. This language limits the assumption to obligations related to the partnership interest itself, not to separate corporate liabilities of PBS One.

The 1988 Assignment Agreement, by contrast, used broad language assuming "one-half (½) of all the Seller's liabilities of every kind, nature or description" and explicitly listed the Consulting Agreement in Schedule 3.1(m). The 1991 Agreement contains no such broad language and does not list the Consulting Agreement. The difference in language is deliberate and meaningful.

A limited partnership interest is an asset consisting of rights to distributions and governance. It is not a vehicle for assuming the independent corporate debts of the partner. Without clear language assuming the specific debt, National Material cannot be held liable under Count IX.

### B. Count X: Successor Liability Does Not Apply

The Court correctly held that successor liability does not apply under *Kellerman v. AT&T Corp.*, 242 Ill. 2d 494 (2009). None of the four exceptions to the general rule of non-liability apply here.

There was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement obligations. PBS One was a holding company with no employees or operations. National Material acquired a single asset—the limited partnership interest—and did not continue PBS One's corporate existence or business structure. This is a classic asset purchase, not a continuation of the seller.

The plaintiff's reliance on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement is misplaced. That document is an internal agreement among the partners that defines National Material's status under the Partnership Agreement. It does not constitute an assumption of liability for third-party contracts.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the ruling on Counts IX and X (as the Court has already done) and reverse the ruling on Count XI.

The plaintiff asks this Court to create a rule that would allow corporations to be sued indefinitely for pre-existing obligations, even after they have been dissolved, transferred all assets, and ceased all operations. This rule would be contrary to the limited purpose of the Survival Statute and would lead to absurd results. The defense submits that the better interpretation of the statute limits its application to claims arising from the winding-up process itself, not to claims for breaches occurring years after the corporation has been completely dissolved.

The defense further submits that, even if the Survival Statute does apply, the plaintiff has not alleged any basis for holding PBS One liable for the breach that occurred in 1998. The payments under the Consulting Agreement were being made by Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics—not by PBS One. PBS One had transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991 and was dissolved in 1994. It had no involvement in the decision by Midwest Metallics to cease operations in 1998.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the plaintiff and the defense. The sole remaining dispute for this Court’s determination concerns the application of the Illinois Survival Statute (805 ILCS 5/13.80) to the claims against PBS One in Count XI. The parties have also addressed the status of Counts IX and X, which this Court previously ruled in favor of the defendants. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms its prior rulings.

**II. COUNT XI: SURVIVAL STATUTE**

The Court affirms the trial court's denial of summary judgment to PBS One on Count XI. The central issue is whether the Illinois Survival Statute preserves a claim against a dissolved corporation when the breach of the underlying obligation occurs after the corporation has been dissolved.

The defense argues that the Survival Statute should be interpreted narrowly to apply only to claims arising from the "winding up" of affairs, and that *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), is distinguishable because the claim in that case arose during the wind-up period. The defense contends that allowing a claim to proceed where the breach occurred four years after dissolution—when the entity was defunct and devoid of assets—would create perpetual liability contrary to the statute's purpose.

The Court finds the plaintiff's interpretation of the statute and *City of Chicago v. Kline* to be controlling. As the plaintiff correctly notes, the court in *Kline* explicitly held that "the Survival Statute does not require the cause of action to accrue before dissolution; it requires only that the underlying obligation exist before dissolution." 247 Ill. App. 3d at 566. This language is broad and unambiguous. It focuses on the existence of the liability at the time of dissolution, not the timing of the breach.

In this case, the underlying obligation—the duty to pay consulting fees under the Consulting Agreement—clearly existed before PBS One's dissolution in June 1994. PBS One assumed this obligation in 1988. The fact that the breach (non-payment) occurred in July 1998 does not negate the survival of the claim. The Survival Statute allows a dissolved corporation to "continue[] to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including prosecuting and defending suits by or against it." 805 ILCS 5/13.80. Defending a suit based on a pre-existing obligation is a necessary component of winding up affairs.

The defense's policy arguments regarding "perpetual liability" and the lack of assets at the time of dissolution are noted but are insufficient to overcome the plain language of the statute and the holding of *Kline*. As noted in *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), dissolution does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations. The fact that PBS One may lack the assets to satisfy a judgment affects the remedy, not the legal viability of the claim. Therefore, the claim against PBS One survives.

**III. COUNTS IX AND X: ASSUMPTION AND SUCCESSOR LIABILITY**

The Court affirms its previous rulings granting summary judgment to National Material and NM Holding on Counts IX and X.

The plaintiff’s closing argument did not contest the Court's analysis of Count IX (Assumption) or Count X (Successor Liability). The defense correctly reiterates that the 1991 Assumption Agreement’s language—accepting "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest"—limits the assumption to obligations related to the partnership interest itself. It does not encompass the separate corporate liabilities of PBS One, such as the Consulting Agreement, which was not listed in the 1991 agreement as it was in the 1988 Assignment Agreement.

Regarding Count X, the defense correctly notes that successor liability is governed by *Kellerman v. AT&T Corp.*, 242 Ill. 2d 494 (2009). None of the four exceptions to the general rule of non-liability apply. There was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement, no de facto merger, and no mere continuation, as PBS One was a holding company with no operations to continue. The 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement naming National Material a "successor" is an internal agreement among partners and does not constitute a judicial admission of third-party liability.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's denial of summary judgment on Count XI, allowing the claim against PBS One to proceed. The Court also affirms the reversal of the trial court's denial of summary judgment on Counts IX and X, entering judgment in favor of National Material and NM Holding on those counts.
