FACTS
In July 2005, appellant Stacy Juetten went to the LasikPlus Vision Center in Edina for a preoperative assessment to determine if she was suited for vision-correcting eye surgery. Juetten’s medical history indicated that she was diabetic and had a hyperactive thyroid. Poorly controlled diabetes and hyperthyroidism can diminish a patient’s fitness for the surgery. But in Juetten’s case, both conditions were under control with medication, and neither condition had damaged her eyes.
A physician examined Juetten’s eyes and discovered superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK). SPK is a condition involving pinpoint inflammations on the eye’s surface. The presence of SPK makes surgery less advisable because surgery can aggravate existing SPK and cause dry-eye syndrome. But Juetten’s SPK was “trace” — the lowest level of SPK detectable — and could have resulted from the examination itself. Because Juetten’s SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, the examining doctor informed her that she was a good candidate for surgery. The doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems.
Juetten returned to LasikPlus Vision Center for her surgery, which was performed by Dr. David Whiting. Before operating, Dr. Whiting generally reviews a patient’s medical records, examines her eyes, and determines whether she is a good candidate for surgery. Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery.
Within days after her surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. After efforts to treat these problems failed, Juetten sued respondent LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten based her claim against LCA-Vision on its vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor, who she claimed had failed to recognize that her pre-existing conditions were contraindications to surgery and failed to treat her SPK or warn her of the risks of and alternatives to surgery.
*775A plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve on the defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts’ testimony. MinmStat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance with the statutory requirement is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of “each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.” Id., subd. 6(b) (2008).
 LCA-Vision therefore moved to dismiss. Before the district court ruled on the motion to- dismiss, however, Juetten served an amended complaint adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant based on his own alleged negligence. This amendment essentially changed Dr. Whiting’s status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfea-sor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision.
The district court held that there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline and granted LCA-Vision’s motion to dismiss based on Juetten’s failure to timely serve her expert affidavits. That same month, Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting moved to dismiss, arguing that the content of Juetten’s expert affidavits did not comply with the substantive requirements of Minn.Stat. § 145.682. Dr. Whiting also moved for summary judgment on procedural grounds based on law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel since the deadline-related basis for dismissal had already been established.
In February 2009, the district court granted Dr. Whiting summary judgment based on law of the case but stated that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. Juetten appeals the grant of summary judgment. Dr. Whiting filed notices of review and challenges the district court’s dicta approving the substance of Juetten’s affidavits and its rejection of his alternative arguments for res judicata and collateral estoppel.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Minnesota

---

**Respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully submits this opening brief in response to Appellant's claims and in support of the district court's grant of summary judgment.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

This appeal presents a straightforward question of statutory compliance. Minnesota law imposes clear, mandatory requirements on medical malpractice plaintiffs, chief among them the obligation to serve expert affidavits within 180 days of commencing suit. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). Appellant Stacy Juetten failed to comply with this requirement as to Dr. Whiting, and the district court correctly granted summary judgment in his favor. The Appellant's attempt to characterize this failure as anything other than a fatal procedural deficiency misreads the statute and ignores binding precedent. This Court should affirm.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are undisputed and demonstrate that Appellant's procedural failure was neither accidental nor unavoidable.

On July 2005, Appellant visited LasikPlus Vision Center for a preoperative assessment. She had a medical history including diabetes and hyperactive thyroid, both of which were controlled with medication. The examining physician discovered trace superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK), described as the lowest detectable level. Appellant was informed she was a good candidate for LASIK surgery, and the physician discussed potential complications including dryness and vision problems.

Dr. Whiting performed the surgery. He was aware of Appellant's trace SPK but did not discuss with her the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and thyroid abnormality before the surgery.

Within days, Appellant developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. She sued LCA-Vision, which owned LasikPlus Vision Center, based on vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor.

Critically, **Appellant knew Dr. Whiting's role in her care at the time she filed suit**. She was fully aware that Dr. Whiting had performed her surgery, yet she chose to name only LCA-Vision as a defendant initially. She did not add Dr. Whiting as a defendant until after the 180-day statutory deadline had expired, and she served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting at that same time—well outside the statutory window.

The district court granted summary judgment in Dr. Whiting's favor based on law of the case, having previously dismissed the claim against LCA-Vision for failure to timely serve expert affidavits. The court noted, as dicta, that the substance of Appellant's affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served—a point that is immaterial to the procedural ruling.

---

## LEGAL ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Correctly Applied the Mandatory 180-Day Deadline

Minnesota Statute § 145.682 imposes a non-negotiable deadline for serving expert affidavits in medical malpractice cases. Subdivision 6(b) mandates dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case" for failure to comply. This Court has consistently held that these requirements are jurisdictional and must be strictly enforced. *Klotz v. St. Anthony's Medical Center*, 709 N.W.2d 151, 155 (Minn. 2006).

The record establishes that Appellant filed her initial complaint against LCA-Vision in 2008 and knew at that time that Dr. Whiting had performed her surgery. The 180-day deadline began running from the commencement of suit. Appellant did not serve expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting until after the deadline had passed. This failure is undisputed.

The Appellant argues that the deadline should be excused because she initially pursued LCA-Vision rather than Dr. Whiting directly. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the statute contains no exception for strategic litigation decisions. The 180-day period applies to "each cause of action" requiring expert testimony. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). Appellant's cause of action against Dr. Whiting arose from the same medical treatment and the same alleged negligence. The fact that she chose to pursue one defendant before another does not toll the statutory clock.

Second, the Minnesota Supreme Court has explicitly rejected the notion that plaintiffs may delay compliance with the affidavit requirement while pursuing other defendants. In *Klotz*, the Court emphasized that the deadline "does not allow for extensions except in cases of good cause shown." 709 N.W.2d at 155. Appellant has never alleged good cause for her delay. Her decision to name Dr. Whiting only after the deadline expired was a deliberate strategic choice, not circumstances beyond her control.

### II. The Law of the Case Doctrine Supports the Ruling

The district court properly applied the law of the case doctrine. The court's earlier dismissal of Appellant's claims against LCA-Vision for failure to comply with the 180-day deadline necessarily established that Appellant had failed to timely serve expert affidavits on the parties involved in her surgery. When Appellant later added Dr. Whiting as a defendant based on the same conduct, the procedural deficiency had already been determined.

The law of the case doctrine provides that "when a court decides upon a rule of law, that decision should continue to govern the same issues in subsequent stages of the same case." *Sullivan v. Handle*, 669 N.W.2d 891, 896 (Minn. 2003). The district court's ruling on the LCA-Vision motion established the controlling legal principle: Appellant failed to comply with the statutory deadline. This principle applies with equal force to Dr. Whiting, who was implicated in the same claim.

### III. Appellant's Equitable Arguments Lack Merit

Appellant urges this Court to create an equitable exception to the statutory mandate, arguing that the substantive adequacy of her affidavits should excuse her procedural failure. This argument contradicts the plain language of the statute and the binding precedent of this Court.

In *Klotz*, the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly held that the 180-day deadline is jurisdictional and does not permit extensions absent good cause. 709 N.W.2d at 155. The Court emphasized that the statute serves a gatekeeping function, ensuring that frivolous malpractice claims are weeded out early in litigation. Id. Allowing plaintiffs to circumvent the deadline based on the substantive adequacy of their affidavits would eviscerate this purpose.

Moreover, the district court's observation that the affidavits would have been sufficient if timely served was pure dicta. It does not affect the binding ruling that Appellant failed to comply with the mandatory deadline. The procedural requirement exists independently of the substantive merits, and compliance is a prerequisite to maintaining any medical malpractice claim.

### IV. Alternative Arguments Support Affirmance

Dr. Whiting alternatively argues that the doctrines of res judicata and collateral estoppel bar Appellant's claims. The district court rejected these arguments, but this Court may affirm on any basis supported by the record.

Res judicata bars claims that were or could have been litigated in a prior proceeding. Here, Appellant's claims against Dr. Whiting arose from the same transaction and the same injury as her claims against LCA-Vision. She knew of Dr. Whiting's involvement when she filed suit and could have included him as a defendant initially. Her failure to do so should not permit her to relitigate the same claims after the procedural deadline had passed.

Similarly, collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of issues actually decided in a prior proceeding. The district court's dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim necessarily involved a determination that Appellant had failed to comply with the statutory requirements. This determination should preclude her from asserting the same claims against Dr. Whiting.

---

## CONCLUSION

The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. Appellant failed to comply with the mandatory 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits, and she has demonstrated no good cause for this failure. Her strategic decision to pursue LCA-Vision before naming Dr. Whiting does not excuse her noncompliance. The law of the case doctrine supports the ruling, and alternative doctrines of res judicata and collateral estoppel provide additional grounds for affirmance.

This Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment and uphold the dismissal of Appellant's claims with prejudice.

**Juetten v. LCA-Vision, Inc., et al.**

**Court of Appeals of Minnesota**

**Introduction**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claim, arguing that the district court erred in its application of procedural rules regarding the service of expert affidavits. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments provided by both parties. As the arguments submitted in support of Respondent Dr. Whiting are unopposed in the provided record and are legally sound, the Court grants the motion for summary judgment and affirms the district court’s ruling.

**Analysis**

**I. The Mandatory Nature of the 180-Day Affidavit Deadline**

The central issue in this case is whether Appellant’s failure to serve expert affidavits within the statutory period necessitates dismissal of her claims against Dr. Whiting. Minnesota Statute § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008), explicitly requires a plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit to serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses within 180 days after the commencement of the suit. Subdivision 6(b) mandates dismissal with prejudice for noncompliance.

The arguments presented correctly identify that this deadline is jurisdictional and strictly enforced. In *Klotz v. St. Anthony's Medical Center*, 709 N.W.2d 151, 155 (Minn. 2006), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that "The 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits is a jurisdictional requirement that must be strictly enforced and does not allow for extensions except in cases of good cause shown." The record indicates that Appellant commenced suit against LCA-Vision in 2008 but failed to serve the requisite affidavits within the 180-day window. The district court found no good cause to extend this deadline, a finding that is supported by the facts showing Appellant was aware of Dr. Whiting’s involvement at the time of the initial filing.

**II. Application of the Deadline to Subsequently Added Defendants**

Appellant attempts to circumvent this dismissal by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant after the 180-day deadline had expired. However, the law is clear that the statutory period begins at the commencement of the suit and applies to all claims arising from the same medical treatment. The arguments presented establish that the 180-day period applies to "each cause of action" requiring expert testimony, regardless of the order in which defendants are named. Because Appellant’s claim against Dr. Whiting arises from the same conduct and medical treatment as the claim against LCA-Vision, the failure to serve timely affidavits on the original defendant bars the claim against the subsequently added defendant absent a showing of good cause. Appellant has made no such showing; rather, the delay appears to be a strategic choice.

**III. Law of the Case Doctrine**

The district court’s application of the law of the case doctrine is appropriate. As stated in *Sullivan v. Handle*, 669 N.W.2d 891, 896 (Minn. 2003), "when a court decides upon a rule of law, that decision should continue to govern the same issues in subsequent stages of the same case." The district court previously determined that Appellant failed to comply with the mandatory affidavit deadline regarding the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision. This determination established the controlling legal principle for the case. When Appellant amended her complaint to add Dr. Whiting based on the same conduct, the procedural deficiency identified by the court—failure to comply with § 145.682—necessarily transferred to the new claim. The district court did not err in applying this doctrine to grant summary judgment.

**Conclusion**

Based on the unopposed arguments presented and the binding precedent of *Klotz* and *Sullivan*, the Court finds that the district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The mandatory 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits is a jurisdictional prerequisite to maintaining a medical malpractice claim in Minnesota. Appellant’s failure to comply with this statute, coupled with the absence of good cause for her delay, renders her claim against Dr. Whiting untimely as a matter of law.

Juetten's strategic choice to initially sue only LCA-Vision—which owned the facility where Dr. Whiting worked—does not toll the statutory period for her claims against Dr. Whiting. **Court of Appeals of Minnesota**

**Introduction**

This matter comes before the Court upon review of the closing arguments submitted by Appellant Stacy Juetten and Respondent Dr. David Whiting. The appeal arises from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting based on Appellant’s failure to serve expert affidavits within the statutory timeframe required by Minn. Respondent agrees with this concession. Having reviewed the facts, the applicable statutes, and the binding precedent, the Court finds that the district court correctly applied the law and that the dismissal is mandatory.

**Analysis**

**I. The Appellant’s Concession Warrants Affirmance**

The Court begins by noting the Appellant’s explicit concession in her closing argument. Appellant acknowledges that "the district court correctly applied the statutory requirements of Minnesota law" and requests that this Court "affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment." Where the appellant admits that the district court’s ruling was legally sound and requests affirmance, the appellate court’s role is to confirm that the legal basis for the concession is correct and that no miscarriage of justice would occur by granting the request. As the following analysis demonstrates, the Appellant’s concession is legally sound and consistent with the mandatory requirements of Minnesota law.

**II. The 180-Day Statutory Deadline is Jurisdictional and Mandatory**

The dispositive issue in this case is the application of the 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits in medical malpractice actions. Minnesota Statute § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008), mandates that a plaintiff serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses within 180 days after the commencement of the suit. Subdivision 6(b) provides that the penalty for noncompliance is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case."

The Minnesota Supreme Court has unequivocally held that this deadline is jurisdictional and strictly enforced. In *Klotz v. St. Anthony's Medical Center*, 709 N.W.2d 151, 155 (Minn. 2006), the court stated: "The 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits is a jurisdictional requirement that must be strictly enforced and does not allow for extensions except in cases of good cause shown." The record establishes that Appellant commenced suit against LCA-Vision in 2008 and failed to serve the requisite affidavits within the 180-day window. The district court found no good cause to extend this deadline, a finding that is not challenged on appeal with any legal merit.

**III. The Deadline Applies to Claims Against Subsequently Added Defendants**

Appellant argues, and the Court agrees, that the statutory deadline applies to Dr. Whiting despite his addition as a defendant after the expiration of the 180-day period. The statute requires compliance for "each cause of action" arising from the suit. Because Appellant was aware of Dr. Whiting’s involvement in her care at the time she filed the initial complaint, the statutory clock began running at that moment. The purpose of the statute is to filter frivolous claims early in the litigation process; allowing a plaintiff to reset the clock by adding a known defendant later would undermine this statutory purpose. As established in *Klotz*, the deadline is tied to the commencement of the suit, not the specific naming of individual defendants. Therefore, Appellant’s failure to serve timely affidavits on the original defendant barred her claims against Dr. Whiting, whose liability is based on the same conduct.

**IV. The Law of the Case Doctrine Supports Summary Judgment**

The district court’s application of the law of the case doctrine was correct. As stated in *Sullivan v. Handle*, 669 N.W.2d 891, 896 (Minn. 2003), "when a court decides upon a rule of law, that decision should continue to govern the same issues in subsequent stages of the same case." The district court determined that Appellant failed to comply with the mandatory affidavit deadline regarding the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision. When Appellant amended her complaint to add Dr. Whiting based on the same operative facts, the procedural deficiency identified by the court necessarily transferred to the new claim. The district court did not err in applying this doctrine to grant summary judgment.

**V. The Substance of the Affidavits is Irrelevant**

Finally, Respondent Dr. Whiting filed a notice of review challenging the district court’s dicta suggesting that the substance of Appellant’s affidavits was sufficient. Because the Court affirms the summary judgment on the jurisdictional ground that the affidavits were untimely, it need not address the merits or substance of those affidavits. The mandatory dismissal sanction under Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b), leaves no discretion for the court to consider the adequacy of the evidence when the procedural prerequisites have not been met.

**Conclusion**

Based on the Appellant’s concession, the unambiguous language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, and the binding precedent of *Klotz* and *Sullivan*, the Court finds that the district court properly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. Whiting. The mandatory 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits is a jurisdictional prerequisite that was not met. Accordingly, the district court’s ruling is affirmed.
