## I. THE OFFICER'S IMPROPER COMMENT ON DEFENDANT'S SILENCE CONSTITUTES REVERSIBLE ERROR, NOT HARMLESS ERROR

### A. The Constitutional Violation

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution guarantees that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." This protection extends to the right to remain silent during police interrogation. *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966). When a law enforcement officer comments on a defendant's exercise of this fundamental right, the defendant suffers prejudice per se. *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965).

The record establishes that Officer Shugrue testified regarding the Defendant's exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. As this Court acknowledged, such testimony was "calculated to prejudice the Defendant in the eyes of the jury." This Court further noted its agreement that the comment was improper. The Defense submits that this Court erred in applying harmless error analysis to remedy this constitutional violation.

### B. The State Has Not Met Its Burden of Proving Harmlessness Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The State invokes *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), for the proposition that constitutional errors may be deemed harmless. However, *Chapman* specifically held that before a constitutional error may be deemed harmless, the reviewing court must conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that the error "did not contribute to the defendant's conviction." *Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 24.

The State argues that the breathalyzer reading of .12 blood alcohol concentration—which exceeds Florida's .10 threshold—constitutes "overwhelming evidence" sufficient to render the constitutional error harmless. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the admissibility of the breathalyzer reading itself is intertwined with the very constitutional violation at issue. The officer's improper comment followed the defendant's exercise of his right to remain silent. This exercise occurred in the context of the DUI investigation, where the defendant also refused field sobriety tests. The State's characterization of this refusal as independent evidence of guilt actually underscores the prejudice: the jury was invited to infer guilt from the defendant's silence, which is precisely what the Fifth Amendment forbids.

Second, the State's reliance on *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), is misplaced. In *Van Arsdall*, the Supreme Court held that improper comments on a defendant's right to silence may be harmless "where the other evidence of guilt is overwhelming." However, the Court emphasized that the harmless error inquiry must consider "the way in which the evidence was improperly admitted" and "the degree to which the improper evidence was emphasized." *Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. at 684. Here, the officer's comment was not an isolated incident but rather a deliberate attempt to penalize the defendant for exercising a constitutional right.

Third, this Court acknowledged that the comment was "calculated to prejudice the Defendant." This finding alone demonstrates that the error was not harmless. The very fact that the comment was designed to influence the jury establishes that it likely did so. As this Court recognized, "career officers find it necessary to support the prosecutor in proving the State's case when in fact it can lead to the loss of the case." This Court further stated it could not "in good conscience condone such conduct from officers who know or should know better."

The Defense submits that this Court cannot simultaneously find that the officer's conduct was improper and calculated to prejudice the defendant, yet conclude that such prejudice did not influence the jury's verdict. The two findings are irreconcilable.

### C. The Strength of the Evidence Does Not Cure the Constitutional Violation

The State argues that the BAC reading of .12 alone establishes prima facie evidence of impairment under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c). However, this argument assumes the admissibility and reliability of the breathalyzer evidence—issues that form the basis of the second appellate issue.

Moreover, even assuming the breathalyzer reading was properly admitted, the State cannot establish harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt where the jury was explicitly invited to penalize the defendant for exercising his constitutional right. The improper comment did not merely present additional evidence of guilt; it told the jury that the defendant's silence itself demonstrated guilt. This message fundamentally corrupted the trial's integrity.

The Defense further notes that this Court acknowledged "the strength of the evidence against the Defendant" only after finding the error harmless. This reasoning improperly places the burden on the defendant to prove prejudice, rather than requiring the State to prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. *Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 24.

## II. THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION REGARDING BREATHALYZER INSPECTION REPORTS REQUIRES REVERSAL

### A. The State's Discovery Obligations

The Defense maintains that the State's failure to disclose the breathalyzer's monthly inspection reports constituted a discovery violation requiring reversal. The State's argument that no duty to disclose existed under § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) misreads the applicable law and ignores fundamental principles of due process.

The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution guarantees that no state shall "deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." This protection extends to criminal defendants and requires that the State disclose exculpatory evidence. *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963). Additionally, Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220 imposes affirmative discovery obligations on the State that are not limited to situations where the defendant first challenges the evidence.

### B. The State's Interpretation Is Contrary to Fundamental Fairness

The State argues that no disclosure was required because the defendant did not challenge the breathalyzer's reliability. This argument turns due process on its head. A defendant cannot challenge what the defendant does not know exists. By failing to disclose the inspection reports, the State deprived the defendant of the opportunity to make an informed decision about whether to challenge the equipment's reliability.

The State's interpretation would allow the State to control entirely the universe of issues litigated at trial by controlling what information the defendant receives. Such a result is incompatible with the adversarial system and due process.

### C. The Inspection Reports Were Material to the Defense

The breathalyzer reading of .12 formed the centerpiece of the State's case. The reliability of this reading depended on the proper functioning and calibration of the testing equipment. The monthly inspection reports would have revealed whether the breathalyzer was properly maintained and calibrated at the time of the defendant's test.

This evidence was thus material to the defense. The State's failure to disclose it deprived the defendant of the ability to challenge the most critical piece of evidence against him. Under *Brady*, this failure alone would require reversal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error as to the third point. Based on the provided facts and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court affirms the conviction in part and remands for further proceedings.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Officer’s Comment on the Defendant’s Silence Was Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial after Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s decision to remain silent. The Defense argues that this comment was "calculated to prejudice" the Defendant and constitutes reversible error per se under *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965). While the Court agrees with the Defense that the officer’s conduct was improper and that the Fifth Amendment guarantees the right to silence, *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), we must determine whether the error was harmless.

The State correctly argues that constitutional errors are subject to harmless error analysis. Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), a constitutional error requires reversal only if the record does not demonstrate beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The State further relies on *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), which holds that improper comments on a defendant's silence do not require reversal where other evidence of guilt is overwhelming.

The record establishes that the Defendant’s blood alcohol concentration was .12, which exceeds the statutory threshold of .10 for prima facie evidence of impairment under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). Additionally, the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests. The Defense argues that the refusal to perform tests is not independent evidence of guilt because it is intertwined with the Fifth Amendment violation. However, the .12 breathalyzer reading is objective, scientific evidence of intoxication that is entirely independent of the officer’s improper comment regarding the Defendant's verbal silence.

The Defense asserts that the comment was "calculated to prejudice" the Defendant, and the record supports this characterization. However, the presence of overwhelming physical evidence of guilt, specifically the .12 BAC reading, satisfies the State’s burden under *Chapman* to show that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. As held in *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), where the State’s evidence of guilt is overwhelming, an improper comment on silence does not warrant reversal. Therefore, the trial court’s denial of the motion for mistrial is affirmed.

**B. The State Did Not Commit a Discovery Violation Regarding the Breathalyzer Inspection Reports**

The Appellant argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Defense contends that this failure violated due process under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), arguing that a defendant cannot challenge the reliability of equipment without first seeing the maintenance records.

The State argues, and the facts confirm, that the duty to produce these documents is governed by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules. The provided facts state that these rules "do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facia case." Rather, the rules serve as a predicate for admissibility "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment."

The Defense’s argument relies on the premise that the State’s interpretation of the rules violates fundamental fairness. However, based on the facts provided, the administrative rules explicitly condition the duty to disclose documentation on a challenge by the Defendant. The record indicates that the Defendant did not challenge the condition of the equipment. Because the statutory framework provided places the burden on the defendant to trigger the duty to produce these specific documents by challenging the equipment's reliability, and because the Defense has not provided facts indicating the reports were exculpatory or that the equipment was faulty, the State’s failure to produce them absent a challenge does not constitute a discovery violation. The State complied with the requirements for establishing a prima facie case under the relevant statutes and rules.

**C. The Imposition of Court Costs on an Indigent Defendant**

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. The Defense agrees. Accordingly, this point is remanded to the trial court to vacate the order taxing costs and for appropriate proceedings consistent with due process.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the judgment of conviction regarding the admission of testimony and the discovery of breathalyzer reports. The Court finds that the officer's improper comment was harmless error in light of the overwhelming evidence of the Defendant's intoxication, specifically the .12 blood alcohol concentration. Furthermore, the State did not violate discovery rules where the Defendant failed to challenge the equipment's reliability as required by the governing administrative rules. The case is remanded solely to vacate the order taxing costs against the Defendant.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

As this Court noted in its preliminary ruling, Officer Shugrue's response was "calculated to prejudice the Defendant in the eyes of the jury." This characterization is significant. The officer's comment was not an inadvertent slip; it was a deliberate reference to the defendant's exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. ### B. The Prosecution Cannot Prove the Comment Did Not Contribute to the Verdict

The prosecution's argument rests on the faulty premise that because .12 BAC is strong evidence of intoxication, the officer's comment necessarily became irrelevant. This logic fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates the sufficiency of evidence with the harmlessness of an error. The question under *Chapman* is not whether enough evidence existed to convict, but whether the improper comment played any role in the jury's determination. The prosecution has presented no analysis of how the jury actually used the evidence—it merely asserts that the BAC reading alone was sufficient.

Second, the prosecution improperly minimizes the prejudicial nature of the comment. When an officer testifies that a defendant remained silent, the jury may infer guilt from that silence—a precisely forbidden inference under *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965). The prosecution emphasizes that the .12 BAC reading is "objective, scientific evidence" entirely separate from the officer's comment. While this characterization has surface appeal, it ignores the practical realities of jury deliberation.

The prosecution cannot establish that the jury's verdict was untainted by the officer's comment. Juries do not compartmentalize evidence in isolation; they consider the totality of what they hear. Even if the BAC evidence was technically independent, the officer's comment may have influenced how the jury evaluated that evidence, or may have tipped the scales when the jury considered the defendant's credibility or character.

Furthermore, the prosecution's reliance on *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), is misplaced. *Muniz* addressed whether field sobriety tests constitute testimonial communication. The instant case involves a different question—whether an officer's comment on a defendant's silence, combined with the defendant's failure to perform roadside tests, created a compounded inference of guilt. This Court found that Officer Shugrue's response was "calculated to prejudice" the defendant. This finding alone should give this Court pause before applying the harmless error doctrine. When improper conduct is intentional rather than inadvertent, courts should be more reluctant to find harmlessness, as the party committing the error has demonstrated a willingness to circumvent constitutional protections.

The prosecution offers no authority for the proposition that intentional constitutional violations should be treated the same as inadvertent ones for harmless error purposes. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error as to the third point. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court affirms the conviction in part and remands for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Officer’s Comment on the Defendant’s Silence Was Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The Appellant reiterates that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial following Officer Shugrue’s comment on the Defendant’s silence. The Defense argues that because the Court previously characterized the officer’s response as "calculated to prejudice" the Defendant, the error cannot be considered harmless. The Defense contends that the prosecution has failed to meet its burden under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), to prove that the error did not contribute to the verdict, arguing that juries do not compartmentalize evidence and that the comment likely tainted the jury's evaluation of the scientific evidence.

The Prosecution counters that the .12 blood alcohol concentration (BAC) reading constitutes overwhelming, objective evidence of guilt that is entirely independent of the officer’s improper comment. The Prosecution relies on *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), for the proposition that improper comments on a defendant's silence do not require reversal where other evidence of guilt is overwhelming. Furthermore, the Prosecution cites *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), to distinguish the Defendant’s refusal to perform roadside sobriety tests—a non-testimonial act—from the protected act of remaining silent.

The Court agrees with the Prosecution that the State has met its burden of proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. While the Court maintains its concern regarding the officer’s conduct, the record reflects that the Defendant’s BAC was .12, which exceeds the statutory threshold of .10 for prima facie evidence of impairment under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). This is objective, scientific evidence of intoxication. As held in *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), the presence of overwhelming physical evidence of guilt can render a constitutional error harmless. The Defense’s argument that the jury may have used the comment to "bolster" the case is speculative in light of the strength of the .12 BAC reading. The refusal to perform field sobriety tests, as noted in *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), is admissible non-testimonial evidence that further supports the conviction independent of the officer's comment on the Defendant's verbal silence. Therefore, the trial court’s denial of the motion for mistrial is affirmed.

**B. The State Did Not Commit a Discovery Violation Regarding the Breathalyzer Inspection Reports**

The Appellant argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Defense characterizes the State’s position as creating a "Catch-22," where a defendant cannot challenge the equipment's reliability without first seeing the reports, yet cannot obtain the reports without challenging the equipment. The Defense asserts that this procedural hurdle violates the due process requirements of *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), which imposes a constitutional duty to disclose material evidence regardless of administrative rules.

The Prosecution responds that the duty to produce these documents is governed by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules, which explicitly conditions the duty to disclose on a challenge by the Defendant regarding the equipment's condition.

The Court finds that the State did not commit a discovery violation. The administrative rules cited in the record establish that the State is not required to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing to establish a prima facie case. Rather, the documentation serves as a predicate for admissibility only "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment." The record indicates that the Defendant did not challenge the condition of the equipment. While the Defense raises a valid theoretical concern regarding access to evidence, a *Brady* violation requires a showing that the suppressed evidence is material and favorable to the defense. *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963). The Defense has not provided facts indicating that the inspection reports were exculpatory or that the equipment was faulty. Absent a specific challenge to the equipment's reliability or a showing that the reports contained material evidence, the State’s failure to produce them does not constitute a violation of the administrative rules or constitutional due process.

**C. The Imposition of Court Costs on an Indigent Defendant**

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Accordingly, this point is remanded to the trial court to vacate the order taxing costs and for appropriate proceedings consistent with due process.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the judgment of conviction regarding the admission of testimony and the discovery of breathalyzer reports. The Court finds that the officer's improper comment was harmless error in light of the overwhelming evidence of the Defendant's intoxication, specifically the .12 blood alcohol concentration. Furthermore, the State did not violate discovery rules where the Defendant failed to challenge the equipment's reliability as required by the governing administrative rules. The case is remanded solely to vacate the order taxing costs against the Defendant.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

### CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE PROSECUTION

Your Honor, we acknowledge the impropriety of the arresting officer's comment regarding the Defendant's silence. As the Court itself recognized in its preliminary ruling, that remark was "calculated to prejudice the Defendant." However, the Defense's argument that this error requires reversal fundamentally misapplies the harmless error doctrine and ignores the overwhelming, objective evidence of guilt that renders the constitutional violation harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. We urge this Court to affirm the conviction on this point.

#### I. The Harmless Error Standard Is Met Under Chapman and Van Arsdall

The Defense claims the prosecution misapplied *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), by failing to prove the error "did not contribute to the verdict." This argument is legally unsound. *Chapman* requires the State to demonstrate beyond a reasonable doubt that the error had no "substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury's verdict." *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). The mere existence of other evidence is insufficient; the inquiry focuses on whether the error affected the jury's decision-making process. Yet the Defense ignores the core principle established in *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986): when "overwhelming" and "uncontroverted" physical evidence of guilt exists, a constitutional error may be deemed harmless. In *Van Arsdall*, the Supreme Court held that improper cross-examination about a witness's bias was harmless where the defendant's own testimony and physical evidence overwhelmingly supported conviction. Here, the Defendant's blood alcohol concentration ("BAC") reading of .12 is precisely such evidence. It exceeds Florida's statutory threshold of .10 for prima facie evidence of impairment under § 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985). This is not merely "strong" evidence—it is objective, scientific, and unassailable. As this Court correctly noted in its ruling, the .12 BAC reading is "entirely independent of the officer’s improper comment." The Defense cannot point to a single instance where the jury was actually influenced by the comment to convict, nor can it explain how a .12 BAC—more than double the legal limit—could be "unaffected" by a single improper remark. The Defense’s speculation that juries "do not compartmentalize evidence" is precisely the type of conjecture *Van Arsdall* rejects. When physical evidence of intoxication is this conclusive, the harmless error standard is met.

#### II. Pennsylvania v. Muniz Confirms the Independence of Non-Testimonial Evidence

The Defense mischaracterizes *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), to suggest it is inapplicable here. In *Muniz*, the Supreme Court explicitly held that a defendant’s refusal to perform field sobriety tests constitutes non-testimonial evidence and is admissible without violating the Fifth Amendment. *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582, 593–94 (1990). This distinction is critical. The officer’s comment addressed the Defendant’s *verbal silence*—a protected constitutional act—but the Defendant’s *refusal to perform roadside tests* is a separate, admissible act of non-testimonial conduct. The Defense conflates these distinct concepts to argue that the comment "compounded" the prejudice. The Defense offers no authority for the proposition that a non-testimonial act (refusing field tests) and a testimonial act (silence) must be "compartmentalized" in a way that elevates the comment’s prejudice. *Muniz* confirms that the refusal to perform tests is a permissible evidentiary element entirely separate from the comment on silence. The State did not need to "escape" constitutional violations by pointing to this evidence—it merely demonstrated that the BAC reading and test refusal together form an overwhelming body of proof unaffected by the officer’s error.

Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673, 684 (1986) ("[T]he question is whether there is a reasonable possibility that the evidence complained of might have contributed to the conviction"). Here, there is no "reasonable possibility" that the comment influenced the verdict when the Defendant’s BAC was .12. The Defense cites no case holding that intentional misconduct automatically negates harmless error analysis—especially where, as here, the physical evidence is so overwhelming. In fact, courts routinely apply harmless error even to deliberate constitutional violations when the evidence of guilt is "overwhelming." *See United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 509 (1983) (prosecutorial misconduct deemed harmless despite intentional nature where evidence was "overwhelming"). The Defense’s attempt to elevate the "calculated" nature of the comment into a standalone basis for reversal is legally unsupportable.

#### IV. No Discovery Violation Occurred Under Brady

The Defense’s claim of a discovery violation regarding breathalyzer inspection reports is equally meritless. The State’s duty to disclose these reports is governed by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules, which explicitly conditions disclosure on a defendant challenging the equipment’s reliability. The Defense did not challenge the breathalyzer’s condition at trial. Under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), the State’s duty to disclose material exculpatory evidence arises only when the evidence is both "favorable to the accused" and "material." *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963). The Defense has never identified what, if anything, the inspection reports contained that would have been "favorable" or "material." It has not alleged the equipment was faulty, nor has it submitted any evidence suggesting the reports were exculpatory. As this Court correctly held, the Defense’s "Catch-22" argument is a red herring: a defendant cannot claim prejudice from withheld evidence when they never sought to challenge the equipment’s reliability in the first place. *Brady* does not require the State to proactively disclose evidence the defense has no basis to question. *See United States v. The .12 BAC reading is overwhelming, uncontroverted evidence of guilt that stands entirely apart from the comment. *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986). # CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the Prosecution's closing statement. While we acknowledge the Prosecution's recognition of the officer's improper comment, we maintain that the Prosecution has failed to meet its heavy burden of proving harmless error beyond a reasonable doubt, and that the discovery violation regarding the breathalyzer inspection reports constitutes reversible error. We ask this Court to reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

---

## I. THE PROSECUTION HAS FAILED TO PROVE HARMLESS ERROR BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT

### A. The "Calculated to Prejudice" Finding Demands Heightened Scrutiny

The Prosecution attempts to dismiss the Court's characterization of the officer's comment as "calculated to prejudice" as mere rhetoric without legal consequence. This argument fails. When this Court initially described Officer Shugrue's response as "calculated to prejudice the Defendant in the eyes of the jury," the Court acknowledged a deliberate act of misconduct by a law enforcement officer who "knows or should know better." This finding is not merely descriptive—it establishes that the officer intentionally violated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment rights.

The Prosecution cites *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499 (1983), for the proposition that intentional constitutional violations can be deemed harmless. However, *Hasting* is distinguishable. In *Hasting*, the prosecutorial misconduct involved comments on the defendant's post-arrest silence, but the Court emphasized that the evidence of guilt was "overwhelming" and the misconduct was "not part of the prosecution's case." Here, by contrast, Officer Shugrue was a State's witness whose testimony was central to establishing the DUI charge. The officer's deliberate intrusion into the Defendant's constitutional rights strikes at the very heart of the adversarial system.

### B. The Prosecution Has Not Met the Chapman Burden

The Prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the officer's comment "did not contribute to the verdict." *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). The Prosecution argues that the .12 BAC reading is "overwhelming" evidence that renders the error harmless. However, this Court must consider the cumulative effect of all the evidence, not simply the existence of some strong evidence.

The Prosecution's reliance on *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), is misplaced. In *Van Arsdall*, the improper cross-examination concerned a witness's bias—a matter collateral to the central issue of guilt. Here, by contrast, the officer's comment directly implicated the Defendant's exercise of a fundamental constitutional right. The Fifth Amendment protects a defendant's right to remain silent precisely because silence is not evidence of guilt. When an officer explicitly links the defendant's silence to guilt—as Officer Shugrue did here—the jury is invited to draw an improper inference that poisons the entire proceeding.

### C. The Defense Is Not Required to Prove Actual Influence

The Prosecution erroneously argues that the Defense must point to "a single instance where the jury was actually influenced by the comment." This misstates the harmless error inquiry. Under *Chapman*, the question is whether there is a "reasonable possibility" that the error contributed to the conviction. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). The Prosecution cannot prove a negative—that the jury was NOT influenced—because the jury's deliberations are inherently不可见 (inaccessible).

The Prosecution's argument that juries "compartmentalize evidence" is not only speculative but contradicts human psychology. Juries evaluate all evidence in context. When a police officer—the state's most credible witness—suggests that the defendant's silence indicates guilt, that suggestion permeates the jury's evaluation of all evidence, including the BAC reading.

### D. Pennsylvania v. Muniz Does Not Support the Prosecution's Position

The Prosecution invokes *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), to argue that the Defendant's refusal to perform field sobriety tests is admissible non-testimonial evidence. The Defense agrees that *Muniz* so holds. However, the Prosecution conflates two distinct issues: (1) the admissibility of the defendant's refusal to perform tests, and (2) the propriety of the officer's comment on the defendant's verbal silence.

*Muniz* explicitly preserved the distinction between testimonial and non-testimonial evidence. The officer's comment here addressed the defendant's *verbal* silence—a protected testimonial act under the Fifth Amendment. The Prosecution cannot bootstrap the admissibility of the defendant's non-testimonial conduct (refusal to perform tests) to immunize its violation of the defendant's constitutional rights regarding testimonial silence.

---

## II. A DISCOVERY VIOLATION OCCURRED REGARDING THE BREATHALYZER INSPECTION REPORTS

### A. Brady v. Maryland Requires Disclosure of Material Evidence

The Prosecution argues that no discovery violation occurred because the Defendant did not first challenge the equipment's reliability. This argument creates an unconscionable Catch-22 that violates due process.

Under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), the State has a constitutional duty to disclose evidence that is "favorable to the accused" and "material" to either guilt or punishment. This duty exists independent of any request by the defense or any challenge to the evidence. The Prosecution cannot bootstrap its own failure to disclose by arguing that the Defendant should have challenged the equipment—how could the Defendant meaningfully challenge the equipment's reliability without first examining the inspection reports?

The Prosecution cites *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985), for the proposition that materiality requires a "reasonable probability" that disclosure would have changed the outcome. However, *Bagley* presupposes that the defense has access to the evidence in question. Here, the Defendant was denied access to the very evidence needed to determine whether a challenge was warranted.

### B. The Administrative Rules Cannot Override Constitutional Rights

The Prosecution argues that § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules governs the disclosure duty. Even assumingarguendo that the administrative rules create a procedural predicate for disclosure, those rules cannot override the Defendant's constitutional right to due process under *Brady*.

The purpose of the discovery rules is to ensure fair trials, not to create procedural traps that deny defendants access to potentially exculpatory evidence. If the inspection reports revealed that the breathalyzer was malfunctioning or improperly calibrated, the Defendant would have had a *Brady* claim. By denying the Defendant access to these reports, the State deprived the Defendant of the ability to make an informed decision about whether to challenge the equipment's reliability.

### C. The Prosecution's Argument Proves Too Much

The Prosecution argues that the Defendant "never sought to challenge the equipment's reliability in the first place." This argument highlights the very problem the Defense identifies. A defendant cannot challenge what the defendant cannot examine. The Prosecution's position would allow the State to shield the breathalyzer's maintenance records from scrutiny indefinitely, precisely because the defendant cannot challenge the equipment without first seeing those records.

This Court should reject the Prosecution's attempt to use procedural technicalities to circumvent constitutional guarantees. The Defendant was entitled to examine the breathalyzer inspection reports to determine whether a challenge was warranted. The State's failure to disclose these reports constitutes a discovery violation.

## III. THE IMPOSITION OF COURT COSTS

The Defense notes that the State has conceded error on this point. The Defense requests that this Court ensure meaningful remand proceedings consistent with due process, including proper notice and an opportunity to be heard before any court costs are imposed.

1. **Reverse** the conviction and remand for a new trial because the Prosecution has failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the officer's comment on the Defendant's Fifth Amendment right to remain silent did not contribute to the verdict;

2. **Reverse** the conviction and remand for a new trial because the State's failure to disclose the breathalyzer inspection reports violated *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), and the Defendant's right to due process; and

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error as to the third point. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court affirms the conviction in part and remands for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Officer’s Comment on the Defendant’s Silence Was Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial following Officer Shugrue’s comment on the Defendant’s silence. The Defense contends that because the Court previously characterized the officer’s response as "calculated to prejudice" the Defendant, the error requires heightened scrutiny and cannot be deemed harmless. The Defense further argues that the Prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499 (1983), is misplaced because the misconduct here was central to the State's case, whereas in *Hasting* it was collateral.

The Court acknowledges the gravity of the officer’s misconduct. As previously noted, it is improper for a law enforcement officer to comment on a defendant’s silence, and we agree with the Defense that such conduct strikes at the heart of the adversarial system. However, the question remains whether this constitutional error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the State bears the burden of demonstrating that the error did not contribute to the verdict.

The Prosecution correctly argues that the record contains overwhelming, uncontroverted evidence of guilt independent of the officer’s comment. The Defendant’s blood alcohol concentration (BAC) was .12, which exceeds the statutory threshold of .10 for prima facie evidence of impairment under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). This objective, scientific evidence is bolstered by the Defendant’s refusal to perform roadside sobriety tests. As held in *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990), the refusal to perform such tests is non-testimonial evidence that is admissible and distinct from the protected act of remaining silent.

While the Defense argues that the "calculated" nature of the comment distinguishes this case from *Hasting*, the Supreme Court in *Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673 (1986), established that the presence of overwhelming physical evidence can render even constitutional errors harmless. The Defense has not shown how the officer’s comment could have overcome the probative value of the .12 BAC reading and the Defendant's failure to perform field sobriety tests. The jury’s verdict was surely based on this physical evidence rather than the improper comment. Therefore, the trial court’s denial of the motion for mistrial is affirmed.

**B. The State Did Not Commit a Discovery Violation Regarding the Breathalyzer Inspection Reports**

The Appellant argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports, creating a "Catch-22" where the Defendant could not challenge the equipment's reliability without first seeing the reports. The Defense asserts that this violates the due process requirements of *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), regardless of the administrative rules.

The Court finds that the State did not commit a discovery violation. The duty to produce these documents is governed by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules. As noted in the record, these rules do not require the State to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing to establish a prima facie case. Rather, the documentation serves as a predicate for admissibility only "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment."

The Defense argues that *Brady* imposes a duty to disclose material evidence independent of these rules. However, to establish a *Brady* violation, the defendant must show that the suppressed evidence is both favorable to the accused and material to guilt or punishment. *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963). Materiality is defined by *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985), as a "reasonable probability" that the result would have been different. Here, the Defendant did not challenge the condition of the equipment, nor has the Defense provided any facts indicating that the inspection reports contained exculpatory information or that the equipment was faulty. Speculation that the reports *might* have contained favorable evidence is insufficient to establish a violation. Because the State met its burden of establishing a prima facie case through the .12 BAC reading, and the Defendant failed to trigger the specific administrative mechanism for challenging the machine's reliability, there was no discovery violation.

**C. The Imposition of Court Costs on an Indigent Defendant**

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Accordingly, this point is remanded to the trial court to vacate the order taxing costs and for appropriate proceedings consistent with due process.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the judgment of conviction regarding the admission of testimony and the discovery of breathalyzer reports. The Court finds that the officer's improper comment was harmless error in light of the overwhelming evidence of the Defendant's intoxication, specifically the .12 blood alcohol concentration and the refusal to perform roadside tests. Furthermore, the State did not violate discovery rules where the Defendant failed to challenge the equipment's reliability as required by the governing administrative rules, and no showing of materiality was made under *Brady*. The case is remanded solely to vacate the order taxing costs against the Defendant.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
