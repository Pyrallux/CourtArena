Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute to preserve Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. Under Illinois law, dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish pre-dissolution liabilities. As this Court held in *Kraemer v. Estate of Gannon*, 132 Ill. 2d 202, 206-07 (1989), the purpose of the Survival Statute is to prevent corporations from escaping liability for their pre-dissolution acts or omissions merely because they have been dissolved. Here, PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 through a valid Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement, thereby incurring a binding liability prior to dissolution. Although the breach of that obligation—nonpayment—occurred after dissolution, the Survival Statute preserves the claim because the underlying liability existed at the time of dissolution. This principle was unequivocally reaffirmed in *Illinois Bank & Trust Co. v. Wackerman*, 161 Ill. App. 3d 305, 311 (1st Dist. 1987), where the court held that a claim based on a pre-dissolution obligation is not barred by dissolution even though the breach occurs after dissolution. Similarly, in *Simpson v. Midwest Steel & Iron Co.*, 352 Ill. App. 3d 889, 895 (1st Dist. 2004), this Court confirmed that the Survival Statute preserves claims for liabilities incurred prior to dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs. The trial court’s ruling was legally sound and consistent with settled Illinois precedent. There was no error in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved Plaintiff’s claim.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The plaintiff恳求 this Court to affirm the trial court's ruling that the Illinois Survival Statute preserves a breach of contract claim against PBS One, a dissolved corporation. However, the plaintiff's argument misapprehends the nature of the obligations assumed and the effect of subsequent transactions on PBS One's liability. Additionally, the plaintiff fails to address critical factual and legal distinctions that require reversal as to Counts IX, X, and XI. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the plaintiff and enter judgment for the defendants, or alternatively, remand for further proceedings.

## I. The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Liability Against PBS One Because PBS One's Obligations Were Validly Transferred and Assumed by Successor Entities

The plaintiff invokes the Survival Statute to preserve liability against PBS One, arguing that because PBS One "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement obligations in 1988, the underlying liability existed at the time of dissolution and survives. This argument fails because it ignores the subsequent valid transfer of those obligations and the principle of novation.

### A. The Subsequent Assignment to National Material Effected a Novation Releasing PBS One

Even assuming arguendo that PBS One assumed liability under the Consulting Agreement in 1988, that liability was subsequently transferred through the December 31, 1990 Assumption Agreement. Under Illinois law, when a creditor consents to the assignment of a debt to a new party and accepts performance from that new party, a novation occurs, releasing the original debtor. *Kramer v. Christie Music Co.*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 81, 86 (1st Dist. 1993) (novation occurs when there is (1) a valid existing obligation, (2) agreement by parties to a new obligation, (3) intent to extinguish the old obligation, and (4) actual performance by the new debtor).

The undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One assigned its entire partnership interest—including all associated rights and obligations—to National Material in 1990. The Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material "accept[s] and assume[s] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations." Following this transfer, PBS One ceased to have any interest in Pielet LP and consequently any obligation to make payments under the Consulting Agreement. Critically, Arthur continued to receive payments from Pielet LP (later renamed Midwest Metallics) for nearly eight years after the 1990 transfer, never objecting or demanding payment from PBS One.

This Court should find that Arthur's acceptance of payments from the successor entities, combined with the explicit assumption by National Material, constitutes a novation releasing PBS One from its obligations. *See Bank of Waukegan v. S.I. International, Ltd.*, 271 Ill. App. 3d 86, 92 (1st Dist. 1995) (creditor's acceptance of payment from new party evidences consent to novation).

### B. The Plaintiff Misreads the Survival Statute

The plaintiff cites *Kraemer v. Estate of Gannon*, 132 Ill. 2d 202 (1989), and *Illinois Bank & Trust Co. v. Wackerman*, 161 Ill. App. 3d 305 (1st Dist. 1987), for the proposition that the Survival Statute preserves claims based on pre-dissolution obligations. However, these cases are distinguishable.

In *Kraemer*, the Illinois Supreme Court held that the Survival Statute applies to "liabilities incurred prior to dissolution" but did not address situations where those liabilities have been validly assigned to and assumed by another entity prior to dissolution. 132 Ill. 2d at 207. Similarly, in *Wackerman*, the court addressed a situation where the corporate debtor remained liable post-dissolution. 161 Ill. App. 3d at 311.

Here, PBS One's alleged liability was not a static obligation that remained with the corporation until dissolution. Rather, PBS One actively transferred its partnership interest and associated obligations in 1990—four years before dissolution. By the time of PBS One's dissolution in 1994, PBS One had no remaining interest in the consulting arrangement and no obligation to Arthur. The Survival Statute was never intended to revive liability for obligations that have been validly transferred and extinguished through subsequent transactions. *See W. Coal & Mining Co. v. Industrial Commission*, 296 Ill. 527, 532 (1921) (survival statutes are remedial and should not be interpreted to create liability where none exists).

## II. National Material Is Not Liable Under Counts IX and X

### A. National Material Did Not Expressly Assume the Consulting Agreement

Count IX alleges that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement, as part of the January 1991 transfer. This claim misreads the Assumption Agreement.

The Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations...in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." The Agreement concerns the transfer of a limited partnership interest, not the direct assumption of specific contractual obligations. A limited partnership interest is a property interest that carries with it certain economic rights and obligations, but it does not constitute an express assumption of every contract to which the partnership may be subject. *See First National Bank of Chicago v. M.F. Glickman Co.*, 56 Ill. 2d 553, 558 (1974) (assumption of obligations must be clear and unambiguous).

The plaintiff argues that because the partnership interest includes the obligation to make consulting payments, National Material necessarily assumed that obligation. However, the assumption of a partnership interest is distinct from the assumption of specific contractual obligations. National Material became a limited partner entitled to distributions; it did not become a party to the Consulting Agreement. *See 805 ILCS 215/102* (Illinois Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act) (limited partner not liable for partnership obligations except to the extent of capital contributions).

### B. National Material Is Not a Successor to PBS One

Count X alleges that National Material is liable as the "successor" of PBS One. Under Illinois law, successor liability generally requires either (1) an explicit assumption of the predecessor's liabilities, (2) a merger where the successor corporation retains the predecessor's assets, or (3) a continuation of the predecessor's business. *See Logan v. K. Duhaime & Co.*, 299 Ill. App. 3d 396, 401 (1st Dist. 1999).

None of these elements are present here. First, as explained above, the Assumption Agreement did not explicitly assume the Consulting Agreement. Second, there was no merger—PBS One transferred its partnership interest but continued to exist until its dissolution in 1994. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: The Survival Statute Does Not Preclude a Finding of Novation**

The plaintiff argues that the trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute to preserve the claim against PBS One because the liability was incurred prior to dissolution. The plaintiff relies on *Kraemer v. Estate of Gannon*, 132 Ill. 2d 202, 206-07 (1989), and *Illinois Bank & Trust Co. v. Wackerman*, 161 Ill. For the foregoing reasons, the defense’s arguments are sustained. The trial court erred in granting summary judgment for the plaintiff. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether a novation occurred releasing PBS One (Count XI), and the plaintiff failed to establish a legal basis for liability against National Material under theories of express assumption (Count IX) or successor liability (Count X).

However, the plaintiff conflates the intent of the parties to the assumption agreement with the intent of the creditor (Arthur). Novation requires the creditor's consent to substitute a new debtor, which may be inferred from conduct. *Bank of Waukegan v. S.I. International, Ltd.*, 271 Ill. App. 3d 86, 92 (1st Dist. 1995).

Arthur's acceptance of payments from Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics—without objection—for nearly eight years creates a factual dispute regarding whether he consented to the substitution. While Zavis testified that he did not intend to obtain a release, he also testified that Arthur was informed the limited partnership "would be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]" and that Arthur "agreed thereto expressing his desire to have the transaction concluded as soon as possible since the new entity would be financially stronger than [Pielet Corp.]."

The question of whether Arthur's conduct evidences intent to release PBS One is a question of fact for the trier of fact. *Anderson v. Village of Libertyville*, 248 Ill. App. 3d 1030, 1037 (1st Dist. 1993). The trial court erred in granting summary judgment because reasonable minds could differ on whether novation occurred.

### C. The December 1990 Assumption Agreement Effected a Novation

The plaintiff argues that the December 1990 Assumption Agreement did not release PBS One because it "explicitly preserves PBS One's role." This argument ignores the plain language of the agreement. The agreement states that National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations... in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest."

This language is ambiguous as to whether National Material assumed PBS One's contractual obligations or merely acquired PBS One's financial interest in the partnership. However, the plaintiff's interpretation—"that National Material's assumption of a partnership interest extinguished PBS One's liability"—actually supports the defense's position. If National Material assumed "obligations" alongside the partnership interest, and Arthur accepted payments from the partnership entities for eight years, a factfinder could conclude that novation occurred.

The plaintiff cannot have it both ways: either National Material assumed the obligations (supporting novation) or it did not (defeating express assumption under Count IX). The ambiguity in the agreement creates a genuine issue of material fact precluding summary judgment.

---

## III. NATIONAL MATERIAL DID NOT EXPRESSLY ASSUME THE CONSULTING AGREEMENT (COUNT IX)

The plaintiff has not established, as a matter of law, that National Material expressly assumed the Consulting Agreement. The December 1990 Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations... in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest."

Under Illinois law, "the assumption of obligations must be clear and unambiguous." *First National Bank of Chicago v. M.F. Glickman Co.*, 56 Ill. 2d 553, 558 (1974). The 1990 agreement does not mention the Consulting Agreement by name, nor does it explicitly reference Arthur's contract. The language refers to obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest—a limitation that suggests the assumption of partnership-related obligations rather than specific contractual liabilities.

The plaintiff argues that PBS One "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement in 1988, and National Material "assumed PBS One's obligations" in 1990. However, the 1990 Assumption Agreement explicitly limited the assumption to "obligations... in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest." The partnership interest is a property interest; the Consulting Agreement is a separate contractual obligation. The assumption of one does not automatically imply assumption of the other. *First National Bank of Chicago*, 56 Ill. 2d at 558.

The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that the assumption of a partnership interest automatically includes all contracts to which the partnership or its partners are parties. To the contrary, the Illinois Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act provides that "the assumption of a partnership interest does not automatically assume all contractual obligations of the partnership." 805 ILCS 215/102. The trial court correctly found that the plaintiff failed to establish express assumption as a matter of law.

---

## IV. NATIONAL MATERIAL IS NOT LIABLE AS A SUCCESSOR (COUNT X)

### A. No Express Agreement of Assumption

As discussed above, the 1990 Assumption Agreement does not clearly and unambiguously assume the Consulting Agreement. Without an express assumption, the plaintiff cannot establish successor liability on this basis. *Logan v. K. Duhaime & Co.*, 299 Ill. App. 3d 396, 401 (1st Dist. 1999).

### B. No Merger or Consolidation

The plaintiff does not argue—and cannot establish—that a merger or consolidation occurred. PBS One continued to exist until its dissolution in 1994. The 1990 transaction was an assignment of a partnership interest, not a merger.

### C. No Continuity of Business Operations

The plaintiff cannot establish the third element of successor liability: continuity of business operations. Under Illinois law, successor liability requires "continuity of the same business operations." *Ng Bros. v. Trieu*, 287 Ill. App. 3d 416, 425 (1st Dist. 1997). PBS One was "merely an investor holding a limited partnership interest; it did not operate the scrap metal business." The undisputed facts establish that PBS One "has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment."

National Material acquired PBS One's financial interest in the partnership but did not continue PBS One's business operations because PBS One had no business operations to continue. *Ng Bros.*, 287 Ill. App. 3d at 425 ("successor liability requires continuity of business operations, not mere transfer of assets"). The plaintiff has provided no evidence to the contrary.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Novation**

The plaintiff argues that the Illinois Survival Statute (735 ILCS 5/2-1009) preserves the claim against PBS One because the liability was incurred prior to dissolution. The plaintiff relies on *Kraemer v. Estate of Gannon*, 132 Ill. 2d 202, 206-07 (1989), and *Illinois Bank & Trust Co. v. Wackerman*, 161 Ill. App. 3d 305, 311 (1st Dist. 1987), for the proposition that a claim survives dissolution regardless of when the breach occurs. The defense correctly counters that the Survival Statute preserves only those liabilities that exist at the time of dissolution; it does not revive liabilities that were validly extinguished prior to dissolution. *Kraemer*, 132 Ill. 2d at 207.

The central dispute is whether a novation occurred prior to PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. Under Illinois law, a novation requires (1) a valid existing obligation, (2) an agreement of the parties to a new contract, (3) the extinguishment of the old obligation, and (4) actual performance by the new debtor. *Kramer v. Christie Music Co.*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 81, 86 (1st Dist. 1993). The plaintiff relies heavily on the testimony of Attorney Michael Zavis to argue that Arthur never intended to release PBS One. Zavis testified that in 1988, he informed Arthur that the limited partnership would be making payments and that Arthur "never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor."

However, the defense persuasively argues that intent to release a debtor may be inferred from conduct, not just the subjective intent at the time of the initial restructuring. *Bank of Waukegan v. S.I. International, Ltd.*, 271 Ill. App. 3d 86, 92 (1st Dist. 1995). The undisputed facts show that following the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement—wherein National Material assumed PBS One’s "right, title, interest[,] and obligations"—Arthur accepted payments from the partnership entities (Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics) for nearly eight years without demanding payment from PBS One. Village of Libertyville*, 248 Ill. App. 3d 1030, 1037 (1st Dist. 1993).

The plaintiff’s reliance on Zavis’s 1988 testimony is insufficient to resolve this factual dispute. The relevant inquiry for a potential novation in 1990 or 1994 is Arthur’s intent and conduct during the years he accepted payments from the new entity. Because reasonable minds could differ on whether Arthur’s conduct evidenced an intent to discharge PBS One, the trial court erred in granting summary judgment on Count XI.

**B. Count IX: National Material Did Not Expressly Assume the Consulting Agreement**

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable because it assumed PBS One’s obligations. The defense correctly argues that the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement does not contain the clear and unambiguous language required to establish an express assumption of the Consulting Agreement.

This language is limited to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. It does not explicitly name the Consulting Agreement, nor does it clearly state that National Material assumed the specific contractual liabilities of PBS One as a general corporate entity. Under Illinois law, "the assumption of obligations must be clear and unambiguous." *First National Bank of Chicago v. M.F. Glickman Co.*, 56 Ill. 2d 553, 558 (1974).

The plaintiff’s argument that the 1988 Asset Purchase Agreement listed the Consulting Agreement as a material contract does not cure the deficiency in the 1990 agreement. The 1990 agreement was a transfer of a partnership interest, not a purchase of assets. The assumption of a partnership interest does not automatically imply the assumption of all contracts to which the transferring partner was a party. The defense is correct that the language "obligations... in and to the extent of" the partnership interest is ambiguous and susceptible to the interpretation that National Material only assumed obligations related to the ownership of the interest, not the underlying debt. Because the plaintiff failed to establish that National Material’s assumption was "clear and unambiguous" regarding the Consulting Agreement, summary judgment on Count IX was improper.

**C. Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor**

The plaintiff failed to provide a sufficient rebuttal to the defense’s argument regarding successor liability. Under Illinois law, successor liability generally requires: (1) an express agreement of assumption, (2) a merger or consolidation, or (3) a continuation of the business where the successor retains the same assets and management. *Logan v. K. Duhaime & Co.*, 299 Ill. App. 3d 396, 401 (1st Dist. 1999).

As established in the analysis of Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Furthermore, there is no evidence of a merger or consolidation; PBS One continued to exist until its voluntary dissolution in 1994. Because National Material acquired only a financial interest and did not continue PBS One’s business operations, the plaintiff cannot establish successor liability as a matter of law.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense’s arguments are sustained. The trial court erred in granting summary judgment for the plaintiff. The dispute centers on whether the parties agreed to extinguish the old obligation and substitute a new debtor.

The plaintiff argues that Attorney Zavis's testimony establishes that Arthur never intended to release PBS One. While this testimony is relevant, the plaintiff mischaracterizes its effect. Zavis testified regarding the 1988 restructuring meeting, not regarding the 1990 Assumption Agreement when National Material entered the picture. The critical period for determining whether a novation occurred is 1990, when PBS One assigned its partnership interest to National Material and Arthur began accepting payments from the partnership entities.

### B. Arthur's Conduct Creates a Genuine Issue of Material Fact

The undisputed facts establish that following the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement, Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics for nearly eight years—from 1991 until July 1998—without ever demanding payment from PBS One. This extended period of accepting payments from the new entity, while PBS One remained nominally obligated, creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether Arthur consented to the substitution of debtors.

The defense acknowledges that under *Bank of Waukegan v. S.I. International, Ltd.*, 271 Ill. App. 3d 86, 92 (1st Dist. 1995), conduct alone cannot establish novation without clear evidence of the creditor's intent to release the original debtor. However, the appellate court correctly found that the totality of the circumstances—including the 1990 Assumption Agreement, the subsequent course of dealing, and the absence of any demand on PBS One—presents a question for the trier of fact. As the court stated in *Anderson v. Village of Libertyville*, 248 Ill. App. 3d 1030, 1037 (1st Dist. 1993), intent to release a debtor may be inferred from conduct when reasonable minds could differ.

The plaintiff argues that Zavis's testimony is "dispositive." This overstates the record. Zavis testified about the 1988 meeting, not about Arthur's intent in 1990 or thereafter. Moreover, Zavis acknowledged that he did not intend to obtain a release from Arthur, but he also testified that Arthur was informed the new entity would be making payments "in lieu of" the original corporation. The defense is not required to prove novation at the summary judgment stage; it need only raise a genuine issue of material fact. The appellate court correctly found that it did.

### C. The Survival Statute Does Not Preclude the Novation Defense

The plaintiff relies on *Kraemer v. Estate of Gannon*, 132 Ill. 2d 202 (1989), and *Illinois Bank & Trust Co. v. Wackerman*, 161 Ill. App. 3d 305 (1st Dist. 1987), for the proposition that the Survival Statute preserves claims regardless of when the breach occurs. This reliance is misplaced. As the appellate court correctly noted, the Survival Statute "does not revive liabilities that were validly extinguished prior to dissolution." *Kraemer*, 132 Ill. 2d at 207. If a novation occurred prior to PBS One's dissolution in 1994, the liability was extinguished, and the Survival Statute does not revive it.

The plaintiff argues that the defense cannot prove novation because Arthur never explicitly agreed to release PBS One. However, the defense has never conceded that Arthur's consent was absent; rather, the defense argues that the evidence regarding Arthur's intent is disputed. The appellate court correctly found that this factual dispute precludes summary judgment.

---

## II. Count IX: National Material Did Not Expressly Assume the Consulting Agreement

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable because it assumed PBS One's obligations under the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement. This argument fails because the Assumption Agreement does not contain the clear and unambiguous language required to establish an express assumption of the Consulting Agreement.

### A. The Language of the 1990 Assumption Agreement Is Ambiguous

The 1990 Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations... in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." This language is limited to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. It does not explicitly name the Consulting Agreement, nor does it clearly state that National Material assumed the specific contractual liabilities of PBS One as a general corporate entity.

Under Illinois law, "the assumption of obligations must be clear and unambiguous." *First National Bank of Chicago v. M.F. Glickman Co.*, 56 Ill. 2d 553, 558 (1974). The plaintiff has not pointed to any language in the 1990 agreement that unambiguously assumes the Consulting Agreement. The agreement speaks of obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest—a limitation that the defense reasonably interprets as referring only to obligations related to that interest, not to all of PBS One's contractual obligations.

### B. The 1988 Asset Purchase Agreement Does Not Cure the Deficiency

The plaintiff argues that because the 1988 Asset Purchase Agreement listed the Consulting Agreement as a material contract, National Material should be bound by that assumption. This argument conflates two distinct transactions. The 1988 Asset Purchase Agreement was between Pielet Corp. and PBS One; it was not a party to the 1990 transaction. The 1990 Assumption Agreement was a transfer of a partnership interest, not a purchase of assets.

Under Illinois law, "the assumption of a partnership interest does not automatically imply the assumption of all contracts to which the transferring partner was a party." The plaintiff has not cited, and the defense is not aware of, any authority holding that a partner's assumption of another partner's partnership interest includes assumption of all the partner's individual contractual obligations. The appellate court correctly found that the plaintiff failed to establish express assumption as a matter of law.

### C. The Plaintiff Misapplies the Assumption Doctrine

The plaintiff argues that National Material's assumption of "all obligations" is sufficient to bind it to the Consulting Agreement. This argument fails because the assumption was limited to obligations "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest. If the parties had intended to assume all of PBS One's obligations, they could have used clearer language—as they did in the 1988 Asset Purchase Agreement, which specifically listed the Consulting Agreement.

The defense submits that the 1990 agreement is, at minimum, ambiguous. Under Illinois law, ambiguities must be construed against the party seeking to enforce the assumption. *First National Bank of Chicago*, 56 Ill. 2d at 558. Because the plaintiff failed to establish that National Material's assumption was clear and unambiguous regarding the Consulting Agreement, summary judgment on Count IX was properly denied.

---

## III. Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable as a successor to PBS One. This argument fails because the plaintiff cannot establish the elements of successor liability under Illinois law.

### A. The Elements of Successor Liability Are Not Met

Under Illinois law, successor liability generally requires: (1) an express agreement of assumption, (2) a merger or consolidation, or (3) a continuation of the business where the successor retains the same assets and management. *Logan v. K. Duhaime & Co.*, 299 Ill. App. 3d 396, 401 (1st Dist. 1999).

The plaintiff cannot establish any of these elements. First, as discussed above, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Second, there was no merger or consolidation; PBS One continued to exist until its voluntary dissolution in 1994. Third, there was no continuation of business operations.

### B. PBS One Was Not Operating a Business

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One was "an investment company" that held a limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. Tang testified at his deposition that PBS One "has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment." PBS One did not operate the scrap metal business; it merely held a financial interest in the partnership that did.

In *Ng Bros. v. Trieu*, 287 Ill. App. 3d 416, 425 (1st Dist. 1997), this Court held that successor liability requires continuity of business operations, not mere transfer of assets. Here, there was no continuity of business operations because PBS One was not operating any business. National Material acquired only a financial interest in the partnership; it did not continue PBS One's (nonexistent) business.

### C. The Plaintiff Failed to Rebut the Defense's Argument

The appellate court's ruling correctly notes that the plaintiff failed to provide a sufficient rebuttal to the defense's argument regarding successor liability. The defense demonstrated that PBS One was merely an investor, not an operating company, and that National Material acquired only a partnership interest. The plaintiff did not cite any authority holding that a party can be a successor to a mere investment entity. The appellate court correctly found that the plaintiff cannot establish successor liability as a matter of law.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the appellate court's ruling reversing the trial court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the plaintiff. The defense has raised genuine issues of material fact regarding whether a novation occurred releasing PBS One (Count XI), and the plaintiff has failed to establish a legal basis for liability against National Material under theories of express assumption (Count IX) or successor liability (Count X).

The plaintiff has not demonstrated that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on any of the three counts. The appellate court correctly reversed the trial court's summary judgment ruling, and its decision should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the plaintiff on Counts IX, X, and XI. The plaintiff argues that the Illinois Survival Statute preserves the claim against PBS One and that National Material is liable as a successor or by assumption of the Consulting Agreement. The defense argues that genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether a novation extinguished PBS One’s liability and that National Material did not expressly assume the contract nor qualify as a successor. After reviewing the closing arguments and the undisputed facts, the Court finds that the defense’s arguments are legally sound. The trial court’s grant of summary judgment was improper because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding the intent to release PBS One, and the plaintiff failed to establish, as a matter of law, the liability of National Material.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Novation**

The plaintiff argues that the Illinois Survival Statute (735 ILCS 5/2-1009) preserves the claim against PBS One because the liability was incurred prior to dissolution. The plaintiff relies on *Kraemer v. Estate of Gannon*, 132 Ill. 2d 202, 206-07 (1989), and *Illinois Bank & Trust Co. v. Wackerman*, 161 Ill. App. 3d 305, 311 (1st Dist. 1987), for the proposition that a claim survives dissolution regardless of when the breach occurs. The defense correctly counters that the Survival Statute preserves only those liabilities that exist at the time of dissolution; it does not revive liabilities that were validly extinguished prior to dissolution. *Kraemer*, 132 Ill. 2d at 207.

The central dispute is whether a novation occurred prior to PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. Under Illinois law, a novation requires (1) a valid existing obligation, (2) an agreement of the parties to a new contract, (3) the extinguishment of the old obligation, and (4) actual performance by the new debtor. *Kramer v. Christie Music Co.*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 81, 86 (1st Dist. 1993). The plaintiff relies heavily on the testimony of Attorney Michael Zavis to argue that Arthur never intended to release PBS One. Zavis testified that in 1988, he informed Arthur that the limited partnership would be making payments and that Arthur "never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor."

However, the defense persuasively argues that intent to release a debtor may be inferred from conduct, not just the subjective intent at the time of the initial restructuring. *Bank of Waukegan v. S.I. International, Ltd.*, 271 Ill. App. 3d 86, 92 (1st Dist. 1995). The undisputed facts show that following the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement—wherein National Material assumed PBS One’s "right, title, interest[,] and obligations"—Arthur accepted payments from the partnership entities (Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics) for nearly eight years without demanding payment from PBS One. This long course of conduct creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether Arthur consented to the substitution of National Material (or the partnership) for PBS One. *Anderson v. Village of Libertyville*, 248 Ill. App. 3d 1030, 1037 (1st Dist. 1993).

The plaintiff’s reliance on Zavis’s 1988 testimony is insufficient to resolve this factual dispute. The relevant inquiry for a potential novation in 1990 or 1994 is Arthur’s intent and conduct during the years he accepted payments from the new entity. Because reasonable minds could differ on whether Arthur’s conduct evidenced an intent to discharge PBS One, the trial court erred in granting summary judgment on Count XI.

**B. Count IX: National Material Did Not Expressly Assume the Consulting Agreement**

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable because it assumed PBS One’s obligations. The defense correctly argues that the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement does not contain the clear and unambiguous language required to establish an express assumption of the Consulting Agreement.

The 1990 agreement states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations... in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." This language is limited to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. It does not explicitly name the Consulting Agreement, nor does it clearly state that National Material assumed the specific contractual liabilities of PBS One as a general corporate entity. Under Illinois law, "the assumption of obligations must be clear and unambiguous." *First National Bank of Chicago v. M.F. Glickman Co.*, 56 Ill. 2d 553, 558 (1974).

The plaintiff’s argument that the 1988 Asset Purchase Agreement listed the Consulting Agreement as a material contract does not cure the deficiency in the 1990 agreement. The 1990 agreement was a transfer of a partnership interest, not a purchase of assets. The assumption of a partnership interest does not automatically imply the assumption of all contracts to which the transferring partner was a party. The defense is correct that the language "obligations... in and to the extent of" the partnership interest is ambiguous and susceptible to the interpretation that National Material only assumed obligations related to the ownership of the interest, not the underlying debt. Because the plaintiff failed to establish that National Material’s assumption was "clear and unambiguous" regarding the Consulting Agreement, summary judgment on Count IX was improper.

**C. Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor**

The plaintiff failed to provide a sufficient rebuttal to the defense’s argument regarding successor liability. Under Illinois law, successor liability generally requires: (1) an express agreement of assumption, (2) a merger or consolidation, or (3) a continuation of the business where the successor retains the same assets and management. *Logan v. K. Duhaime & Co.*, 299 Ill. App. 3d 396, 401 (1st Dist. 1999).

As established in the analysis of Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Furthermore, there is no evidence of a merger or consolidation; PBS One continued to exist until its voluntary dissolution in 1994. Finally, the defense correctly argues that there was no continuity of business operations. PBS One was merely an investor holding a limited partnership interest; it did not operate the scrap metal business. The undisputed facts show that PBS One "has no employees, no creditors. We don’t owe anybody. We don’t have anything. Just an investment." *Ng Bros. v. Trieu*, 287 Ill. App. 3d 416, 425 (1st Dist. 1997) (successor liability requires continuity of business operations, not mere transfer of assets). Because National Material acquired only a financial interest and did not continue PBS One’s business operations, the plaintiff cannot establish successor liability as a matter of law.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense’s arguments are sustained. The trial court erred in granting summary judgment for the plaintiff. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether a novation occurred releasing PBS One (Count XI), and the plaintiff failed to establish a legal basis for liability against National Material under theories of express assumption (Count IX) or successor liability (Count X).

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the plaintiff is reversed, and the cause is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
