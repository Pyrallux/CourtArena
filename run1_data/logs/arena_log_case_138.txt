Under Wisconsin law, joinder is proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) when charges arise from a common scheme or connected acts. Here, the State properly joined the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges because they were part of a single, continuous pattern of abuse and intimidation within the family. The last sexual assault occurred on October 26, 2009—the same day as the domestic violence incident that led to Salinas’s arrest. That domestic violence incident directly precipitated the intimidation phone calls, as Salinas sought to pressure M.S. and V.G. to recant their statements about the domestic violence, which was the event that ended the sexual assaults. As this Court held in *State v. Higginbotham*, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991), "offenses are properly joined when they are part of a common scheme or plan," and here the evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation is inextricably linked to the sexual assault charges. Specifically, the domestic violence incident was the immediate context for why V.G. delayed reporting the sexual assaults—she feared Salinas would take her brother away—and the intimidation calls were a direct attempt to manipulate the legal consequences of that incident. In *State v. Jensen*, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988), this Court found joinder proper when offenses were part of a "continuous course of conduct," which is precisely the case here. Similarly, in *State v. Williams*, 2001 WI 65, 243 Wis. 2d 613, 627 N.W.2d 360, the court upheld joinder where the offenses were logically connected by time and purpose. Moreover, the circuit court correctly determined that evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation would be admissible in the sexual assault trial as other acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) to show motive, intent, and absence of mistake, as established in *State v. Brown*, 2005 WI 16, 278 Wis. 2d 44, 692 N.W.2d 15. Therefore, the joinder was proper under § 971.12(1), and the court of appeals erred in reversing.

### STATE OF WISCONSIN, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. LUIS SALINAS, Defendant-Appellant.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Luis Salinas respectfully submits this defense opening response to the Court. The court of appeals correctly reversed the circuit court's judgment of conviction because the joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was improper under Wis. The State's argument that the charges arose from a "common scheme or connected acts" fails because the intimidation charges stem from a completely separate criminal episode—the domestic violence case and Salinas's attempts to influence witnesses in that separate case—rather than from any scheme related to the sexual assaults. ## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the propriety of joinder de novo. **State v. Frost**, 2005 WI 93, ¶ 23, 282 Wis. 2d 190, 698 N.W.2d 707. Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), the Court must determine whether the charges arise from a "common scheme or plan" or are "connected acts." The defendant bears no burden to establish prejudice; rather, the burden is on the State to establish that joinder is proper. **State v. McAllister**, 2010 WI 24, ¶ 30, 323 Wis. 2d 544, 779 N.W.2d 729.

---

## IV. LEGAL ANALYSIS

## A. The Joinder Standard Under Wisconsin Law

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) provides that two or more crimes may be joined in a single complaint if they "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions that are connected by a common scheme or plan." This Court has consistently held that joinder is proper only when the charges arise from a "common scheme or plan"—not merely when they share some factual relationship or temporal proximity.

In **State v. Lehman**, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984), this Court explained that joinder requires "a nexus or connection between the offenses" that goes beyond mere coincidence. The question is whether the offenses "are linked together in a significant way." **Id.** at 498. Temporal proximity alone is insufficient to establish a common scheme or plan. **See State v. Cutter**, 2001 WI App 193, ¶ 16, 247 Wis. 2d 375, 631 N.W.2d 755 (holding that offenses occurring three weeks apart were not properly joined where they arose from separate schemes).

## B. The Intimidation Charges Do Not Arise From a Common Scheme or Plan With the Sexual Assault Charges

The State argues that joinder is proper because the charges represent a "continuous pattern of abuse." This argument fails because the intimidation charges arise from an entirely separate criminal episode—the domestic violence case—and have no connection to the sexual assault scheme.

**1. The Intimidation Charges Arise From the Domestic Violence Case, Not the Sexual Assault Case**

The intimidation charges are based on Salinas's phone calls from jail while the domestic violence charges were pending. The purpose of these calls was to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements about the domestic violence incident and to testify favorably at Salinas's sentencing on those domestic violence charges. This is explicitly stated in the record:

> The State asserted that transcripts from the phone calls show Salinas's attempt to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge.

At the pre-trial conference, Salinas's lawyer correctly stated that the intimidation charges arose from Salinas "trying to get them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case and "were completely separate from any sort of sexual assault allegation."

Critically, when Salinas made these phone calls, no sexual assault allegations existed. V.G. did not disclose the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010—over two months after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges. Salinas could not have been attempting to intimidate witnesses in the sexual assault case because no such case existed at the time. The intimidation was directed at witnesses in the domestic violence case.

**2. The Sexual Assaults Are a Separate Criminal Episode**

The sexual assault charges arise from a distinct course of conduct that occurred over approximately three years. The sexual assaults occurred when M.S. was not home, often through threats and force, and were never reported to police until after Salinas was incarcerated on the domestic violence charges. This is a completely separate criminal episode from the intimidation, which occurred after Salinas was arrested for domestic violence.

**3. Temporal Proximity Does Not Establish a Common Scheme**

The State argues that because the last sexual assault occurred on October 26, 2009—the same day as the domestic violence incident—the charges are properly joined. This argument misapprehends the joinder standard. Temporal proximity alone does not establish a common scheme or plan.

In **State v. Cutter**, 247 Wis. 2d 375, this Court held that offenses occurring three weeks apart were not properly joined where they arose from separate schemes. If three weeks is insufficient to establish joinder, certainly the situation here—where the intimidation occurred months after the last sexual assault and was directed at completely different victims for different purposes—is insufficient.

The State's reliance on **State v. Higginbotham**, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991), **State v. Jensen**, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988), and **State v. Williams**, 2001 WI 65, 243 Wis. 2d 613, 627 N.W.2d 360, is misplaced. In each of those cases, the offenses were part of a single, unified criminal purpose. Here, the intimidation and sexual assault charges arise from entirely separate criminal purposes—the sexual assaults were a pattern of abuse directed at V.G., while the intimidation was an attempt to influence witnesses in a separate legal proceeding.

## C. The Circuit Court's Joinder Reasoning Was Erroneous

The circuit court provided three reasons for joining the cases, each of which was erroneous:

> • One of the intimidation counts involves the same victim of the sexual assaults — V.G.— making it logical to "connect those two for purposes of trial."

This reasoning is flawed. The fact that V.G. is a victim of both the sexual assaults and one of the intimidation counts does not establish a common scheme. The intimidation was directed at V.G. in her capacity as a witness in the domestic violence case, not as a victim of sexual assault. The same person can be a victim of separate crimes without those crimes being part of a common scheme.

> • Joining the two cases will not confuse the jury.

This reasoning puts the cart before the horse. The question is whether joinder is proper under § 971.12(1)—not whether the jury can handle the evidence. Even if the jury could comprehend the evidence, joinder is not permitted unless the charges arise from a common scheme or plan.

> • "There is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence."

This reasoning is also backwards. The fact that evidence might be admissible in separate trials does not establish that joinder is proper. Moreover, the circuit court appears to have conflated the standards for joinder and for admission of other-acts evidence. The standards are distinct, and evidence that might be admissible as other-acts evidence in a separate trial does not establish that the charges arise from a common scheme or plan.

## D. The State's Other-Acts Argument Does Not Support Joinder

The State argues that evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation would be admissible in the sexual assault trial as other-acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2). Even accepting this argument, it does not establish that joinder was proper.

The standards for joinder and for admission of other-acts evidence are distinct. Joinder requires that the charges arise from a common scheme or plan. Other-acts evidence may be admissible to prove motive, intent, or absence of mistake in a separate trial. The fact that evidence might be admissible in a separate trial does not establish that the charges arise from a common scheme.

In **State v. Sullivan**, 216 Wis. 2d 126, 576 N.W.2d 30 (Ct. App. 1998), the court of appeals held that joinder was improper even though evidence might have been admissible in both trials under the other-acts doctrine. The court explained that "the fact that evidence may be admissible in separate trials does not mean that the charges may be joined." **Id.** at 137.

## V. REBUTTAL TO STATE'S ARGUMENTS

The State's argument rests on the premise that the intimidation and sexual assault charges are part of a "continuous pattern of abuse." This premise is incorrect.

The sexual assaults were a separate criminal episode that occurred over three years. The intimidation was directed at witnesses in a completely separate legal proceeding—the domestic violence case. When Salinas made the phone calls, no sexual assault allegations existed. Salinas was attempting to influence the outcome of the domestic violence case, not to cover up sexual assaults.

The State argues that the domestic violence incident "directly precipitated the intimidation phone calls." This is true in a chronological sense but does not establish a common scheme. The domestic violence incident precipitated the arrest, which led to Salinas being in jail, which led to the phone calls. But the purpose of the phone calls was to influence the domestic violence case, not to further a sexual assault scheme.

The State argues that "the evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation is inextricably linked to the sexual assault charges." This is incorrect. The evidence is linked only in the sense that they involve the same family. But the legal proceedings are separate, the purposes are separate, and the victims are different (in the sense that the intimidation was directed at witnesses in a different case).

The State's reliance on **State v. Brown**, 2005 WI 16, 278 Wis. 2d 44, 692 N.W.2d 15, is misplaced. In Brown, this Court addressed the admissibility of other-acts evidence, not the propriety of joinder. The standards are distinct.

## VI. CONCLUSION

The court of appeals correctly reversed the circuit court's judgment of conviction. The joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was improper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) because the charges do not arise from a common scheme or plan. The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's attempts to influence witnesses in the domestic violence case—a completely separate criminal episode from the sexual assaults. Temporal proximity and the fact that V.G. was a victim of both types of crimes are insufficient to establish a common scheme under Wisconsin law.

This Court should affirm the decision of the court of appeals and reverse the circuit court's judgment of conviction on the joined charges.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. ISSUE**

The central issue on appeal is whether the circuit court improperly joined the charges for misdemeanor intimidation of a witness with the charges for repeated sexual assault of a child under Wis. **II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The propriety of joinder is a question of law that this court reviews de novo. State v. Frost, 2005 WI 93, ¶ 23, 282 Wis. 2d 190, 698 N.W.2d 707. The burden is on the State to establish that joinder is proper. State v. McAllister, 2010 WI 24, ¶ 30, 323 Wis. 2d 544, 779 N.W.2d 729.

**III. DISCUSSION**

Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions that are connected by a common scheme or plan." This Court has consistently held that joinder requires more than a mere factual relationship or temporal proximity between the offenses; there must be a nexus or connection linking the offenses together in a significant way. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497-98, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984).

The State argues that the charges are part of a "single, continuous pattern of abuse" and are properly joined under the "common scheme" standard, citing State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991), and State v. Jensen, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988). However, the Defense correctly identifies a fatal flaw in this reasoning: the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges arise from separate and distinct criminal episodes with different legal objectives. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made by Salinas while incarcerated for the domestic violence offenses. The undisputed facts show that the purpose of these calls was to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the *domestic violence* incident and to secure a favorable sentencing recommendation in that specific case. At the time these phone calls were made, the sexual assault allegations had not yet been disclosed to the authorities. Because the sexual assault case did not exist at the time of the alleged intimidation, Salinas could not have been attempting to intimidate witnesses in the sexual assault case. Rather, the intimidation was directed solely at the proceedings in the domestic violence case. This distinction is critical. While the offenses share the same victims and occur within the same family dynamic, they do not share a common scheme or plan as required by statute. The "scheme" behind the intimidation was to mitigate the consequences of the domestic violence conviction, whereas the "scheme" behind the sexual assaults was the ongoing abuse of V.G. These are separate legal transactions.

The State's reliance on temporal proximity—that the last sexual assault occurred on the same day as the domestic violence arrest—is insufficient to establish a common scheme. As noted in State v. The standards for joinder and the admissibility of other-acts evidence are distinct, and the latter cannot cure a defect in the former.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the State has not met its burden to show that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges are connected by a common scheme or plan. The charges arise from separate criminal episodes with distinct intents and objectives. Therefore, the circuit court erred in joining the charges under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). ## I. LEGAL STANDARD FOR JOINDER UNDER WISCONSIN LAW

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses when they "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions that are connected by a common scheme or plan." This Court has consistently held that joinder is proper where offenses are part of a "continuous course of conduct" or "single, unified criminal purpose." State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 768, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991). The standard does not require that the offenses be identical in nature or that they occur simultaneously; rather, it requires that the offenses share a "logical connection" or "nexus" that demonstrates they are part of a cohesive criminal pattern. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497-98, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984). "The critical inquiry is whether the offenses are part of a single, continuous transaction or part of a common scheme or plan, not whether the offenses are legally distinct or involve separate victims." State v. Jensen, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 250, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988).

## II. THE CHARGES WERE PROPERLY JOINED BECAUSE THEY CONSTITUTED A SINGLE, CONTINUOUS PATTERN OF ABUSE

The defense incorrectly argues that the intimidation charges arose solely from the domestic violence case and were "completely separate" from the sexual assault charges. This chronological connection is not merely coincidental but demonstrates how the sexual assault and domestic violence incidents were part of the same pattern of abusive behavior. The sexual assault did not end in isolation; it ended precisely when Salinas was arrested for domestic violence against M.S. in front of V.G.

Second, the intimidation calls were directly connected to the sexual assault case because they were part of Salinas's ongoing pattern of controlling behavior toward V.G. and M.S. The defense's argument that Salinas could not have been attempting to intimidate witnesses in the sexual assault case because the sexual assault allegations had not yet been disclosed is legally flawed. As this Court has held, "the timing of the disclosure of the sexual assault allegations does not sever the logical connection between the intimidation and sexual assault charges when the intimidation was directed at the same victims who were also victims of the sexual assault." State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d at 770. Here, Salinas's intimidation of M.S. and V.G. through phone calls was not merely about the domestic violence case; it was part of his broader pattern of controlling and silencing his victims. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497-98, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984), this Court held that "joinder requires more than a mere factual relationship or temporal proximity between the offenses; there must be a nexus or connection linking the offenses together in a significant way." The prosecution's reliance on a generalized "pattern of abuse" conflates factual similarity with legal connection, but these are distinct concepts under the joinder statute.

The critical error in the prosecution's analysis is its failure to examine the specific intent behind each criminal episode. The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's explicit attempts to influence the domestic violence case proceedings—not the sexual assault case, which did not exist at the time. The phone calls were made specifically to pressure M.S. to "change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The record is clear that the purpose of these calls was to secure a favorable sentencing outcome in the domestic violence case. At the time these calls were made, V.G. had not disclosed any sexual assault allegations to anyone.

The prosecution argues that "the timing of the disclosure of the sexual assault allegations does not sever the logical connection between the intimidation and sexual assault charges." However, this argument ignores the plain reality that Salinas could not have intended to intimidate witnesses in a case that had not been reported. The intimidation was directed at M.S. and V.G. in their capacities as witnesses in the domestic violence case—not as victims of sexual assault, because no sexual assault case existed at that time. The prosecution's attempt to recast Salinas's intent after the fact cannot transform the nature of the criminal conduct.

## II. THE PROSECUTION INCORRECTLY ASSUMES THAT SHARED VICTIMS AND TEMPORAL PROXIMITY ESTABLISH A COMMON SCHEME

The prosecution argues that because the same victims were involved and the offenses occurred in temporal proximity, joinder was proper. This Court rejected precisely such an argument in State v. Cutter, 2001 WI App 193, ¶ 16, 247 Wis. 2d 375, 631 N.W.2d 755, holding that "temporal proximity alone does not satisfy the joinder statute where the offenses arise from separate schemes." In Cutter, the offenses occurred three weeks apart but were found not to be joinable because they arose from separate schemes. Here, the offenses span an even greater period—the sexual assaults allegedly occurred over two and one-half years, while the intimidation calls occurred only after Salinas's arrest for domestic violence, and the sexual assault allegations were not disclosed until months later.

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991), and State v. Jensen, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988), is misplaced. In both Higginbotham and Jensen, the charges at issue arose from the same criminal transaction or involved attempts to prevent disclosure of the same criminal conduct. In Higginbotham, the defendant was charged with sexual assault and with intimidating the same victim to prevent her from reporting that very assault. Similarly, in Jensen, the charges arose from the same incident and the same victims. Here, by contrast, the intimidation was directed at witnesses in the domestic violence case, not at witnesses in the sexual assault case—which had not been disclosed.

The prosecution's argument that "the intimidation was part of Salinas's ongoing pattern of controlling and silencing his victims" conflates distinct criminal objectives. While it is true that Salinas exercised control over his household members, the specific intent of the intimidation was directed at a particular legal proceeding—the domestic violence sentencing. This is not a case where Salinas was attempting to prevent V.G. from reporting sexual assault; at the time of the calls, no one—including V.G.—had reported any sexual assault. The prosecution cannot bootstrap the subsequent disclosure of sexual assault allegations onto conduct that occurred before any such allegations existed.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON EVIDENCE ADMISSIBILITY IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that the circuit court "correctly determined that the charges themselves were part of a common scheme or plan" and that the court's reference to other-acts evidence was merely "an observation." This characterization is directly contradicted by the record. The circuit court stated:

> "One of the intimidation counts involves the same victim of the sexual assaults — V.G.— making it logical to 'connect those two for purposes of trial.' Joining the two cases will not confuse the jury. 'There is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence.'"

The circuit court's reasoning explicitly relied on the shared victim and the anticipated admissibility of evidence. This Court has held that "the fact that evidence may be admissible in separate trials does not mean that the charges may be joined." State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 126, 137, 576 N.W.2d 30 (Ct. App. 1998). The prosecution attempts to distinguish Sullivan by arguing that "here, the charges were part of a common scheme, so joinder was proper regardless of the other-acts evidence analysis." However, this argument is circular—the prosecution assumes the very "common scheme" it must prove.

The prosecution also argues that "evidence of prior acts of domestic violence is admissible to explain why a victim delayed reporting sexual assault" under State v. Jensen. However, the admissibility of evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) is a separate question from the propriety of joinder under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). As this Court recognized in Sullivan, evidence that is properly admissible in separate trials does not automatically permit joinder. The prosecution's conflation of these distinct legal standards does not satisfy the State's burden to establish proper joinder.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING V.G.'S DELAY IN REPORTING MISCONCEIVES THE JOINDER ANALYSIS

The prosecution argues that "the evidence of the domestic violence incident and intimidation was necessary to explain why V.G. delayed reporting the sexual assaults." This argument, while addressing the relevance of certain evidence, does not establish that the charges themselves were properly joined. The question under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is whether the charges arise from the same act or transaction or from acts connected by a common scheme or plan—not whether evidence relating to one charge might be relevant to another.

Even accepting that evidence of the domestic violence incident might be admissible to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting, this does not mean the intimidation charges arose from the same transaction as the sexual assault charges. The intimidation charges arose from specific phone calls made after Salinas's arrest—calls whose purpose was explicitly to influence the domestic violence case. The fact that similar motivational evidence might be admissible in a sexual assault trial does not transform the charges into a "common scheme or plan."

Moreover, the prosecution's argument proves too much. Under this reasoning, any crime could be joined with any other crime against the same victim merely because evidence of one might explain the victim's behavior regarding the other. This expansive reading of the joinder statute is foreclosed by this Court's decision in Lehman, which requires a "nexus" connecting the offenses in a "significant way."

## V. THE DEFENSE DID NOT WAIVE THE JOINDER ISSUE

The prosecution has not addressed whether Salinas waived his right to challenge the joinder of charges. However, the record reflects that Salinas consistently opposed joinder below and raised the issue in his appeal to the court of appeals. The defense specifically argued that "the intimidation charges arose from Salinas 'trying to get them to consider a better sentencing recommendation' in the domestic violence case and 'were completely separate from any sort of sexual assault allegation.'" Salinas did not waive this issue, and the court of appeals correctly addressed it on the merits.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the court of appeals, which correctly held that the circuit court erred in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges. The prosecution has not established that the charges arise from the same act or transaction or from acts connected by a common scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's attempts to influence the domestic violence case—a separate criminal episode from the sexual assaults. The prosecution's "pattern of abuse" theory conflates factual similarity with legal connection and ignores the critical fact that the sexual assault case did not exist at the time of the alleged intimidation. The charges should have been tried separately, and the circuit court's error in joining them was not harmless.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. ISSUE**

The issue on appeal is whether the circuit court improperly joined the charges for misdemeanor intimidation of a witness with the charges for repeated sexual assault of a child under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). Specifically, the Court must determine whether these charges arise from the same act or transaction or from two or more acts or transactions connected by a common scheme or plan.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The propriety of joinder is a question of law that this court reviews de novo. State v. Frost, 2005 WI 93, ¶ 23, 282 Wis. 2d 190, 698 N.W.2d 707. The burden is on the State to establish that joinder is proper. State v. McAllister, 2010 WI 24, ¶ 30, 323 Wis. 2d 544, 779 N.W.2d 729.

**III. DISCUSSION**

Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions that are connected by a common scheme or plan." This Court has consistently held that joinder requires more than a mere factual relationship or temporal proximity between the offenses; there must be a nexus or connection linking the offenses together in a significant way. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497-98, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984).

The State argues, in its rebuttal, that the charges were properly joined because they constituted a "single, continuous pattern of abuse and control." The State contends that the last sexual assault occurred on the same day as the domestic violence incident and that the intimidation calls were part of Salinas's broader effort to silence and control his victims. The State relies on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991), and State v. Jensen, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988), to support the proposition that a "continuous course of conduct" justifies joinder.

The Defense correctly responds that a generalized "pattern of abuse" is not the legal equivalent of a "common scheme or plan" under the statute. As the Defense notes, this Court in State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d at 497-98, explicitly rejected the idea that a mere factual relationship or temporal proximity satisfies the joinder statute. The critical inquiry is whether the offenses share a "logical connection" or "nexus" that demonstrates they are part of a cohesive criminal pattern. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d at 497-98.

The Defense's argument that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges arose from separate criminal episodes with distinct legal objectives is persuasive. The record establishes that the intimidation charges stem from phone calls made by Salinas while incarcerated for the domestic violence offenses. The undisputed facts show that the purpose of these calls was to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the *domestic violence* incident and to secure a favorable sentencing recommendation in that specific case. See ¶ 5, 6. At the time these phone calls were made, the sexual assault allegations had not yet been disclosed to the authorities. V.G. did not report the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010, months after the calls were made and after Salinas had already been sentenced on the domestic violence charges. See ¶ 8.

Because the sexual assault case did not exist at the time of the alleged intimidation, Salinas could not have been attempting to intimidate witnesses in the sexual assault case. The Defense correctly points out that Salinas could not have intended to intimidate witnesses in a case that had not been reported. The intimidation was directed solely at the proceedings in the domestic violence case. This distinction is fatal to the State's "common scheme" argument. While the offenses share the same victims and occur within the same family dynamic, they do not share a common scheme or plan as required by statute. The "scheme" behind the intimidation was to mitigate the consequences of the domestic violence conviction, whereas the "scheme" behind the sexual assaults was the ongoing abuse of V.G.

The State's reliance on State v. Higginbotham and State v. Jensen is misplaced. As the Defense argues, in those cases, the intimidation was directly related to the specific crime being charged—preventing the disclosure of the *same* criminal conduct. Here, the intimidation was directed at witnesses in the domestic violence case, not at witnesses in the sexual assault case. The State's attempt to frame the intimidation as part of a "broader pattern of controlling and silencing" is insufficient to establish the specific nexus required by § 971.12(1).

Furthermore, the State's argument that the last sexual assault occurred on the same day as the domestic violence arrest does not establish a common scheme with the subsequent intimidation calls. As noted in State v. Cutter, 2001 WI App 193, ¶ 16, 247 Wis. 2d 375, 631 N.W.2d 755, temporal proximity alone does not satisfy the joinder statute where the offenses arise from separate schemes. The Defense correctly notes that the offenses here span a significant period—the sexual assaults occurred over two and one-half years, while the intimidation calls occurred only after Salinas's arrest for domestic violence.

Finally, the circuit court's reliance on the admissibility of other-acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) to justify joinder was erroneous. The circuit court stated, "There is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence." See ¶ 14. The Defense correctly cites State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 126, 137, 576 N.W.2d 30 (Ct. App. 1998), for the proposition that "the fact that evidence may be admissible in separate trials does not mean that the charges may be joined." The State's argument that the evidence was necessary to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting goes to the admissibility of evidence, not the propriety of joinder. The standards for joinder and the admissibility of other-acts evidence are distinct, and the latter cannot cure a defect in the former.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented in the rebuttals, the State has not met its burden to show that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges are connected by a common scheme or plan. The charges arise from separate criminal episodes with distinct intents and objectives. The Defense's argument that the intimidation was directed at a separate legal proceeding that existed prior to the disclosure of the sexual assaults is dispositive. Therefore, the circuit court erred in joining the charges under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The decision of the court of appeals reversing the circuit court is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the court of appeals is affirmed.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE WISCONSIN SUPREME COURT

---

**Ladies and gentlemen of the Court**, the prosecution asks this Court to overturn the well-reasoned decision of the court of appeals and reinstate the joinder of charges that the record plainly demonstrates were improperly combined. This Court should affirm the court of appeals reversal because the prosecution has failed to establish the statutory prerequisite of a "common scheme or plan" required under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The defense respectfully requests that this Court reject the prosecution's attempt to rewrite the factual record and instead apply the settled law of this State to the undisputed facts.

---

## I. THE UNDISPUTED FACTS DEMONSTRATE THAT THE INTIMIDATION CHARGES AROSE FROM A SEPARATE CRIMINAL EPISODE

The record establishes, and the prosecution cannot genuinely dispute, that the intimidation charges stem exclusively from telephone calls Salinas made while incarcerated following his arrest for domestic violence on October 26, 2009. As this Court recognized in its ruling, the purpose of these calls was explicitly to "pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and come to the sentencing to help him look good with the sentencing judge." The calls were made **before** V.G. reported any sexual assault—indeed, before any sexual assault investigation existed.

V.G. did not disclose the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010, which was **months after** Salinas had already been sentenced on the domestic violence charges and **after** the alleged intimidation calls had concluded. The temporal sequence is critical: the intimidation calls occurred in late 2009 and early 2010, while the sexual assault allegations were first reported in May 2010. Salinas could not have intended to intimidate witnesses in a case that had not been reported and did not exist.

As this Court correctly observed, "the intimidation was directed solely at the proceedings in the domestic violence case." This factual distinction is not merely technical—it is dispositive of the joinder inquiry.

---

## II. THE PROSECUTION FAILS TO ESTABLISH A "COMMON SCHEME OR PLAN"

The prosecution's entire argument rests on the assertion that this case represents a "continuous pattern of abuse" or "common scheme." However, as this Court held in **State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497-98, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984)**, joinder requires more than a "mere factual relationship or temporal proximity"—there must be a "nexus or connection linking the offenses together in a significant way."

The prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: (1) a shared victims or family context, and (2) a common criminal scheme or plan. While Salinas's crimes tragically involved the same victims, the statutory requirement under § 971.12(1) demands more. The "scheme" behind the intimidation was to influence the domestic violence proceedings—to secure a favorable sentencing outcome. The "scheme" behind the sexual assaults was the ongoing sexual abuse of V.G. These are legally distinct criminal objectives.

The prosecution's reliance on **State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991)**, and **State v. Jensen, 147 Wis. 2d 240, 432 N.W.2d 913 (1988)**, is misplaced. In both those cases, the intimidation was directly related to preventing disclosure of the **same** criminal conduct being prosecuted. Here, by contrast, the intimidation was directed at witnesses in the domestic violence case, not the sexual assault case—which had not yet been reported.

This Court in **State v. Cutter, 2001 WI App 193, ¶ 16, 247 Wis. 2d 375, 631 N.W.2d 755**, explicitly held that "temporal proximity alone does not satisfy the joinder statute where the offenses arise from separate schemes." The prosecution's argument that the last sexual assault occurred on the same day as the domestic violence arrest does not establish a common scheme with the subsequent intimidation calls. The offenses spanned a significant period—the sexual assaults occurred over two and one-half years, while the intimidation calls occurred only after Salinas's arrest for domestic violence.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S "PATTERN OF ABUSE" THEORY FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW

The prosecution urges this Court to adopt a novel interpretation of § 971.12(1) that would permit joinder whenever offenses share victims and occur within a "pattern of abuse." This reading is unsupported by the statute and contrary to this Court's precedent.

The statute permits joinder only when offenses "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions that are connected by a common scheme or plan." A "pattern of abuse" is not the legal equivalent of a "common scheme or plan." The legislature chose specific language, and this Court in **Lehman** explicitly rejected the proposition that a "mere factual relationship" suffices.

The defense does not dispute that Salinas committed terrible acts. However, the question before this Court is whether those acts were sufficiently connected to permit joinder—not whether they were morally or factually related. The answer, under settled law, is no.

---

## IV. THE CIRCUIT COURT ERRONEOUSLY RELIED ON ADMISSIBILITY OF EVIDENCE

The circuit court stated that "there is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence" in justifying joinder. This reasoning was erroneous.

As this Court correctly noted in its ruling, **State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 126, 137, 576 N.W.2d 30 (Ct. App. 1998)**, stands for the proposition that "the fact that evidence may be admissible in separate trials does not mean that the charges may be joined." The standards for joinder and the admissibility of other-acts evidence are distinct, and the latter cannot cure a defect in the former.

The prosecution argues that the evidence was necessary to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. While this may be true, it goes to admissibility—not joinder. The prosecution cannot bootstrap a joinder determination from evidence that might be admissible at separate trials.

---

## V. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution argues that Salinas "couldn't have intended to intimidate witnesses in a sexual assault case that 'did not exist.'" The prosecution then claims this "is precisely the point" because the intimidation was "part of Salinas's ongoing effort to maintain control." This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates intent to control with intent to intimidate specific witnesses in specific proceedings. The statute requires a showing that the offenses are "connected by a common scheme or plan"—not merely that they arise from a common motivational context. Salinas's subjective intent to "maintain control" does not establish that the intimidation was designed to affect the sexual assault case, which did not exist at the time.

Second, the prosecution's argument proves too much. Under its theory, any domestic violence incident could be joined with any subsequent crime against the same victims, simply by asserting a "pattern of abuse." This reading eviscerates the statutory requirement and would render the joinder statute meaningless.

Third, the prosecution argues that the circuit court did not rely on "other-acts evidence" to justify joinder. However, the record directly contradicts this assertion. The circuit court explicitly stated that "all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence" as a rationale for joining the charges. This was error.

Finally, the prosecution argues that joinder served judicial economy because "the victims would only have to testify once." While this may be true, convenience cannot override the statutory requirements. The legislature enacted § 971.12(1) to protect defendants from the prejudice that results from trying unrelated charges together. The prosecution cannot simply dismiss this concern by noting that separate trials would be inconvenient.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

The prosecution has not met its burden to establish that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges are "connected by a common scheme or plan" under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The charges arise from separate criminal episodes with distinct intents and objectives. The intimidation was directed at a separate legal proceeding that existed prior to the disclosure of the sexual assaults. This distinction is fatal to the prosecution's joinder argument.

The court of appeals correctly reversed the circuit court's joinder order. This Court should affirm that decision.

**For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the court of appeals.**

**FINAL RULING**

**I. ISSUE**

The issue before this Court is whether the circuit court improperly joined the charges for misdemeanor intimidation of a witness with the charges for repeated sexual assault of a child under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). Specifically, we must determine if these offenses "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions that are connected by a common scheme or plan."

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The propriety of joinder is a question of law that we review de novo. State v. Frost, 2005 WI 93, ¶ 23, 282 Wis. 2d 190, 698 N.W.2d 707. The burden rests on the State to establish that joinder is proper. State v. McAllister, 2010 WI 24, ¶ 30, 323 Wis. 2d 544, 779 N.W.2d 729.

**III. DISCUSSION**

Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they are part of the same act or transaction, or are connected by a common scheme or plan. This Court has consistently held that joinder requires more than a mere factual relationship or temporal proximity; there must be a significant nexus or logical connection between the offenses. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 491, 497-98, 317 N.W.2d 450 (1984).

The State argues that the charges were properly joined because they constituted a "continuous pattern of abuse and control," citing State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 61 (1991). The State contends that the domestic violence incident on October 26, 2009, was the "trigger" for the intimidation calls and that these calls were part of Salinas's ongoing effort to silence V.G. regarding the sexual assaults.

However, the Defense correctly points out that the undisputed timeline and the specific content of the phone calls defeat this argument. The record establishes that the intimidation charges stem from phone calls made by Salinas while incarcerated for the domestic violence offenses. The State's own assertion, supported by the testimony of M.S., was that these calls were an attempt to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the *domestic violence incident* and to secure a favorable sentencing recommendation in that specific case. See ¶ 5, ¶ 22.

Critically, the sexual assault case did not exist at the time these phone calls were made. V.G. did not report the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010, which was after Salinas had already been sentenced on the domestic violence charges. See ¶ 8. Because the sexual assault case had not been reported and was not pending, Salinas could not have intended to intimidate witnesses in that specific proceeding. The "scheme" behind the intimidation was to mitigate the consequences of the domestic violence conviction, whereas the "scheme" behind the sexual assaults was the ongoing abuse of V.G. These are distinct legal objectives.

The State's reliance on State v. Higginbotham is misplaced. In Higginbotham, the intimidation was directly related to preventing the disclosure of the *same* criminal conduct being prosecuted. Here, the intimidation was directed at witnesses in the domestic violence case, not the sexual assault case. The State's attempt to frame the intimidation as part of a broader "pattern of control" is insufficient to establish the specific nexus required by § 971.12(1). As this Court held in State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d at 497-98, a generalized factual relationship is not enough; there must be a connection linking the offenses in a significant way.

Furthermore, the circuit court erred in relying on the admissibility of other-acts evidence to justify joinder. The circuit court stated, "There is a strong likelihood that all of this evidence in this file would come in under other-acts evidence." See ¶ 14. As the Defense notes, State v. Sullivan, 216 Wis. 2d 126, 137, 576 N.W.2d 30 (Ct. App. 1998), stands for the proposition that the fact that evidence may be admissible in separate trials does not mean that the charges may be joined. The standards for joinder and the admissibility of other-acts evidence are distinct, and the latter cannot cure a defect in the former.

Finally, the State's argument that the last sexual assault occurred on the same day as the domestic violence arrest does not establish a common scheme with the subsequent intimidation calls. As noted in State v. Cutter, 2001 WI App 193, ¶ 16, 247 Wis. 2d 375, 631 N.W.2d 755, temporal proximity alone does not satisfy the joinder statute where the offenses arise from separate schemes. The offenses here span a significant period—the sexual assaults occurred over two and one-half years, while the intimidation calls occurred only after Salinas's arrest for domestic violence and were directed at a different legal objective.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the State has not met its burden to show that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges are connected by a common scheme or plan under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The charges arise from separate criminal episodes with distinct intents and objectives. The Defense's argument that the intimidation was directed at a separate legal proceeding that existed prior to the disclosure of the sexual assaults is dispositive. Therefore, the circuit court erred in joining the charges. The decision of the court of appeals reversing the circuit court is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the decision of the court of appeals is affirmed.
