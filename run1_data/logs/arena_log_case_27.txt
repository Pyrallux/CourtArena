**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, as well as the factual background provided in this matter. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion or violated statutory procedure in granting an additur and immediately entering judgment. For the reasons set forth below, the court finds in favor of the defendants and reverses the judgment of the trial court.

**I. Procedural Error Regarding Additur**

The court agrees with the arguments presented by both parties that the trial court committed reversible error by failing to offer the defendants the option to reject the additur and proceed to a new trial. Connecticut General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b establish a mandatory procedural framework for additur. These statutes unambiguously require that when a trial court determines a damages award is inadequate and orders an additur, it must present the parties with a choice: accept the increased award or submit to a new trial on the issue of damages.

The trial court in this case ordered an additur of $15,000 on July 12, 2002, and "immediately rendered judgment for the plaintiff, including the additur." By doing so, the court deprived the defendants of their statutory right to elect a new trial. This action is directly contrary to the controlling precedent of this jurisdiction.

In *Mendes v. Board of Education*, 192 Conn. 513 (1984), this Court held that "the trial court must offer the defendant the option of accepting the additur or having a new trial on the issue of damages." This requirement is not a mere suggestion; it is a condition precedent to the entry of judgment. The failure to adhere to this mandate was reiterated in *Ciccone v. Licciardi*, 193 Conn. 60 (1984), where the Court explicitly stated that "failure to provide the defendant with the option of accepting the additur or having a new trial constitutes reversible error." The trial court’s failure to offer the defendants this choice constitutes structural error that undermines the integrity of the judicial process.

Furthermore, in *Hill v. Town of Stratford*, 220 Conn. 675 (1992), the Court reaffirmed that "Connecticut law mandates that when a trial court orders additur, it must first give the parties the opportunity to elect between accepting the additur or proceeding to a new trial." The trial court’s immediate entry of judgment following the additur order was a clear violation of this mandate. As argued by the prosecution, this error is not subject to harmless error analysis and requires reversal.

**II. Substantive Adequacy of Damages**

While the procedural error identified above is sufficient to warrant reversal, the court further finds merit in the defense’s argument regarding the substantive adequacy of the jury’s award. The defense correctly notes that the standard for granting an additur is high. Under *Gaudiano v. C & S Construction Co.*, 189 Conn. 432 (1983), an additur is appropriate only when the jury’s award is "so inadequate as to shock the conscience and be without support in the evidence."

The defense argues that the evidence presented at trial, which described a rear-end collision resulting in "minor injuries," supports the jury’s award of $1,715 in noneconomic damages. The trial court’s conclusion that this award was insufficient as a matter of law appears to contradict the "shock the conscience" standard established in *Gaudiano*. While the trial court is vested with discretion in reviewing damages, that discretion is not unlimited. The defense has demonstrated that the trial court may have abused its discretion by substituting its own judgment for that of the jury without the requisite evidentiary basis showing the award was grossly inadequate.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the court concludes that the trial court’s judgment must be reversed. The trial court violated mandatory statutory procedure by failing to offer the defendants the option of a new trial prior to entering judgment on the additur, in contravention of General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b and the precedents set forth in *Mendes v. Board of Education*, *Ciccone v. Licciardi*, and *Hill v. Town of Stratford*. Furthermore, the trial court abused its discretion in granting the additur where the jury’s verdict was supported by the evidence and did not shock the conscience.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial on the issue of damages.

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the plaintiff's response to our opening arguments. As demonstrated below, the plaintiff's assertions fail to overcome the fundamental procedural and substantive defects in the trial court's judgment. The court should reverse the judgment and remand for a new trial.

## I. THE PROCEDURAL VIOLATION IS NOT SUBJECT TO HARMLESS ERROR ANALYSIS

The plaintiff's characterization of the additur procedural requirement as a "technical violation" subject to harmless error analysis fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the right at stake. This Court has consistently held that the requirement to offer the defendant the choice between accepting an additur or proceeding to a new trial is mandatory and structural.

In *Mendes v. Board of Education*, 192 Conn. 513 (1984), this Court explicitly held that "the trial court must offer the defendant the option of accepting the additur or having a new trial on the issue of damages." The Court did not characterize this as a mere technical requirement; rather, it recognized that the right to elect a new trial is a fundamental procedural protection. Similarly, in *Ciccone v. Licciardi*, 193 Conn. 60 (1984), the Court explicitly stated that "failure to provide the defendant with the option of accepting the additur or having a new trial constitutes reversible error." The use of the term "reversible error" rather than "harmless error" is dispositive.

The plaintiff's reliance on *State v. Smith*, 259 Conn. 613 (2002), is misplaced. That case addressed harmless error in the context of evidentiary rulings, not the fundamental structural right to elect between accepting an additur or proceeding to a new trial. The right to elect a new trial is a substantive procedural right that cannot be waived by mere participation in hearings. The defendants objected to the additur motion, but that objection does not substitute for the mandatory offer required by statute.

The defense specifically objected to the additur motion and participated in oral argument precisely because they were seeking to preserve their right to a new trial. The trial court's failure to then offer them that choice deprived them of the very relief they were requesting. This is not a situation where the defendants failed to object; rather, the trial court ignored their objection and proceeded in direct contravention of their stated position. Under *Hill v. Town of Stratford*, 220 Conn. 675 (1992), such a failure requires reversal regardless of any perceived prejudice.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO MEET THE HIGH BURDEN REQUIRED FOR ADDITUR

Even if this Court were to find the procedural error harmless (which it should not), the trial court nonetheless abused its discretion in granting the additur because the plaintiff failed to meet the demanding "shock the conscience" standard articulated in *Gaudiano v. C & S Construction Co.*, 189 Conn. 432 (1983).

The plaintiff argues that a thirty-three-year-old woman with a "35 percent disability" warrants an additur. However, the plaintiff mischaracterizes the evidence. The trial record establishes that this was a rear-end collision at a busy intersection where the plaintiff's vehicle was stopped. The defendants presented evidence that the plaintiff sustained minor injuries. The jury's award of $1,715 in noneconomic damages, while modest, was not "so inadequate as to shock the conscience."

The plaintiff bears the burden of proving that the jury's award was grossly inadequate. The mere existence of some disability does not satisfy this burden. The trial court failed to articulate how the evidence demonstrated that the award was shocking or without support in the evidence. The plaintiff argues that "expert testimony on permanent impairment and ongoing pain" supported the additur, but the defense notes that such testimony would have been presented to the jury, which nonetheless awarded the amount it did. The jury, as the trier of fact, was entitled to assess the credibility and weight of such evidence.

Connecticut law does not require that every jury award match the maximum possible value of documented injuries. Juries are permitted to make reasonable assessments, and trial courts may not simply substitute their judgment for that of the jury merely because they would have awarded a different amount. The "shock the conscience" standard requires something more—a finding that the award is grossly disproportionate to the evidence presented. The plaintiff has not demonstrated that the trial court made such a finding, nor that such a finding would have been warranted.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON PRECEDENT IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff cites *Hill v. Town of Stratford* for the proposition that trial courts must correct manifestly inadequate verdicts. However, that case actually reinforces the defense's position. In *Hill*, this Court reversed an additur because the trial court failed to follow proper procedure. The Court stated that "Connecticut law mandates that when a trial court orders additur, it must first give the parties the opportunity to elect between accepting the additur or proceeding to a new trial." The plaintiff cannot rely on *Hill* to justify the additur while ignoring the very procedural requirements that *Hill* reaffirms.

Similarly, *Ciccone v. Licciardi* does not support the plaintiff's position. While that case affirmed the trial court's authority to grant additur, it explicitly required reversal because the trial court failed to offer the defendant the choice between accepting the additur or proceeding to a new trial. The plaintiff's attempt to distinguish *Ciccone* on its facts fails; the principle is clear and applies directly here.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense requests that this Court affirm the reversal of the trial court's judgment. The trial court committed reversible structural error by failing to offer the defendants the option to reject the additur and proceed to a new trial, in violation of General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b and the precedent established in *Mendes v. Board of Education*, *Ciccone v. Licciardi*, and *Hill v. Town of Stratford*. General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b mandate that when a court determines an additur is necessary, it must provide the defendant with the choice to accept the increased award or proceed to a new trial on the issue of damages.

The prosecution argues that this error was harmless because the defendants had previously objected to the motion for additur, citing *State v. Smith*, 259 Conn. 613 (2002). This argument is rejected. The right to elect is distinct from the right to be heard on a motion. As established in *Mendes v. Board of Education*, 192 Conn. 513 (1984), the election must occur *after* the court has determined the specific amount of the additur. A defendant’s prior objection to the concept of an additur does not constitute a formal election regarding a specific, court-determined sum. The prosecution’s reliance on *State v. Smith* is misplaced; that case addressed evidentiary harm in a criminal context and does not override the mandatory procedural safeguards required in civil additur proceedings.

In *Ciccone v. Licciardi*, 193 Conn. 60 (1984), the court held that "failure to provide the defendant with the option of accepting the additur or having a new trial constitutes reversible error." This is a structural error affecting the framework of the trial, not merely a harmless technicality. The trial court’s immediate entry of judgment deprived the defendants of this statutory right, necessitating reversal.

**II. Substantive Adequacy of Damages**

The court further finds that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the additur. The standard for granting an additur is strict; the award must be "shockingly inadequate" and "without support in the evidence." *Gaudiano v. C & S Construction Co.*, 189 Conn. 432 (1983).

The prosecution contends that the jury’s award of $1,715 in noneconomic damages was shockingly inadequate because the plaintiff is a "thirty-three-year-old woman with a documented 35 percent permanent disability" and there was "expert testimony on permanent impairment." The court cannot accept this argument. The provided case background contains no mention of a "35 percent permanent disability," "expert testimony," or the specific medical severity of the plaintiff's injuries. The background facts are limited to the occurrence of a rear-end collision and the jury’s total award of $5,000.

The prosecution asks this court to assume facts not in evidence to justify the additur. Under *Hill v. Town of Stratford*, 220 Conn. 675 (1992), a trial court’s authority to correct an inadequate verdict is predicated on the evidence actually presented at trial. Because the provided record does not support the prosecution’s assertions of permanent disability, the court cannot conclude that the jury’s verdict was so inadequate as to shock the conscience. The defense correctly argues that the trial court substituted its judgment for that of the jury without a sufficient evidentiary basis.

**III. Conclusion**

The prosecution’s rebuttal fails to demonstrate that the trial court’s procedural violation was harmless, nor does it establish that the jury’s verdict was inadequate based on the facts provided. The defense has proven that the trial court violated mandatory statutory procedure and exceeded its discretion by granting an additur unsupported by the record.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial on the issue of damages.

**SO ORDERED.**
