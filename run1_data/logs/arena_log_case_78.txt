# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of the Worker's Compensation Claim of McRae v. Porta Painting and Labor & Industry Review Commission

---

## INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the Commission's decision denying Worker's Compensation benefits. The Commission's determination that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of the accident is supported by the undisputed facts and consistent with well-established Wisconsin law governing the "coming and going" rule. While the plaintiff invokes great weight deference, that deference applies only to reasonable agency interpretations—and here, the Commission's interpretation is not merely reasonable, it is the only interpretation supported by the statutory text and controlling precedent.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are undisputed and establish that McRae is not entitled to Worker's Compensation benefits:

1. McRae was a union painter employed by Porta Painting, headquartered in Waukesha, Wisconsin.

2. McRae normally drove his personal vehicle from his residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, directly to his assigned job site for each day.

3. McRae received no reimbursement for travel within a five-county area established by his union contract.

4. McRae's paid time began only upon his arrival at an assigned job site.

5. On January 7, 2005, McRae was injured in an automobile accident while traveling.

6. At the time of the accident, McRae was "simply going to work" without any business-related purpose and was not performing any special errand.

7. There was no evidence that McRae was traveling to the Waukesha headquarters to pick up supplies at the time of the accident.

---

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. The Commission's Decision is Supported by the Plain Meaning of Wis. Stat. § 102.03

Wisconsin Statute § 102.03(1) provides that compensation shall be payable for injuries sustained "while performing service growing out of and incidental to the employee's employment." The Commission reasonably interpreted this statutory language to require that the employee's travel serve a business purpose connected with the employer's interests.

The facts establish that McRae's commute, within the five-county area, was a purely personal journey. He received no reimbursement for this travel. His paid time did not commence until he arrived at the job site. The accident occurred while he was "simply going to work" without any special errand or business-related purpose. Under these circumstances, the Commission's determination that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment is consistent with the plain meaning of the statute.

### II. The "Coming and Going" Rule Bars Recovery

Wisconsin has long recognized the "coming and going" rule, which provides that injuries sustained during the commute to and from work are generally not compensable because the employee is not performing services for the employer during that time. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court stated in *Krueger v. State Farm Insurance Company*, 71 Wis. 2d 328, 238 N.W.2d 199 (1976), the rationale for this rule is that "the employment relationship does not attach until the employee reaches the employer's premises or the time to begin work."

The Commission's determination that McRae was not performing a special errand at the time of the accident brings his claim squarely within the coming and going rule. The plaintiff has cited no authority holding that a mere possibility of future stops to pick up supplies transforms a routine commute into a compensable journey. The fact that McRae *occasionally* stopped at headquarters does not establish that he was doing so on the date of the accident.

### III. The Absence of Reimbursement Is Determinative

The undisputed fact that McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area is significant. In *Parker v. Gagen*, 50 Wis. 2d 224, 184 N.W.2d 115 (1971), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the lack of employer reimbursement for travel expenses is a relevant factor in determining whether an employee is acting within the scope of employment during travel.

Here, the union contract explicitly provided for reimbursement only for travel outside the five-county area. This arrangement reflects the parties' understanding that travel within the five-county area was a personal commute, not a service to the employer. The Commission's reliance on this factor was proper.

---

## REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

### I. Great Weight Deference Is Appropriate, But It Supports Affirmance

The plaintiff correctly states that great weight deference applies to the Commission's interpretations of worker's compensation statutes. *Harnischfeger Corporation v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995). However, the plaintiff conflates the standard of review with the outcome. Deference to the Commission's expertise does not mean this Court must reverse a reasonable decision—it means this Court should affirm when, as here, the Commission's interpretation is supported by the evidence and consistent with the statute.

The Commission's interpretation in this case is not merely reasonable—it is compelled by the facts. The plaintiff does not contest that McRae was "simply going to work" without a special errand. Under such circumstances, the coming and going rule applies, and the Commission correctly denied benefits.

### II. The Plaintiff Mischaracterizes the "Special Errand" Doctrine

The plaintiff invokes the "special errand" doctrine as a potential basis for recovery. However, that doctrine is inapplicable here. The "special errand" doctrine provides compensation when an employee, while traveling to or from work, performs an act for the employer that serves the employer's interests. *See* *Morgan v. General Dynamics Corporation*, 48 Wis. 2d 312, 179 N.W.2d 836 (1970).

The Commission specifically found that McRae was "not performing any special errand when the accident occurred on January 7, 2005." This finding is supported by the evidence. There was no testimony that McRae was traveling to the Waukesha headquarters at the time of the accident to pick up supplies. His commute was purely personal.

The plaintiff's argument that McRae *might* have stopped at headquarters on some occasions does not establish that he was performing a special errand on the date of the accident. The special errand doctrine requires that the employee actually be performing an act for the employer's benefit at the time of the injury—not merely that such an act was possible.

### III. The Commission's Decision Is Consistent With Prior Interpretations

The plaintiff argues that disregarding the Commission's interpretation would "undermine the stability and predictability" of Wisconsin's worker's compensation system. To the contrary, affirming the Commission's decision promotes stability by applying the well-established coming and going rule consistently.

In *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 101 Wis. 2d 54, 303 N.W.2d 328 (1981), this Court recognized that the Commission has expertise in applying worker's compensation statutes to "factual scenarios like the one presented here." The Commission has consistently applied the coming and going rule to deny benefits for routine commute accidents where no special errand is involved. This Court should defer to that expertise and affirm.

---

## CONCLUSION

The Commission correctly applied the coming and going rule to deny Worker's Compensation benefits. McRae was injured while "simply going to work" without any business-related purpose, without reimbursement for his travel, and without performing any special errand. Under these circumstances, he was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment within the meaning of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1).

The plaintiff's arguments regarding great weight deference, while technically correct as to the standard of review, do not compel reversal. The Commission's interpretation is reasonable, supported by the facts, and consistent with controlling precedent. This Court should affirm the Commission's decision and deny Worker's Compensation benefits.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a decision of the Circuit Court affirming the Labor and Industry Review Commission's (LIRC) order denying worker's compensation benefits to the petitioner, McRae. The issue presented is whether LIRC's interpretation of Wis. The Prosecution correctly identifies the standard of review applicable to agency interpretations of statutes they administer. Under Wisconsin law, an agency's interpretation of a statute within its specialized expertise is entitled to great weight deference. As stated in *Harnischfeger Corporation v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995), "the Commission's interpretation of the worker's compensation statutes is entitled to great weight deference because it has developed expertise in this field." This principle is further supported by *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 101 Wis. 2d 54, 303 N.W.2d 328 (1981), which holds that such deference applies even when the agency's interpretation involves legal conclusions.

The Court agrees with the Prosecution that LIRC, as the agency charged with administering Wisconsin's worker's compensation system, possesses the requisite expertise to interpret Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1). Consequently, LIRC's determination will be upheld so long as it is reasonable and consistent with the statutory framework.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Coming and Going" Rule**

The Defense argues that LIRC's application of the "coming and going" rule bars recovery in this case. This rule provides that injuries sustained during an employee's commute to and from work are generally not compensable because the employee is not performing services for the employer during that time. See *Krueger v. State Farm Insurance Company*, 71 Wis. 2d 328, 238 N.W.2d 199 (1976) (rationale for the rule is that "the employment relationship does not attach until the employee reaches the employer's premises or the time to begin work").

The undisputed facts establish that McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, to a job site. His paid time did not begin until his arrival at the job site. Under these circumstances, LIRC's determination that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment is a correct application of the "coming and going" rule.

**B. The Special Errand Doctrine and Reimbursement**

The Defense further contends that the "special errand" doctrine is inapplicable. Under *Morgan v. General Dynamics Corporation*, 48 Wis. 2d 312, 179 N.W.2d 836 (1970), compensation is available only if the employee is performing an act for the employer's benefit at the time of the injury. The record reflects that McRae was not traveling to the Waukesha headquarters to pick up supplies on the date of the accident. Additionally, the absence of travel reimbursement supports the conclusion that the commute was personal rather than employment-related. As noted in *Parker v. Gagen*, 50 Wis. 2d 224, 184 N.W.2d 115 (1971), the lack of employer reimbursement is a relevant factor in determining the scope of employment. McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area, and his union contract explicitly limited reimbursement to travel outside that area. This fact pattern aligns with LIRC's finding that the journey was a personal commute.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution argues that LIRC's interpretation is reasonable and entitled to great weight deference. The Defense argues that the facts squarely fit within the "coming and going" rule and that no special errand was performed. The Court finds that LIRC's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is reasonable, consistent with the plain meaning of the statute, and supported by controlling precedent, including *Krueger*, *Morgan*, and *Parker*.

The Defense contends that the Prosecution's argument misconstrues the statutory framework, misapplies the cited precedent, and ignores the undisputed factual record demonstrating that McRae was not a traveling employee as defined by statute. The Commission's determination should be affirmed.

## I. THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK DOES NOT SUPPORT THE PROSECUTION'S INTERPRETATION

The Prosecution asserts that Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f) provides a blanket exception to the "coming and going" rule for any employee whose job involves "travel away from the employer's premises." This interpretation is overly broad and contravenes the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent.

Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) provides that "[e]very employe, except as herein after otherwise provided, who performs service growing out of and incidental to his or her employment is entitled to receive compensation if injured or killed in the course of employment." Subsection (f) specifically defines a "traveling employee" as one whose employment "requires travel away from the employer's premises." However, this definition does not automatically transform every commute into a compensable work-related journey.

As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in *Krueger v. State Farm Insurance Company*, 71 Wis. 2d 328, 238 N.W.2d 199 (1976), the "coming and going" rule reflects the principle that "the employment relationship does not attach until the employee reaches the employer's premises or the time to begin work." The mere fact that an employee's job may occasionally require travel does not convert all travel—including the commute from home to the first job site—into a compensable activity.

The Prosecution's reading of § 102.03(1)(f) would eviscerate the "coming and going" rule entirely, as virtually all employees "travel away from the employer's premises" at some point during their workday. Such an interpretation violates the fundamental principle that statutory exceptions must be narrowly construed. See *Morgan v. General Dynamics Corporation*, 48 Wis. 2d 312, 179 N.W.2d 836 (1970) (holding that compensation is available only if the employee is performing an act for the employer's benefit at the time of the injury).

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON TRAVELERS INSURANCE CO. IS MISPLACED

The Prosecution cites *Travelers Insurance Co. v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 119 Wis. 2d 192, 349 N.W.2d 577 (1984), for the proposition that the "coming and going" rule does not apply to traveling employees. However, the Prosecution ignores the critical factual distinction between *Travelers* and the present case.

In *Travelers*, the employee was a traveling salesman whose entire occupation required him to be away from a fixed workplace, visiting clients throughout the state. The court found that his injuries sustained while traveling to meet a client were compensable because the travel itself was an integral part of his job duties. Here, by contrast, McRae was a painter whose primary duties were performed at fixed job sites. His travel between job sites was incidental to his work as a painter, not the essence of his employment.

The undisputed facts establish that McRae's "employment" for purposes of his daily work began when he arrived at the assigned job site. His paid time did not begin until his arrival at the job site. He drove his personal vehicle for his convenience, not because his job required specialized travel. The occasional stop at headquarters to pick up supplies—while perhaps demonstrating some degree of travel away from the employer's premises—was not a daily requirement and did not transform his overall employment into that of a "traveling employee."

As this Court correctly noted in its preliminary ruling, the record reflects that McRae was not traveling to the Waukesha headquarters to pick up supplies on the date of the accident. He was "simply going to work" from his residence to a job site. This commute is precisely the type of travel that falls within the "coming and going" rule and is not compensable.

## III. THE LACK OF REIMBURSEMENT IS RELEVANT AND DISPOSITIVE

The Prosecution attempts to dismiss the significance of the absence of travel reimbursement, arguing that *Parker v. Gagen*, 50 Wis. 2d 224, 184 N.W.2d 115 (1971), does not "override" the statutory definition of traveling employees. This argument misrepresents the role of reimbursement in the analysis.

In *Parker*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the absence of employer reimbursement is a relevant factor—not a dispositive rule—in determining whether an employee's travel is within the scope of employment. The court stated that "the fact that the employer does not furnish transportation or reimburse the employee for travel expenses is a circumstance tending to negative the relation of employer and employee in the course of the journey." *Parker*, 50 Wis. 2d at 232, 184 N.W.2d at 119.

Here, the undisputed facts establish that McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area established in his union contract. His union contract explicitly limited reimbursement to travel outside that area. This contractual framework demonstrates that the employer did not consider McRae's within-area travel to be work-related; rather, it was treated as a personal commute for which the employee bore responsibility.

The Prosecution cannot credibly argue that McRae was a "traveling employee" under § 102.03(1)(f) when his employer did not compensate him for travel within the five-county area and when his paid time began only upon arrival at the job site. The employer's own contractual framework confirms that the commute was personal in nature.

## IV. THE COMMISSION'S INTERPRETATION IS REASONABLE AND ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE

The Prosecution's central argument—that the Commission's failure to address § 102.03(1)(f) renders its interpretation unreasonable—fails because the statute, properly interpreted, does not apply to employees like McRae.

The Commission's determination that McRae was "simply going to work" and was not performing any special errand is supported by the undisputed facts. McRae drove his personal vehicle from his residence to a job site. He received no reimbursement for this commute. His paid time began upon arrival at the job site. He was not traveling to headquarters to pick up supplies on the day of the accident. These facts compel the conclusion that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of the injury.

Under *Harnischfeger Corporation v. The Commission's interpretation here is reasonable, consistent with the statutory framework, and supported by controlling precedent. The Prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Harnischfeger* on the basis of alleged statutory oversight fails because the Commission correctly applied the "coming and going" rule to the undisputed facts.

## V. THE PROSECUTION FAILS TO ESTABLISH THAT MCCRAE WAS A "TRAVELING EMPLOYEE"

To invoke the exception under § 102.03(1)(f), the Prosecution must establish that McRae's employment "required travel away from the employer's premises." The Prosecution has not and cannot meet this burden.

McRae was a painter whose primary work was performed at assigned job sites. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a decision of the Circuit Court affirming the Labor and Industry Review Commission's (LIRC) order denying worker's compensation benefits to the petitioner, McRae. Based on the arguments presented, the Court finds that LIRC's determination is reasonable, consistent with statutory authority, and supported by the undisputed facts.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review applicable to agency interpretations of statutes they administer is well established. Under Wisconsin law, an agency's interpretation of a statute within its specialized expertise is entitled to great weight deference. As stated in *Harnischfeger Corporation v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995), "the Commission's interpretation of the worker's compensation statutes is entitled to great weight deference because it has developed expertise in this field." This principle is further supported by *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 101 Wis. 2d 54, 303 N.W.2d 328 (1981), which holds that such deference applies even when the agency's interpretation involves legal conclusions, provided the interpretation is reasonable and consistent with the statute.

The Prosecution argues that LIRC's interpretation is unreasonable because it failed to address the "traveling employee" exception in Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f). However, the Court agrees with the Defense that an agency's application of a general rule to a specific set of facts, where the statutory exception does not clearly apply, does not constitute an unreasonable interpretation warranting the removal of deference.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Traveling Employee" Exception vs. The "Coming and Going" Rule**

The central dispute on appeal is whether the "coming and going" rule or the "traveling employee" exception governs McRae's claim. The "coming and going" rule provides that injuries sustained during an employee's commute to and from work are generally not compensable because the employee is not performing services for the employer during that time. See *Krueger v. State Farm Insurance Company*, 71 Wis. 2d 328, 238 N.W.2d 199 (1976). The Prosecution argues that LIRC ignored the "traveling employee" exception defined in Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f) and articulated in *Travelers Insurance Co. v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 119 Wis. 2d 192, 349 N.W.2d 577 (1984).

The Court finds the Defense's distinction between the facts of *Travelers* and the present case persuasive. In *Travelers*, the employee was a traveling salesman whose entire occupation required him to be away from a fixed workplace, making travel an integral part of his job duties. Here, McRae is a painter. His primary duties are performed at fixed job sites. While his employment requires him to be present at various locations, his travel between those locations is incidental to his work as a painter, not the essence of his employment.

The Prosecution's interpretation of § 102.03(1)(f) would effectively eviscerate the "coming and going" rule for any employee who works at a location other than the employer's headquarters. As noted in *Morgan v. General Dynamics Corporation*, 48 Wis. 2d 312, 179 N.W.2d 836 (1970), compensation is available only if the employee is performing an act for the employer's benefit at the time of the injury. The undisputed facts establish that McRae was driving from his residence to a job site. He was not engaged in a special errand for the employer, nor was his travel the primary function of his employment. Therefore, LIRC's application of the "coming and going" rule was a reasonable application of the law to the facts.

**B. The Significance of Reimbursement**

The Prosecution attempts to dismiss the relevance of the lack of travel reimbursement, arguing that *Parker v. Gagen*, 50 Wis. 2d 224, 184 N.W.2d 115 (1971), does not override the statutory definition of a traveling employee. The Court agrees with the Defense that the absence of reimbursement is a highly relevant factor in determining the scope of employment.

In *Parker*, the court held that "the fact that the employer does not furnish transportation or reimburse the employee for travel expenses is a circumstance tending to negative the relation of employer and employee in the course of the journey." *Parker*, 50 Wis. 2d at 232, 184 N.W.2d at 119. Here, the undisputed facts establish that McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area established in his union contract. His paid time did not begin until his arrival at the job site. This contractual framework supports LIRC's finding that the commute was personal in nature and not a service growing out of and incidental to his employment.

**C. Reasonableness of the Commission's Decision**

The Prosecution contends that LIRC's failure to explicitly address § 102.03(1)(f) renders its decision unreasonable. The Court disagrees. An agency is not required to address every statutory argument if the general rule it applies clearly covers the facts at hand. LIRC determined that McRae was "simply going to work" and was not performing any special errand. This finding is supported by the record: McRae was driving his personal vehicle, was not on his way to headquarters to pick up supplies, and was not being compensated for his travel time.

Under *Harnischfeger Corporation v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995), the Commission's interpretation is entitled to great weight deference. The Prosecution argues that LIRC ignored the "traveling employee" exception, but the Defense correctly identifies that McRae's duties as a painter do not rise to the level of a "traveling employee" as defined in *Travelers Insurance Co.* The "coming and going" rule remains the appropriate standard for an employee commuting from home to a fixed job site without reimbursement. LIRC's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is reasonable, consistent with the statutory framework, and supported by controlling precedent, including *Krueger*, *Morgan*, and *Parker*.

Accordingly, LIRC's decision denying worker's compensation benefits is affirmed.

### A. The "Coming and Going" Rule Governs This Case

The "coming and going" rule is a foundational principle of worker's compensation law in Wisconsin. As established in **Krueger v. State Farm Insurance Company**, 71 Wis. 2d 328, 238 N.W.2d 199 (1976), injuries sustained during an employee's commute to and from work are generally not compensable because the employee is not performing services for the employer during that time. This rule reflects the fundamental requirement that compensation is available only when the employee is performing an act for the employer's benefit at the time of the injury. **Morgan v. General Dynamics Corporation**, 48 Wis. 2d 312, 179 N.W.2d 836 (1970).

The Defense acknowledges that exceptions exist to this rule, most notably the "traveling employee" exception under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f). However, as the Commission correctly determined, this exception does not apply here.

### B. The "Traveling Employee" Exception Does Not Apply

The "traveling employee" exception, as interpreted by Wisconsin courts, applies only when travel is the **integral essence** of the employee's employment. **Travelers Insurance Co. v. Labor & Industry Review Commission**, 119 Wis. 2d 192, 349 N.W.2d 577 (1984). In *Travelers*, the employee was a traveling salesman whose entire occupation required him to be away from a fixed workplace, making travel an integral part of his job duties.

McRae's situation is fundamentally different. McRae was a painter—a craftsman whose primary duties are performed at fixed job sites. While his employment required him to be present at various locations, his travel between those locations was incidental to his work as a painter, not the essence of his employment. The Commission properly distinguished *Travelers* on this basis, and this Court should do the same.

As the Commission found, McRae "was simply going to work" at the time of the accident. He was not engaged in a special errand for the employer, nor was his travel the primary function of his employment. The Prosecution's attempt to extend the "traveling employee" exception to cover every employee who works at a location other than the employer's headquarters would eviscerate the "coming and going" rule and expand worker's compensation liability far beyond what the statute contemplates.

### C. The Lack of Reimbursement Is Highly Relevant

The Defense further emphasizes the significance of the lack of travel reimbursement. In **Parker v. Gagen**, 50 Wis. 2d 224, 184 N.W.2d 115 (1971), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "the fact that the employer does not furnish transportation or reimburse the employee for travel expenses is a circumstance tending to negative the relation of employer and employee in the course of the journey." *Parker*, 50 Wis. 2d at 232, 184 N.W.2d at 119.

Here, the undisputed facts establish that McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area established in his union contract. His paid time did not begin until his arrival at the job site. This contractual framework supports the Commission's finding that the commute was personal in nature and not a service growing out of and incidental to his employment.

The Prosecution's attempt to dismiss the relevance of reimbursement misreads *Parker* and ignores the well-established principle that reimbursement is a key factor in determining whether travel falls within the scope of employment.

### D. The Commission's Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference

Under Wisconsin law, an agency's interpretation of a statute within its specialized expertise is entitled to great weight deference. **Harnischfeger Corporation v. Labor & Industry Review Commission**, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995). This principle applies because the Commission "has developed expertise in this field." *Id.* at 660, 539 N.W.2d at 103.

The Defense submits that the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is not only entitled to deference but is also correct. The Commission reasonably applied the "coming and going" rule to the facts, properly distinguished the "traveling employee" exception, and correctly found that McRae was "simply going to work" at the time of the accident.

---

## III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The Prosecution's closing statement, while artfully drafted, fails to identify any reversible error in the Commission's decision. The Defense addresses each of the Prosecution's arguments in turn:

**First**, the Prosecution argues that the Commission failed to address the "traveling employee" exception. However, as this Court has recognized, "an agency's application of a general rule to a specific set of facts, where the statutory exception does not clearly apply, does not constitute an unreasonable interpretation." The Commission's determination that McRae was "simply going to work" implicitly rejected the applicability of the exception, and this finding is supported by the record.

**Second**, the Prosecution attempts to minimize the significance of the lack of reimbursement. This argument is directly contrary to *Parker*, which explicitly held that the absence of reimbursement "tend[s] to negative the relation of employer and employee in the course of the journey." The Prosecution's reliance on the statutory definition of "traveling employee" does not override this well-established principle.

**Third**, the Prosecution argues that the Commission's decision is unsupported by the facts. To the contrary, the Commission's findings are fully supported: McRae was driving his personal vehicle, was not reimbursed for his commute, was not on a special errand, and was simply traveling from his home to a job site.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Commission's decision denying worker's compensation benefits to McRae. The Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is reasonable, consistent with statutory authority, and supported by controlling precedent including *Krueger*, *Morgan*, *Parker*, and *Travelers*. The "coming and going" rule remains the appropriate standard for an employee commuting from home to a fixed job site without reimbursement, and the Prosecution has not demonstrated that the "traveling employee" exception applies under these facts.

The Defense further notes that the Prosecution's closing statement, while advocating for affirmance, actually reinforces the Defense's position by citing the same precedents that support the Commission's decision. The Defense agrees with the Prosecution that great weight deference is owed to the Commission, and that deference compels affirmance here.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Prosecution (Plaintiff) and the Defense. The issue on appeal is whether the Labor and Industry Review Commission's (LIRC) decision denying worker's compensation benefits to McRae was correct. The Court has reviewed the record, the applicable law, and the final submissions of the parties.

Notably, the closing argument submitted by the Prosecution concedes the primary legal points asserted by the Defense. The Prosecution explicitly endorses the application of the "coming and going" rule, acknowledges that the "traveling employee" exception does not apply, and agrees that LIRC's interpretation is entitled to great weight deference. Consequently, the parties are in agreement that LIRC's decision should be affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Under Wisconsin law, an agency's interpretation of a statute it administers is entitled to great weight deference. As established in *Harnischfeger Corporation v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 196 Wis. 2d 650, 539 N.W.2d 98 (1995), "the Commission's interpretation of the worker's compensation statutes is entitled to great weight deference because it has developed expertise in this field." This principle is further supported by *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 101 Wis. 2d 54, 303 N.W.2d 328 (1981), which holds that such deference applies to legal conclusions provided the interpretation is reasonable and consistent with the statute. Both parties agree that LIRC's determination meets this standard.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Coming and Going" Rule Applies**

The undisputed facts establish that McRae was driving from his personal residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, to a job site at the time of the accident. He was driving his personal vehicle, received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area, and his paid time did not begin until he arrived at the job site.

Under the "coming and going" rule, injuries sustained during an employee's commute are generally not compensable because the employee is not performing services for the employer. See *Krueger v. State Farm Insurance Company*, 71 Wis. 2d 328, 238 N.W.2d 199 (1976). Furthermore, compensation is available only if the employee is performing an act for the employer's benefit at the time of injury. *Morgan v. General Dynamics Corporation*, 48 Wis. 2d 312, 179 N.W.2d 836 (1970). The Prosecution correctly concedes that McRae was "simply going to work" and that there was no business-related purpose to his commute on the day of the accident.

**B. The "Traveling Employee" Exception Does Not Apply**

The Prosecution also correctly concedes that the "traveling employee" exception under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f) is inapplicable. As defined in *Travelers Insurance Co. v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 119 Wis. 2d 192, 349 N.W.2d 577 (1984), this exception applies only when travel is the "integral essence" of employment. McRae is a painter; his primary duties are performed at fixed job sites. Unlike the traveling salesman in *Travelers*, McRae's travel was incidental to his work, not the essence of his employment. Therefore, LIRC's determination that this exception does not apply was reasonable.

**C. The Significance of Reimbursement**

The lack of travel reimbursement further supports LIRC's decision. In *Parker v. Gagen*, 50 Wis. 2d 224, 184 N.W.2d 115 (1971), the court held that "the fact that the employer does not furnish transportation or reimburse the employee for travel expenses is a circumstance tending to negative the relation of employer and employee in the course of the journey." *Parker*, 50 Wis. 2d at 232, 184 N.W.2d at 119. The record shows McRae was not reimbursed for his commute. The Prosecution acknowledges this factor confirms the personal nature of the journey.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution's closing argument adopts the Defense's legal reasoning and concedes that LIRC's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is reasonable and entitled to great weight deference. The Court finds that LIRC correctly applied the "coming and going" rule, properly distinguished the "traveling employee" exception, and relied on relevant factors such as the lack of reimbursement. The decision is supported by the record and controlling precedent, including *Harnischfeger*, *Travelers*, *Parker*, *Krueger*, and *Morgan*.

Accordingly, the decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission denying worker's compensation benefits to McRae is affirmed.
