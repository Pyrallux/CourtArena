**Introduction and Summary of Argument**

May it please the Court, the defense respectfully submits that the family court's ruling should be affirmed. ## I. A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) Must Be Applied Gender-Neutrally

### A. The Plain Language and Purpose of the Statute Support Gender-Neutral Application

Turner argues that the statute's use of "man" and "father" unambiguously excludes women from its reach. This argument fails because it ignores the statute's essential purpose and the established principle that statutes must be interpreted in light of their remedial objectives.

In *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), this Court correctly analyzed the statutory purpose and held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read gender-neutrally. As the Court explained, the presumption of paternity exists to establish legal parentage for children born during marriage, ensuring that children receive the protections and benefits of having legally recognized parents. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 121. This purpose is equally served whether the married spouse is male or female.

The statute's use of "man" and "father" reflects traditional drafting conventions, not a legislative intent to exclude same-sex spouses. As the *McLaughlin* court recognized, when the legislature amended the parentage statutes to reflect the recognition of same-sex marriage in Arizona, it demonstrated an intent to treat spouses equally regardless of sex. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 13, 382 P.3d at 121. To hold otherwise would produce absurd results: a child born to a married opposite-sex couple would have two legal parents, but a child born to a married same-sex couple would have only one—an outcome the legislature clearly did not intend.

### B. *McLaughlin* Is Binding Precedent That This Court Should Follow

Turner urges this Court to reject *McLaughlin* as wrongly decided. This Court should decline that invitation. *McLaughlin* is binding precedent that correctly interpreted A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) in accordance with statutory purpose and constitutional principles.

The *McLaughlin* court applied the well-established principle that statutes must be interpreted to avoid absurd results and to effectuate legislative intent. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 121. The court properly considered the statute's purpose—establishing parentage for children born during marriage—and concluded that this purpose would be undermined by a gendered reading. Id.

Turner's reliance on *State v. Hansen*, 215 Ariz. 287, 160 P.3d 166 (2007), and *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. 132, 791 P.2d 677 (Ct. App. 1990), is misplaced. In *Hansen*, the Court applied unambiguous statutory language because the text was clear and produced no absurd result. 215 Ariz. at 291 ¶ 14, 160 P.3d at 170. Here, applying the statute exclusively to men would produce the absurd result of denying parentage to children born to married same-sex couples. In *K.A.S.*, the Court declined to read gender-neutral language into a statute that genuinely was limited to one sex. 164 Ariz. at 136, 791 P.2d at 681. Here, by contrast, the statute's purpose—establishing parentage for children of married couples—applies equally to all married couples.

This Court should follow *McLaughlin* and affirm the family court's gender-neutral application of the presumption.

## II. The Family Court Correctly Applied Equitable Estoppel

### A. Turner Is Estopped From Denying Oakley's Parental Rights

Independent of the statutory presumption, the family court correctly held that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental rights. This Court should affirm on this alternative ground.

Equitable estoppel applies when: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends that the other party rely on the conduct; (3) the other party is ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the other party relies on the conduct to his detriment. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 19, 382 P.3d at 123.

The evidence establishes all elements. Turner knew Oakley was not biologically related to C.T. yet represented to Oakley, to medical providers, and to the public that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent. Oakley has now been deprived of the opportunity to formalize her parental status through adoption due to Turner's representations.

### B. The Evidence Supports the Family Court's Factual Findings

The family court heard extensive testimony and considered documentary evidence demonstrating Turner's representations. Additionally, Turner posted on social media referring to Oakley as C.T.'s mother and celebrating their family unit.

Turner cannot now contradict these representations. As this Court held in *McLaughlin*, "where a biological mother has agreed to share parental rights with her partner, has held out her partner as a parent to the child, and has accepted the benefits of her partner's participation in raising the child, she is estopped from denying the partner's parental status." 240 Ariz. at 566-67 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 123-24.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for special action review regarding the family court’s determination of parental rights following the dissolution of a marriage between Turner and Oakley. The central issue is whether A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), which establishes a presumption of paternity for a "man" married to the mother, applies to Oakley, a woman in a same-sex marriage. Turner argues that the statute’s plain language limits its application to men, while Oakley contends the statute must be applied gender-neutrally and that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental status.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Application of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)**

The family court applied A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to Oakley based on the precedent established by this Court in *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016). Turner urges this Court to disregard *McLaughlin* and adhere strictly to the gendered text of the statute, citing *State v. Hansen*, 215 Ariz. 287, 160 P.3d 166 (2007), and *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. 132, 791 P.2d 677 (Ct. App. 1990), for the proposition that courts cannot rewrite clear statutory language.

While the principle of adhering to plain language is fundamental, as stated in *State v. Hansen*, 215 Ariz. 287, 160 P.3d 166 (2007), the Court is bound by the controlling precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016). In *McLaughlin*, this Court specifically held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. The *McLaughlin* court determined that applying the statute only to men would lead to absurd results, specifically that a child born to a married same-sex couple would be deprived of the legal protections of having two parents, whereas a child born to an opposite-sex couple would not. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

Turner’s reliance on *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. 132, 791 P.2d 677 (Ct. App. 1990), is unpersuasive in light of *McLaughlin*. *K.A.S.* involved a distinct statutory context where the court found the legislature’s use of gender-specific terms was deliberate. However, *McLaughlin* has since addressed the specific statute at issue here, A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), within the context of same-sex marriage. As a panel of this Court, we must follow the binding precedent set by *McLaughlin* unless and until it is overruled by a higher court. Therefore, the family court did not err in applying the presumption of parentage to Oakley.

**B. Equitable Estoppel**

Alternatively, the family court ruled that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of parentage. The doctrine of equitable estoppel applies where: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends the other to act upon those facts; (3) the other party is ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the other party relies on the facts to their detriment. *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 19, 382 P.3d at 123.

The record supports the family court’s finding. Turner and Oakley planned for the child together, with Oakley participating in the selection of a sperm donor and attending medical appointments. Turner executed a will naming Oakley as the sole custodian of the child in the event of Turner’s death. Furthermore, Turner listed Oakley on the birth certificate and publicly represented Oakley as the child’s mother on social media. These actions demonstrate Turner’s knowledge and intent that Oakley be recognized as a parent. Oakley relied on these representations to her detriment by investing time, emotion, and resources into the parental relationship. As held in *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 566-67 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 123-24, a biological mother who holds out her partner as a parent and accepts the benefits of that partnership is estopped from later denying that parental status.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The family court correctly applied the binding precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), in holding that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies gender-neutrally. Furthermore, the evidence supports the finding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley’s parental rights. Accordingly, the family court’s ruling is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the petition for special action review is denied.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Family Court Correctly Applied *McLaughlin v. Jones*, Which Is Binding Precedent Requiring Gender-Neutral Application of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)

Turner urges this Court to disregard *McLaughlin v. Jones* and revert to a strictly gendered reading of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1). This argument fails because *McLaughlin* constitutes binding precedent that this Court must follow unless overruled by a higher court.

In *McLaughlin*, this Court specifically addressed whether A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)—the same statute at issue here—applies to same-sex spouses. The Court held that "A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The *McLaughlin* court reasoned that applying the statute only to men would produce absurd results: "a child born to a married same-sex couple would be deprived of the legal protections of having two parents, whereas a child born to an opposite-sex couple would not." Id.

Turner attempts to distinguish *McLaughlin* by arguing that it "improperly disregarded" plain language principles and misapplied the absurdity doctrine. However, Turner cannot escape binding precedent by rearguing the merits of *McLaughlin*. As a panel of this Court, we are bound by the holding in *McLaughlin* unless and until it is overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or by this Court sitting en banc. *See State v. Garcia*, 227 Ariz. 215, 219 ¶ 13, 255 P.3d 1066, 1070 (App. 2011) (explaining that one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by decisions of other panels unless overruled).

Turner's reliance on *State v. Hansen*, 215 Ariz. 287, 160 P.3d 166 (2007), and *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. 132, 791 P.2d 677 (Ct. App. 1990), does not alter this analysis. Both cases were decided before *McLaughlin* and addressed different statutory contexts. *McLaughlin* specifically analyzed A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) in the context of same-sex marriage and concluded that gender-neutral application is required. The *McLaughlin* court was aware of the principles articulated in *Hansen* and *K.A.S.* but nonetheless held that the absurdity doctrine applied because "applying the statute only to men" would produce "absurd results." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

Turner argues that the legislature's use of "man" and "father" demonstrates deliberate intent to restrict the presumption to males. However, *McLaughlin* explicitly addressed this argument and rejected it, noting that the legislature could not have intended a result that denies children of same-sex couples the legal protections afforded to children of opposite-sex couples. Id. at 564-65 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The *McLaughlin* court recognized that Arizona's adoption of same-sex marriage through *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), changed the statutory landscape and required reexamination of previously gender-specific statutes. 240 Ariz. at 563-64 ¶ 12, 382 P.3d at 121-22.

Accordingly, this Court is bound by *McLaughlin* and must apply A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) gender-neutrally. The family court did not err in doing so.

### B. Turner's Separation of Powers Argument Is Foreclosed by *McLaughlin*

Turner argues that applying the statute gender-neutrally violates separation of powers because it amounts to judicial rewriting of legislation. This argument is precisely what *McLaughlin* considered and rejected.

In *McLaughlin*, the Court acknowledged that "courts lack authority to amend a statute to correct perceived defects or to achieve policy goals the legislature has not enacted." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 15, 382 P.3d at 122 (quoting *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. at 136, 791 P.2d at 681). However, the Court explained that the absurdity doctrine permits courts to avoid absurd results that the legislature could not have intended:

> "When construing a statute, we presume that the legislature did not intend absurd results. If applying the statute literally would produce an absurd result, we construe the statute to avoid that result if a reasonable construction exists."

Id. (internal citations and quotations omitted).

The *McLaughlin* court found that a reasonable construction exists: reading "man" to include spouses regardless of gender. This construction avoids the absurd result of denying children of same-sex marriages the legal protections of having two parents—protections that the legislature presumptively intended when it enacted the presumption of parentage. Id. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

Turner's argument that the legislature's use of gender-specific language is "deliberate" and must be given effect was considered and rejected in *McLaughlin*. The Court recognized that the legislature enacted the statute in a different era, before same-sex marriage was recognized, and could not have contemplated its application to same-sex couples. Applying the statute gender-neutrally honors legislative intent to protect parent-child relationships rather than subverting it.

### C. The Equitable Estoppel Analysis Independently Supports the Ruling Below

Even if this Court were to find error in the statutory interpretation analysis— which it should not—the family court's alternative holding that Turner is equitably estopped from denying Oakley's parental status independently supports affirmance.

The doctrine of equitable estoppel applies when: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends the other to act upon those facts; (3) the other party is ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the other party relies on the facts to their detriment. *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 19, 382 P.3d at 123.

The record amply supports the family court's findings on each element.

**First**, Turner knew the facts. She knew that Oakley was not the biological mother of C.T. and that no formal adoption had occurred. She also knew that she had represented to Oakley, to medical providers, and to the public that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent.

**Second**, Turner intended Oakley to act upon these facts. She included Oakley in the fertility process, allowed Oakley to attend medical appointments, listed Oakley on the birth certificate, and executed a will naming Oakley as custodian of the child. Turner argues that equitable estoppel "cannot create a statutory presumption where none exists." This argument mischaracterizes the doctrine. Equitable estoppel does not "create" a statutory presumption; rather, it prevents a party from denying the existence of a parental relationship that she herself created and acknowledged. As *McLaughlin* explained:

> "[W]e hold that [the biological mother] is equitably estopped from disputing that her wife is the legal parent of the child... [The biological mother] held her wife out as a parent, benefited from her wife's assumption of parental duties, and agreed that her wife would be a co-parent."

240 Ariz. at 566-67 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 123-24.

The facts here are even more compelling than in *McLaughlin*. Turner cannot now deny the parental status she so vigorously conferred.

Turner's argument that the family court "need not address equitable estoppel" because the presumption is "inapplicable as a matter of law" puts the cart before the horse. The family court found that the presumption applies, and that holding is correct under *McLaughlin*. As the Arizona Supreme Court held in *State v. The legislature did not use gender-neutral terms like “person” or “spouse”; it used “man” and “father”—terms that inherently denote maleness.  

This Court’s prior decision in *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. 132, 791 P.2d 677 (Ct. App. 1990), directly controls. In *K.A.S.*, the court confronted a statute referring to “husband” and explicitly rejected efforts to read it gender-neutrally: “The legislature used the term husband in the statute, and we cannot ignore that language.” Id. at 136, 791 P.2d at 681. The court emphasized that “courts lack authority to amend a statute to correct perceived defects or to achieve policy goals the legislature has not enacted.” Id. The same principle applies here: the legislature used “man” and “father,” not “parent” or “spouse,” and this Court must enforce that language as written.  

### II. *McLaughlin v. Jones* Is Not Binding Precedent and Was Wrongly Decided  
The defense insists *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), binds this Court. But that premise collapses under basic principles of stare decisis. The Arizona Supreme Court granted review in *McLaughlin* on April 18, 2017, and that review remains pending. As this Court held in *State v. Garcia*, 227 Ariz. 215, 255 P.3d 1066 (App. 2011), “one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by decisions of other panels unless overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or by the Court of Appeals sitting en banc.” A decision under review by the supreme court is not “final” and cannot be binding precedent. See *Garcia*, 227 Ariz. at 219 ¶ 13, 255 P.3d at 1070 (stating that “binding precedent” requires finality). Here, *McLaughlin* is in limbo—its fate rests with the supreme court—and this panel cannot rely on it as authority.  

Even if *McLaughlin* were binding, it was wrongly decided. The *McLaughlin* court claimed that applying A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) literally would produce “absurd results” by denying children of same-sex couples “the legal protections of having two parents.” 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. But the absurdity doctrine does not permit courts to rewrite statutes to fix perceived policy gaps. As *Hansen* instructs, courts must apply unambiguous language even if the result seems unfair to some. 215 Ariz. at 291 ¶ 14, 160 P.3d at 170. The legislature could have intended the presumption to apply only to men based on historical context—when the statute was enacted, same-sex marriage was unrecognized, and paternity laws universally assumed male-female relationships. That is not “absurd”; it is a policy choice for the legislature. *K.A.S.* squarely rejected judicial rewriting for this very reason: “Courts cannot amend a statute to correct perceived defects or to achieve policy goals the legislature has not enacted.” 164 Ariz. at 136, 791 P.2d at 681.  

### III. Judicial Rewriting Violates Separation of Powers  
The defense argues that applying the statute gender-neutrally avoids “absurd results,” but this is a classic separation-of-powers violation. Courts may not “amend a statute to correct perceived defects” or “achieve policy goals the legislature has not enacted.” *K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. at 136, 791 P.2d at 681. The legislature—not this Court—must decide whether to update A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to include same-sex spouses. If the legislature intended gender-neutral application, it would have used neutral terms like “parent” or “spouse,” as it has in other statutes. See, e.g., A.R.S. § 25-807 (defining “parent” without gendered language). Instead, the legislature chose “man” and “father”—and this Court must respect that choice.  

### IV. Equitable Estoppel Cannot Salvage the Error  
Because the presumption of paternity under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) is inapplicable as a matter of law to Oakley, this Court need not—and should not—reach equitable estoppel. Equitable estoppel is a doctrine that prevents a party from denying a representation they made, but it cannot create a statutory presumption where none exists. *See In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. at 136, 791 P.2d at 681 (holding courts cannot “create” rights through estoppel when the statute does not authorize them). Here, the statute explicitly limits the presumption to men, so Oakley cannot claim it merely because Turner represented she was a co-parent. To hold otherwise would allow courts to rewrite statutes under the guise of equity—a result the law forbids.  

### Conclusion  
The family court erred by applying A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)—a statute that says “a man is presumed to be the father”—to Oakley, a woman. The plain language is unambiguous, *McLaughlin* is not binding precedent, and judicial rewriting violates separation of powers. Because the presumption is inapplicable, equitable estoppel cannot save the ruling. This Court must reverse the family court’s decision and hold that Oakley has no presumption of parentage under the statute. We respectfully request reversal.

Arizona courts recognize that when literal application of statutory language produces absurd results that the legislature could not have intended, courts may interpret the statute more broadly to avoid such results. *State v. Arizona courts apply equitable estoppel when: (1) the party to be estopped knows the facts; (2) the party intends the other to act upon those facts; (3) the other party is ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the other party relies on the facts to their detriment. *McLaughlin v. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on a petition for special action review regarding the family court’s determination of parental rights following the dissolution of the marriage between Turner and Oakley. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Binding Precedent and the Status of *McLaughlin***

The family court relied on *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), to hold that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies gender-neutrally. Turner argues that *McLaughlin* is not binding precedent because the Arizona Supreme Court has granted review in that matter. This argument is unpersuasive.

Under the doctrine of *stare decisis*, "one panel of the Court of Appeals is bound by decisions of another panel unless overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or by the Court of Appeals sitting en banc." *State v. Garcia*, 227 Ariz. 215, 219 ¶ 13, 255 P.3d 1066, 1070 (App. 2011). The mere grant of review by the Arizona Supreme Court does not constitute an overruling of the Court of Appeals' decision. Until the Supreme Court issues a decision reversing *McLaughlin*, the opinion remains the law of this jurisdiction and binds this panel. The defense correctly notes that to hold otherwise would create instability in the law, allowing parties to challenge settled precedent merely by seeking higher court review. Therefore, the family court did not err in relying on *McLaughlin*.

**B. Application of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)**

Turner argues that the plain language of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), which presumes a "man" is the "father" if married to the mother, is unambiguous and precludes application to women. She relies on *State v. Hansen*, 215 Ariz. 287, 160 P.3d 166 (2007), for the proposition that unambiguous statutory language must be applied as written, and *In re Marriage of K.A.S.*, 164 Ariz. 132, 791 P.2d 677 (Ct. App. 1990), for the proposition that courts cannot rewrite gender-specific terms.

The *McLaughlin* court acknowledged the gendered language but applied the absurdity doctrine, reasoning that applying the statute only to men would lead to absurd results—specifically, depriving a child born to a married same-sex couple of the legal protections of having two parents. *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The court further noted that following the legalization of same-sex marriage in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 690 (2015), the legislature could not have intended to exclude children of same-sex marriages from the presumption of parentage. *McLaughlin*, 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122.

Turner invites this Court to disregard *McLaughlin* and follow *K.A.S.*, but *McLaughlin* is the more recent and directly on-point authority regarding the specific statute at issue, A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1). Furthermore, *McLaughlin* distinguished or implicitly rejected the strict textualist approach Turner advocates for in this specific context. As a panel bound by the decisions of other panels, this Court must follow *McLaughlin* and apply the presumption of parentage gender-neutrally.

**C. Equitable Estoppel**

Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 566-67 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 123-24.
