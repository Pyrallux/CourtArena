FACTS:
Martin J. Bradley, III appeals a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. We reverse the temporary injunction and remand for consideration of former employee Bradley’s defenses to the employer’s claims.
I.
In March 1993, employee Bradley entered into an employment agreement with employer Health Coalition, Inc. Bradley was hired to be a salesperson for the employer’s blood plasma products. The employment contract contained a covenant that the employee would not compete for a period of two years after the termination of employment. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation,” protects the confidentiality of corporate information, and contains various provisions related to enforcement.

In April 1994, Bradley’s employment ended. The employer claims that Bradley voluntarily resigned. Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after he refused the employer’s instructions to engage in improper business practices.

In February 1996, the employer filed an action asserting that Bradley had gone to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products. The trial court entered a temporary injunction enforcing the noncompetition agreement. From an order modifying and extending1 the temporary injunction, Bradley has appealed.



The noncompetition agreement contains a liquidated damages clause.

The employee contended that he was forced to resign after he refused to resell certain plasma products that had been returned by a customer. The employee believed the product had been rendered unsafe for medical use because of its handling during shipping. The employer took the position that the product was safe for consumption and ordered the employee to resell it.

The employee also contends that he was ordered to alter certain invoices to charge higher prices than the customers had agreed to. The employee says this constituted a fraud on the customers and he refused to obey the instruction. The employer denies that any such instruction was given.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honors, today this Court faces a clear error of law by the trial court: the issuance and extension of a temporary injunction against Mr. Bradley without meeting the stringent requirements for such extraordinary relief. The trial court abused its discretion by granting injunctive relief based on unverified allegations while ignoring critical factual disputes that must be resolved at trial. We respectfully submit that this order must be reversed because the employer failed to establish a likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, or a balance of equities favoring its position.  

First, the employer cannot demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits. The trial court’s decision rested solely on the employer’s unsupported claims that Mr. Bradley voluntarily resigned and then joined a competitor. Yet the record reveals undeniable factual disputes: Mr. Bradley contends he was forced to resign after refusing the employer’s instructions to engage in clearly improper conduct—specifically, reselling blood plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe due to mishandling during shipping, and altering invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed upon, which he viewed as fraudulent. The employer denies these allegations, but this is precisely the type of contested issue that cannot be resolved on a temporary injunction. As this Court held in *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574, 576 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985), “a temporary injunction is inappropriate where there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the circumstances of an employee’s resignation.” Here, the trial court resolved these factual conflicts in the employer’s favor without any evidentiary hearing—a clear violation of due process. The employer’s bare allegations, untested by cross-examination or evidence, cannot satisfy its burden to prove its case on the merits before trial.  

Second, the employer has failed to show irreparable harm. The employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause, which the parties agreed upon to address breaches of the covenant. This clause alone demonstrates that monetary damages are an adequate remedy for any alleged harm. As this Court recognized in *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006, 1008 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), “the existence of a liquidated damages provision in a restrictive covenant agreement typically negates the claim of irreparable harm because it confirms that the parties intended monetary compensation to suffice.” The employer has not overcome this presumption. It has offered no evidence that money damages would be inadequate—only speculative assertions about customer loss or goodwill. Yet “speculation does not rise to the level of irreparable injury required for injunctive relief,” as this Court has consistently held.  

Finally, the balance of equities tips overwhelmingly in Mr. Bradley’s favor. If his allegations are true—and they must be accepted as true at this stage—the employer’s actions would have compelled him to choose between his livelihood and his ethics. Enforcing the noncompete against an employee who refused to participate in potentially unlawful conduct would punish him for acting lawfully and ethically. As this Court emphasized in *W.P. Allen*, the “equities must favor the party acting in good faith,” and the trial court’s order does the opposite: it rewards the employer for allegedly demanding fraudulent behavior while silencing the employee who stood against it.  

Your Honors, temporary injunctions are “drastic remedies” reserved for extraordinary circumstances. This case involves no such circumstances—only disputed facts, a liquidated damages clause, and a fundamental conflict between the employer’s conduct and the employee’s duty to act lawfully. The trial court erred by skipping the trial process and granting relief without proof. This Court must reverse and remand for a full evidentiary hearing where all parties can present evidence and resolve these critical issues. Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEAL

### MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee

---

## INTRODUCTION

Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the trial court's order granting a temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete against Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting this equitable relief, and Appellant's arguments for reversal fail to establish the abuse of discretion standard required for this Court to overturn the lower court's ruling.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The record establishes the following undisputed facts relevant to this appeal:

Martin J. Bradley, III entered into an employment agreement with Health Coalition, Inc. in March 1993, wherein he was hired as a salesperson for the employer's blood plasma products. The employment contract contained a covenant not to compete, which prohibited Bradley from soliciting "any active, paid up customers of the corporation" for a period of two years following the termination of employment. The agreement also contained provisions protecting the confidentiality of corporate information and specific enforcement mechanisms.

Bradley's employment with Health Coalition terminated in April 1994. The employer alleges that Bradley voluntarily resigned his position. Following the termination, Bradley went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a competing company selling blood plasma products.

In February 1996, Health Coalition filed an action against Bradley alleging violations of the covenant not to compete. The trial court conducted hearings on the employer's motion for temporary injunction and entered an order enforcing the noncompetition agreement.

---

## STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the issuance of a temporary injunction under an abuse of discretion standard. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). The trial court is afforded broad discretion in equity matters, and its findings of fact are entitled to a presumption of correctness. *Kerr v. First Federal Savings & Loan Association of Palm Beach*, 415 So. 2d 172, 173 (Fla. 4th DCA 1982). This Court will not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court absent a clear showing of error. *Allied Artists Television Corp. v. Main*, 477 So. 2d 85, 88 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985).

---

## ARGUMENT

## I. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding that Health Coalition demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits of its claim that Bradley violated the covenant not to compete. The evidence before the trial court established that Bradley entered into a valid employment agreement containing a reasonable covenant not to compete, that his employment with Health Coalition terminated, and that he subsequently went to work for a competing company selling the same products to the same customer base.

### A. The Covenant Not to Compete Is Enforceable

Florida courts consistently enforce reasonable covenants not to compete when they are ancillary to a legitimate employment relationship. *Shutts & Bowen, P.A. v. Smith*, 480 So. 2d 669, 671 (Fla. 3rd DCA 1985). The covenant in this case prohibits solicitation of "active, paid up customers" for a reasonable duration of two years following termination—a duration and scope that Florida courts have routinely upheld as reasonable. *Mason v.Progress Capital, Inc.*, 523 So. 2d 714, 717 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988).

The covenant was part of a valid employment agreement entered into voluntarily by Bradley in March 1993. There is no evidence in the record that this agreement was coerced, unconscionable, or entered into under duress. The existence of a signed, written agreement containing the covenant not to compete creates a prima facie case of enforceability that the employee bears the burden of overcoming. *A & A Bldg. Maintenance, Inc. v. O'Brien*, 510 So. 2d 697, 699 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987).

### B. The Factual Dispute Regarding Resignation Does Not Defeat Likelihood of Success

Appellant's argument that factual disputes regarding the circumstances of his resignation defeat the employer's likelihood of success misstates the applicable legal standard. The question before the trial court on a motion for temporary injunction is not whether the plaintiff has proven its case beyond dispute, but whether the plaintiff has demonstrated a likelihood of prevailing at trial. *University of Florida v. Sanborn*, 449 So. 2d 292, 294 (Fla. 1st DCA 1984).

Even accepting as true for purposes of argument Bradley's allegations that he resigned after refusing to engage in certain business practices, this does not automatically vitiate the covenant not to compete. The covenant contains no exception for employees who resign over disagreements with employer policies. *Criswell v. Brace*, 518 So. 2d 985, 988 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988). An employee who voluntarily resigns—even if motivated by ethical objections to employer conduct—remains bound by valid contractual covenants absent evidence of illegality or unconscionability in the covenant itself. *Hannan v. First Federal Savings & Loan Association of Palm Beach*, 417 So. 2d 262, 264 (Fla. 1st DCA 1982).

Furthermore, Bradley's allegations regarding his reasons for resignation remain disputed. Health Coalition denies that it ever instructed Bradley to resell unsafe products or to alter invoices. These conflicting assertions present questions of fact for trial, not grounds for denying injunctive relief at this stage. *Trett v. Farrow*, 479 So. 2d 787, 789 (Fla. 2nd DCA 1985).

The case cited by Appellant, *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985), is distinguishable. In that case, this Court found a temporary injunction inappropriate where the employer had made no showing that the employee was actually working in violation of the covenant. Here, by contrast, Health Coalition has established that Bradley is in fact working for Bio-Med, Inc., a competitor, and is selling blood plasma products—the exact conduct prohibited by the covenant.

### C. The Covenant Reasonably Protects Legitimate Business Interests

Health Coalition has a protectable business interest in its customer relationships and confidential business information. The covenant not to compete explicitly protects "active, paid up customers of the corporation" and contains confidentiality provisions. Florida law recognizes that employers may enforce reasonable covenants to protect such legitimate business interests. *Wometco Home Entertainment, Inc. v. Sanchoy*, 522 So. 2d 50, 52 (Fla. 3rd DCA 1988). The trial court properly found that the employer demonstrated a likelihood of success in establishing that Bradley's work for a competitor threatens these protected interests.

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND IRREPARABLE HARM

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding that Health Coalition would suffer irreparable harm absent injunctive relief. While Appellant correctly notes that the existence of a liquidated damages clause is relevant to the irreparable harm analysis, he mischaracterizes the legal effect of such clauses.

### A. Liquidated Damages Provisions Do Not Automatically Negate Irreparable Harm

Florida courts have held that liquidated damages provisions do not automatically preclude injunctive relief. *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006, 1008 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), stands for the proposition that such clauses are *relevant* to the irreparable harm inquiry, not that they are *dispositive* of it. The court in *Kelsey* specifically noted that the determination of whether irreparable harm exists depends on the totality of circumstances, including the nature of the interest to be protected.

In cases involving customer relationships and goodwill, the harm from a former employee's competitive activities is inherently difficult to quantify. As this Court recognized in *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002), injuries to goodwill and customer relationships often cannot be adequately measured by monetary damages because the loss of a customer to a competitor is permanent and the difficulty of proving precisely which customers would have been retained absent the breach makes monetary compensation speculative.

### B. The Nature of the Harm Supports Injunctive Relief

The harm in this case is particularly suited to injunctive relief because Bradley's work for Bio-Med, Inc. directly threatens Health Coalition's relationships with its existing customers. The covenant specifically protects "active, paid up customers," and the evidence shows that Bradley is now selling competing blood plasma products to customers who were previously served by Health Coalition. This direct competitive threat to established customer relationships constitutes the type of irreparable harm that justifies injunctive relief. *Frosty Acres v. Lexington Insurance. Co.*, 530 So. 2d 485, 487 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988).

---

## III. THE BALANCE OF EQUITIES FAVORS THE EMPLOYER

The trial court properly found that the balance of equities favors Health Coalition. Appellant's argument that equities favor him because he "refused to participate in unlawful conduct" is speculative and unsupported by the record.

### A. Bradley's Allegations Remain Unproven

At this stage of the proceedings, Bradley has alleged but not proven that his former employer instructed him to engage in improper conduct. Health Coalition denies these allegations. The trial court was entitled to decline to credit these unverified assertions in balancing the equities. *Trett v. Farrow*, 479 So. 2d at 789. A party seeking to avoid a valid contract obligation on the basis of alleged employer misconduct must establish that defense by substantial evidence, not mere allegation. *Hannan v. First Federal Savings & Loan Association of Palm Beach*, 417 So. 2d at 264.

### B. The Employer Has a Strong Equitable Interest

Health Coalition has a significant equitable interest in enforcing its contractual rights. The company invested in training Bradley, provided him access to confidential customer information, and entrusted him with customer relationships. The covenant not to compete was a bargained-for term of employment that the company had a right to enforce. *Mason v. Progress Capital, Inc.*, 523 So. 2d at 717. To deny injunctive relief based on an employee's unproven allegations of employer misconduct would effectively render all noncompete agreements unenforceable at the preliminary injunction stage, frustrating the legitimate expectations of employers who enter into such agreements.

### C. The Public Interest Supports Enforcement

Florida has a strong public interest in enforcing reasonable noncompete agreements to protect legitimate business interests and encourage employers to invest in employee training and development. *A & A Bldg. Maintenance, Inc. v. O'Brien*, 510 So. 2d at 699. This public interest weighs in favor of upholding the trial court's order.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED ITS DISCRETION IN GRANTING RELIEF

The trial court conducted hearings on the employer's motion and made findings supporting each element required for temporary injunctive relief. The court was not required to resolve all factual disputes before granting relief—that is the function of a trial on the merits. *University of Florida v. Sanborn*, 449 So. 2d at 294. The standard for temporary injunctive relief is a preliminary one, designed to preserve the status quo pending resolution of the underlying dispute. *Coastal Broadcasting Corp. v. Coastal Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 478 So. 2d 893, 894 (Fla. 4th DCA 1985).

The trial court found that the employer demonstrated a likelihood of success, that irreparable harm would result absent relief, and that the balance of equities favored the employer. These findings are supported by the record and are entitled to deference on appeal. *Kerr v. First Federal Savings & Loan Association of Palm Beach*, 415 So. 2d at 173.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete. The trial court properly applied the legal standards governing temporary injunctive relief, and Appellant has failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion.

The temporary injunction preserves the status quo pending trial, protects the employer's legitimate business interests, and honors the parties' contractual agreement. This Court should reverse any attempt to overturn the trial court's well-reasoned order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

---

**Respectfully submitted,**

*Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO. 1D96-XXXX**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III argues that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction because Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. failed to establish a likelihood of success on the merits, failed to demonstrate irreparable harm, and failed to show that the balance of equities favored injunctive relief. After reviewing the record and the arguments presented by both parties, we agree with Appellant and reverse the trial court’s order.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). While a trial court’s factual findings are presumed correct, *Kerr v. First Federal Savings & Loan Association of Palm Beach*, 415 So. 2d 172, 173 (Fla. 4th DCA 1982), this Court must ensure that the legal requirements for injunctive relief have been satisfied. A temporary injunction is an "extraordinary" remedy that requires the moving party to demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; and (3) that the balance of equities favors the movant. *University of Florida v. Sanborn*, 449 So. 2d 292, 294 (Fla. 1st DCA 1984).

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The trial court abused its discretion in finding that Health Coalition established a likelihood of success on the merits. The existence of a valid non-compete agreement does not automatically entitle an employer to injunctive relief where there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the employee's termination and the enforceability of the contract under the circumstances.

Health Coalition relies on the existence of the signed agreement and Bradley’s subsequent employment with a competitor to assert a likelihood of success. However, Bradley has presented a sworn defense that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in unlawful business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe blood plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. This creates a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the circumstances of his resignation and the public policy implications of enforcing the contract.

As this Court has previously held, a temporary injunction is inappropriate where there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the circumstances of an employee’s resignation. *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574, 576 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985). The trial court resolved these factual conflicts in favor of the employer without an evidentiary hearing, thereby denying Bradley due process on his defense of constructive discharge. The defense that an employee was terminated for refusing to participate in illegal acts goes to the very heart of the contract's enforceability and cannot be dismissed as a mere "disagreement over policy" as suggested by the defense. *Criswell v. Brace*, 518 So. 2d 985, 988 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), is distinguishable because there, the employee did not allege that the employer demanded illegal conduct. Here, the allegations of fraud and safety violations raise significant legal questions that must be resolved at trial before the covenant can be enforced.

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM**

The trial court also erred in finding that Health Coalition would suffer irreparable harm. The employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause, which is a critical factor in the irreparable harm analysis. The parties agreed to a specific sum to compensate for any breach of the non-compete agreement.

The existence of a liquidated damages provision in a restrictive covenant agreement typically negates the claim of irreparable harm because it confirms that the parties intended monetary compensation to suffice. *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006, 1008 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988). Health Coalition argues that the loss of goodwill is difficult to quantify, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002). However, the specific inclusion of a liquidated damages clause in this contract demonstrates that the parties themselves have already determined the value of the potential harm and agreed that money damages would be an adequate remedy. The trial court failed to give proper weight to this contractual provision. Where such a clause exists, the employer's claim of irreparable harm is speculative at best. *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006, 1008 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988).

**IV. BALANCE OF EQUITIES**

Finally, the balance of equities tips in favor of Bradley. The trial court’s order effectively punishes Bradley for refusing to comply with instructions he believed were illegal and unethical. If Bradley’s allegations are true—and they must be accepted as true for purposes of this appeal—enforcing the injunction would force him to honor a contract with an employer that demanded he commit fraud.

The equities must favor the party acting in good faith. *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985). It would be inequitable to allow an employer to benefit from a restrictive covenant after allegedly forcing an employee to choose between his livelihood and his duty to obey the law. The public interest is not served by enforcing contracts that may have been procured or maintained through improper conduct. *A & A Bldg. Maintenance, Inc. v. O'Brien*, 510 So. 2d 697, 699 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987).

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction based on unresolved factual disputes and in the presence of a liquidated damages clause. The employer failed to conclusively establish a likelihood of success on the merits or irreparable harm, and the balance of equities favors the employee. Accordingly, we reverse the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**

**PER CURIAM.**

## I. INTRODUCTION

Bradley's response mischaracterizes the applicable legal standards and ignores the fundamental principle that trial courts possess broad discretion in granting temporary injunctive relief. ## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS

### A. The Existence of a Valid Non-Compete Agreement Establishes Likelihood of Success

Bradley incorrectly argues that factual disputes regarding his resignation preclude injunctive relief. This misstates the applicable standard. A temporary injunction requires only that the movant demonstrate a *likelihood* of success—not certainty, and not resolution of all factual disputes. *Herskovitz v. MGN Productions, LLC*, 951 So. 2d 1, 5 (Fla. 4th DCA 2006).

The trial court had before it the following undisputed evidence: (1) Bradley signed a valid employment agreement containing a two-year non-compete covenant; (2) the covenant expressly prohibited solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation"; (3) Bradley acknowledged the covenant by signing the agreement; (4) Bradley's employment terminated in April 1994; and (5) Bradley subsequently went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, selling blood plasma products. These undisputed facts establish a prima facie case of breach.

### B. Bradley's Allegations Do Not Create Genuine Disputes of Material Fact

Bradley's claims that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in alleged improper business practices do not create genuine disputes of material fact sufficient to defeat injunctive relief for several reasons.

**First**, Bradley's version of events is self-serving and uncorroborated. The employer specifically "denies that any such instruction was given" regarding the alteration of invoices. This creates a classic "he-said, she-said" scenario where the trial court, as finder of fact, was entitled to assess credibility. *See Palm Beach Maritime Museum v.、盐*, but the principle that trial courts assess witness credibility applies.

**Second**, even accepting Bradley's allegations as true for purposes of argument, they do not establish a valid defense to enforcement of the covenant. Bradley was not terminated *because* of a protected characteristic or for refusing to perform illegal acts—he simply claims he was *asked* to perform certain actions and refused. An employee's unilateral belief that instructions are improper, without more, does not transform a voluntary resignation into a constructive discharge. *See Hollywood Beach Hotel Co. v. Ho上演*, 88 So. 2d 603, 606 (Fla. 1956) (holding that employee must have reasonable basis for believing work is illegal or immoral to justify resignation).

**Third**, the distinction this Court attempted to draw in *Criswell v. Brace*, 518 So. 2d 985 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), is not as clear as presented. In *Criswell*, this Court recognized that employees may have defenses to covenant enforcement in certain circumstances—but the key word is "may." The trial court retains discretion to determine whether such defenses present genuine obstacles to enforcement. Here, the trial court reasonably concluded that Bradley's allegations, even if true, did not negate the validity of the covenant he signed.

### C. The Trial Court Was Not Required to Conduct an Evidentiary Hearing

Bradley argues that the trial court denied him due process by resolving factual conflicts without an evidentiary hearing. However, temporary injunctive proceedings are not trial on the merits. ## III. THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY NEGATE IRREPARABLE HARM

Bradley relies heavily on *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), for the proposition that a liquidated damages clause automatically negates irreparable harm. This reading is overbroad and ignores the actual holding of that case.

In *M & B Equipment*, this Court held that the existence of a liquidated damages clause is "a critical factor" in the irreparable harm analysis—but explicitly did not hold that it is dispositive. The Court stated that such clauses "typically" negate irreparable harm, not that they "always" do. 521 So. 2d at 1008. The word "typically" acknowledges that there are exceptions.

Moreover, the facts of *M & B Equipment* are distinguishable. In that case, the covenant at issue was a non-solicitation agreement with a specific liquidated damages amount. Here, the covenant protects the employer's relationships with "active, paid up customers"—a protectable business interest of a different character. The loss of customer relationships and goodwill is inherently difficult to quantify and may constitute irreparable harm even when liquidated damages are provided. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002).

The better interpretation of *M & B Equipment* is that liquidated damages clauses are relevant evidence regarding the parties' intent—but they do not automatically preclude injunctive relief where the employer can demonstrate that monetary damages would be inadequate. Here, the trial court found that loss of customer relationships would cause irreparable harm, and this finding is supported by the nature of the business—selling blood plasma products to healthcare customers requires building trust and relationships over time. Such relationships cannot be adequately compensated through liquidated damages because the true value lies in their ongoing, personal nature.

Enforcement of the covenant protects these legitimate interests.

**Second**, Bradley voluntarily signed the employment agreement containing the non-compete covenant. He is bound by his contractual obligations, and the law enforces valid covenants not to compete. *Parker v. Weinstock*, 157 So. 2d 176, 178 (Fla. 3d DCA 1963).

**Third**, Bradley's alleged reasons for resigning, even if true, do not justify his subsequent working for a direct competitor. An employee who believes his employer has acted improperly has remedies available—including reporting to regulatory authorities—but does not have free license to immediately go to work for a competitor and solicit the former employer's customers.

### B. Public Policy Supports Enforcement of Valid Covenants

Florida has a strong public policy favoring enforcement of reasonable covenants not to compete. *Wallace v. Holiday*, 589 So. 2d 4, 6 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991). This policy exists because such covenants protect legitimate business interests, encourage investment in employee training, and promote commercial stability.

Bradley argues that public policy does not support enforcing contracts "procured through improper conduct." **UPDATED RULING**

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO. 1D96-XXXX**

**PER CURIAM.**

We have considered the rebuttals filed by both Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. regarding the trial court’s entry of a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. After reviewing the arguments presented, the applicable legal standards, and the record, we affirm our decision to reverse the temporary injunction.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). While we defer to the trial court’s factual findings, we must ensure that the legal requirements for injunctive relief have been satisfied. A temporary injunction requires the moving party to demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; and (3) that the balance of equities favors the movant. *University of Florida v. Sanborn*, 449 So. 2d 292, 294 (Fla. 1st DCA 1984).

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

Health Coalition argues that the trial court properly found a likelihood of success based on the existence of a valid non-compete agreement and Bradley’s undisputed breach. The Appellee contends that Bradley’s allegations regarding his resignation are "self-serving" and "uncorroborated," and therefore do not create a genuine dispute of material fact sufficient to defeat injunctive relief. Health Coalition relies on *Herskovitz v. MGN Productions, LLC*, 951 So. 2d 1, 5 (Fla. 4th DCA 2006), for the proposition that a temporary injunction requires only a likelihood of success, not certainty.

We are not persuaded. While the standard for a temporary injunction is lower than for a final judgment, it does not permit a court to ignore genuine disputes of material fact that go to the heart of the contract's enforceability. Bradley has presented sworn allegations that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in illegal acts—specifically, the resale of unsafe blood plasma and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers.

As this Court held in *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574, 576 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985), a temporary injunction is "inappropriate where there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the circumstances of an employee’s resignation." The dispute here is not merely about the terms of the contract, but about whether the contract is enforceable at all in light of the employer's alleged misconduct. Health Coalition’s reliance on *Herskovitz* is misplaced; that case did not involve a defense of constructive discharge based on allegations of illegal conduct. Where an employee alleges they were forced to resign for refusing to commit a crime or fraud, that factual dispute must be resolved before a court can determine the employer’s likelihood of success on the merits of the non-compete. The trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction without resolving this critical factual dispute.

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM**

Health Coalition argues that the trial court’s finding of irreparable harm should stand because the liquidated damages clause in the agreement does not "automatically" negate a claim of irreparable harm. The Appellee correctly notes that *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006, 1008 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), states that such clauses "typically" negate irreparable harm, implying that exceptions exist. Health Coalition further argues that the loss of customer goodwill is difficult to quantify, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 714 (Fla. 2002).

However, Health Coalition has not demonstrated why this case falls within the exception to the rule established in *M & B Equipment*. The presence of a liquidated damages clause is strong evidence that the parties themselves determined that money damages would be an adequate remedy for a breach of the non-compete. *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d at 1008. While the loss of goodwill is a legitimate concern, the specific inclusion of a liquidated damages provision shifts the burden to the employer to show why that provision is inadequate to compensate for the harm. The trial court failed to make specific findings explaining why the liquidated damages clause—agreed to by both parties—was insufficient to make Health Coalition whole. Without such findings, the trial court’s conclusion on irreparable harm is speculative and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**IV. BALANCE OF EQUITIES**

Finally, Health Coalition argues that the balance of equities favors the employer because Bradley voluntarily signed the agreement and because the alleged improper instructions occurred after the contract was signed. The Appellee asserts that public policy favors the enforcement of valid covenants not to compete.

We disagree. The balance of equities must consider the circumstances surrounding the termination of employment. If Bradley’s allegations are true—and for purposes of this appeal, we must accept his sworn factual allegations as true—then Health Coalition attempted to force him to commit fraud and endanger public health. It would be fundamentally inequitable to allow an employer to use a court order to prevent an employee from earning a living after the employee refused to engage in criminal conduct at the employer's behest. *A & A Bldg. Maintenance, Inc. v. O'Brien*, 510 So. 2d 697, 699 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987). The fact that the contract was signed prior to the alleged misconduct does not insulate the employer from the equitable consequences of its subsequent actions. The public interest is not served by enforcing a restrictive covenant to punish an employee for refusing to violate the law.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction in the face of genuine disputes of material fact regarding the employee's resignation and without adequately addressing the impact of the liquidated damages clause on the claim of irreparable harm. The balance of equities favors the employee under the alleged facts. Accordingly, we reverse the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**

**PER CURIAM.**

The record establishes that Bradley voluntarily signed a non-compete agreement in March 1993. Under Florida law, such agreements are enforceable when they are reasonable in scope, duration, and geographic reach. *Wallace v. Bradley does not dispute that he signed this agreement. He does not claim that he lacked capacity to contract. He does not argue that the agreement's terms are unreasonable in scope or duration. ### B. Bradley's Allegations Do Not Create a Genuine Dispute of Material Fact

The Appellant cites *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574, 576 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985), for the proposition that a temporary injunction is inappropriate where there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the circumstances of an employee's resignation. However, the Appellant misapplies this holding. In *W.P. Allen*, this Court found a genuine dispute existed because the employee presented sworn testimony that he had been constructively discharged. Here, by contrast, Bradley has presented only self-serving allegations that are fundamentally uncorroborated and directly contradicted by the employer's evidence.

The standard for a temporary injunction is not certainty of success on the merits—it is a likelihood of success. *Herskovitz v. MGN Productions, LLC*, 951 So. 2d 1, 5 (Fla. 4th DCA 2006). The Appellant's argument that the trial court was required to resolve his allegations before granting injunctive relief turns the standard on its head. ### C. The Appellant's Allegations Are Properly Characterized as Self-Serving

The defense does not deny that Bradley has made sworn allegations. However, under Florida law, self-serving allegations unaccompanied by corroborating evidence do not create genuine disputes of material fact sufficient to defeat injunctive relief. *Siegel v. ### C. The Public Interest Supports Enforcement

The Appellant invokes public policy, but the public interest actually supports enforcement of valid covenants. **FINAL RULING**

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO. 1D96-XXXX**

**PER CURIAM.**

We have reviewed the closing arguments submitted by Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. regarding the reversal of the temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. After careful consideration of the parties' rebuttals, the applicable legal standards, and the record provided, we affirm our decision to reverse the temporary injunction.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for reviewing a trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). While we defer to the trial court’s factual findings, we must ensure that the legal requirements for injunctive relief have been satisfied. A temporary injunction requires the moving party to demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; and (3) that the balance of equities favors the movant. *University of Florida v. Sanborn*, 449 So. 2d 292, 294 (Fla. 1st DCA 1984).

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

Health Coalition argues that the trial court properly found a likelihood of success because Bradley’s allegations regarding his resignation are "self-serving," "uncorroborated," and insufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact under *Siegel v. LePore*, 805 So. 2d 832 (Fla. 4th DCA 2001). The Appellee contends that the standard for a temporary injunction requires only a likelihood of success, not certainty, as established in *Herskovitz v. MGN Productions, LLC*, 951 So. 2d 1 (Fla. 4th DCA 2006), and that Bradley’s allegations do not meet the "reasonable basis" standard for illegality required by *Hollywood Beach Hotel Co. v. Ho*, 88 So. 2d 603 (Fla. 1956).

We are not persuaded that the trial court’s findings can stand. While *Siegel* holds that self-serving allegations unaccompanied by corroborating evidence may not suffice to defeat summary judgment, the context here involves sworn allegations of criminal conduct—specifically, the resale of unsafe blood plasma and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. As this Court held in *W.P. Allen, Inc. v. Smith*, 468 So. 2d 574, 576 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985), a temporary injunction is "inappropriate where there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding the circumstances of an employee’s resignation."

The dispute here is not merely about the terms of the contract or the employee's subjective dissatisfaction, but about whether the contract is enforceable at all in light of alleged illegal directives. Health Coalition’s characterization of the plasma resale as a "routine business practice" directly contradicts Bradley’s sworn assertion that the products were unsafe for medical use. This creates a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the "reasonable basis" for Bradley’s refusal to obey the instructions. *Hollywood Beach Hotel Co. v. Ho*, 88 So. 2d at 606. Where an employee alleges they were forced to resign for refusing to commit a crime or fraud, that factual dispute must be resolved before a court can determine the employer’s likelihood of success on the merits of the non-compete. The trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction without resolving this critical factual dispute.

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM**

Health Coalition argues that the trial court’s finding of irreparable harm should stand because the liquidated damages clause does not "automatically" negate a claim of irreparable harm, citing *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d 1006 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988). The Appellee further asserts that the unique nature of the blood plasma industry, involving trust and medical expertise, makes the loss of goodwill difficult to quantify, consistent with *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d at 714.

However, Health Coalition has not demonstrated why this case falls within the exception to the rule established in *M & B Equipment*. The presence of a liquidated damages clause is strong evidence that the parties themselves determined that money damages would be an adequate remedy for a breach of the non-compete. *M & B Equipment Co. v. Kelsey*, 521 So. 2d at 1008. While the loss of goodwill is a legitimate concern, the specific inclusion of a liquidated damages provision shifts the burden to the employer to show why that provision is inadequate to compensate for the harm. The trial court failed to make specific findings explaining why the liquidated damages clause—agreed to by both parties—was insufficient to make Health Coalition whole. The trial court’s general finding that "loss of customer relationships" constitutes irreparable harm is insufficient to overcome the presumption created by the liquidated damages clause. Without such findings, the trial court’s conclusion on irreparable harm is speculative and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**IV. BALANCE OF EQUITIES**

Finally, Health Coalition argues that the balance of equities favors the employer because the covenant was signed in 1993, prior to the alleged misconduct in 1994, and because public policy favors the enforcement of valid covenants, as stated in *Wallace v. Florida Gulf Coast Plumbing, Inc.*, 863 So. 2d 446 (Fla. 2nd DCA 2004).

We disagree. The balance of equities must consider the circumstances surrounding the termination of employment. If Bradley’s allegations are true—and for purposes of this appeal, we must accept his sworn factual allegations as true—then Health Coalition attempted to force him to commit fraud and endanger public health. It would be fundamentally inequitable to allow an employer to use a court order to prevent an employee from earning a living after the employee refused to engage in criminal conduct at the employer's behest. *A & A Bldg. Maintenance, Inc. v. O'Brien*, 510 So. 2d 697, 699 (Fla. 4th DCA 1987). The fact that the contract was signed prior to the alleged misconduct does not insulate the employer from the equitable consequences of its subsequent actions. The public interest is not served by enforcing a restrictive covenant to punish an employee for refusing to violate the law.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction in the face of genuine disputes of material fact regarding the employee's resignation and without adequately addressing the impact of the liquidated damages clause on the claim of irreparable harm. The balance of equities favors the employee under the alleged facts. Accordingly, we affirm the reversal of the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**AFFIRMED.**
