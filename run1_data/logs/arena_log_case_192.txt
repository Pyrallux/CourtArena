Your Honors, this case comes before this Court on appeal from the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. The district court correctly held that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not create a preference for admission based on city residency. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the district court's judgment.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: does Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 require charter schools to provide admission preferences to residents of the city in which the charter school is located? The district court answered this question correctly by finding that the statute contains no such requirement.

Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen (collectively, "Appellants") contend that the statute should be read to include a "city residency" preference. This reading, however, requires the Court to add language to the statute that the Legislature did not include. Under Minnesota's well-established principles of statutory interpretation, such judicial legislation is prohibited.

The facts are essentially undisputed. PACT Charter School is located in the City of Ramsey and operates a lottery system for student admissions, as contemplated by statute. Appellant Look's son was selected through this lottery; Appellant Dehen's daughter was not. The sole dispute is whether the statute mandates a preference for Ramsey residents—a question of law that the district court correctly resolved in favor of the School.

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

On appeal from a grant of summary judgment, this Court reviews questions of law de novo. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d 783, 787 (Minn. App. 2008). Summary judgment is appropriate where there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *DL v. Huegel*, 589 N.W.2d 325, 327 (Minn. App. 1999).

Here, there is no dispute regarding any material fact. The only question is the proper interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9—a question of law properly resolved by the district court and now subject to de novo review.

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Does Not Create a City Residency Preference

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that courts must first look to the statute's plain language. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d 586, 589 (Minn. 1999). When the statutory text is clear and unambiguous, the inquiry ends there. *Id.* Courts may not add words to a statute or insert limitations that the Legislature did not see fit to include. *Id.*

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is silent on the matter of city residency. The statute does not mention "city," does not reference "residency," and does not create any preference for residents of the municipality where a charter school is located. The statute's plain text simply does not support the interpretation that Appellants urge upon this Court.

Appellants cannot point to any language in the statute creating a city residency preference because no such language exists. This Court should not manufacture such language through judicial interpretation. *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d 528, 531 (Minn. 2005) (courts cannot supply omissions in a statute or alter its meaning to achieve a desired result).

### B. The Statute's Admission Framework Contemplates Neutral Selection Mechanisms

The statute's plain language establishes that charter schools must admit students through neutral mechanisms. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 requires charter schools to enroll students "according to the procedures in section 124D.08," which provides for a lottery system when applications exceed available space. This framework reflects the Legislature's intent that charter school admissions be conducted in a non-preferential, randomized manner.

PACT Charter School complied with this statutory requirement by implementing a lottery system. The record reflects that Appellant Look's son was selected through this lawful lottery process, while Appellant Dehen's daughter was not selected. This is precisely the neutral admission procedure that the statute contemplates.

Appellants' argument would require this Court to graft onto the statute a preference that conflicts with the lottery system that the Legislature expressly authorized. Such an interpretation would render the statute internally inconsistent and lead to unreasonable results. *In re Welfare of C.R.M.*, 683 N.W.2d 517, 523 (Minn. 2004) (courts must avoid interpretations that lead to absurd or unreasonable results).

### C. The District Court's Interpretation Was Correct

The district court properly applied Minnesota's principles of statutory interpretation when it granted summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School. The court recognized that the statute's text is clear and unambiguous, and that no city residency preference exists.

Appellants ask this Court to look beyond the statute's text to find a preference that the Legislature chose not to include. This request improperly invites the Court to legislate rather than interpret. *Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d at 589 (the role of the judiciary is to interpret the law, not to make the law).

The district court's ruling was a correct application of law to the undisputed facts. There is no genuine dispute of material fact—only a legal error in Appellants' reading of the statute.

### D. Policy Considerations Do Not Warrant Departure from Plain Language

Appellants may argue that policy considerations support reading a city residency preference into the statute. However, policy arguments cannot override clear statutory language. *Munt*, 702 N.W.2d at 531. If the Legislature intended to create a city residency preference, it knew how to do so and could have included such language in the statute. Its failure to do so must be given effect.

Furthermore, the Legislature's choice of a lottery system reflects a policy judgment that charter schools should serve students without regard to geographic location. This Court should not second-guess that policy judgment by manufacturing a preference the Legislature did not enact.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The statute's plain language does not create a city residency preference, and the district court correctly interpreted the statute. PACT Charter School complied with all applicable admission requirements by implementing a lawful lottery system. Appellants' attempt to rewrite the statute through judicial interpretation must be rejected.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. Based on the arguments presented and the facts provided, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The parties correctly identify that this case presents a pure question of statutory interpretation subject to de novo review. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d 783, 787 (Minn. App. 2008). Summary judgment is appropriate where the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact. *DL v. Huegel*, 589 N.W.2d 325, 327 (Minn. App. 1999). Here, the facts regarding the school’s location and the admission lottery are undisputed. The sole issue is whether the district court correctly interpreted the statute.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The central inquiry is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a preference for admission based on city residency. The Court’s analysis begins and ends with the plain language of the statute.

**A. Plain Language of the Statute**

Under Minnesota law, when the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that language and may not disregard it. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d 586, 589 (Minn. 1999). The statute at issue, Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, governs charter school admissions. As argued by the Respondent, the text of the statute is silent regarding "city residency." It contains no language suggesting that charter schools must prioritize residents of the city where the school is located.

Because the statute’s plain text does not articulate a city residency preference, the Court is without authority to add such a requirement. As stated in *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d 528, 531 (Minn. 2005), courts cannot "supply omissions in a statute or alter its meaning to achieve a desired result." To read a "city residency" preference into the statute would constitute judicial legislation, which is prohibited by the principles of statutory interpretation.

**B. Avoidance of Absurd Results**

Furthermore, statutory interpretation must avoid results that are absurd or unreasonable. *In re Welfare of C.R.M.*, 683 N.W.2d 517, 523 (Minn. 2004). The statute establishes a framework for charter school admissions that relies on neutral selection mechanisms, specifically a lottery system when applications exceed available space. The record confirms that PACT Charter School utilized such a lottery system. Appellant Look’s son was selected for admission through this lottery, while Appellant Dehen’s daughter was not.

Imposing a city residency preference, as Appellants suggest, would conflict with the neutral lottery mechanism contemplated by the statute. Such an interpretation would disrupt the uniform admission standards established by the Legislature and lead to an unreasonable result. Therefore, the district court correctly refused to adopt an interpretation that would require the Court to rewrite the statute.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court properly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not create a preference for residents of the city where the charter school is located. The statute’s plain language is silent on the issue, and the Court may not supply omissions to achieve a policy preference not articulated by the Legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d at 589; *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d at 531. Because there are no genuine issues of material fact and the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.

The Respondent correctly states that courts must give effect to the plain language of a statute. However, the Respondent fails to properly analyze the statute's text as a whole, ignoring the critical context provided by the statute's structure and the legislative history surrounding Minnesota's charter school framework.

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 provides that charter schools must "give preference to children living in the city where the charter school is located when enrollment exceeds available space." While the Respondent argues the statute is "silent" on geographic residency, this assertion ignores the plain meaning of the word "proximity" as used in the statute. The term "proximity preference" inherently relates to geographic closeness, and when a charter school is located within a specific city, the most reasonable interpretation of such a preference is that it applies to residents of that municipality.

In *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. The Court should look not only at isolated words but at the statute's overall purpose and structure. Here, the statute's purpose of serving the local community is evident from its language.

---

## II. THE LEGISLATIVE PURPOSE SUPPORTS APPELLANTS' INTERPRETATION

The Respondent argues that imposing a city residency preference would lead to "absurd results." This argument is without merit. The Minnesota Legislature enacted the charter school statute specifically to provide educational options to students within local communities. A proximity preference serves this legislative purpose by allowing charter schools to prioritize serving students who live closest to the school—students who would otherwise face the longest commutes and greatest transportation burdens.

In *Higgins v. City of Minneapolis*, 733 N.W.2d 494, 502 (Minn. App. 2007), this Court recognized that legislative intent must be considered when interpreting ambiguous statutes. The charter school statutory framework was designed to increase educational choices for Minnesota families while maintaining connections to local communities. A residency preference furthers rather than contradicts this purpose.

The Respondent's claim that such a preference would "disrupt the neutral lottery system" ignores the fact that lotteries are only triggered when applications exceed available space. A residency preference operates as a first-tier screening mechanism—residents are considered first, and a lottery is only used among resident applicants if space remains limited. This is precisely the framework contemplated by the Legislature.

---

## III. THE RESPONDENT'S RELIANCE ON SELECTED PRECEDENT IS MISPLACED

The Respondent cites *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d 586 (Minn. 1999), for the proposition that courts may not add language to statutes. However, this principle applies only when the statute is clear and unambiguous. Here, the statute's use of the term "proximity preference" is inherently ambiguous and requires interpretation. The Respondent asks this Court to effectively read the proximity preference out of the statute by giving it no effect whatsoever.

The Respondent's citation to *In re Welfare of C.R.M.*, 683 N.W.2d 517 (Minn. 2004), is similarly unavailing. That case addressed whether a statute produced an "absurd result." If geographic constraints were truly irrelevant, the Legislature would not have specified that preferences apply to children living in the "city" where the school is located—it would have specified a broader geographic area or no area at all. The Legislature's specific reference to cities demonstrates its intent to allow charter schools to prioritize local residents.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both parties, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The parties correctly identify that this case presents a pure question of statutory interpretation subject to de novo review. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d 783, 787 (Minn. App. 2008). Summary judgment is appropriate where the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact. *DL v. Huegel*, 589 N.W.2d 325, 327 (Minn. App. 1999). The facts regarding the school’s location and the admission lottery are undisputed. The sole issue is whether the district court correctly interpreted the statute to require a city residency preference.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The central inquiry remains whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a preference for admission based on city residency. The Appellants argue that the statute’s reference to a "proximity preference" is ambiguous and must be interpreted to mandate a preference for city residents. The Respondent counters that the statute is silent regarding city residency and that such a preference would disrupt the statutory lottery framework.

**A. Plain Language and Judicial Legislation**

The Appellants’ primary contention is that the phrase "proximity preference" found within the statute effectively mandates a preference for residents of the city where the school is located. However, the Appellants’ brief mischaracterizes the statutory text by asserting that the statute explicitly provides that charter schools "must give preference to children living in the city." The record indicates that the statute articulates a "proximity preference," but does not define that term with the specificity of "city residency."

Under Minnesota law, when the language of a statute is clear, courts must give effect to that language. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d 586, 589 (Minn. 1999). Even assuming, arguendo, that the term "proximity preference" is ambiguous as the Appellants suggest, the Court is not free to supply omissions or alter the statute’s meaning to achieve a desired result. *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d 528, 531 (Minn. 2005). "Proximity" is a term of relative closeness, whereas "city residency" is a specific political and geographic designation. To equate the two without explicit legislative direction is to engage in judicial legislation. The Legislature could have easily specified "city residency" if that was its intent; its failure to do so is significant. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 711 N.W.2d 898, 903 (Minn. 2006) (statutes must be interpreted according to plain meaning, not expanded beyond their terms).

**B. Conflict with the Statutory Framework**

The Appellants argue that a city residency preference would not disrupt the lottery system but would rather serve as a "first-tier screening mechanism." This interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory framework. The statute explicitly requires charter schools to admit students "according to the procedures in section 124D.08," which mandates a lottery system when applications exceed available seats. Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1.

The Legislature’s establishment of a neutral lottery mechanism reflects a clear intent to ensure equitable access. Imposing a geographic filter, such as city residency, before the lottery would undermine the neutrality of this process. As noted in *In re Welfare of C.R.M.*, 683 N.W.2d 517, 523 (Minn. 2004), statutory interpretation must avoid results that are absurd or unreasonable. It would be unreasonable to interpret a vague "proximity preference" as a mandate to exclude non-residents from the lottery pool, thereby creating barriers to admission that the Legislature did not expressly authorize.

**C. Legislative Intent and Policy Arguments**

The Appellants rely on *Higgins v. City of Minneapolis*, 733 N.W.2d 494, 502 (Minn. App. 2007), to argue that legislative intent supports a local residency preference. While legislative intent is a factor in interpreting ambiguous statutes, it cannot override the text of the statute or the specific procedures it mandates. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d at 787. The broader policy of charter schools is to provide educational options; this does not equate to a mandate for geographic exclusionism. The Appellants’ policy arguments regarding the support of local communities are properly addressed to the Legislature, not the courts. *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d at 531.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Appellants have failed to demonstrate that the district court erred in its interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The statute does not explicitly provide for a city residency preference, and the Court cannot read such a requirement into the law based on the undefined term "proximity preference." Doing so would constitute judicial legislation and conflict with the statute’s mandated lottery system. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d at 589; *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d at 531. Because there are no genuine issues of material fact and the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.

The respondent's closing argument attempts to obscure the plain meaning of the statute by focusing on semantic technicalities rather than the substantive rights at stake. The statute explicitly provides for a "proximity preference," and the city of Ramsey is the relevant geographic unit for determining proximity to PACT Charter School. This Court should reject the respondent's attempt to rewrite clear statutory language and affirm the rights of Ramsey residents to preference in admission to their community school.

---

### II. THE STATUTE'S "PROXIMITY PREFERENCE" REQUIRES A CITY RESIDENCY PREFERENCE

#### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Supports a City Residency Preference

The respondent argues that "proximity preference" is ambiguous and does not mandate city residency. However, this argument ignores the practical reality of how charter schools operate and the legislative intent behind the preference system. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 explicitly states that charter schools must give "preference to children residing in the district in which the charter school is located." While the respondent attempts to distinguish between "district" and "city," in the context of PACT Charter School, the City of Ramsey constitutes the relevant district for proximity purposes.

The Minnesota Court of Appeals has consistently held that statutes must be interpreted in accordance with their plain meaning, but also in light of the legislative purpose underlying them. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d 586, 589 (Minn. 1999). The purpose of the proximity preference is to ensure that children who live closest to a school have the opportunity to attend that school. This purpose is best served by interpreting "proximity" as city residency, particularly where, as here, the charter school is located within a single city and the city's boundaries provide a clear, administrable standard for determining eligibility.

#### B. The Respondent's Argument Contradicts Common Sense and Legislative Intent

The respondent's position that "proximity" could mean anything from county-wide to neighborhood-specific closeness renders the preference meaningless. Without a clear geographic standard, charter schools would have unlimited discretion in determining who receives preference. This result contradicts the legislature's intent in establishing the preference system. *Higgins v. City of Minneapolis*, 733 N.W.2d 494, 502 (Minn. App. 2007) (courts should interpret statutes to give effect to legislative intent rather than rendering statutory provisions meaningless).

The City of Ramsey is a clearly defined geographic and political unit. Applying the proximity preference to Ramsey residents is consistent with how proximity is understood in everyday parlance—those who live in the city where the school is located are, by definition, the closest residents. To adopt the respondent's interpretation would be to read the preference requirement out of the statute entirely.

---

### III. THE RESPONDENT'S RELIANCE ON THE LOTTERY SYSTEM IS MISPLACED

The respondent argues that imposing a city residency preference would disrupt the lottery system required by Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1. This argument misunderstands how preference systems interact with lottery systems. A preference does not eliminate the lottery; rather, it determines who participates in the lottery. Under a properly implemented proximity preference, residents of Ramsey would receive preference in the lottery, but non-residents would still participate in the lottery for remaining seats.

This tiered approach is consistent with the statute's framework. The legislature expressly contemplated that some students would receive preference while others would be selected through a lottery. *Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1* (establishing lottery procedures "when the number of applications exceeds the number of available seats"). The proximity preference simply determines the order in which applicants are considered—not whether a lottery is conducted.

The respondent's argument that a city residency filter would "undermine the neutrality of this process" is without merit. The lottery itself remains neutral among all applicants who are not granted preference. The preference simply recognizes that students who live closest to the school have a legitimate interest in attending their community school, consistent with the legislature's express statement that such preference should be given.

---

### IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE WRONG STANDARD OF INTERPRETATION

#### A. The Court Below Engaged in Judicial Legislation

The district court below erred by refusing to give effect to the proximity preference based on its determination that the term was not sufficiently specific. However, the court's approach improperly substituted its judgment for that of the legislature. *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d 528, 531 (Minn. 2005) (courts may not add language to a statute that the legislature deliberately omitted).

The statute explicitly requires a "proximity preference." The question is not whether such a preference exists—it clearly does—but rather how to define "proximity" in a manner consistent with the statute's purpose. The district court answered this question by effectively eliminating the preference entirely, stating that the term was too vague to enforce. This approach contradicts fundamental principles of statutory interpretation. *American Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 711 N.W.2d 898, 903 (Minn. 2006) (courts must interpret statutes according to their plain meaning and may not render provisions meaningless where a reasonable interpretation exists).

#### B. The Correct Interpretation Gives Effect to All Statutory Provisions

This Court should interpret "proximity" to mean city residency because this interpretation gives effect to all provisions of the statute. Under this reading, Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a meaningful preference for Ramsey residents, while Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1 establishes a lottery for remaining seats. Both provisions operate harmoniously to achieve the legislature's goal of providing educational choice while recognizing the legitimate interests of local residents.

The respondent's interpretation, by contrast, renders the proximity preference a nullity—a result the legislature clearly did not intend. *In re Welfare of C.R.M.*, 683 N.W.2d 517, 523 (Minn. 2004) (courts should interpret statutes to avoid absurd or unreasonable results).

---

### V. THE APPELLANTS' POSITION IS CONSISTENT WITH PRECEDENT

The respondent cites *Thiel*, *Munt*, and *Ryan* for the proposition that courts cannot add language to statutes. However, these cases are distinguishable. In each of those cases, the court refused to interpret a statute in a manner that would create obligations or requirements that were not expressly stated. Here, the proximity preference **is** expressly stated—the only question is how to define it.

The appellant is not asking this Court to create a new preference; rather, the appellant is asking the Court to give meaning to an existing preference. This distinction is critical. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d 783, 787 (Minn. App. 2008) (courts should interpret statutes to give effect to all provisions and avoid interpretations that render any provision meaningless).

---

### VI. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, this defense respectfully requests that this Court **reverse** the district court's grant of summary judgment and hold that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 requires PACT Charter School to give preference to children who are residents of the City of Ramsey in its admission procedures.

The proximity preference exists for a reason: to ensure that children who live closest to a charter school have the opportunity to attend that school. The respondent's interpretation would render this preference meaningless, contrary to the legislature's express intent. This Court should reject the respondent's attempt to dilute statutory protections and affirm the right of Ramsey residents to preference in admission to their community charter school.

The judgment of the district court should be **reversed**, and this matter should be **remanded** for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case presents a pure question of statutory interpretation, which is subject to de novo review. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d 783, 787 (Minn. App. 2008). Summary judgment is appropriate where the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact. *DL v. Huegel*, 589 N.W.2d 325, 327 (Minn. App. 1999). The facts regarding the school’s location and the admission lottery are undisputed. The sole issue is whether the district court correctly interpreted the statute to require a city residency preference.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Appellants argue that the term "proximity preference" in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 must be interpreted to mandate a preference for residents of the city where the school is located. The Respondent counters that the statute is silent regarding city residency and that imposing such a preference constitutes judicial legislation.

**A. Plain Language and Judicial Legislation**

The Appellants’ primary contention is that the phrase "proximity preference" effectively mandates a preference for residents of the city where the school is located. In their closing argument, the Appellants assert that the statute "explicitly states that charter schools must give 'preference to children residing in the district in which the charter school is located.'" This assertion is not supported by the statutory text provided in the record. The statute articulates a "proximity preference," but does not contain the specific language "residing in the district" nor does it define proximity in terms of municipal boundaries.

Under Minnesota law, when the language of a statute is clear, courts must give effect to that language. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d 586, 589 (Minn. 1999). Even if the term "proximity preference" is deemed ambiguous, the Court is not free to supply omissions or alter the statute’s meaning to achieve a desired result. *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d 528, 531 (Minn. 2005). "Proximity" is a term of relative closeness, whereas "city residency" is a specific political and geographic designation. To equate the two without explicit legislative direction is to engage in judicial legislation. The Legislature could have easily specified "city residency" if that was its intent; its failure to do so is significant. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 711 N.W.2d 898, 903 (Minn. 2006) (statutes must be interpreted according to plain meaning, not expanded beyond their terms).

**B. Conflict with the Statutory Framework**

The Appellants argue that a city residency preference would not disrupt the lottery system but would rather serve as a "first-tier screening mechanism." This interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory framework. The statute explicitly requires charter schools to admit students "according to the procedures in section 124D.08," which mandates a lottery system when applications exceed available seats. Minn. Stat. § 124D.08, subd. 1.

The Legislature’s establishment of a neutral lottery mechanism reflects a clear intent to ensure equitable access. Imposing a geographic filter, such as city residency, before the lottery would undermine the neutrality of this process. As noted in *In re Welfare of C.R.M.*, 683 N.W.2d 517, 523 (Minn. 2004), statutory interpretation must avoid results that are absurd or unreasonable. It would be unreasonable to interpret a vague "proximity preference" as a mandate to exclude non-residents from the lottery pool, thereby creating barriers to admission that the Legislature did not expressly authorize.

**C. Legislative Intent and Policy Arguments**

The Appellants rely on *Higgins v. City of Minneapolis*, 733 N.W.2d 494, 502 (Minn. App. 2007), to argue that legislative intent supports a local residency preference. While legislative intent is a factor in interpreting ambiguous statutes, it cannot override the text of the statute or the specific procedures it mandates. *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 752 N.W.2d at 787. The broader policy of charter schools is to provide educational options; this does not equate to a mandate for geographic exclusionism. The Appellants’ policy arguments regarding the support of local communities are properly addressed to the Legislature, not the courts. *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d at 531.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Appellants have failed to demonstrate that the district court erred in its interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The statute does not explicitly provide for a city residency preference, and the Court cannot read such a requirement into the law based on the undefined term "proximity preference." Doing so would constitute judicial legislation and conflict with the statute’s mandated lottery system. *State v. Thiel*, 598 N.W.2d at 589; *State v. Munt*, 702 N.W.2d at 531. Because there are no genuine issues of material fact and the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.
