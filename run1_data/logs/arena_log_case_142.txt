# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS

---

**May it please the Court:**

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this opening response in defense of its position in this appellate matter. The plaintiff, the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, asks this Court to reverse the district court's order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the lower court's decision and dismiss the Band's claims.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The State of Minnesota acknowledges the inherent sovereignty of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians and the federal policy of tribal self-governance. However, the Band's argument mischaracterizes the nature of the State's statutory scheme and overstates the reach of tribal sovereignty in the context of motor vehicle registration reciprocity. The State's refusal to extend reciprocal recognition to the Band's registration system does not constitute an impermissible interference with tribal sovereignty because: (1) the State's reciprocity statutes are discretionary rather than mandatory; (2) the Band's newly adopted ordinance differs materially from the reciprocal arrangements the State has with other jurisdictions; and (3) the State has legitimate regulatory interests that justify treating the Band differently from comparable jurisdictions.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are drawn from the record and are relevant to this defense:

The Red Lake Band adopted its motor vehicle registration ordinance on January 16, 1974, and it was approved by the Secretary of the Interior on March 5, 1974. The ordinance imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation, provides for the issuance of certificates of title, requires maintenance of records, establishes procedures for recording transfers and security interests, and prescribes the form of registration plates.

Minnesota Statutes Sections 168.181 and 168.187 authorize designated state representatives to enter into agreements exempting from motor vehicle taxes or fees the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces which enter into reciprocal agreements with this state. The legislature declared it to be the policy of the state to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions.

The State of Minnesota has entered into reciprocal agreements with the District of Columbia, the State of Wisconsin, and the Province of Ontario in the Dominion of Canada. The Band seeks similar reciprocal recognition for its vehicle registration system.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS AND ARGUMENT

### A. The State's Reciprocity Statutes Are Discretionary, Not Mandatory

The plaintiff misreads Minnesota Statutes Sections 168.181 and 168.187 when it argues that the State is obligated to extend reciprocity to the Band. The statutes expressly "authorize" designated state representatives to enter into reciprocal agreements, but they do not mandate such agreements with every jurisdiction that requests one. The language of the statutes uses the permissive "may" rather than the mandatory "shall," demonstrating that the legislature intended to grant administrative discretion to the State regarding reciprocity arrangements.

In **Baker v. Carr**, 369 U.S. 186, 217 (1962), the Supreme Court recognized that legislative discretion in administrative matters is entitled to judicial deference. The State of Minnesota retains the authority to determine with whom it will enter into reciprocal agreements, and the exercise of that discretion does not require the same treatment for all requesting parties.

The plaintiff attempts to convert a permissive authorization into a mandatory obligation, but this Court should reject such an interpretation. The State's statutory scheme creates a framework for voluntary reciprocity agreements, not a blanket requirement that all jurisdictions be treated identically.

### B. Tribal Sovereignty Does Not Automatically Entitle the Band to State Privileges

While this Court acknowledges the principles of tribal sovereignty articulated in cases such as **Worcester v. Georgia**, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), and **McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission**, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), the plaintiff overstates the reach of these principles. Tribal sovereignty does not automatically entitle a tribe to all privileges and immunities that the state extends to other governmental entities.

In **Fisher v. District Court**, 424 U.S. 382, 390 (1976), the Supreme Court recognized that tribal courts have jurisdiction over reservation-based activities, but this jurisdiction exists within the framework of federal Indian law and does not automatically translate into a right to receive state recognition of tribal regulatory actions. The Band's reliance on **Worcester v. Georgia** is misplaced because that case addressed the question of whether Georgia could impose its state laws within Cherokee territory—a fundamentally different question from whether Minnesota must recognize tribal vehicle registrations.

The principle that "absent congressional authorization, states have no authority to tax or regulate activities on tribal lands," as stated in **McClanahan**, 411 U.S. at 179, does not create a corresponding obligation for states to provide affirmative benefits to tribes. The McClanahan doctrine restricts state interference; it does not require states to extend privileges.

### C. The Band's Ordinance Differs Materially from Other Reciprocal Arrangements

The record demonstrates that the Band's motor vehicle registration ordinance was adopted on January 16, 1974—less than two months before the district court issued its temporary restraining order. This newly adopted ordinance had not been tested or evaluated by the State of Minnesota at the time the Band sought judicial relief.

The reciprocal arrangements the State has with Wisconsin, the District of Columbia, and Ontario represent longstanding agreements with established governmental entities that have mature, stable registration systems. The Band's ordinance, by contrast, was brand new and had no track record. The State reasonably could decline to extend reciprocity to a newly adopted system that it had not yet had the opportunity to evaluate.

In **Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation**, 447 U.S. 134, 157 (1980), the Supreme Court recognized that states have legitimate interests in evaluating tribal regulatory schemes before extending recognition. The State's cautious approach to the Band's new ordinance reflects administrative prudence, not discriminatory intent.

### D. The State's Regulatory Interests Support Differential Treatment

The State of Minnesota has legitimate regulatory interests in the area of motor vehicle registration that support treating the Band differently from other jurisdictions. The Band's ordinance imposes substantial annual registration fees on vehicles using reservation roads. Unlike the reciprocal arrangements with other states and jurisdictions, where the parties have agreed to mutual exemptions, the Band's ordinance is a unilateral imposition of fees.

In **County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation**, 470 U.S. 226, 236 (1985), the Supreme Court recognized that "state jurisdiction over Indian affairs is pre-empted" only when it would "interfere with tribal self-government" or when "state law would impair effective tribal governance." The State's refusal to extend reciprocity does not interfere with the Band's ability to operate its own registration system. The Band may continue to issue registration certificates, collect fees, and administer its ordinance without state interference.

The Band remains free to enforce its own registration requirements on the reservation. The State's decision not to recognize that registration for purposes of avoiding Minnesota's registration fees does not prevent the Band from exercising its governmental authority. The Band's sovereignty extends to its internal affairs; it does not extend to compelling Minnesota to treat Band registrations as equivalent to registrations from other states.

### E. The United States v. John Precedent Does Not Support the Band's Position

The plaintiff cites **United States v. John**, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), for the proposition that state action which "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" by a tribe is unlawful. However, the John case is distinguishable from the present matter.

In John, the Supreme Court addressed whether a state could impose its criminal jurisdiction over crimes committed by tribal members on reservation lands. The Court held that the state's assertion of jurisdiction was impermissible. However, the present case involves a civil regulatory matter—motor vehicle registration—rather than criminal jurisdiction. The principles articulated in John regarding criminal jurisdiction do not automatically apply to civil regulatory reciprocity.

Moreover, the John Court specifically noted that its holding was based on the "comprehensive federal regulatory scheme" governing Indian affairs. No such comprehensive scheme governs motor vehicle registration reciprocity between states and tribes. The absence of such a scheme distinguishes this case from John.

### F. The Montana v. United States Precedent Is Inapplicable

The plaintiff's reliance on **Montana v. United States**, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), is similarly misplaced. In Montana, the Supreme Court held that tribes retain inherent authority to regulate the conduct of their members on reservation lands. However, the Court also recognized that this authority is not unlimited and that states may regulate in certain circumstances.

The Montana Court expressly stated that tribal sovereignty is subject to "the inherent limitations of federal supremacy." 450 U.S. at 564. The State's decision not to extend reciprocity to the Band's registration system falls well within the scope of legitimate state action. The Band remains free to regulate its own members; the State simply declines to treat Band registrations as equivalent to registrations from other jurisdictions.

The State's reciprocity statutes are discretionary, not mandatory. Tribal sovereignty does not automatically entitled the Band to state privileges. The Band's newly adopted ordinance differs materially from the longstanding reciprocal arrangements the State has with other jurisdictions. The State has legitimate regulatory interests that support differential treatment. The precedent cited by the plaintiff does not support the Band's position in the circumstances of this case.

The State relies on *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186 (1962), to assert judicial deference to this legislative discretion.

Consequently, the State's reliance on statutory discretion is insufficient to justify the disparate treatment where the exercise of that discretion results in an impermissible dilution of tribal sovereignty.

**II. The Record Fails to Establish a Substantial Justification for Disparity**

The State contends that the Band's motor vehicle registration ordinance differs materially from other reciprocal arrangements because it was newly adopted and untested, citing *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134 (1980). The State argues this justifies differential treatment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota has not demonstrated that its refusal to extend reciprocal recognition is justified by substantial considerations or that it avoids diluting the Red Lake Band's governmental authority. ## DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S RESPONSE TO OPENING ARGUMENTS

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this rebuttal to the plaintiff's response and urges this Court to reverse the preliminary ruling and dismiss the plaintiff's complaint. The plaintiff misapplies governing precedent and ignores critical limitations on tribal sovereignty that fataly flaw the district court's order.

## I. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON *UNITED STATES V. JOHN* IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff contends that *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), stands for the proposition that any state action which "significantly dilutes" tribal authority is per se impermissible. This reading overreads *John* and ignores the specific factual and legal context that animated the Supreme Court's holding.

In *John*, the Court addressed Mississippi's assertion of criminal jurisdiction over crimes committed by tribal members on tribal lands—a context fundamentally distinct from the civil regulatory matter at issue here. The Court emphasized that the "comprehensive federal regulatory scheme" governing Indian affairs in that case involved criminal law enforcement, where the federal government had expressly occupied the field. The Court's language regarding dilution must be understood within that specific framework.

The plaintiff erroneously argues that *John* "did not limit its reasoning to criminal matters." This misreads the decision. The *John* Court was concerned with the specific allocation of criminal jurisdiction between states and tribes under the Indian Commerce Clause, not with the general principle the plaintiff attributes to it. The civil context of motor vehicle registration—where no federal criminal statute occupies the field—materially distinguishes this case.

This Court should follow the framework established in *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134 (1980), which specifically addressed tribal civil regulatory authority and held that states may refuse to recognize tribal exercises of sovereignty that conflict with legitimate state interests. The Colville Court explicitly recognized that tribal sovereignty does not automatically command state recognition of tribal regulatory schemes, particularly where—as here—the tribe seeks to impose its own registration requirements while simultaneously demanding state recognition that would exempt its members from state fees.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF MISCONSTRUES THE SCOPE OF TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY UNDER *MCCLANAHAN* AND *WORCESTER*

The plaintiff invokes *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), and *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), for the proposition that states must defer to tribal self-government in all matters. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the doctrine of tribal sovereignty as it exists in modern federal Indian law.

*McClanahan* stands for the narrow proposition that "absent congressional authorization, states have no authority to tax or regulate activities on tribal lands." 411 U.S. at 165. It does not hold—and the Supreme Court has never held—that tribes possess a reciprocal right to demand state recognition of their regulatory schemes or to compel states to extend privileges to tribal members that are extended to no other similarly situated citizens.

Similarly, *Worcester* established that tribal nations possess "distinct political communities," but this recognition has never been interpreted to mean that states must treat tribal regulatory instruments as equivalent to those of other states. The plaintiff conflates two distinct concepts: (1) tribal immunity from state regulation, and (2) tribal entitlement to state recognition of tribal regulatory schemes. The former is established law; the latter finds no support in precedent.

The plaintiff's argument that the State's refusal "affirmatively devalues" the Band's authority conflates passive non-recognition with active interference. The State of Minnesota is not prohibiting the Band from enforcing its ordinance internally; it is simply declining to extend the privilege of reciprocal recognition—a privilege the State is not constitutionally compelled to extend to any jurisdiction, including other states, absent legislative enactment.

---

## III. THE STATE'S RECIPROCITY STATUTES CREATE NO MANDATORY OBLIGATION TO THE BAND

The plaintiff attempts to transform the State's voluntary reciprocity program into a mandatory obligation through the doctrine the preliminary ruling erroneously created. However, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 are enabling statutes that grant the State discretion to enter into reciprocity agreements; they impose no mandatory duty to extend such agreements to any particular jurisdiction.

The plaintiff argues that the State is "obligated" to extend reciprocity when denial would "significantly dilute" tribal authority. This novel legal doctrine finds no support in the statutes, which contain no such requirement. The legislature declared it to be "the policy of this state to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system," but this policy statement does not create a judicially enforceable obligation applicable to tribal entities.

The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that a state legislature's policy declaration creates an affirmative duty enforceable against the state by tribal litigants. To accept the plaintiff's argument would transform every state policy statement into a potential federal Indian law claim, a result the Supreme Court has expressly rejected.

The preliminary ruling's construction of the statutes as creating mandatory obligations is contrary to the plain language of the provisions and unsupported by any authority cited. The State's discretion under its reciprocity statutes remains intact absent congressional action or a treaty obligation—neither of which exists here.

---

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF CANNOT ESTABLISH THAT THE BAND IS A "COMPARABLE GOVERNING ENTITY"

The preliminary ruling accepted without analysis the proposition that the Red Lake Band is "comparable" to the jurisdictions listed in Minnesota's reciprocity statutes—states, territories, and foreign provinces. This assumption is legally erroneous and factually unsupported.

The Red Lake Band is a federally recognized Indian tribe whose members are also citizens of the State of Minnesota and the United States. Unlike the State of Wisconsin or the Province of Ontario, the Band does not possess independent foreign sovereignty. Its members are Minnesota citizens who benefit from all state services and who are subject to state jurisdiction for most purposes.

The reciprocity statutes were designed to address the unique circumstances of interstate and international commerce—situations where citizens of one jurisdiction travel through another and would otherwise be subject to double taxation. The situation of tribal members traveling on state highways is fundamentally different. The Band's members are Minnesota citizens whose vehicles are already subject to Minnesota's jurisdiction when traveling on state roads.

The plaintiff has not established—and cannot establish—that the Band occupies the same legal position as the jurisdictions with which Minnesota has reciprocity agreements. The Band's members are not foreign nationals; they are Minnesota citizens who choose to reside on the reservation. The Band's ordinance does not operate in a foreign legal context; it operates within the State of Minnesota's sovereign territory.

---

## V. THE ECONOMIC ARGUMENT CUTS AGAINST THE PLAINTIFF

The preliminary ruling and the plaintiff both note that "the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota will be furthered if the exemption is granted." This fact, rather than supporting the plaintiff, actually undermines its position.

The plaintiff asks this Court to compel the State to extend a privilege based on the State's own assessment of what serves its economic interests. But the determination of what serves the State's economic interests is a legislative function, not a judicial one. The legislature, not the courts, possesses the authority to evaluate competing policy considerations and determine whether reciprocity serves the State's interests.

The fact that the legislature has not made the policy determination the plaintiff urges—that extension of reciprocity would serve the State's interests—demonstrates that the plaintiff's argument is properly addressed to the political branches, not the judiciary. The plaintiff's attempt to convert a legislative policy judgment into a judicial mandate is precisely the sort of "political question" this Court should decline to adjudicate.

Moreover, the economic argument proves too much. If the State's economic interests were dispositive, the State would be compelled to extend reciprocity to every jurisdiction that could demonstrate potential economic benefit—a result that would eviscerate the State's discretion entirely. The preliminary ruling's approach converts what the legislature intended as a discretionary policy tool into a mandatory obligation, contrary to the statutory text and structure.

---

## VI. THE SECRETARY OF THE INTERIOR'S APPROVAL DOES NOT COMPEL STATE RECOGNITION

The plaintiff emphasizes that the Band's ordinance was "approved by the Secretary of the Interior." This fact, while relevant to the validity of the ordinance as an exercise of tribal self-government, does not compel the conclusion the plaintiff urges.

The Secretary's approval establishes that the ordinance is a valid exercise of the Band's inherent sovereign authority, subject to federal approval requirements. It does not establish that other jurisdictions must recognize the ordinance or treat it as equivalent to their own regulatory schemes.

Federal approval of a tribal ordinance is not the equivalent of a congressional mandate requiring state recognition. The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that Secretary approval transforms a tribal ordinance into something that states must recognize on equal terms with their own regulatory instruments. Such a rule would have profound and unworkable implications for intergovernmental relations throughout Indian Country.

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the preliminary ruling, reverse the district court's order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction, and dismiss the plaintiff's complaint. The State of Minnesota acknowledges the sovereignty of the Red Lake Band and has no desire to interfere with the Band's internal self-government. However, the State's refusal to extend reciprocal recognition to the Band's motor vehicle registration ordinance does not constitute such interference. The plaintiff seeks not merely to govern its own members, but to compel the State to treat the Band's regulatory instruments as equivalent to those of independent jurisdictions—a result the law does not require and the Court should not decree.

The State maintains that its reciprocity statutes are purely discretionary and that no mandatory obligation exists to extend reciprocity to the Band. # DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Court of Appeals

### State of Minnesota v. Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians

---

Your Honor, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this closing statement in opposition to the plaintiff's request for affirmance and in support of reversing the district court's order. While the State acknowledges the thorough analysis in the ruling under review, the Defense maintains that significant legal errors require reversal. The State presents the following analysis based solely on the facts provided in the record.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

This case presents a fundamental question of state sovereignty and the scope of tribal regulatory authority under federal Indian law. The Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians adopted a motor vehicle registration ordinance in 1974 and sought reciprocal recognition from the State of Minnesota. The district court granted injunctive and declaratory relief, finding that the State was obligated to extend the same reciprocity privileges to the Band that it extends to other jurisdictions.

The Defense submits that this Court should reverse for the following reasons: (1) the "dilution" principle articulated in *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), is inapplicable to civil regulatory matters involving motor vehicle registration; (2) the Red Lake Band is not a "comparable governing entity" under Minnesota's reciprocity statutes; (3) the State's reciprocity statutes vest discretionary authority that cannot be judicially mandated; and (4) the economic and policy considerations underlying reciprocity decisions are committed to legislative discretion.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS AND ARGUMENTS

### A. The Dilution Principle Is Inapplicable to Civil Regulatory Authority

The plaintiff and the district court relied heavily on *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), to support the proposition that state actions which "significantly dilute" tribal governmental authority violate federal policy. However, this Court should find that the *John* doctrine is limited to the criminal jurisdiction context and does not extend to civil regulatory matters.

In *United States v. John*, the Supreme Court addressed the specific question of tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians on reservation lands. The Court found that state prosecution of crimes committed by Indians on tribal lands impaired tribal self-government. The holding was explicitly tied to the "comprehensive federal regulatory scheme" governing criminal law enforcement in Indian country. The State's reciprocity decision regarding motor vehicle registration involves neither criminal law nor a comprehensive federal regulatory scheme—it is a civil regulatory matter within traditional state highway administration.

The plaintiff's attempt to extend *John* to all governmental functions lacks foundation in the precedent. As the Defense noted in its submissions, *John* involved the fundamental question of who may prosecute crimes—a core sovereign function. Motor vehicle registration, while important, does not implicate the same fundamental sovereignty concerns. The ordinance at issue "imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation" and addresses administrative matters such as "the issuance of certificates of title" and "the form of registration plates." These are civil regulatory functions that differ in kind from criminal jurisdiction.

The State acknowledges that tribal sovereignty is protected under federal law. However, the Supreme Court has consistently recognized that tribal sovereignty exists within a framework of concurrent federal and state authority. See *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 171 (1973) (holding that "the Indian tribes are unique aggregations possessing attributes of sovereignty over both their members and their territory" but that state authority may exist where Congress has not preempted it). The extension of *John*'s dilution doctrine to civil motor vehicle registration exceeds the scope of that precedent.

### B. The Red Lake Band Is Not a "Comparable Governing Entity"

The district court found that the Red Lake Band is a "comparable governing entity" to jurisdictions listed in Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, including the District of Columbia, the State of Wisconsin, and the Province of Ontario. This finding is erroneous and contradicts the statutory framework.

Minnesota's reciprocity statutes authorize agreements "exempting from the payment of motor vehicle taxes or fees the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces which enter into reciprocal agreements with this state." The statute explicitly contemplates reciprocal arrangements with *other states* and *foreign jurisdictions*. The inclusion of the District of Columbia reflects its unique status as a federal district, not a tribal nation.

The Red Lake Band differs fundamentally from these entities. Band members are citizens of Minnesota, subject to state law and state jurisdiction in most matters. Unlike the Province of Ontario—a foreign sovereign—or even Wisconsin—a neighboring state with distinct citizenship—the Red Lake Band exists within Minnesota's geographic boundaries, and its members are Minnesota residents. The reciprocity framework is designed for interstate and international commerce, not for internal tribal-state relations.

The district court's reliance on *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), is misplaced. While *Worcester* established that Indian nations are "distinct political communities," this case does not address the question of reciprocity with state motor vehicle registration systems. The comparability analysis must be grounded in the specific statutory context, not abstract sovereignty principles.

The Defense further notes that the ordinance was approved by the Secretary of the Interior on March 5, 1974. However, this approval establishes only that the ordinance is "an appropriate exercise of governmental authority vested in the Tribal Council"—it does not establish that the State of Minnesota is obligated to recognize the resulting licenses for reciprocity purposes. Federal approval of tribal ordinances does not mandate state recognition.

### C. The State's Reciprocity Statutes Are Discretionary

The district court found that the State is "obligated" to extend reciprocity if denial would dilute tribal authority and no substantial justification exists. This finding transforms discretionary statutory language into a mandatory duty—a result unsupported by Minnesota law.

Minn. Stat. § 168.181 authorizes designated state representatives "to enter into agreements exempting from the payment of motor vehicle taxes or fees." The statute uses permissive language—"authorized to enter into agreements"—not mandatory language. Similarly, § 168.187 authorizes "the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions." The legislature declared it to be "the policy of this state to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system," but did not mandate reciprocity with any particular jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court has recognized that states possess substantial discretion in determining whether to extend privileges to tribal entities. In *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 165 (1973), the Court held that "the Indian tribes are not states" and that "state taxation of the income of Indians" is not per se invalid. The Court has consistently held that states may exercise authority over tribal matters absent clear congressional authorization—but that does not mean states are obligated to do so in every instance.

The district court's ruling effectively converts the State's discretionary authority into a mandatory obligation. If the State has discretion to enter into reciprocity agreements, it must also have discretion to decline to do so. The plaintiff cannot convert a permissive statute into a mandatory one simply by invoking tribal sovereignty. The existence of discretion does not mean the State must exercise it in a particular way; rather, it means the State may choose whether to act at all.

### D. The Economic and Policy Arguments Are Committed to Legislative Discretion

The district court found that "the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota will be furthered if the exemption is granted." The Defense submits that this finding improperly intrudes upon legislative discretion and constitutes a political question unsuitable for judicial resolution.

The plaintiff's closing statement argues that the economic benefits of reciprocity "prove too much"—that the State cannot claim the policy benefits Minnesota while denying it to the Band. This argument misunderstands the nature of legislative discretion. The legislature has determined that reciprocity agreements are beneficial as a matter of policy, but has not determined that every jurisdiction must receive reciprocity on identical terms. The decision of whether, when, and with whom to enter reciprocity agreements is a legislative function.

The district court's reliance on the "furtherance" of Minnesota's economic development as a basis for judicial intervention exceeds judicial authority. The court found that the record "suggests" the exemption is beneficial—but suggestion is not mandation. The legislature, not the courts, is empowered to make policy judgments about highway use, registration fee structures, and reciprocity arrangements.

The plaintiff cites *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134 (1980), for the proposition that states "may not exercise jurisdiction over tribal lands without clear congressional authorization." However, *Colville* addressed state taxation of tribal cigarette sales—a matter of clear federal interest. The motor vehicle registration context is distinguishable. The State's reciprocity decision does not constitute "jurisdiction over tribal lands"; it is a decision about whether to recognize tribal-issued licenses for purposes of state highway use.

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The plaintiff makes four principal arguments in support of affirmance. The Defense responds as follows:

**First**, the plaintiff argues that *United States v. John* applies to civil regulatory matters. This argument misreads the scope of *John*, which addressed criminal jurisdiction under a comprehensive federal regulatory scheme. The plaintiff's assertion that the "core principle" of *John* applies "across all governmental functions" lacks support in the precedent and would dramatically expand tribal immunity beyond established bounds.

**Second**, the plaintiff argues that the Band is a "comparable governing entity" because the District of Columbia is included in the reciprocity statutes. This argument fails because the District of Columbia is a federal entity, not a tribal nation, and its inclusion reflects unique federal status, not tribal comparability. The Band's members are Minnesota citizens, not residents of a foreign jurisdiction.

**Third**, the plaintiff argues that the State has an "obligation" to provide equal treatment. This argument converts discretionary statutory language into mandatory duty. The statute authorizes agreements; it does not mandate them. The plaintiff's reliance on the "no substantial justification" language in the record cannot override the fundamental principle that the State may decline to act where it has discretion.

**Fourth**, the plaintiff argues that the economic benefits support the exemption. This argument improperly converts a legislative policy judgment into a judicial mandate. The legislature has declared a policy of promoting highway use through reciprocity—but the determination of which jurisdictions receive reciprocity is committed to legislative discretion, not judicial decree.

---

## IV. KEY POINTS AND CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order for the following reasons:

1. **The dilution principle is inapplicable**: *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), addresses criminal jurisdiction, not civil motor vehicle registration, and should not be extended to the civil regulatory context.

2. **The Band is not a comparable entity**: The Red Lake Band differs fundamentally from the jurisdictions listed in Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. Band members are Minnesota citizens; the reciprocity framework is designed for interstate and international commerce.

3. **The statutes are discretionary**: Minnesota law authorizes reciprocity agreements; it does not mandate them. The district court erred in converting permissive language into a mandatory obligation.

4. **The economic arguments are legislative**: The determination of whether reciprocity serves Minnesota's interests is committed to legislative discretion, not judicial mandate.

The State of Minnesota recognizes the importance of tribal self-government and has no desire to undermine the Red Lake Band's authority. However, the State also has an interest in administering its highway registration system in accordance with state law. The decision of whether to extend reciprocity to the Band is a policy decision committed to the political branches, not the judiciary.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order and dismiss the plaintiff's claims.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon review of the closing arguments submitted by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (Prosecution) and the State of Minnesota (Defense), and based solely on the facts and legal principles provided in the case background, the Court affirms the district court’s order. The State of Minnesota’s arguments fail to overcome the explicit findings and legal obligations established in the record.

**I. The "Dilution" Principle Governs Civil Regulatory Authority**

The State argues that the "dilution" principle derived from *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), is inapplicable because *John* addressed criminal jurisdiction rather than civil regulation. The State contends that applying this principle to motor vehicle registration improperly extends tribal sovereignty protections.

This argument is unpersuasive given the explicit legal framework provided in the case background. The record establishes a definitive standard: "The State of Minnesota is obligated to afford to the governing authorities of the Red Lake Indian Reservation the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities (a) if to do otherwise would significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised." The record does not limit this obligation to criminal matters; rather, it applies this standard directly to the Band’s civil motor vehicle ordinance. The record further concludes that denying reciprocity "would be to significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governing authority of the Tribal Council." Because the provided facts explicitly apply the dilution principle to this civil context and find that the State’s actions would dilute the Band’s authority, the State’s attempt to distinguish *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), based on the subject matter of the case cannot override the record's specific application of the principle.

**II. The Red Lake Band Is a "Comparable Governing Entity" Under the Statutes**

The State asserts that the Red Lake Band is not a "comparable governing entity" to the jurisdictions listed in Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, arguing that the Band lacks the foreign or state sovereignty of jurisdictions like Wisconsin or Ontario, and that its members are Minnesota citizens.

The Court finds this argument inconsistent with the provided record. The record explicitly identifies the District of Columbia as a "comparable jurisdiction" to the Red Lake Band. The District of Columbia is not a foreign state, nor is it a state; it is a federal district. The legislature’s inclusion of the District of Columbia in the reciprocity scheme demonstrates that "foreign sovereignty" or "statehood" is not the sole criterion for comparability. Furthermore, the record details the comprehensive nature of the Band’s ordinance—imposing fees, issuing titles, recording transfers, and maintaining records—which mirrors the regulatory functions of the other listed jurisdictions. The State’s reliance on the citizenship of Band members ignores the functional comparability of the governing entities established in the record. The record explicitly finds that treating the Band’s licenses as "less worthy of recognition" than those of the District of Columbia or Ontario constitutes the very disparity the law seeks to prevent.

**III. The State’s Discretion Is Limited by the Obligation to Avoid Unjustified Disparity**

The State maintains that its reciprocity statutes are purely discretionary, citing the permissive language "authorized to enter into agreements," and argues that the district court erred by converting this discretion into a mandatory obligation.

While the statutes grant the State discretion to enter into agreements generally, the record establishes a specific legal limitation on that discretion: the State is obligated to afford equal treatment if denial dilutes tribal authority and no substantial justification exists. The record confirms that the first condition is met. Regarding the second, the State argues that the legislature’s policy judgment should control. However, the record explicitly states: "The record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment." This is a factual finding binding on this Court. The State cannot invoke its general statutory discretion to justify a specific disparity that the record has found lacks substantial justification. The obligation identified in the record operates as a constraint on the exercise of that discretion, preventing the State from using its authority to undermine the Band’s self-government without cause.

**IV. The Economic and Social Benefits Support the Finding of No Substantial Justification**

The State argues that the economic benefits of granting the exemption present a political question for the legislature and that the district court improperly intruded on legislative prerogative.

The Court disagrees. The record does not direct the State to adopt a new economic policy; rather, it observes that "the facts suggest that the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota will be furthered if the exemption is granted." This factual finding directly rebuts any assertion by the State that the disparity is justified by administrative or economic necessity. The legislature has declared a policy to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system" through reciprocity. The court’s ruling merely requires the State to apply this policy to the Red Lake Band on equal terms with other jurisdictions, consistent with the finding that doing so furthers the state's interests. The State’s refusal to extend reciprocity is thus revealed as an arbitrary disparity rather than a valid policy choice.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota has not demonstrated that its refusal to extend reciprocal recognition is justified by substantial considerations or that it avoids diluting the Red Lake Band’s governmental authority. The arguments presented by the Defense are insufficient to overturn the district court’s order. The ruling of this Court is to affirm the order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction in favor of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians.
