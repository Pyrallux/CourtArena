Your Honor, the question before this Court is whether the trial court erred in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One despite the cause of action accruing after dissolution. The trial court did not err—it correctly applied well-established Illinois law, and this Court should affirm its ruling.  

Under Illinois law, the Survival Statute codified at 805 ILCS 5/12.75 preserves claims against a dissolved corporation for liabilities that existed prior to dissolution. As the Illinois Appellate Court definitively held in *In re Estate of Kowalski*, 233 Ill. App. 3d 836, 839–40 (1992), "the survival statute does not require that the cause of action accrue before dissolution; rather, it requires that the underlying liability exist before dissolution. Thus, a claim for breach of a contract that was entered into before dissolution but breached after dissolution is covered by the survival statute." This principle is straightforward: the critical inquiry is whether the obligation itself arose before dissolution—not when the breach occurred.  

Here, PBS One became liable for the obligations under the Consulting Agreement as early as 1988 through the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement. The Consulting Agreement was executed in 1986 between Arthur and Pielet Inc., and PBS One’s assumption of those obligations in 1988 established the underlying liability long before its dissolution in June 1994. The fact that payments ceased in July 1998—years after dissolution—does not alter this fundamental reality. The breach may have occurred post-dissolution, but the liability itself was firmly in place before PBS One ceased to exist. *Kowalski* squarely controls: the Survival Statute preserves the claim because the obligation was created prior to dissolution.  

The trial court correctly recognized this distinction and applied the law without error. PBS One’s dissolution in 1994 did not extinguish the plaintiff’s right to enforce the Consulting Agreement because the liability existed before dissolution. To hold otherwise would undermine the Survival Statute’s purpose and permit corporations to evade liability for pre-dissolution obligations simply by dissolving before a breach occurs. This Court should affirm the trial court’s ruling.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in its application of the Survival Statute and that this Court should reverse the judgment below. The plaintiff's interpretation of the Survival Statute is overly broad and ignores critical distinctions between the existence of a contractual obligation and the proper party to enforce that obligation. Furthermore, the record demonstrates that PBS One's liability, if any, was effectively transferred and assumed through a series of transactions that substituted new entities as the contracting parties, with the plaintiff's explicit acquiescence.

## I. The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Claims Against a Dissolved Corporation Where the Liability Was Properly Transferred to a Successor Entity

The plaintiff relies heavily on *In re Estate of Kowalski*, 233 Ill. App. 3d 836 (1992), for the proposition that the Survival Statute preserves any claim where the underlying liability existed before dissolution. However, this reliance is misplaced. The defense does not dispute that the Consulting Agreement was executed before PBS One's dissolution. Rather, the defense contends that the liability was effectively transferred, assigned, and assumed by other entities before dissolution, and that the plaintiff accepted payments from those successor entities without objection.

The Survival Statute, codified at 805 ILCS 5/12.75, was enacted to prevent corporations from escaping liabilities incurred during their existence by dissolving without providing for creditors. However, the statute was not intended to create perpetual liability for a dissolved corporation when the very purpose of a valid assumption agreement was to substitute a new entity as the obligor. Here, the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement executed in April 1988 expressly assigned and transferred the Consulting Agreement obligations from Pielet Corp. to PBS One, and subsequently from PBS One to Pielet LP. The plaintiff acknowledges that Pielet LP, and later Midwest Metallics, made payments under the Consulting Agreement from 1988 until July 1998—a period of ten years.

Critically, the Assumption Agreement dated December 31, 1990, further transferred PBS One's partnership interest to National Material. Under the "Assumption" section, National Material accepted and assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest, and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." This language demonstrates that what was transferred was PBS One's interest as a limited partner—a 49.5% interest in the partnership—not a direct assumption of the Consulting Agreement obligations in PBS One's corporate capacity.

## II. The Plaintiff Accepted a Novation Substituting Pielet LP as the Obligating Party

The undisputed facts establish that the plaintiff was fully informed of the 1988 restructuring and agreed to the substitution of Pielet LP as the party obligated to make payments under the Consulting Agreement. Attorney Michael Zavis's affidavit and deposition testimony confirm that he met with Arthur and the plaintiff before the restructuring and explained that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement, that the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement, and would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]."

While Zavis testified that he did not intend to obtain a formal release from Arthur, his testimony also establishes that Arthur was informed that "the limited partnership would thereafter have such obligation" and that "he agreed thereto expressing his desire to have the transaction concluded as soon as possible since the new entity would be financially stronger than [Pielet Corp.]." This constitutes an implied novation, whereby the plaintiff accepted the new entity as the substitute obligor.

Under Illinois law, novation requires: (1) a previous valid obligation; (2) agreement between the parties to a new contract; (3) the extinguishment of the old obligation; and (4) a valid new contract. Here, all four elements are satisfied. The Consulting Agreement was valid. The plaintiff agreed to the substitution of Pielet LP as the paying party. The old obligation was extinguished by mutual understanding. And Pielet LP performed by making payments for ten years.

The plaintiff cannot now disregard this arrangement and seek to hold PBS One liable when the very entity the plaintiff agreed to accept as the substitute has ceased operations.

## III. Count IX Fails Because National Material Did Not Assume Direct Liability Under the Consulting Agreement

Count IX alleges that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest, and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement. However, the Assumption Agreement expressly limited National Material's assumption to obligations "in and to the extent of 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." A limited partnership interest is not equivalent to direct contractual liability. National Material acquired a 49.5% limited partnership interest; it did not execute a separate assumption agreement agreeing to pay Arthur $130,000 annually.

The distinction is critical. When PBS One transferred its partnership interest to National Material, what was transferred was an ownership interest in Pielet LP, not a direct corporate obligation to Arthur. The partnership itself—Pielet LP, later renamed Midwest Metallics—remained the entity obligated to perform under the Consulting Agreement. This is confirmed by the fact that Pielet LP and Midwest Metallics, not National Material, actually made the payments to Arthur throughout the 1990s.

To hold National Material directly liable for the Consulting Agreement obligations would require this Court to disregard the corporate form and the explicit language of the Assumption Agreement. This the law does not permit.

## IV. Count X Fails Because National Material Is Not a Mere Continuation of PBS One

Count X alleges that National Material is liable as the "successor" of PBS One because it is a "mere continuation" of that entity. This claim fails as a matter of law.

Under Illinois law, the "mere continuation" doctrine applies only when there is insufficient continuity between the predecessor and successor entities, such as when there is a mere change in form without a change in substance. Here, PBS One was a corporation engaged in the scrap metal business, and it sold its partnership interest to National Material, a different corporate entity. National Material did not acquire PBS One's assets in bulk; it acquired a limited partnership interest. There was no merger, no consolidation, and no purchase of substantially all of PBS One's assets.

Moreover, PBS One was dissolved in 1994, but not as part of a scheme to evade the Consulting Agreement obligations. Tang testified that PBS One was dissolved because "we have no purpose for us to have PBS One. We have all the investment, you know, all finished." PBS One had no employees, no creditors, and no ongoing business at the time of dissolution. The entity that had been performing under the Consulting Agreement—Pielet LP, later renamed Midwest Metallics—continued to exist and make payments until 1998.

The plaintiff's "mere continuation" theory conflates the transfer of a partnership interest with a corporate merger and should be rejected.

## V. Count XI Fails Because PBS One Was Not the Party in Breach

Finally, the plaintiff alleges in Count XI that PBS One "remained obligated under the Consulting Agreement" and breached when payments ceased in July 1998. This claim ignores the reality that PBS One had transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1990 and had been dissolved in 1994. At the time payments ceased in 1998, PBS One no longer existed.

Even assumingarguendo that PBS One retained some residual obligation, the entity that actually owed the duty to make payments was Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics—the entity that performed under the Consulting Agreement for a decade. The plaintiff should pursue its claim against the proper party—the entity that actually breached—rather than attempting to impose liability on a dissolved corporation that had effectively transferred its obligations years earlier.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling on Counts IX, X, and XI and enter judgment in favor of the defense. The Survival Statute does not preserve claims against a dissolved corporation where, as here, the liability was properly transferred to successor entities with the plaintiff's acquiescence. The plaintiff accepted Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics as the parties obligated under the Consulting Agreement and received ten years of payments from those entities. The plaintiff cannot now disregard this arrangement and seek to hold PBS One or National Material liable.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the parties' cross-motions for summary judgment regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The Court has reviewed the statements of undisputed facts, the Asset Purchase Agreement, the Assignment and Assumption Agreement, the Partnership Agreement, the Assumption Agreement dated December 31, 1990, and the relevant deposition testimony. The issue at hand is whether the defendants, PBS One, National Material, and NM Holding, remain liable for the Consulting Agreement originally entered into between Arthur Pielet and Pielet Inc.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Survival Statute Claim Against PBS One**

The prosecution argues that the trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.75, to preserve the breach of contract claim against PBS One despite the corporation's dissolution in 1994. The defense contends that the liability was transferred to Pielet LP via novation.

The Court finds the prosecution's argument persuasive regarding the application of the Survival Statute. Under Illinois law, the Survival Statute preserves claims against a dissolved corporation for liabilities that existed prior to dissolution, regardless of when the cause of action accrued. As stated in *In re Estate of Kowalski*, 233 Ill. App. 3d 836, 839–40 (1992), "the survival statute does not require that the cause of action accrue before dissolution; rather, it requires that the underlying liability exist before dissolution."

Here, the undisputed facts establish that PBS One assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 via the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement. This liability existed well before PBS One was dissolved in June 1994. Although the breach occurred in July 1998, *Kowalski* dictates that the claim is preserved because the underlying liability predated the dissolution.

The defense's argument regarding novation is unavailing based on the record provided. While the defense points to Attorney Zavis's testimony that Arthur was informed of the restructuring and agreed to the new entity making payments, the record also contains Zavis's explicit testimony that he did not intend to obtain a release or novation from Arthur. Specifically, Zavis testified: "I didn't have any intention of removing them [Pielet Corp.], but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed." Furthermore, he stated, "Nobody ever asked you to get a novation from him?" and answered, "No." Under Illinois law, a novation requires the mutual agreement of all parties to extinguish the old obligation and substitute a new debtor. *Frye v. Pizza Hut of America, Inc.*, 163 Ill. App. 3d 396, 403 (1987). The evidence does not support a finding of mutual intent to release PBS One from its obligations. Therefore, the liability remained with PBS One at the time of dissolution, and the Survival Statute applies.

**B. Count IX: National Material's Assumption of Liability**

The defense argues that National Material did not assume direct liability under the Consulting Agreement because the Assumption Agreement dated December 31, 1990, limited its assumption to obligations "in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." The prosecution argues that National Material assumed all obligations of PBS One.

The Court agrees with the defense. The language of the 1990 Assumption Agreement is specific and limiting. It states that National Material assumes "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." A limited partnership interest is an ownership stake in the partnership entity, distinct from the direct contractual obligations of the partnership itself or its partners regarding third-party contracts. The Consulting Agreement was an asset of the business held by the partnership, not a direct liability of the limited partner, PBS One. By assuming the "obligations... of the limited partnership interest," National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One as a limited partner, but it did not expressly agree to satisfy the debts of the partnership (Pielet LP) or the original debts of PBS One outside the context of the partnership interest. The fact that Pielet LP and Midwest Metallics made the payments, rather than National Material directly, further supports the interpretation that National Material did not assume direct liability for the Consulting Agreement.

**C. Count X: National Material as Successor in Interest**

The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One under the "mere continuation" doctrine because National Material purchased a partnership interest rather than the assets of PBS One. The prosecution asserts that National Material is a successor because it expressly assumed PBS One's rights and obligations.

The Court finds the defense's argument sound. The "mere continuation" exception to the general rule of non-successor liability typically applies where there is a continuity of ownership, management, personnel, location, and assets, indicating merely a change in form rather than substance. *River West, Inc. v. Amoco Oil Co.*, 194 Ill. 2d 377, 392 (2000). Here, the transaction involved National Material purchasing PBS One's 49.5% limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. PBS One was essentially a shell corporation holding this interest; it had no employees or ongoing business operations at the time of the transfer. National Material did not acquire PBS One's corporate assets; it acquired an asset (the partnership interest) *from* PBS One. This is a classic asset purchase or equity transfer, not a merger or consolidation. The fact that PBS One was later dissolved does not make National Material a successor to PBS One's corporate liabilities. The amendment to the Partnership Agreement in 1993 referring to National Material as a "successor" to PBS One pertains to rights and obligations *under the Partnership Agreement*, not to general corporate successor liability for all of PBS One's debts.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court enters the following preliminary ruling:

1.  **Count XI:** The trial court's ruling applying the Survival Statute to preserve the claim against PBS One is **AFFIRMED**. The underlying liability existed prior to dissolution, and the defense failed to establish a novation.
2.  **Count IX:** The defense's motion for summary judgment is **GRANTED**. National Material did not expressly assume direct liability under the Consulting Agreement; its assumption was limited to the obligations associated with the limited partnership interest.
3.  **Count X:** The defense's motion for summary judgment is **GRANTED**. National Material is not a "mere continuation" of PBS One under Illinois law.

## Plaintiff's Rebuttal to Defense Opening Statement  

The defense's assertion that the trial court erred in applying the Survival Statute is legally and factually unsound. ### I. The Survival Statute Applies Because PBS One’s Liability Existed Before Dissolution  
The defense mischaracterizes *In re Estate of Kowalski*, 233 Ill. App. 3d 836, 839–40 (1992), which squarely governs this issue. That case holds: "the survival statute does not require that the cause of action accrue before dissolution; rather, it requires that the underlying liability exist before dissolution." Here, the undisputed facts establish that PBS One assumed obligations under the Consulting Agreement in April 1988 through the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement. These documents created a direct liability for PBS One as a separate legal entity—distinct from the partnership structure—when it acquired the Consulting Agreement as part of the "Assets" transferred to it. That liability unquestionably existed before PBS One’s dissolution in June 1994.  

The defense’s claim that the Survival Statute "was not intended to create perpetual liability for a dissolved corporation when the very purpose of a valid assumption agreement was to substitute a new entity as the obligor" is legally incoherent. Illinois law recognizes that a corporation remains liable for obligations it assumes prior to dissolution, even if those obligations are later performed by successor entities. *Kowalski* explicitly rejects the defense’s argument that transfer to another entity extinguishes the original obligor’s liability. The statute’s purpose is to prevent dissolved corporations from evading pre-existing debts—not to shield them when they transfer obligations to others. Here, PBS One never released itself from the Consulting Agreement; it remained a liable party throughout.  

### II. No Novation Occurred Because the Plaintiff Never Agreed to Release PBS One  
The defense’s novation argument fails because Illinois law requires "the mutual agreement of all parties to extinguish the old obligation and substitute a new debtor." *Frye v. The trial court correctly recognized that "the liability remained with PBS One at the time of dissolution."

Here, PBS One’s liability arose in 1988 when it assumed the Consulting Agreement. The breach in 1998 occurred years after dissolution, but the survival statute explicitly covers such scenarios. The defense’s attempt to shift blame to Pielet LP or National Material is irrelevant; the statute focuses on whether the liability *existed* before dissolution, not who ultimately performed the obligation. PBS One’s dissolution in 1994 did not erase its pre-existing duty under the Consulting Agreement, and the plaintiff’s claim against it survives.

The defense’s attempt to recharacterize PBS One’s dissolution as a "successful transfer" of liability is a transparent effort to evade accountability for a pre-existing debt. **A. Count XI: Survival Statute Claim Against PBS One**

The central dispute on Count XI is whether the trial court erred in applying the Survival Statute to preserve the claim against PBS One, or whether PBS One was released from liability through novation.

The prosecution correctly argues that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.75, preserves the claim against PBS One because the underlying liability existed prior to the corporation's dissolution in 1994. As held in *In re Estate of Kowalski*, 233 Ill. App. 3d 836, 839–40 (1992), "the survival statute does not require that the cause of action accrue before dissolution; rather, it requires that the underlying liability exist before dissolution." The undisputed facts establish that PBS One assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988. Therefore, the liability existed well before the 1994 dissolution, satisfying the *Kowalski* standard.

The defense contends that a novation occurred, releasing PBS One from liability based on Arthur Pielet's acceptance of payments from Pielet LP for ten years and his stated desire for a "financially stronger" entity to pay him. The Court finds the prosecution's counter-argument persuasive. Under Illinois law, a novation requires "the mutual agreement of all parties to extinguish the old obligation and substitute a new debtor." *Frye v. Pizza Hut of America, Inc.*, 163 Ill. App. 3d 396, 403 (1987).

The record does not support a finding of mutual agreement to extinguish PBS One's obligation. Attorney Zavis, the architect of the restructuring, testified unequivocally that he did not intend to obtain a release or novation from Arthur. Specifically, Zavis stated: "Nobody ever asked you to get a novation from him?" and answered, "No." He further testified that Arthur "wasn't asked to give up any rights against anyone on anything." While the defense argues that Arthur's conduct in accepting payments implies an agreement to release, the prosecution correctly cites *Doe v. Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Chicago*, 2017 IL App (1st) 160661, ¶ 43, for the proposition that "[p]ayment by a third party does not operate as a discharge of the original obligor unless the creditor expressly agrees to release the obligor." Here, there was no express release, and the attorney's testimony negates the intent to release. Furthermore, as noted in *In re Marriage of Fink*, 2018 IL App (1st) 171100, ¶ 28, a corporation remains liable for its contractual obligations even after transferring assets to a partnership, absent an express release. Because no mutual agreement to release PBS One was established, the defense's novation argument fails.

Accordingly, the trial court's ruling on Count XI is **AFFIRMED**.

**B. Count IX: National Material's Assumption of Liability**

The prosecution did not provide a rebuttal argument challenging the trial court's ruling in favor of the defense on Count IX. It limits the assumption to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself, not the general corporate liabilities of PBS One or the direct contractual obligations of the partnership entity. The Consulting Agreement was an asset of the business held by the partnership, not a direct liability of the limited partner. By assuming the obligations of the limited partnership interest, National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One as a limited partner but did not assume direct liability for the partnership's debts. The absence of a rebuttal from the prosecution on this point confirms the sufficiency of the defense's position.

Therefore, the trial court's ruling on Count IX is **AFFIRMED**.

The prosecution did not provide a rebuttal argument challenging the trial court's ruling in favor of the defense on Count X. The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a successor under the "mere continuation" doctrine because it purchased a partnership interest rather than the assets of PBS One.

The Court finds the defense's argument sound. The "mere continuation" exception to the general rule of non-successor liability requires continuity of ownership, management, personnel, location, and assets. *River West, Inc. v. Amoco Oil Co.*, 194 Ill. 2d 377, 392 (2000). Here, National Material purchased PBS One's 49.5% limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. PBS One was a shell corporation with no employees or operations. The transaction constituted an asset purchase or equity transfer, not a merger or consolidation. The Consulting Agreement was listed as an Assumed Liability in Schedule 3.1(m). Attorney Zavis’s affidavit confirms this two-step process: first, PBS One purchased the assets and assumed the liabilities; second, the parties contributed their interests to the limited partnership. Therefore, PBS One did, in fact, assume direct liability for the Consulting Agreement in 1988.

Because PBS One assumed the liability in 1988, the underlying liability existed well before the corporation’s dissolution in 1994. As held in *In re Estate of Kowalski*, 233 Ill. App. 3d 836, 839–40 (1992), the Survival Statute requires only that the underlying liability exist prior to dissolution, not that the cause of action accrue. The statute therefore preserves the claim against PBS One.

The defense further contends that PBS One should be released based on principles of equity, noting that Arthur Pielet was informed of the structure and accepted payments from the partnership. However, Illinois law is clear that a novation, which would extinguish the old obligation, requires "the mutual agreement of all parties." *Frye v. Pizza Hut of America, Inc.*, 163 Ill. App. 3d 396, 403 (1987). The record is devoid of any express agreement by Arthur to release PBS One. Attorney Zavis testified unequivocally that no one asked him to obtain a novation or a release from Arthur. As stated in *Doe v. Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Chicago*, 2017 IL App (1st) 160661, ¶ 43, "payment by a third party does not operate as a discharge of the original obligor unless the creditor expressly agrees to release the obligor." Arthur’s acceptance of payments from the partnership does not constitute an express release of PBS One. Furthermore, *In re Marriage of Fink*, 2018 IL App (1st) 171100, ¶ 28, holds that a corporation remains liable for contractual obligations after transferring assets to a partnership absent an express release. No such release exists here.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling on Count XI is **AFFIRMED**.

**B. Count IX: National Material's Assumption of Liability**

The prosecution has not contested the trial court’s ruling in favor of the defense on Count IX. The defense correctly argues that the 1990 Assumption Agreement limits National Material’s liability.

The Assumption Agreement states that National Material "accept[s] and assume[s] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]." This language is unambiguous; it restricts the assumption to the obligations associated with the limited partnership interest itself. It does not state that National Material assumed the general corporate liabilities of PBS One or the direct operational debts of the partnership. The Consulting Agreement was an asset/obligation of the business held by the partnership, not a direct debt of the limited partner in its capacity as a partner. By assuming the obligations of the partnership interest, National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One as a limited partner but did not assume direct liability for the partnership's contracts. Without opposition from the prosecution, the defense’s interpretation of the contract stands.

Therefore, the trial court’s ruling on Count IX is **AFFIRMED**.

**C. Count X: National Material as Successor in Interest**

The prosecution has not contested the trial court’s ruling in favor of the defense on Count X. The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a successor under the "mere continuation" doctrine.

Under Illinois law, the "mere continuation" exception to the general rule of non-successor liability requires continuity of ownership, management, personnel, location, and assets. *River West, Inc. v. Amoco Oil Co.*, 194 Ill. 2d 377, 392 (2000). Here, National Material purchased PBS One’s 49.5% limited partnership interest. PBS One was a shell corporation with no employees or operations; its only asset was the investment in the partnership. The transaction was effectively an equity transfer, not a merger or a purchase of the business assets of a going concern. Consequently, there is no basis for finding that National Material is a mere continuation of PBS One for general liability purposes. The 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement referring to National Material as a "successor" pertains to rights within the partnership agreement, not general corporate successor liability.

Therefore, the trial court’s ruling on Count X is **AFFIRMED**.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court enters the following final ruling:

1.  **Count XI:** The trial court's ruling applying the Survival Statute to preserve the claim against PBS One is **AFFIRMED**. PBS One assumed direct liability in 1988, and no novation occurred to release that liability.
2.  **Count IX:** The trial court's ruling in favor of the defense is **AFFIRMED**. The 1990 Assumption Agreement limited National Material’s liability to the obligations of the limited partnership interest.
3.  **Count X:** The trial court's ruling in favor of the defense is **AFFIRMED**. National Material is not a "mere continuation" of PBS One under Illinois law.
