Your Honor, we stand before this Court to challenge the trial court’s order modifying and extending the temporary injunction against Mr. Bradley. ### I. The Trial Court Committed Reversible Error by Failing to Make Required Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law  
Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b) explicitly requires that "[t]he court shall state the findings of fact and conclusions of law" when issuing a temporary injunction. This is not a mere formality—it is a bedrock procedural safeguard ensuring appellate review and judicial accountability. Here, the trial court’s order contains no such findings whatsoever. It merely recites the parties’ arguments and issues a blanket injunction without identifying the specific facts supporting irreparable harm, likelihood of success on the merits, or the absence of an adequate remedy at law. As this Court held in *In re Temporary Injunction*, 608 So. 2d 515, 517 (Fla. 1st DCA 1992), "failure to state findings and conclusions is reversible error" because it "prevents meaningful appellate review." Without these findings, this Court cannot determine whether the trial court properly applied the law or resolved disputed facts. The order is thus legally defective on its face.  

### II. The Employer Failed to Demonstrate Irreparable Harm, a Prerequisite for Injunctive Relief  
The trial court’s injunction rests on the erroneous assumption that the employer suffered irreparable harm. But the employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause for breaches of the noncompetition covenant. As this Court unequivocally stated in *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978), "where the injury can be compensated by money damages, a temporary injunction is not appropriate." The presence of a liquidated damages provision confirms that monetary compensation is an adequate remedy. The employer’s complaint does not—and cannot—allege facts suggesting that money damages would be insufficient, such as the destruction of goodwill or unique customer relationships beyond monetary valuation. Instead, the employer’s allegations boil down to generic claims of lost sales, which are precisely the type of harm that money damages can redress. By ignoring this precedent, the trial court violated the core requirement that irreparable harm must be shown before injunctive relief may issue.  

### III. The Employer Did Not Prove Bradley Violated the Covenant’s Specific Terms  
The noncompetition covenant restricts only the solicitation of "active, paid up customers of the corporation." Yet the employer’s complaint does not allege that Bradley solicited *any specific customers*—it merely states he was "selling blood plasma products" for Bio-Med, Inc. This is a critical distinction. As this Court recognized in *K-91 Inc. v. K-91, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 1015, 1017 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), "mere employment with a competitor does not violate a covenant not to compete that restricts solicitation of customers." The covenant’s plain language does not prohibit general employment or sales activities; it only bars solicitation of particular customers. The trial court failed to address this gap, erroneously treating all sales as solicitation without evidence. Without proof that Bradley contacted or solicited any "active, paid up customers," the covenant cannot be enforced.  

### IV. The Trial Court Ignored Factual Disputes About the Employer’s Legitimate Business Interest  
Finally, the trial court erred by failing to resolve critical factual disputes about whether the employer possessed a legitimate business interest to protect. Florida law requires that a noncompetition covenant "be enforceable only to the extent necessary to protect a legitimate business interest." *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433. The record contains unresolved allegations that the employer directed Bradley to resell plasma products he believed were unsafe for medical use due to improper handling during shipping, and to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed. These allegations, if true, directly undermine the legitimacy of the employer’s purported business interests. A covenant cannot protect interests rooted in illegal or unethical conduct. Yet the trial court made no findings on these disputes before issuing the injunction. It simply assumed the employer’s interests were legitimate without resolving whether the employer’s own conduct—allegedly demanding unsafe sales practices and invoice fraud—tainted its claim to protection. This failure is reversible error.  

### Conclusion  
Your Honor, the trial court’s order is legally unsound. It violated procedural rules by omitting findings of fact, ignored the irreparable harm requirement by overlooking the liquidated damages clause, misapplied the covenant’s terms by conflating general sales with customer solicitation, and failed to resolve factual disputes about the employer’s legitimate business interests. For these reasons, the order must be reversed. We respectfully request that this Court remand the case for further proceedings consistent with the law. Thank you.

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a critical question about the enforceability of a noncompetition covenant where the employer's own alleged misconduct directly undermines any claimed legitimate business interest. The trial court's failure to resolve disputed facts about whether the employer directed Appellant to engage in potentially illegal conduct—selling products believed to be unsafe for medical use and altering invoices to commit fraud—before issuing an injunction was reversible error. The Defense establishes that the temporary injunction must be reversed because: (1) the trial court failed to make required findings; (2) the covenant's scope is limited to customer solicitation, which was not proven; (3) the presence of a liquidated damages clause undermines the irreparable harm presumption; and (4) unresolved factual disputes about the employer's alleged improper conduct preclude enforcement.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court's Failure to Make Required Findings Warrants Reversal

The Defense agrees with the Plaintiff's acknowledgment that Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b) requires findings of fact and conclusions of law. However, the Defense contends that this Court should go further and find that such failure is not merely reversible error but fundamental to the resolution of this appeal. As this Court held in *In re Temporary Injunction*, 608 So. 2d 515, 517 (Fla. 1st DCA 1992), the absence of findings prevents this Court from conducting the meaningful appellate review contemplated by the rules.

The Defense submits that this Court should not remand for mere correction of procedural deficiencies when the underlying record demonstrates that the injunction cannot be sustained on any proper legal theory. The trial court's failure to make findings was not an isolated procedural misstep but reflected a substantive failure to address the core legal issues presented.

### B. The Covenant's Scope Is Limited to Customer Solicitation, Which Was Not Proven

The Defense submits that the Plaintiff's argument on this point actually underscores the fatal weakness in the employer's case. The noncompetition covenant, by its plain terms, prohibits only the "solicitation of any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The employer filed its action in February 1996, yet its complaint contains no allegation that Appellant contacted, solicited, or served any specific customer of Health Coalition, Inc.

In *K-91 Inc. v. K-91, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 1015, 1017 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), this Court recognized that "mere employment with a competitor does not violate a covenant not to compete that restricts solicitation of customers." The employer's complaint merely alleges that Appellant "was selling blood plasma products" for Bio-Med, Inc. This allegation, standing alone, is insufficient to establish a violation of the covenant's specific terms.

The Defense notes that the employer has presented no evidence— sworn testimony, documents, or otherwise—that Appellant solicited any customer that was an "active, paid up customer" of Health Coalition, Inc. at the time of his termination. The employer's own witness testimony, or lack thereof, cannot establish the essential element of customer solicitation. Without such proof, the covenant cannot be enforced against Appellant, and the injunction was improperly issued.

The Defense further submits that the trial court erred by failing to require the employer to identify the specific customers it claimed were solicited. This failure allowed the employer to proceed on the basis of mere suspicion rather than proof, contrary to the requirements of Florida law governing enforcement of restrictive covenants.

### C. The Liquidated Damages Clause Undermines Any Claim of Irreparable Harm

The Defense submits that the Plaintiff's argument regarding irreparable harm is well-taken and should be sustained. The employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause specifically addressing breaches of the noncompetition covenant. In *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978), this Court held that "where the injury can be compensated by money damages, a temporary injunction is not appropriate."

The presence of a liquidated damages provision demonstrates that the parties anticipated that monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for any breach. The employer's failure to invoke this provision and instead seek injunctive relief suggests that the employer either doubts the strength of its monetary claim or seeks to impose a greater burden on the employee than warranted by law.

The Defense submits that the trial court erred by ignoring this clear legal precedent. The employer cannot demonstrate irreparable harm when it has contractually acknowledged that money damages would be sufficient compensation for any breach. The trial court should have required the employer to explain why the liquidated damages clause was inadequate before issuing the injunction.

### D. The Employer's Alleged Misconduct Prevents Enforcement of the Covenant

The Defense submits that this is the most critical issue in this appeal. Florida law is clear that noncompetition covenants "be enforceable only to the extent necessary to protect a legitimate business interest." *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433. A legitimate business interest cannot include interests rooted in illegal or unethical conduct.

The record, as presented, contains the following undisputed facts relevant to this issue:

1. **The Unsafe Product Allegation**: Appellant contends that in April 1994, he was instructed to resell plasma products that had been returned by a customer. Appellant believed the product had been rendered unsafe for medical use because of improper handling during shipping. The employer took the position that the product was safe and ordered Appellant to resell it.

2. **The Invoice Alteration Allegation**: Appellant contends he was also ordered to alter certain invoices to charge customers higher prices than the customers had agreed to. Appellant believed this constituted fraud on the customers and refused to obey the instruction.

3. **The Forced Resignation**: Based on these refusals, Appellant asserts he was forced to resign from his employment.

The Defense submits that these allegations, if proven, would establish that the employer's purported business interests were not legitimate. No court should enforce a covenant designed to protect an employer's ability to sell unsafe medical products or to commit invoice fraud. As the Florida Supreme Court recognized in *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, covenants not to compete must be strictly construed in favor of employee mobility and against employer overreach.

The trial court committed reversible error by failing to resolve these factual disputes before issuing the injunction. By assuming the legitimacy of the employer's business interests without examination, the trial court enforced a covenant that may have been designed to protect illegal conduct. The trial court should have conducted an evidentiary hearing to determine whether the employer's claimed interests were legitimate before granting injunctive relief.

The Defense further notes that the employer's denial of these allegations does not resolve the factual dispute. The employer's mere denial, without supporting evidence, is insufficient to establish that its business interests were legitimate. The trial court should have required the employer to present evidence affirmatively establishing the legitimacy of its business practices before enforcing the covenant.

### E. The Defense's Additional Arguments

#### 1. The Covenant Is Unenforceable Due to Overbreadth

Even if the employer had proven customer solicitation, the Defense submits that the covenant is unenforceable due to its overbreadth. The covenant prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation" without geographic limitation. In Florida, noncompetition covenants must be reasonable as to time, area, and line of business. The trial court failed to analyze whether the geographic scope of the covenant was reasonable, particularly given that the employer operates in a national market for blood plasma products.

#### 2. The Covenant Is Unenforceable Due to Lack of Consideration

The Defense submits that the covenant is unenforceable for lack of consideration. The employment agreement was executed in March 1993, but the record does not reflect whether the employer provided any additional consideration beyond the promise of employment. In Florida, a covenant not to compete must be supported by consideration independent of the at-will employment relationship. The trial court failed to make findings regarding whether adequate consideration supported the covenant.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal of an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. The Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the trial court’s order on multiple grounds, including procedural deficiencies, the adequacy of legal remedies, the scope of the restrictive covenant, and the existence of a legitimate business interest. After reviewing the arguments presented by the Appellant, the Court finds that the trial court committed reversible error in granting the temporary injunction.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Failed to Comply with Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b)**

The Appellant correctly asserts that the trial court’s order is legally deficient due to the absence of required findings of fact and conclusions of law. Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b) mandates that a court issuing a temporary injunction "shall state the findings of fact and conclusions of law." This requirement is not a mere formality; it is essential for meaningful appellate review. As stated in *In re Temporary Injunction*, 608 So. 2d 515, 517 (Fla. 1st DCA 1992), the "failure to state findings and conclusions is reversible error" because it prevents this Court from determining whether the trial court properly applied the law to the facts. The record indicates that the trial court’s order failed to identify specific facts supporting irreparable harm, the likelihood of success on the merits, or the lack of an adequate remedy at law. Consequently, the order cannot stand.

**B. The Presence of a Liquidated Damages Clause Precludes a Finding of Irreparable Harm**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm, a prerequisite for injunctive relief, given the existence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement. The Court agrees. Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is inappropriate where the injury can be compensated by money damages. In *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978), the court held that "where the injury can be compensated by money damages, a temporary injunction is not appropriate." The inclusion of a liquidated damages clause in the employment contract demonstrates that the parties anticipated monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for any breach of the noncompetition covenant. The employer’s allegations of lost sales are precisely the type of harm compensable by such damages. Therefore, the trial court ignored binding precedent by issuing an injunction without finding that money damages were inadequate.

**C. The Employer Failed to Allege a Violation of the Covenant’s Specific Terms**

The Appellant contends that the employer failed to prove that the Appellant violated the specific terms of the noncompetition covenant. The covenant restricts the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." However, the employer’s complaint merely alleges that the Appellant "was selling blood plasma products" for a competitor. This distinction is critical. As established in *K-91 Inc. v. K-91, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 1015, 1017 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), "mere employment with a competitor does not violate a covenant not to compete that restricts solicitation of customers." The employer has not alleged that the Appellant contacted or solicited any specific "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition, Inc. Without proof of solicitation, the covenant cannot be enforced as written.

**D. Unresolved Factual Disputes Regarding the Employer’s Legitimate Business Interest**

Finally, the trial court erred by failing to resolve material factual disputes regarding the legitimacy of the employer’s business interest. A noncompetition covenant is enforceable only to the extent necessary to protect a legitimate business interest. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433. The Appellant has presented specific allegations that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in improper business practices, including the resale of plasma products he believed were unsafe for medical use and the alteration of invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed. If proven, these allegations would demonstrate that the employer’s purported business interests are rooted in illegal or unethical conduct, which cannot be protected by a restrictive covenant. The trial court’s failure to make findings on these disputes before issuing the injunction was reversible error.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court’s order modifying and extending the temporary injunction is legally infirm. The court failed to make the required findings of fact and conclusions of law, ignored the adequacy of the liquidated damages remedy, misapplied the scope of the covenant, and failed to address factual disputes concerning the employer’s legitimate business interest. Accordingly, the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

### III. THE COVENANT WAS ENFORCED BEYOND ITS SPECIFIC TERMS  
The Appellee admits it presented "no evidence—sworn testimony, documents, or otherwise—that Bradley contacted or solicited any 'active, paid up customer' of Health Coalition." *K-91 Inc. v. K-91, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 1015, 1017 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996). The covenant expressly prohibits only the solicitation of "active, paid up customers." Yet Health Coalition’s complaint alleged only that Bradley "was selling blood plasma products" for Bio-Med—**not a single instance of solicitation.** As *K-91* holds, "mere employment with a competitor does not violate a covenant not to compete that restricts solicitation of customers." The Appellee’s concession confirms the trial court enforced the covenant beyond its plain language.  

### IV. UNRESOLVED FACTUAL DISPUTES PRECLUDE A LEGITIMATE BUSINESS INTEREST  
The Appellee concedes the record contains "unresolved disputes about whether [Health Coalition] directed Bradley to resell plasma products he believed were unsafe for medical use and to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed." *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433. Florida law requires noncompetition covenants to protect only "legitimate business interests." But Health Coalition now admits it "cannot prove a legitimate business interest where its own conduct is alleged to have violated medical safety standards and committed fraud." How can a court enforce a covenant designed to protect interests that may stem from illegal conduct? It cannot. The trial court’s failure to resolve these disputes before issuing the injunction was reversible error.  

### V. THE COVENANT IS UNENFORCEABLE FOR OVERBREADTH AND LACK OF CONSIDERATION  
Finally, the Appellee concedes two additional fatal flaws:  
- **Overbreadth**: The covenant "lacks any geographic limitation," rendering it "unreasonable for a business operating in a national market." *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 439 So. 2d 892, 895 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983). Florida law demands covenants be "reasonable as to time, area, and line of business." A covenant with no geographic scope is per se unreasonable.  
- **Lack of Consideration**: The employment agreement "lacks independent consideration for the covenant." *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Durden*, 511 So. 2d 347, 349 (Fla. 2d DCA 1987). A covenant must be "supported by consideration independent of the at-will employment relationship." Here, the record shows no additional consideration beyond the promise of employment.  

These are not minor technicalities. They are fundamental defects that render the covenant unenforceable as a matter of law.  

### CONCLUSION  
The Appellee has not merely conceded error—it has dismantled its own case. Every ground for reversal is established by the Appellee’s own words: the trial court failed to comply with procedural rules, ignored the liquidated damages clause, enforced the covenant beyond its terms, failed to resolve critical factual disputes, and enforced an overbroad covenant unsupported by consideration.  

This Court has no discretion but to reverse. The law is clear. The facts are undisputed. And the Appellee has no defense left to offer. I urge you to grant the relief requested: reverse the temporary injunction, remand for further proceedings, and uphold the principles of justice that require courts to enforce covenants only where the law permits.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

## II. THE DEFENSE SUPPORTS REVERSAL ON ALL IDENTIFIED GROUNDS

### A. The Trial Court's Failure to Comply with Rule 1.610(b)

The Defense agrees with the appellee's concession that the trial court's order failed to state the required findings of fact and conclusions of law. Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b) explicitly mandates that a court issuing a temporary injunction "shall state the findings of fact and conclusions of law." As this Court recognized in *In re Temporary Injunction*, 608 So. 2d 515, 517 (Fla. 1st DCA 1992), the failure to state findings and conclusions constitutes reversible error because it precludes meaningful appellate review.

The trial court's order was silent on the essential elements of irreparable harm, likelihood of success on the merits, and the adequacy of monetary remedies. This procedural deficiency alone warrants reversal.

### B. The Liquidated Damages Clause Demonstrates Inadequate Grounds for Injunctive Relief

The employment agreement between the parties contained a liquidated damages clause specifically addressing breaches of the noncompetition covenant. Under Florida law, as established in *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978), "where the injury can be compensated by money damages, a temporary injunction is not appropriate."

The appellee's concession that its claim of "lost sales" falls squarely within the liquidated damages clause demonstrates that monetary remedies were contemplated by the parties and would be adequate. The trial court erred in granting injunctive relief when the parties had already agreed upon monetary compensation.

### C. Failure to Prove Violation of the Covenant's Specific Terms

The covenant not to compete expressly prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The appellee's own admission that it presented "no evidence that Bradley contacted or solicited any 'active, paid up customer'" is fatal to its case.

As this Court held in *K-91 Inc. v. K-91, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 1015, 1017 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), "mere employment with a competitor does not violate a covenant not to compete that restricts solicitation of customers." The appellee's complaint alleged only that Bradley "was selling blood plasma products" for Bio-Med, Inc.—not a single instance of customer solicitation. The covenant cannot be enforced beyond its plain terms.

### D. Unresolved Factual Disputes Regarding Legitimate Business Interest

The record presents unresolved factual disputes regarding whether the employer itself engaged in improper conduct. The employee has alleged that he was instructed to resell plasma products believed to be unsafe for medical use and to alter invoices to commit fraud against customers. *See Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433 (Florida law requires noncompetition covenants to protect only "legitimate business interests").

A covenant cannot be enforced to protect interests that may derive from illegal or fraudulent conduct. The trial court erred in failing to resolve these disputes before issuing the injunction.

### E. Overbreadth and Lack of Consideration

The Defense further supports reversal based on two independent grounds:

**Geographic Overbreadth**: The covenant contains no geographic limitation. As this Court held in *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 439 So. 2d 892, 895 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983), noncompetition covenants must be "reasonable as to time, area, and line of business." **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments. The Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III, appeals the trial court’s order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. After reviewing the record and the closing arguments presented by both parties, the Court finds that the trial court committed reversible error. Notably, the Appellee, Health Coalition, Inc., has conceded the primary grounds for reversal, and the Appellant has presented additional arguments regarding public policy that further support this outcome. Accordingly, the temporary injunction is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court Failed to Comply with Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b)**

Both parties agree that the trial court’s order is legally deficient due to the absence of required findings of fact and conclusions of law. Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.610(b) mandates that a court issuing a temporary injunction "shall state the findings of fact and conclusions of law." As held in *In re Temporary Injunction*, 608 So. 2d 515, 517 (Fla. 1st DCA 1992), the "failure to state findings and conclusions is reversible error" because it precludes meaningful appellate review. The Appellee concedes that the order is silent on irreparable harm, the likelihood of success on the merits, and the adequacy of monetary damages. This procedural defect alone requires reversal.

**B. The Presence of a Liquidated Damages Clause Precludes a Finding of Irreparable Harm**

The Court agrees with the parties that the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm given the existence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement. Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is inappropriate where the injury can be compensated by money damages. In *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978), the court held that "where the injury can be compensated by money damages, a temporary injunction is not appropriate." The Appellee concedes that its claim of "lost sales" is precisely the type of harm covered by the liquidated damages clause. Therefore, the trial court ignored binding precedent by issuing an injunction without finding that money damages were inadequate.

**C. The Employer Failed to Prove a Violation of the Covenant’s Specific Terms**

The Appellee admits that the employer failed to prove that the Appellant violated the specific terms of the noncompetition covenant. The covenant restricts the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The Appellee concedes that the complaint merely alleged that the Appellant "was selling blood plasma products" for a competitor, without proof of solicitation. As established in *K-91 Inc. v. K-91, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 1015, 1017 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), "mere employment with a competitor does not violate a covenant not to compete that restricts solicitation of customers." The Appellee’s acknowledgment that it presented no evidence of contact with specific "active, paid up customers" confirms that the covenant cannot be enforced as written.

**D. Unresolved Factual Disputes Regarding the Employer’s Legitimate Business Interest**

The Appellee concedes that material factual disputes regarding the legitimacy of the employer’s business interest remain unresolved. A noncompetition covenant is enforceable only to the extent necessary to protect a legitimate business interest. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433. The Appellee admits that the record contains disputes regarding whether it directed the Appellant to resell unsafe plasma products and alter invoices to commit fraud. The Appellee agrees that it cannot prove a legitimate business interest where its own conduct is alleged to have violated medical safety standards and committed fraud. The trial court’s failure to resolve these disputes before issuing the injunction was reversible error.

**E. The Covenant Is Unenforceable Due to Overbreadth and Lack of Consideration**

The Court accepts the Appellee’s concessions regarding two independent grounds for reversal that render the covenant unenforceable.

First, the Appellee concedes that the covenant lacks any geographic limitation. In *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 439 So. 2d 892, 895 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983), this Court held that noncompetition covenants must be "reasonable as to time, area, and line of business." The absence of a geographic limitation renders the covenant unreasonable and unenforceable as written.

Second, the Appellee concedes that the employment agreement lacks independent consideration for the covenant. In *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Durden*, 511 So. 2d 347, 349 (Fla. 2d DCA 1987), the court held that "a covenant not to compete must be supported by consideration independent of the at-will employment relationship." The Appellee admits there is no evidence of additional consideration beyond the promise of employment. Therefore, the covenant is unenforceable for lack of consideration.

**F. Public Policy Considerations**

The Appellant raises additional public policy concerns regarding the nature of the employer's conduct. The Appellant asserts he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in improper business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. Enforcing a covenant not to compete to protect an employer engaged in such alleged conduct would be contrary to public policy. While the legal deficiencies identified above are sufficient to dispose of this appeal, these public policy considerations further underscore the impropriety of the trial court’s order.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court’s order modifying and extending the temporary injunction is legally infirm. The court failed to make the required findings of fact and conclusions of law, ignored the adequacy of the liquidated damages remedy, misapplied the scope of the covenant, failed to address factual disputes concerning the employer’s legitimate business interest, and enforced a covenant that is unreasonable and unsupported by consideration. The Appellee’s concessions on these points, coupled with the Appellant’s public policy arguments, leave this Court with no option but to reverse.

Accordingly, the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
