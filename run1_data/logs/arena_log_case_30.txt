FACTS:
The State of Hawaii appeals a circuit court order granting a motion to suppress evidence filed by defendant David A. Haili before trial. Defendant was charged with possession of prohibited firearm in violation of Hawaii Revised Statutes § 134-8 (1976).

At approximately 10:30 p.m. on May 5, 1979, a security guard posted at the Kapaa Quarry heard 6-7 gunshots and also heard a bullet hit a building next to his. He immediately reported the incident to the police and added that he felt that the shots were coming from the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway. The guard furnished no other information.

A police bulletin of the incident was broadcasted. Within minutes, Officer Charles Davis was at the designated portion of the freeway and stood with his rifle at the chain link fence which serves to barricade the uncompleted end of the freeway. A few minutes later, Officer Davis observed a car travelling at a reasonable speed on the uncompleted freeway approaching with headlights on. He stopped the car. From a distance of sixty feet, he ordered the four occupants out of the car. 
At this point, additional officers arrived and they conducted a “pat down” of the four occupants. The four then sat on the ground and were guarded by one officer. The other officers scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight and spotted a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. They also saw a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat. Upon pushing the front seat forward, the officers saw a ukulele case, unlatched although the cover was down. Officer Davis opened the case and found a modified rifle and plastic boxes containing .22-caliber rifle ammunition.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Supreme Court of the State of Hawaii

### STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence, and this Court should affirm that ruling. The prosecution's argument fails for multiple reasons: the initial stop of the vehicle was unlawful, the subsequent search exceeded any permissible scope, and the "pat down" of the occupants was unsupported by reasonable articulable suspicion. The evidence obtained as a result of these constitutional violations must be suppressed.

## I. THE INITIAL STOP OF THE VEHICLE WAS UNLAWFUL

The prosecution contends that Officer Davis had sufficient justification to stop the vehicle, but this argument ignores critical facts establishing that the stop was unsupported by reasonable suspicion.

The sole basis for the stop was that Officer Davis observed a car traveling at a "reasonable speed" on the uncompleted H-3 freeway at 10:30 p.m. with headlights on. The prosecution argues this alone justified the stop, but this reasoning proves too much. An uncompleted freeway, while not open to public traffic, is not a crime scene. The mere presence of a vehicle on an unfinished roadway does not, without more, establish reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. The Supreme Court has consistently held that an investigatory stop must be based on "specific and articulable facts" demonstrating that the person stopped is engaged in criminal activity, as established in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968).

Here, Officer Davis observed nothing more than a car driving on the uncompleted freeway. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with headlights on—conduct entirely consistent with lawful use of the roadway, even if that use was technically prohibited. There was no evidence of traffic violations, erratic driving, or any behavior suggesting criminal activity. The security guard's report of gunshots established only that shots had been fired from the general area; it did not identify any suspect vehicle or provide description linking this particular car to the gunfire. The prosecution's reliance on the totality of circumstances under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), is misplaced because the totality of circumstances here fails to establish reasonable suspicion for the initial stop.

## II. THE FLASHLIGHT OBSERVATION EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF PERMISSIBLE PLAIN VIEW

The prosecution invokes *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), for the proposition that flashlight use is not a search, but this argument misapplies the holding. In *Texas v. Brown*, the officer was lawfully stopped and the observation occurred during a lawful traffic stop where the officer had already lawfully approached the vehicle. The Court emphasized that the observation occurred because the driver "opened the door" and the officer was "in a position to observe" what was inside.

Here, by contrast, Officer Davis observed the vehicle from sixty feet away while standing outside the chain-link fence. He did not approach the vehicle, nor did he have a lawful basis to do so. The prosecution's argument that shining a flashlight from sixty feet away constitutes permissible "plain view" observation stretches the doctrine beyond recognition. The officers were not lawfully positioned inside the vehicle or at a location where they had a right to be observing its interior. The "plain view" doctrine requires that the officer have a lawful right to be in the position from which he observes the evidence. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971). Officer Davis was positioned outside the barricade, not inside or adjacent to the vehicle in any lawful manner.

Moreover, the subsequent actions of the officers demonstrate that the flashlight observation was merely the first step in a far more extensive search. The officers did not simply observe; they "scanned the interior," pushed the front seat forward to see behind it, and opened a ukulele case. These actions constitute a search, not mere observation.

## III. THE SEARCH EXCEEDED ANY PERMITTED SCOPE

Even assuming arguendo that some initial observation was permissible, the subsequent search of the vehicle exceeded all constitutional bounds.

After observing the rifle casing on the front floor—an observation the prosecution contends was lawful—the officers proceeded to conduct what was unmistakably a full search of the vehicle's interior. They pushed the front seat forward to view the rear floor, examined a partially concealed black case, and ultimately opened a ukulele case to discover the modified rifle and ammunition. Each step exceeded the scope of any lawful search.

The automobile exception under *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), permits warrantless searches of vehicles only when there is probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime. However, the exception does not authorize a wholesale search of every interior space and container. The Supreme Court has recognized limits, requiring that the search be "reasonable" in scope. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982). Here, the officers had no probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime at the time they began their search. The security guard's report of gunshots, without more, did not establish that this particular vehicle contained evidence of any crime.

The prosecution argues that the totality of circumstances established probable cause, but this conflates the standard for stop with the standard for search. Even accepting that the officer had reasonable suspicion to investigate, that suspicion does not automatically escalate to probable cause for a full vehicle search. The discovery of a rifle casing came only after the officers exceeded the scope of any lawful stop.

## IV. THE "PAT DOWN" OF THE OCCUPANTS WAS UNLAWFUL

The defense further submits that the "pat down" of the four occupants was unlawful and unsupported by any facts indicating the occupants were armed or dangerous.

The prosecution makes no argument whatsoever justifying the pat-down search. This is telling. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a pat-down search for weapons requires that the officer have reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous. Here, the officers had no such belief. The occupants were ordered out of the car and sat on the ground. There was no indication they were armed, no threat was made, and no suspicious bulges or movements were observed. The pat-down was a blanket search of all four occupants without individualized reasonable suspicion.

## V. THE FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE DOCTRINE

Because each of the above constitutional violations formed the basis for discovering the evidence, the evidence must be suppressed under the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The initial unlawful stop led to the unlawful pat-down, which led to the unlawful search of the vehicle interior, which led to the discovery of the rifle and ammunition. None of this evidence is admissible.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the initial stop, the pat-down, or the subsequent search of the vehicle were lawful. The evidence was obtained through constitutional violations, and its suppression was proper.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State argues that the warrantless search of the vehicle was justified under the automobile exception and that the officer’s observations were lawful. The Defendant contends that the initial stop was unsupported by reasonable suspicion, the subsequent search exceeded the permissible scope, and the "pat down" of the occupants was unlawful. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the Court reverses the circuit court’s suppression order.

**I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The threshold issue is whether Officer Davis had reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle. The Fourth Amendment requires that an investigatory stop be based on "specific and articulable facts" suggesting criminal activity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The Defendant argues that merely driving on an uncompleted freeway at a reasonable speed does not constitute criminal activity sufficient to justify a stop. However, this view isolates the vehicle’s presence from the context in which it was observed. The record establishes that a security guard reported hearing six to seven gunshots and a bullet striking a building, specifically identifying the "unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway" as the source. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231-32 (1983) (holding that probable cause is based on the totality of the circumstances).

When Officer Davis arrived at the designated location, he observed a car driving on that same "unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway" at 10:30 p.m. The unfinished freeway was barricaded and closed to public traffic. The coincidence of the vehicle's presence in the exact location where a violent crime was reported minutes prior provides the specific and articulable facts required to justify an investigatory stop. See *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981) (allowing officers to draw on their experience and common sense to make inferences from the facts). Therefore, the stop was lawful under *Terry*.

**II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN WAS NOT A SEARCH**

The Defendant contends that the flashlight scan constituted an unlawful search because Officer Davis was sixty feet away and not lawfully positioned to view the interior. The Court disagrees.

The Supreme Court has explicitly held that the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983). In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court reasoned that the use of artificial light merely enhances what is otherwise visible to the naked eye and does not constitute an intrusion on privacy. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. at 740.

Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene of a reported crime, standing at the chain-link fence barricade. From this vantage point, he shone a flashlight into the vehicle. Under the "plain view" doctrine, an officer may seize evidence that is in plain view if the officer is lawfully present in the place from which the evidence is viewed. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465-66 (1971). Because Officer Davis was lawfully responding to a report of gunfire, his observation of the rifle casing on the front floor was permissible and did not violate the Defendant's Fourth Amendment rights.

**III. THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE WAS JUSTIFIED BY PROBABLE CAUSE**

Once the officers observed the .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor, they had probable cause to search the entire vehicle. The "automobile exception" to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement permits a warrantless search of a vehicle if there is probable cause to believe it contains evidence of a crime. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153-54 (1925).

The totality of the circumstances established probable cause: a report of gunshots from a specific, restricted location; the vehicle’s presence in that restricted location; and the discovery of rifle casing in plain view. This combination of facts created a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained evidence of the reported crime. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238.

Furthermore, the scope of the search was reasonable. Under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823-24 (1982), police may search every part of a vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search. Upon seeing the casing, the officers were authorized to look for the firearm. Pushing the front seat forward to check the rear floor and examining the unlatched ukulele case were logical steps in the search for a weapon, particularly given the report of multiple gunshots. The fact that the ukulele case was unlatched supports the conclusion that opening it was a minimal intrusion consistent with the search for a rifle.

**IV. THE UNLAWFUL PAT-DOWN DOES NOT REQUIRE SUPPRESSION OF THE EVIDENCE FOUND IN THE CAR**

The Defense correctly notes that the State failed to justify the "pat down" of the four occupants. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. at 27, a pat-down is only justified if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The State provided no argument or facts to support this specific intrusion.

However, the motion to suppress targets the firearm and ammunition found inside the vehicle, not on the occupants. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), requires exclusion only if the evidence was obtained as a result of the constitutional violation. Here, the discovery of the rifle casing during the lawful flashlight scan provided independent probable cause to search the vehicle. The search of the vehicle and the discovery of the rifle were not the fruit of the unlawful pat-down, but rather the result of the plain view observation and the valid application of the automobile exception. Therefore, the illegality of the pat-down does not taint the evidence seized from the car.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion, the flashlight observation did not constitute a search, and the subsequent search of the vehicle was justified by probable cause under the automobile exception. While the pat-down of the occupants was not justified by the State, it does not require the suppression of the evidence found in the vehicle. Accordingly, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **REVERSED**.

## I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

The Prosecution's argument that the vehicle's presence on the unfinished freeway constituted sufficient reasonable suspicion rests on a faulty foundation that conflates mere proximity with articulable suspicion of criminal activity.

The Prosecution invokes *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), but that case addressed probable cause, not the lower threshold of reasonable suspicion. Even under the more lenient probable cause standard, the Gates Court emphasized the importance of corroboration. Here, the Defense submits that the guard's vague assertion—based on nothing more than a "feeling"—cannot satisfy the specific and articulable facts requirement of *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

### B. Mere Presence in a Location Does Not Establish Reasonable Suspicion

The Prosecution's argument that the vehicle's presence on a closed roadway creates reasonable suspicion improperly relies on the doctrine of guilt by association. The fact that a vehicle was on an unfinished freeway at 10:30 p.m. is insufficient to justify a stop. As this Court recognized in the preliminary ruling, the Defendant argued that "merely driving on an uncompleted freeway at a reasonable speed does not constitute criminal activity." The Prosecution has not addressed this argument directly but instead attempts to obscure it by referencing the totality of circumstances.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981), for the proposition that officers may draw reasonable inferences. The mere coincidence of a vehicle being in a location where gunshots were reported—without more—does not create a reasonable inference that the vehicle's occupants committed a crime. The Prosecution's reliance on *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), is misplaced. While that case held that the use of a flashlight does not constitute a search per se, it did not hold that officers may use flashlights to peer into vehicles from distances that would otherwise render interior contents invisible.

### A. Officer Davis Was Not Lawfully Positioned to View the Vehicle's Interior

The facts establish that Officer Davis observed the vehicle from a distance of sixty feet while standing at the chain-link fence. At such a distance, the interior of the vehicle would not be visible to the naked eye without artificial illumination. The Prosecution argues that *Texas v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465-66 (1971).

When an officer uses artificial light to enhance visibility beyond what would be naturally observable from a lawful vantage point, such action constitutes a search implicating Fourth Amendment protections. The officer's position at the fence—sixty feet from the vehicle—was not a position from which the interior would be naturally observable. The flashlight scan was not a mere enhancement of natural vision but an intrusion into the vehicle's private space.

### B. The Rifle Casing Was Not "Immediately Apparent"

Even assuming Officer Davis was lawfully positioned, the plain view doctrine requires that the evidence be "immediately apparent" without further investigation. *Coolidge*, 403 U.S. at 466. A rifle casing on the front floor of a vehicle, observable only through the use of a flashlight from sixty feet away, is not immediately apparent. The officers needed to use a flashlight specifically to locate the casing—indicating that its discovery required more than passive observation.

The Prosecution argues that the totality of circumstances created a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. Rifle casings are legitimate items that lawful citizens may possess.

The Prosecution's reliance on *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), is misplaced. In *Carroll*, the Court upheld the search of a vehicle only where there was probable cause to believe the vehicle contained bootleg liquor. Here, the discovery of a single casing does not create a fair probability that the vehicle contains evidence of a crime. The report of gunshots, without more, does not establish that the vehicle's occupants were involved.

### B. The Search of the Ukulele Case Exceeded Permissible Scope

Even if probable cause existed to search the vehicle, the search of the ukulele case exceeded the scope of the automobile exception. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823-24 (1982), permits searches of areas where the object of the search might be concealed. However, the officers pushed the front seat forward to access the rear floor, opened an unlatched ukulele case, and discovered a modified rifle. This sequence of actions exceeds the scope of a search for evidence of the reported crime.

A ukulele case is not a typical container for a rifle, and the officers had no probable cause to believe that the rifle would be found in a musical instrument case. The intrusion into the ukulele case was not justified by the automobile exception and constitutes an unlawful search.

The Prosecution argues that the unlawful pat-down does not require suppression because the evidence was discovered through the lawful flashlight scan. This argument fails for two reasons.

The officers then conducted the flashlight scan, discovered the casing, and searched the vehicle. Each step in this chain is tainted by the initial constitutional violation. The fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine requires suppression of all evidence derived from the unlawful stop. *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 484.

**STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State argues that the warrantless search of the vehicle was justified under the automobile exception and that the officer’s observations were lawful. The Defendant contends that the initial stop was unsupported by reasonable suspicion, the subsequent search exceeded the permissible scope, and the "pat down" of the occupants was unlawful. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both parties, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling reversing the circuit court’s suppression order.

**I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The threshold issue is whether Officer Davis had reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle. The Fourth Amendment requires that an investigatory stop be based on "specific and articulable facts" suggesting criminal activity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The Defendant argues that the security guard’s report lacked the indicia of reliability required to justify a stop, citing *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000). In *J.L.*, an anonymous tip reported that a young male in a plaid shirt was carrying a gun, but provided no predictive information or corroborating details. Here, the situation is distinct. The security guard was not an anonymous tipster reporting a prediction of future crime; he was an eyewitness reporting a completed violent act—hearing six to seven gunshots and a bullet striking a building. While the guard stated he "felt" the shots were coming from the unfinished freeway, this was a directional assessment based on his immediate sensory experience of the gunfire, not a speculative guess.

Furthermore, the "totality of the circumstances" supports the stop. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231-32 (1983). Officer Davis arrived at the specific, restricted location identified by the guard within minutes of the report. He found a vehicle driving on a barricaded, uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. The coincidence of the vehicle’s presence in the exact location where a violent crime was reported provides the specific and articulable facts required to justify an investigatory stop. See *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981). The vehicle was not merely "present" in a public area; it was in a closed construction site where it had no lawful business, immediately following a report of gunfire. This constitutes reasonable suspicion.

**II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN WAS NOT A SEARCH**

The Defendant contends that the flashlight scan constituted an unlawful search because Officer Davis was sixty feet away and the interior was not naturally visible. The Court disagrees.

The Supreme Court has explicitly held that the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983). In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court reasoned that the use of artificial light merely enhances what is otherwise visible to the naked eye and does not constitute an intrusion on privacy. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. at 740. The Defendant’s argument that the sixty-foot distance renders the observation unlawful ignores the holding of *Texas v. Brown*. There is no proximity requirement in the Fourth Amendment for plain view observations; the requirement is that the officer be lawfully present.

Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene of a reported crime, standing at the chain-link fence barricade. From this vantage point, he shone a flashlight into the vehicle. Under the "plain view" doctrine, an officer may seize evidence that is in plain view if the officer is lawfully present in the place from which the evidence is viewed. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465-66 (1971). Because Officer Davis was lawfully responding to a report of gunfire, his observation of the rifle casing on the front floor was permissible and did not violate the Defendant's Fourth Amendment rights. The fact that the casing was not "immediately apparent" to the naked eye in the dark does not transform the use of a flashlight into a search; it merely confirms the necessity of the light.

**III. THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE WAS JUSTIFIED BY PROBABLE CAUSE**

Once the officers observed the .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor, they had probable cause to search the entire vehicle. The "automobile exception" to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement permits a warrantless search of a vehicle if there is probable cause to believe it contains evidence of a crime. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153-54 (1925).

The Defendant argues that a single rifle casing is insufficient to establish probable cause because casings can be possessed lawfully. However, this argument ignores the context. The totality of the circumstances established probable cause: a report of multiple gunshots from a specific, restricted location; the vehicle’s presence in that restricted location at night; and the discovery of a rifle casing in plain view inside the vehicle. This combination of facts created a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained evidence of the reported crime. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238. A rifle casing, in the context of a reported shooting, is not an innocent item; it is evidence linking the vehicle to the crime.

Furthermore, the scope of the search was reasonable. Under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823-24 (1982), police may search every part of a vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search. Upon seeing the casing, the officers were authorized to look for the firearm. The Defendant argues that searching a ukulele case exceeded the scope because it is not a typical container for a rifle. However, *Ross* authorizes the search of any container that could conceal the object of the search. A rifle can fit inside a ukulele case, as evidenced by the fact that the rifle was indeed found there. Pushing the front seat forward to check the rear floor and examining the unlatched ukulele case were logical steps in the search for a weapon.

**IV. THE UNLAWFUL PAT-DOWN DOES NOT REQUIRE SUPPRESSION OF THE EVIDENCE FOUND IN THE CAR**

The Defense correctly notes that the State failed to justify the "pat down" of the four occupants. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. at 27, a pat-down is only justified if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The State provided no argument or facts to support this specific intrusion.

However, the motion to suppress targets the firearm and ammunition found inside the vehicle, not on the occupants. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), requires exclusion only if the evidence was obtained as a result of the constitutional violation. Here, the discovery of the rifle casing during the lawful flashlight scan provided independent probable cause to search the vehicle. The search of the vehicle and the discovery of the rifle were not the fruit of the unlawful pat-down, but rather the result of the plain view observation and the valid application of the automobile exception. Therefore, the illegality of the pat-down does not taint the evidence seized from the car.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion, the flashlight observation did not constitute a search, and the subsequent search of the vehicle was justified by probable cause under the automobile exception. While the pat-down of the occupants was not justified by the State, it does not require the suppression of the evidence found in the vehicle. Accordingly, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **REVERSED**.

**Second, the flashlight scan did not constitute a search.** The defense’s reliance on *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465-66 (1971), is misplaced because it ignores *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), which directly controls this issue. In *Texas v. Brown*, the Supreme Court unequivocally held that "the use of artificial light merely enhances what is otherwise visible to the naked eye and does not constitute an intrusion on privacy." The Court rejected the notion that proximity or darkness transforms a plain view observation into a search. Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene of a reported crime, standing at the chain-link fence barricade as he responded to the security guard’s report. From that vantage point, he shone a flashlight into the vehicle to illuminate its interior. The fact that the interior was dark does not create a Fourth Amendment violation; it simply necessitated light to see what was there. As *Texas v. Brown* makes clear, "the plain view doctrine does not require that an officer be able to see the object without artificial illumination." The defense’s claim that the sixty-foot distance rendered the observation unlawful is contradicted by the absence of any proximity requirement in the plain view doctrine. The officers were not "peering" into the vehicle from an unauthorized location—they were observing from a lawful position while investigating a reported shooting. The rifle casing was discovered during a lawful observation, not an unlawful search.

**Third, the discovery of the rifle casing established probable cause for the vehicle search.** The defense wrongly contends that a single rifle casing is insufficient to support probable cause, but this ignores the context of the entire incident. Probable cause requires a "fair probability" that evidence of a crime will be found, not absolute certainty. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238. Here, the totality of circumstances included: (1) a credible eyewitness report of multiple gunshots from a specific location; (2) a vehicle found driving on a barricaded, uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. in direct proximity to that location; and (3) the immediate discovery of a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. A rifle casing in the context of a reported shooting is not an "innocent item"; it is direct evidence linking the vehicle to the crime. As *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153-54 (1925), establishes, the automobile exception permits warrantless searches when probable cause exists to believe a vehicle contains evidence of a crime. The defense’s reliance on *Carroll* to argue for suppression is ironic—it is *Carroll* that authorizes such searches when probable cause is present. The search of the ukulele case was fully justified under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823-24 (1982), which holds that police may search "every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search." A rifle can fit inside a ukulele case, as the evidence proved, and the officers were searching for the firearm linked to the reported gunfire. Pushing the front seat forward to access the rear floor and examining the unlatched case were reasonable steps to locate the weapon.

**Fourth, the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine does not apply.** The defense argues that the entire search sequence is tainted by an unlawful stop, but this is incorrect. As shown above, the stop was lawful under *Terry* and *Gates*. Even if the pat-down of the occupants were improper—which the State concedes—the evidence found in the vehicle was not derived from that violation. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), requires suppression only when evidence is "come at by exploitation of [an] illegality." Here, the rifle casing was discovered during the lawful flashlight scan—prior to any pat-down or search of the vehicle. The discovery of the casing independently established probable cause for the vehicle search, breaking any causal chain from the pat-down. The Supreme Court has consistently held that evidence obtained from a lawful observation is not tainted by unrelated constitutional violations. The defense’s attempt to taint the evidence based on the pat-down ignores this well-established principle.

In sum, the defense mischaracterizes the facts and misapplies controlling precedent. The initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion based on the security guard’s eyewitness report and the vehicle’s suspicious presence on the barricaded freeway. The flashlight scan was a lawful plain view observation under *Texas v. Brown*, and the subsequent search was justified by probable cause under *Carroll* and *Ross*. The evidence was not obtained through any constitutional violation, and the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine does not apply. For these reasons, the Court must reverse the suppression order and admit the evidence.

**STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State argues that the warrantless search of the vehicle was justified under the automobile exception and that the officer’s observations were lawful. The Defendant contends that the initial stop was unsupported by reasonable suspicion, the subsequent search exceeded the permissible scope, and the "pat down" of the occupants was unlawful. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling reversing the circuit court’s suppression order.

**I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The threshold issue is whether Officer Davis had reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle. The Fourth Amendment requires that an investigatory stop be based on "specific and articulable facts" suggesting criminal activity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The Defendant argues that the security guard’s report lacked the indicia of reliability required to justify a stop, citing *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000). In *J.L.*, an anonymous tip reported that a young male in a plaid shirt was carrying a gun, but provided no predictive information or corroborating details. Here, the situation is distinct. The security guard was not an anonymous tipster reporting a prediction of future crime; he was an eyewitness reporting a completed violent act—hearing six to seven gunshots and a bullet striking a building. While the guard stated he "felt" the shots were coming from the unfinished freeway, this was a directional assessment based on his immediate sensory experience of the gunfire, not a speculative guess.

The Defendant relies on *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 51-52 (1979), to argue that mere presence in a location is insufficient for reasonable suspicion. However, *Brown v. Texas* involved a refusal to identify oneself in a high-crime area where the defendant was simply walking down a street. Here, the vehicle was found on a "barricaded," "uncompleted" freeway at 10:30 p.m. This is not merely a public area; it is a restricted construction site where the public has no lawful business. The "totality of the circumstances" supports the stop. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231-32 (1983). Officer Davis arrived at the specific, restricted location identified by the guard within minutes of the report. He found a vehicle driving on a barricaded, uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. The coincidence of the vehicle’s presence in the exact location where a violent crime was reported provides the specific and articulable facts required to justify an investigatory stop. See *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981). The vehicle was not merely "present" in a public area; it was in a closed construction site where it had no lawful business, immediately following a report of gunfire. This constitutes reasonable suspicion.

**II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN WAS NOT A SEARCH**

The Defendant contends that the flashlight scan constituted an unlawful search because Officer Davis was sixty feet away and the interior was not naturally visible. The Court disagrees.

The Supreme Court has explicitly held that the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983). In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court reasoned that the use of artificial light merely enhances what is otherwise visible to the naked eye and does not constitute an intrusion on privacy. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. at 740. The Defendant’s argument that the sixty-foot distance renders the observation unlawful ignores the holding of *Texas v. Brown*. There is no proximity requirement in the Fourth Amendment for plain view observations; the requirement is that the officer be lawfully present.

Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene of a reported crime, standing at the chain-link fence barricade. From this vantage point, he shone a flashlight into the vehicle. Under the "plain view" doctrine, an officer may seize evidence that is in plain view if the officer is lawfully present in the place from which the evidence is viewed. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465-66 (1971). Because Officer Davis was lawfully responding to a report of gunfire, his observation of the rifle casing on the front floor was permissible and did not violate the Defendant's Fourth Amendment rights. The fact that the casing was not "immediately apparent" to the naked eye in the dark does not transform the use of a flashlight into a search; it merely confirms the necessity of the light. The Defendant's distinction that the light "created" the view rather than "enhanced" it is a semantic one not supported by precedent; the critical factor is that the officer viewed the interior from a lawful vantage point.

**III. THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE WAS JUSTIFIED BY PROBABLE CAUSE**

Once the officers observed the .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor, they had probable cause to search the entire vehicle. The "automobile exception" to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement permits a warrantless search of a vehicle if there is probable cause to believe it contains evidence of a crime. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153-54 (1925).

The Defendant argues that a single rifle casing is insufficient to establish probable cause because casings can be possessed lawfully. However, this argument ignores the context. The totality of the circumstances established probable cause: a report of multiple gunshots from a specific, restricted location; the vehicle’s presence in that restricted location at night; and the discovery of a rifle casing in plain view inside the vehicle. This combination of facts created a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained evidence of the reported crime. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238. A rifle casing, in the context of a reported shooting, is not an innocent item; it is evidence linking the vehicle to the crime.

Furthermore, the scope of the search was reasonable. Under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823-24 (1982), police may search every part of a vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search. Upon seeing the casing, the officers were authorized to look for the firearm. The Defendant argues that searching a ukulele case exceeded the scope because it is not a typical container for a rifle. However, *Ross* authorizes the search of any container that could conceal the object of the search. A rifle can fit inside a ukulele case, as evidenced by the fact that the rifle was indeed found there. Pushing the front seat forward to check the rear floor and examining the unlatched ukulele case were logical steps in the search for a weapon. The Defendant's argument that the search was unreasonable because a ukulele case is not a "typical" container for a rifle is rejected; the standard is whether the container *could* conceal the object, not whether it is customarily used for that purpose.

**IV. THE UNLAWFUL PAT-DOWN DOES NOT REQUIRE SUPPRESSION OF THE EVIDENCE FOUND IN THE CAR**

The Defense correctly notes that the State failed to justify the "pat down" of the four occupants. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. at 27, a pat-down is only justified if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The State provided no argument or facts to support this specific intrusion.

However, the motion to suppress targets the firearm and ammunition found inside the vehicle, not on the occupants. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), requires exclusion only if the evidence was obtained as a result of the constitutional violation. Here, the discovery of the rifle casing during the lawful flashlight scan provided independent probable cause to search the vehicle. The search of the vehicle and the discovery of the rifle were not the fruit of the unlawful pat-down, but rather the result of the plain view observation and the valid application of the automobile exception. Therefore, the illegality of the pat-down does not taint the evidence seized from the car.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the initial stop was supported by reasonable suspicion, the flashlight observation did not constitute a search, and the subsequent search of the vehicle was justified by probable cause under the automobile exception. While the pat-down of the occupants was not justified by the State, it does not require the suppression of the evidence found in the vehicle. Accordingly, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **REVERSED**.
