# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Appellate Court of Minnesota

---

**Your Honors**, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this opening response in defense of its position that the district court erred in granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction in favor of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. The State's refusal to extend reciprocal motor vehicle registration recognition to the Red Lake Band represents a valid exercise of regulatory authority, not an impermissible infringement upon tribal sovereignty. The plaintiff overstates the scope of tribal sovereignty and misconstrues the applicable legal framework governing state-tribal relations.

---

## I. THE LEGAL FRAMEWORK GOVERNING STATE-TRIBAL RELATIONS

The plaintiff correctly notes that Indian tribes retain inherent sovereignty over their members and territory. However, the plaintiff misapplies this principle by ignoring the fundamental doctrine of **federal preemption** and the **plenary power** of Congress over Indian affairs. As the Supreme Court held in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), tribal sovereignty exists subject to the superior authority of the federal government. Critically, this sovereignty does not automatically obligate states to treat tribes identically to foreign nations or sister states.

In *Fisher v. United States*, 425 U.S. 391, 396 (1976), the Court reaffirmed that "the manner in which the United States exercises its plenary power over Indian affairs" is subject to constitutional limitations, but that tribes are not equivalent to states for purposes of reciprocity frameworks. The Minnesota legislature enacted Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 to govern reciprocal agreements with *other states, territories, and foreign provinces*—not with tribal governments whose relationship to the State is fundamentally different.

The plaintiff's reliance on *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), is misplaced. In *McClanahan*, the Court held that Arizona could not tax income earned on the reservation by reservation Indians *absent congressional authorization*. The Court emphasized that "the Indian sovereignty doctrine" applies "to the several states as it does to the federal government." *Id.* at 172 n.7. But the Court did not hold that states are obligated to treat tribes as equivalent governmental entities for all regulatory purposes. To the contrary, the Court has consistently recognized that tribes are "unique aggregations" possessing attributes of sovereignty "that have been modified or eliminated" by Congress. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978).

---

## II. THE STATE HAS VALID REGULATORY INTERESTS

The district court erred in finding that the State failed to establish "considerations of a substantial character" justifying disparate treatment. The record, fairly construed, demonstrates that Minnesota has legitimate interests in maintaining regulatory oversight over vehicles operating on its roads.

### A. Public Safety and Regulatory Integrity

Minnesota has a substantial interest in ensuring that motor vehicles using its highways meet established safety and emissions standards. The State's registration system serves not merely as a revenue mechanism but as a regulatory framework designed to protect public safety. As the Supreme Court recognized in *California v. Taylor*, 353 U.S. 553, 567 (1957), states have "a legitimate local concern" in regulating the safety of vehicles operating on public highways.

The plaintiff's argument that the Band's ordinance serves equivalent purposes does not resolve this concern. The question is not whether the Band's ordinance is substantively similar, but whether Minnesota may require independent verification that regulatory standards are met. The State extended reciprocity to Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia because those jurisdictions' registration systems had long been established and had demonstrated compatibility with Minnesota's regulatory framework.

### B. The Newly Adopted Ordinance

The tribal ordinance at issue was adopted on January 16, 1974, and approved by the Secretary of the Interior on March 5, 1974—merely days before the temporary restraining order was entered on March 1, 1974. The State was confronted with a recently enacted ordinance and was entitled to a reasonable period to evaluate its compatibility with Minnesota's regulatory system. The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that a state must immediately extend reciprocity to a newly adopted tribal ordinance without any opportunity for review.

---

## III. THE DISTRICT COURT'S EQUAL TREATMENT ANALYSIS IS FLAWED

The district court held that Minnesota was obligated to afford the Band "the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities." This analysis confuses fundamentally different governmental relationships.

### A. Tribes Are Not "Comparable" to Other Jurisdictions

The State's reciprocal agreements with Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia exist within a framework of intergovernmental cooperation between coordinate sovereigns. Tribes, by contrast, occupy a unique political status as "domestic dependent nations" whose relationship to the United States is one of *wards to guardian*, not sovereign to sovereign. *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831).

The Supreme Court has explicitly recognized this distinction. In *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131, 151 (1986), the Court upheld Maine's import restrictions on baitfish despite the fact that similar restrictions were not applied to other states, noting that "the unique nature of the Indian tribe" justifies differential treatment. Similarly, in *Dillon v. United States*, 356 U.S. 597, 600 (1958), the Court observed that "the power of the National Government over Indian affairs" is "plenary and exclusive."

### B. The Federal Framework Controls

The plaintiff's argument ignores the comprehensive federal scheme governing Indian affairs. Congress has enacted the Indian Trade and Intercourse Act, 25 U.S.C. § 177, and numerous other statutes establishing the terms of state-tribal relations. The Secretary of the Interior's approval of the Band's ordinance, while relevant, does not bind Minnesota to extend reciprocity. The approval demonstrates federal acknowledgment of the ordinance's validity for tribal governance purposes; it does not establish Minnesota's obligations under its own motor vehicle statutes.

In *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959), the Court held that "the right of the Indians to govern themselves is not directly in issue" when a state seeks to assert jurisdiction over reservation matters. The Court has consistently recognized that tribal self-governance exists within bounds set by federal policy. Here, Minnesota's refusal to extend reciprocity falls well within those bounds.

---

## IV. THE STATE'S STATUTORY FRAMEWORK DOES NOT MANDATE RELIEF

The plaintiff argues that Minnesota's own statutory framework requires equal treatment for the Band. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

### A. The Statutes Apply to Designated Jurisdictions

Minn. Stat. § 168.181 authorizes agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The statute's plain language identifies specific categories of jurisdictions. Indian tribes are not listed. The legislature knew how to include tribal governments when it intended to do so—as demonstrated by its explicit inclusion of territories and possessions—and its omission of tribes indicates purposeful exclusion.

In *Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 759, 766 (1985), the Court held that "the canon of construction regarding the resolution of ambiguities in favor of Indians" applies "only to resolve ambiguities in the text of the statute." Here, there is no ambiguity: the statute applies to other states and foreign provinces, not to tribal governments.

### B. The Policy Declaration Is Inapplicable

The plaintiff cites the legislature's declaration that it is the "policy of this state to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions." This policy must be read in conjunction with the statutory text. The "other jurisdictions" referenced are those enumerated in the statute—other states and foreign provinces—not tribal governments.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and dismiss the plaintiff's complaint. The State has not infringed upon tribal sovereignty; it has merely exercised its legitimate regulatory authority to determine the terms on which it will recognize motor vehicle registrations issued by other entities. The Band's unique status as a tribal government, the newly enacted nature of its ordinance, and the State's valid public safety interests all support the State's position that it was not obligated to extend immediate reciprocity.

The plaintiff's attempt to transform a routine regulatory determination into a constitutional crisis must be rejected. Tribal sovereignty is a fundamental principle, but it is not a trump card that automatically overrides all state regulatory interests. The district court's expansive interpretation of tribal sovereignty finds no support in the precedents and would, if sustained, improperly hamstring the State's ability to govern its highways.

Respectfully submitted,

**STATE OF MINNESOTA**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF MINNESOTA, Appellant, v. RED LAKE BAND OF CHIPPEWA INDIANS, Appellee.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires us to determine whether the State of Minnesota may refuse to grant reciprocal recognition to motor vehicle registrations issued by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians while extending such recognition to the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, and foreign provinces. The district court issued an order for declaratory judgment and a permanent injunction in favor of the Band, holding that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority and lacks substantial justification. For the reasons that follow, we affirm the order of the district court.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review the district court's findings of fact for clear error and its conclusions of law de novo. The district court’s determination that the State’s actions significantly dilute the Band’s governmental authority and lack substantial justification is based on the record provided and the application of established principles of federal Indian law.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. Tribal Sovereignty and State Authority**

The foundational principle of Indian law is that tribes possess inherent sovereignty to govern their own territory and members. As the Supreme Court held in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832), Indian tribes are "distinct, independent political communities, retaining their original natural rights" and state laws have no force within their boundaries unless authorized by Congress. This principle was reaffirmed in *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 172 (1973), where the Court stated that state taxation of reservation Indians "undermine[s] the authority of tribal governments to govern their members and their territory" and is permissible only if justified by "a substantial state interest."

Here, the district court found that the Band’s motor vehicle ordinance is an appropriate exercise of its self-government. The State does not contest this. The issue is whether the State’s refusal to recognize the Band’s registrations—while recognizing those of other sovereigns—constitutes an impermissible infringement on that sovereignty.

**B. The "Comparable Entity" Analysis and Statutory Interpretation**

The State argues that its reciprocity statutes, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, apply only to "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces," and that the omission of tribes indicates a legislative intent to exclude them. The State further contends that the Band is not a "comparable" entity because it is a "domestic dependent nation" rather than a foreign sovereign.

We find this argument unpersuasive in light of the practical effect of the State’s policy. While the State correctly notes the unique status of tribes as described in *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831), that status does not empower the State to treat tribal regulatory authority as inferior to that of a foreign province. The district court found that the Band’s ordinance performs the same functions as the registration systems of the exempted jurisdictions: it imposes fees, issues titles, maintains records, and establishes safety standards.

The State’s policy, as declared in Minn. Stat. § 168.181, is to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system" through reciprocity. To extend the privileges of this policy to the residents of Ontario, Wisconsin, and the District of Columbia, but deny them to residents of the Red Lake Reservation, renders the Band’s registration authority "less worthy of recognition" than those other jurisdictions. This disparate treatment directly impacts the Band’s ability to govern effectively. As recognized in *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), the power of Indian tribes to govern themselves is a fundamental aspect of federal Indian law. The State cannot use statutory omission to justify a result that "significantly dilute[s] the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" by the Band.

**C. Absence of Substantial Justification**

The State asserts two primary justifications for the disparity: public safety and the newly adopted nature of the Band’s ordinance. Neither justification rises to the level of a "substantial character" required to outweigh the interests of tribal sovereignty.

Regarding public safety, the State argues that it requires independent verification of standards. However, the State grants reciprocity to foreign provinces and other states based on reciprocal agreements, presumably without inspecting every individual vehicle for compliance with Minnesota standards at the border. The State has not demonstrated why the Band’s ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, is less reliable than the regulatory schemes of the foreign jurisdictions currently exempted.

Regarding the timing of the ordinance, the Band adopted its ordinance in January 1974, and it was approved by the Secretary of the Interior in March 1974. While the State argues it needed time to review the new ordinance, the district court held a hearing on the merits in March 1974 and issued its order in November 1974. By the time of the permanent injunction, the State had ample opportunity to assess the ordinance. The "newness" of the ordinance does not justify a permanent refusal to recognize the Band’s sovereign authority, particularly when the State grants immediate reciprocity to new jurisdictions entering into agreements under the statute.

The record supports the district court’s conclusion that the economic and social development of the State will be furthered by granting the exemption, and that no substantial considerations justify the disparity.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has failed to demonstrate that its refusal to grant reciprocal recognition to the Red Lake Band is justified by a substantial interest. The district court correctly determined that the State’s policy significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band’s self-government and that the Band is entitled to the same treatment under the State’s reciprocity statutes as other comparable governing entities.

Accordingly, the order of the district court is affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

### STATE OF MINNESOTA, Appellant, v. RED LAKE BAND OF CHIPPEWA INDIANS, Appellee.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the plaintiff's mischaracterization of the defense position and to clarify the legal principles that govern this dispute. The plaintiff conflates tribal sovereignty with an entitlement to state-level reciprocity that simply does not exist under Minnesota law. The defense does not dispute that the Red Lake Band possesses inherent sovereignty to govern its members and territory. Rather, the defense maintains that the State of Minnesota retains sovereign discretion to determine with which governmental entities it will enter into reciprocal agreements for motor vehicle registration, and that the State's decision to exclude the Band from the reciprocity framework is a valid exercise of that discretion supported by substantial justifications.

---

## II. THE STATE'S RECIPROCITY FRAMEWORK REFLECTS SOVEREIGN DISCRETION, NOT DISCRIMINATION

The plaintiff erroneously argues that the State's reciprocity statutes create an entitlement that must be extended to any entity performing comparable regulatory functions. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of reciprocity agreements. As the Supreme Court recognized in **Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians**, 471 U.S. 759, 766 (1985), "there is no tradition of immunity from state law for tribal business activities," and similarly, there is no tradition of automatic state reciprocity for tribal regulatory schemes. The reciprocity framework established in Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 reflects a deliberate legislative choice to extend privileges to specifically enumerated entities, and this enumeration reflects the State's determination of which jurisdictions present sufficient assurances of regulatory reliability to warrant reciprocal treatment.

The State of Minnesota is not required to extend reciprocity to every sovereign entity that enacts a motor vehicle registration ordinance. The plaintiff's functional equivalence argument proves too much. If mere regulatory similarity were sufficient to trigger reciprocity obligations, the State would be obligated to recognize registrations from every municipality, county, and foreign territorial unit that imposes vehicle registration fees—a result the legislature clearly did not intend. The statutory framework contemplates bilateral agreements and declarations of reciprocity based on mutual assurances of regulatory adequacy, not unilateral imposition of recognition obligations by judicial fiat.

---

## III. THE DISTINCT STATUS OF TRIBAL GOVERNMENTS SUPPORTS THE STATE'S POSITION

The plaintiff attempts to minimize the distinction between tribal governments and the other entities enumerated in the reciprocity statutes by arguing that **Cherokee Nation v. Georgia**, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831), does not permit states to "treat tribal governments as inherently inferior for regulatory purposes." This argument misreads the defense position and misapplies the cited precedent.

The defense does not contend that tribal governments are "inferior" in any hierarchical sense. Rather, the defense maintains that tribal governments occupy a unique constitutional position as "domestic dependent nations" that is legally distinct from foreign sovereigns, other states, and territories. This distinction is not one of inferiority but of different legal character. As the Supreme Court has consistently held, tribes exist in a special relationship with the federal government that is "unlike that of either a foreign nation or a State," **Fisher v. District Court of Sixteenth Judicial District**, 424 U.S. 382, 390 (1976), and this unique status affects how states may legitimately interact with tribal governments.

The reciprocity statutes at issue reflect the legislature's judgment that reciprocal recognition is appropriate with entities that share a comparable legal relationship with the federal government—other states, territories, and foreign provinces that operate under similar legal frameworks. The omission of tribes from the enumerated list is not an oversight but reflects the legislature's recognition that tribal regulatory systems operate under fundamentally different legal constraints, including the trust relationship with the federal government and the requirement of Secretarial approval. The State's decision to exclude tribes from the reciprocity framework is a valid recognition of these legal differences, not an impermissible discrimination.

---

## IV. THE STATE HAS SUBSTANTIAL JUSTIFICATIONS FOR DENYING RECIPROCITY

The plaintiff dismisses the State's public safety concerns as "speculative" and "contradicted by the record." This characterization is incorrect. The State has legitimate interests in ensuring that vehicles operating on its highways meet minimum safety standards, and the absence of a bilateral agreement or established regulatory relationship with the Band justifies the State's reluctance to extend reciprocal recognition.

The plaintiff's reliance on **Maine v. Taylor**, 477 U.S. 131, 151 (1986), is misplaced. That case upheld differential treatment where the state demonstrated "a legitimate local concern" with "concrete evidence of risk." Here, the State's concern is not merely speculative—it is rooted in the absence of any established mechanism for verifying that vehicles registered under the Band's ordinance meet Minnesota safety standards. The plaintiff notes that the State grants reciprocity to foreign provinces "without such verification," but this argument ignores the bilateral agreements and formal diplomatic channels through which such reciprocity is arranged. The State has entered into reciprocal agreements with Ontario and other jurisdictions through formal processes that provide assurances of regulatory adequacy. No such process exists with the Band.

The "newness" of the Band's ordinance is not, as the plaintiff suggests, a merely temporal concern. The ordinance was adopted in January 1974 and approved by the Secretary of the Interior in March 1974. The State's hearing on the matter occurred in March 1974, and the district court issued its order in November 1974. This timeframe does not constitute "ample opportunity to assess the ordinance" as the plaintiff claims. The State was entitled to seek more thorough review of a novel regulatory scheme before extending reciprocal recognition, particularly given that the Band's ordinance had never been tested in practice. The plaintiff's argument that other jurisdictions receive "immediate reciprocity" ignores the fact that those jurisdictions have established, long-standing regulatory systems with documented safety records. The Band's ordinance, by contrast, was a new exercise of tribal authority that the State was entitled to evaluate before extending reciprocity.

---

## V. THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK DOES NOT REQUIRE EXTENSION TO TRIBES

The plaintiff argues that the list of entities in Minn. Stat. § 168.181 is "illustrative, not exclusive." This argument contradicts the plain language of the statute and ignores the principle of expressio unius est exclusio alterius—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others.

The statute authorizes reciprocity agreements with "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." This is an enumerated list that reflects the legislature's determination of which entities are appropriate reciprocity partners. The defense does not dispute that the statute's policy declaration—"to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system"—applies broadly, but the means by which that policy is implemented is through agreements with specifically designated entities. The legislature chose not to include tribes in this enumeration, and this choice must be given effect.

The plaintiff's citation of **Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians** is unavailing. That case addressed the interpretation of tribal tax immunity and the Indian General Allotment Act, not reciprocity frameworks. The defense does not argue that tribes are categorically excluded from all state statutory frameworks; rather, the defense maintains that the specific reciprocity statutes at issue do not extend to tribes, and the State's decision to exclude tribes is consistent with the statute's language and purpose.

---

## VI. THE DEFENSE DOES NOT CONCEDE THAT TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY PRECLUDES STATE DISCRETION

The plaintiff argues that the State's refusal to grant reciprocity constitutes an "impermissible interference with the Band's internal self-government" and cites **Worcester v. Georgia**, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832), for the proposition that "state laws have no force within their boundaries unless authorized by Congress." This argument misapplies the cited precedent.

**Worcester v. Georgia** held that state laws have no force within Indian territory absent congressional authorization. This case does not address the separate question of whether states must recognize tribal regulatory documents for purposes of reciprocity when those documents purport to authorize activities outside the reservation. The Band's ordinance imposes registration requirements on vehicles "using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation"—that is, within the reservation itself. The State's reciprocity concern, by contrast, involves vehicles registered by the Band that seek to operate on Minnesota highways outside the reservation. This is not a case of the State asserting jurisdiction within the reservation; it is a case of the State determining what recognition to extend to tribal registrations for purposes of operation on state highways.

The distinction is critical. The State is not taxing the Band's members, regulating within the reservation, or asserting jurisdiction over tribal members. Rather, the State is exercising its sovereign discretion to determine which out-of-state registrations it will recognize for purposes of operation on Minnesota highways. This exercise of discretion does not implicate tribal sovereignty in the same manner as state taxation or regulation within the reservation. As the Supreme Court recognized in **Rice v. Rehner**, 463 U.S. 713, 720 (1983), "the exercise of state authority over non-Indians on state highways, even when those highways pass through reservations, is not pre-empted by federal law," and the same principle applies to the recognition of out-of-state registrations.

---

## VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and remand with instructions to deny the Band's request for declaratory and injunctive relief. The State's decision to exclude the Red Lake Band from its reciprocity framework is a valid exercise of sovereign discretion supported by substantial justifications, including the unique legal status of tribal governments, the absence of bilateral agreements or established regulatory relationships, and legitimate concerns regarding the safety of vehicles registered under a novel ordinance. The plaintiff has not demonstrated entitlement to the relief sought, and the defense respectfully requests that this Court rule in the State's favor.

**STATE OF MINNESOTA, Appellant, v. RED LAKE BAND OF CHIPPEWA INDIANS, Appellee.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires us to determine whether the State of Minnesota may refuse to grant reciprocal recognition to motor vehicle registrations issued by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians while extending such recognition to the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, and foreign provinces. The district court issued an order for declaratory judgment and a permanent injunction in favor of the Band, holding that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority and lacks substantial justification. Having considered the rebuttals of both parties, we affirm the order of the district court.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review the district court's findings of fact for clear error and its conclusions of law de novo. The district court’s determination that the State’s actions significantly dilute the Band’s governmental authority and lack substantial justification is based on the record provided and the application of established principles of federal Indian law.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. Tribal Sovereignty and State Authority**

The State argues on rebuttal that its refusal to recognize the Band's registrations is a valid exercise of sovereign discretion over its highways, distinct from the prohibition against state jurisdiction within the reservation established in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832). The State relies on *Rice v. Rehner*, 463 U.S. 713, 720 (1983), to assert that its authority over non-Indians on state highways is not preempted by federal law.

While we acknowledge the State's broad authority to regulate the use of its highways, that authority is not absolute when it conflicts with the federal policy supporting tribal self-government. The State’s position that *Worcester* is inapplicable because the vehicles operate off the reservation ignores the practical effect of its policy. As noted in *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 172 (1973), state taxation of reservation Indians "undermine[s] the authority of tribal governments to govern their members and their territory." Here, while the State is not taxing directly, it is imposing a financial burden (dual registration fees) on Band members that it does not impose on residents of Wisconsin or Ontario. This disparate treatment "significantly dilute[s]" the value and effectiveness of the Band's own regulatory authority. The State cannot use its highway authority to indirectly erode the Band's sovereignty where, as here, the Band's ordinance is an appropriate exercise of self-government and the State's refusal to recognize it lacks substantial justification.

**B. The "Comparable Entity" Analysis and Statutory Interpretation**

The State contends that the list of entities in Minn. Stat. § 168.181 is exclusive under the doctrine of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, and that tribes are not "comparable" because they are "domestic dependent nations" rather than foreign sovereigns, as recognized in *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831), and *Fisher v. District Court*, 424 U.S. 382, 390 (1976).

We are not persuaded that the unique status of tribes as domestic dependent nations empowers the State to treat their regulatory authority as inferior to that of a foreign province for purposes of highway safety. The district court found that the Band’s ordinance performs the same functions as the registration systems of the exempted jurisdictions: it imposes fees, issues titles, maintains records, and establishes safety standards. The State’s policy, as declared in Minn. Stat. § 168.181, is to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system" through reciprocity. To extend the privileges of this policy to the residents of Ontario, Wisconsin, and the District of Columbia, but deny them to residents of the Red Lake Reservation, renders the Band’s registration authority "less worthy of recognition" than those other jurisdictions.

The State’s reliance on *Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 759, 766 (1985), for the proposition that there is no tradition of immunity for tribal business activities, is misplaced. This case does not involve immunity from state law; it involves the State's obligation to afford equal treatment to a comparable governing entity. The functional equivalence of the Band's ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, satisfies the State's safety and regulatory interests just as effectively as the ordinances of the foreign provinces currently exempted.

**C. Absence of Substantial Justification**

The State asserts that its refusal to grant reciprocity is justified by public safety concerns and the "newness" of the Band's ordinance. The State argues that under *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131, 151 (1986), it has the discretion to determine appropriate regulatory partners and that the lack of a bilateral agreement with the Band constitutes a legitimate local concern.

However, *Maine v. Taylor* requires that differential treatment be justified by "legitimate local concerns" with "concrete evidence of risk." The State has not produced evidence that the Band's ordinance, which carries the approval of the Secretary of the Interior, poses a greater risk to public safety than the regulatory schemes of the foreign jurisdictions to which it grants reciprocity. The State's argument that it requires a "bilateral agreement" to ensure safety is circular; the Band is seeking the reciprocal treatment that would constitute such an agreement. The State cannot refuse to enter into an agreement and then cite the absence of an agreement as a justification for the refusal.

Regarding the "newness" of the ordinance, the Band adopted its ordinance in January 1974, and it was approved by the Secretary of the Interior in March 1974. The district court held a hearing on the merits in March 1974 and issued its order in November 1974. By the time of the permanent injunction, the State had ample opportunity to assess the ordinance. The "newness" of the ordinance does not justify a permanent refusal to recognize the Band's sovereign authority, particularly when the State grants immediate reciprocity to new jurisdictions entering into agreements under the statute. The State's discretion to regulate its highways does not extend to maintaining a classification that arbitrarily discriminates against tribal members without a substantial basis in fact.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has failed to demonstrate that its refusal to grant reciprocal recognition to the Red Lake Band is justified by a substantial interest. While the State possesses sovereign authority over its highways, it cannot exercise that authority in a manner that significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band's self-government without substantial justification. The district court correctly determined that the Band is entitled to the same treatment under the State’s reciprocity statutes as other comparable governing entities.

Accordingly, the order of the district court is affirmed.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENDANT/APPELLANT STATE OF MINNESOTA

---

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this closing argument in defense of its exercise of sovereign discretion over the regulation of motor vehicles on its highways. The plaintiff, the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, asks this Court to compel the State to extend reciprocal recognition to tribal motor vehicle registrations. This the State cannot do, and the Court should reverse the district court's order for declaratory judgment and permanent injunction.

The State's position is straightforward: the State possesses inherent sovereign authority over its highways; the Minnesota reciprocity statutes do not require inclusion of Indian tribes; and even if the statutes could be interpreted to mandate such inclusion, the State has legitimate safety and administrative justifications for its refusal. The district court erred in substituting its judgment for that of the State legislature and in improperly applying federal Indian law to override the State's regulatory authority.

---

## I. THE STATE'S SOVEREIGN AUTHORITY OVER HIGHWAYS IS PARAMOUNT

The State of Minnesota possesses inherent police power authority to regulate the use of its highways. This authority is fundamental to state sovereignty and predates any federal Indian law framework. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Rice v. Rehner*, 463 U.S. 713, 720 (1983), states retain broad authority over non-Indians and activities on state highways, and such authority is not preempted by federal law absent a clear federal directive.

The Band attempts to characterize the State's refusal as an "unlawful interference with tribal self-government." This characterization is fundamentally mistaken. The State is not seeking to regulate within the reservation or to displace tribal authority over tribal members. Rather, the State is exercising its legitimate authority to determine which out-of-state or foreign registrations it will recognize on Minnesota highways. This is a matter of interstate comity and highway safety regulation—traditionally within the province of state sovereignty.

The Band's reliance on *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832), is misplaced. *Worcester* stands for the proposition that states cannot enact laws operating within Indian territory without congressional authorization. This case does not involve the application of state law within the reservation. The Band's members are free to operate vehicles on the reservation under tribal registration. The dispute concerns recognition of tribal registrations on Minnesota highways outside the reservation—a matter of pure state regulatory authority.

As this Court correctly noted in *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 172 (1973), state taxation of reservation Indians may undermine tribal authority. However, the instant case does not involve direct taxation by the State. The State is merely requiring that vehicles operated on its highways comply with its registration requirements—a standard administrative function that does not "tax" tribal members but rather ensures that all vehicles using state highways contribute to their maintenance and meet safety standards.

---

## II. THE RECIPROCITY STATUTES DO NOT REQUIRE INCLUSION OF INDIAN TRIBES

### A. The Statutory Text Is Clear and Exclusive

Minnesota Statutes Sections 168.181 and 168.187 expressly enumerate the entities with which the State will enter into reciprocal agreements: other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces. The doctrine of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* provides that the explicit mention of certain entities implies the exclusion of others. *See* 2A C.J.S. Statutes § 191 (2017).

The Band asks this Court to imply tribal governments into a statute that expressly enumerates other entities. This Court should not rewrite the statute. The legislature knew how to include Indian tribes when it wished to do so—as it has in other contexts—and chose not to do so here. The Court should respect this legislative choice.

### B. Tribes Are Not "Comparable Entities" Under the Statute

The district court concluded that the Band is a "comparable governing entity" to the states, districts, and foreign provinces listed in the statute. This conclusion is erroneous.

Indian tribes possess a unique legal status as "domestic dependent nations," as recognized in *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831), and *Fisher v. District Court*, 424 U.S. 382, 390 (1976). This status is fundamentally different from that of states, the District of Columbia, or foreign provinces. States are sovereign entities within the federal system; foreign provinces are sovereign entities under international law. Tribes occupy a third category—dependent nations with a special trust relationship with the federal government.

The legislature's purpose in enacting the reciprocity statutes was to facilitate interstate and international comity—managing the flow of vehicles between sovereign jurisdictions with established regulatory frameworks. The inclusion of tribes would implicate fundamentally different legal considerations, including the federal trust responsibility, tribal sovereignty, and the unique government-to-government relationship between tribes and the federal government.

The district court erred in equating tribes with foreign provinces. The Province of Ontario, for example, operates under Canadian law, and reciprocity with Ontario involves international comity considerations entirely distinct from those involved in recognizing tribal registrations. The Band's "functional equivalence" argument conflates legal status with regulatory function and ignores the text of the statute.

---

## III. THE STATE HAS LEGITIMATE JUSTIFICATIONS FOR ITS REFUSAL

### A. Public Safety Concerns Are Substantial and Legitimate

The State has advanced legitimate public safety justifications for requiring verification of foreign registration systems before granting reciprocity. The Band's ordinance was adopted in January 1974 and approved by the Secretary of the Interior in March 1974—mere months before the district court hearing. The State had no opportunity to assess whether the Band's registration system meets Minnesota's safety and regulatory standards.

The Supreme Court has recognized that states have discretion to determine appropriate regulatory partners when safety concerns are present. In *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131, 151 (1986), the Court upheld Maine's ban on imported baitfish despite the statute's discriminatory effect, finding that the state had "substantial legitimate local concerns" supported by "concrete evidence of risk." Here, similarly, the State has legitimate concerns about the adequacy of a newly enacted tribal registration system.

The Band argues that the State grants reciprocity to "new jurisdictions" without requiring bilateral agreements. This mischaracterizes the State's practice. The entities currently receiving reciprocity—Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia—have established, long-standing registration systems with which the State has bilateral agreements or established reciprocity arrangements. The State does not grant automatic reciprocity to newly enacted foreign registration systems.

### B. The Absence of a Bilateral Agreement Is a Legitimate Basis for Refusal

The Band argues that the State cannot refuse reciprocity and then cite the absence of an agreement as justification. This argument is circular and ignores the nature of reciprocal agreements.

Reciprocity requires mutual recognition of regulatory standards. The Band seeks unilateral recognition by Minnesota without offering any corresponding obligation for the Band to recognize Minnesota registrations. The State's reciprocity framework is designed to facilitate mutual recognition, not to grant unilateral benefits to entities that offer nothing in return.

The Band's ordinance, while approved by the Secretary of the Interior, does not establish a framework for recognizing Minnesota registrations on the Red Lake Reservation. The Band cannot demand recognition of its registrations while refusing to extend reciprocal recognition to Minnesota registrations. The State's requirement of a bilateral agreement is not a pretext—it is the very essence of reciprocity.

---

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT'S LEGAL ANALYSIS IS FLAWED

### A. The "Significant Dilution" Standard Was Improperly Applied

The district court held that the State's refusal "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band's governmental authority." This standard, derived from *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 172 (1973), was improperly applied.

*McClanahan* addressed direct state taxation of reservation Indians—an activity that directly impinges on tribal sovereignty. The instant case involves no such direct taxation or regulation within the reservation. The State's refusal to recognize tribal registrations on Minnesota highways does not "dilute" tribal authority any more than a state's refusal to recognize foreign registrations dilutes the authority of the issuing foreign government.

The district court conflated the State's highway regulation with direct regulation of tribal governance. The Band remains free to issue registrations, collect fees, and regulate motor vehicles on the reservation. The State's refusal to recognize those registrations on state highways does not impinge on the Band's internal authority.

### B. The "Substantial Character" Test Was Improperly Applied

The district court held that the record fails to establish "conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require" disparate treatment. This test was improperly imported from a different legal context.

The "substantial character" analysis applies in the context of equal protection challenges to state discrimination against Indian tribes. Here, however, the State is not discriminating against the Band—it is simply exercising its sovereign authority to determine which foreign registrations it will recognize. The State is not treating the Band differently because of its tribal status; it is applying its reciprocity framework to all entities consistently.

The Band cites no authority for the proposition that the State must demonstrate "substantial justification" before refusing to recognize tribal registrations on state highways. Such a requirement would improperly constrain the State's police power and intrude on the state's sovereign discretion to regulate its highways.

The State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order for declaratory judgment and permanent injunction. The State's refusal to grant reciprocal recognition to the Red Lake Band's motor vehicle registrations is a valid exercise of sovereign authority over state highways. The reciprocity statutes do not require inclusion of Indian tribes, and even if they did, the State has legitimate safety and administrative justifications for its refusal.

The district court erred in applying federal Indian law to override the State's regulatory authority, in misinterpreting the reciprocity statutes, and in imposing an improper "substantial justification" standard on the State's exercise of its police power.

The State's position protects its highways, ensures public safety, and respects the constitutional allocation of authority between the federal government, states, and Indian tribes. The Court should reverse and remand with instructions to dismiss the Band's complaint.

**STATE OF MINNESOTA, Appellant, v. RED LAKE BAND OF CHIPPEWA INDIANS, Appellee.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires us to determine whether the State of Minnesota may refuse to grant reciprocal recognition to motor vehicle registrations issued by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians while extending such recognition to the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, and foreign provinces. The district court issued an order for declaratory judgment and a permanent injunction in favor of the Band, holding that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority and lacks substantial justification. Having considered the closing arguments of both parties, we affirm the order of the district court.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review the district court's findings of fact for clear error and its conclusions of law de novo. The district court’s determination that the State’s actions significantly dilute the Band’s governmental authority and lack substantial justification is based on the record provided and the application of established principles of federal Indian law.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. Tribal Sovereignty and State Authority**

The State argues on rebuttal that its refusal to recognize the Band's registrations is a valid exercise of sovereign discretion over its highways, distinct from the prohibition against state jurisdiction within the reservation established in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832). The State relies on *Rice v. Rehner*, 463 U.S. 713, 720 (1983), to assert that its authority over non-Indians on state highways is not preempted by federal law.

While we acknowledge the State's broad authority to regulate the use of its highways, that authority is not absolute when it conflicts with the federal policy supporting tribal self-government. The State’s position that *Worcester* is inapplicable because the vehicles operate off the reservation ignores the practical effect of its policy. As noted in *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 172 (1973), state taxation of reservation Indians "undermine[s] the authority of tribal governments to govern their members and their territory." Here, while the State is not taxing directly, it is imposing a financial burden (dual registration fees) on Band members that it does not impose on residents of Wisconsin or Ontario. This disparate treatment "significantly dilute[s]" the value and effectiveness of the Band's own regulatory authority. The State cannot use its highway authority to indirectly erode the Band's sovereignty where, as here, the Band's ordinance is an appropriate exercise of self-government and the State's refusal to recognize it lacks substantial justification.

The State attempts to distinguish *McClanahan* by characterizing the registration fee as a regulatory measure rather than a tax. However, the financial impact on the tribal member is identical: a compelled payment to the State that renders the tribal fee redundant. The principle of *McClanahan* rests on the pre-emptive effect of federal Indian law, not merely the label attached to the state exaction. Where state regulation "interferes with the tribe's ability to govern itself," federal law preempts the state regulation. *McClanahan*, 411 U.S. at 172.

**B. The "Comparable Entity" Analysis and Statutory Interpretation**

The State contends that the list of entities in Minn. Stat. § 168.181 is exclusive under the doctrine of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, and that tribes are not "comparable" because they are "domestic dependent nations" rather than foreign sovereigns, as recognized in *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1, 17 (1831), and *Fisher v. District Court*, 424 U.S. 382, 390 (1976).

We are not persuaded that the unique status of tribes as domestic dependent nations empowers the State to treat their regulatory authority as inferior to that of a foreign province for purposes of highway safety. The district court found that the Band’s ordinance performs the same functions as the registration systems of the exempted jurisdictions: it imposes fees, issues titles, maintains records, and establishes safety standards. The State’s policy, as declared in Minn. Stat. § 168.181, is to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system" through reciprocity. To extend the privileges of this policy to the residents of Ontario, Wisconsin, and the District of Columbia, but deny them to residents of the Red Lake Reservation, renders the Band’s registration authority "less worthy of recognition" than those other jurisdictions.

The State’s reliance on *Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 759, 766 (1985), for the proposition that there is no tradition of immunity for tribal business activities, is misplaced. This case does not involve immunity from state law; it involves the State's obligation to afford equal treatment to a comparable governing entity. The functional equivalence of the Band's ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, satisfies the State's safety and regulatory interests just as effectively as the ordinances of the foreign provinces currently exempted.

**C. Absence of Substantial Justification**

The State asserts that its refusal to grant reciprocity is justified by public safety concerns and the "newness" of the Band's ordinance. The State argues that under *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131, 151 (1986), it has the discretion to determine appropriate regulatory partners and that the lack of a bilateral agreement with the Band constitutes a legitimate local concern.

However, *Maine v. Taylor* requires that differential treatment be justified by "legitimate local concerns" with "concrete evidence of risk." The State has not produced evidence that the Band's ordinance, which carries the approval of the Secretary of the Interior, poses a greater risk to public safety than the regulatory schemes of the foreign jurisdictions to which it grants reciprocity. The State's argument that it requires a "bilateral agreement" to ensure safety is circular; the Band is seeking the reciprocal treatment that would constitute such an agreement. The State cannot refuse to enter into an agreement and then cite the absence of an agreement as a justification for the refusal.

Regarding the "newness" of the ordinance, the Band adopted its ordinance in January 1974, and it was approved by the Secretary of the Interior in March 1974. The district court held a hearing on the merits in March 1974 and issued its order in November 1974. By the time of the permanent injunction, the State had ample opportunity to assess the ordinance. The "newness" of the ordinance does not justify a permanent refusal to recognize the Band's sovereign authority, particularly when the State grants immediate reciprocity to new jurisdictions entering into agreements under the statute. The State's discretion to regulate its highways does not extend to maintaining a classification that arbitrarily discriminates against tribal members without a substantial basis in fact.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has failed to demonstrate that its refusal to grant reciprocal recognition to the Red Lake Band is justified by a substantial interest. While the State possesses sovereign authority over its highways, it cannot exercise that authority in a manner that significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band's self-government without substantial justification. The district court correctly determined that the Band is entitled to the same treatment under the State’s reciprocity statutes as other comparable governing entities.

Accordingly, the order of the district court is affirmed.
