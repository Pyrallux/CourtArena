# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The Defense respectfully submits that this Court should reverse the Court of Appeals and dismiss the attempted first-degree theft conviction. The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Guevara* is misplaced, and the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish the elements of attempted first-degree theft. Additionally, the statute as applied violates the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment because it fails to provide clear standards for determining "value" in theft-by-deception cases. The Defense addresses each argument in turn.

## I. The Statute's Definition of "Value" Is Unconstitutionally Vague As Applied

The prosecution incorrectly argues that *Guevara* provides a clear, objective standard that satisfies due process. To the contrary, defining "value" solely as "the amount the defendant received or sought to receive" creates an unconstitutionally vague standard that fails to give ordinary citizens fair notice of what conduct is prohibited.

The fundamental problem is circularity. In a theft-by-deception case, the defendant is charged with obtaining property through fraud. The "value" of that property—determining whether the offense is first, second, or third-degree theft—cannot logically be measured by the fraudulent amount obtained. This Court recognized in *State v. Doe*, 87 Wash. 2d 771, 774, 557 P.2d 310, 312 (Wash. 1976), that the value determination must relate to the actual property obtained, not merely the representation made about it.

The prosecution cites *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. App. 753, 765, 319 P.3d 1090, 1096 (2014), but that case is distinguishable. In *Lui*, the defendant had actually received specific amounts from victims, and the court could compare those amounts to statutory thresholds. Here, the Georges had purchased the truck from Potter for $1,800 and were attempting to resell it. The State's theory conflates the sale price with the "value" of the truck, but this ignores the basic principle that a seller cannot steal property they already own.

The Due Process Clause requires that statutes provide "clear standards" that "set the outer boundaries of the proscribed conduct." *State v. O'Neill*, 148 Wash. 2d 438, 450, 62 P.3d 489, 495 (Wash. 2003). A definition of value that depends entirely on the amount a defendant requests—without any reference to the actual worth of the underlying property—fails this requirement. A defendant cannot know whether their conduct constitutes first-degree theft (property worth $5,000 or more) or third-degree theft (property worth less than $250) because the statute provides no objective benchmark.

## II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support a Conviction for Attempted First-Degree Theft

Even accepting arguendo the prosecution's interpretation of "value," the evidence was insufficient to prove the truck was worth $5,000 or more.

### A. The State Failed to Prove the Truck Had a Value of $5,000 or More

The prosecution's entire case rested on the amount the Georges sought to receive from the undercover officers. However, this approach conflates the *asking price* with the *actual value*. The truck at issue had the following conditions according to the facts: the rear wheel differential was broken, rendering the truck inoperable for two years; it had sat outside, uncovered, during that time; the engine had been replaced with a different model; and the odometer showed 70,000 miles although the actual mileage was 185,000 miles.

At trial, the State presented *no evidence* of the truck's actual market value. As the trial court record reflects, "Because of the vehicle's age, Detective Stokke could not find standardized pricing information." The only evidence the State presented regarding value was the amount the Georges paid Potter—$1,800.

This Court has held that "the State must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *State v. Riley*, 137 Wash. 2d 904, 908, 966 P.2d 405, 407 (Wash. 1998). For attempted first-degree theft, the State must prove the object of the theft was property worth $5,000 or more. RCW 9A.56.030(1)(a). The prosecution cannot meet this burden by showing merely what a defendant asked for or received—the statute requires proof of the property's actual value.

### B. The Georges Did Not Obtain Property Through Deception

The charging theory in this case is also fundamentally flawed. The Georges had purchased the truck from Jerome Potter. They owned the truck. A person cannot "steal" property they already lawfully own. The crime of theft by deception requires that the defendant "obtain" property "by means of a deception." RCW 9A.56.020(1)(a).

Even assuming the Georges made misrepresentations to the undercover officers, those misrepresentations related to the condition and history of the truck they were selling. The officers were not deceived into giving up their money—they received the truck. The deception, if any, went to the *quality* of the property, not to the fact of transferring ownership. This does not constitute theft by deception.

In *State v. Hutchens*, 77 Wash. App. 441, 447, 890 P.2d 113, 117 (1995), the Court held that theft by deception requires proof that "the deception caused the victim to transfer property." Where the victim receives the property they bargained for—even if that property is misrepresented—the crime is not theft but rather fraud or breach of contract. The victim has suffered a loss, but it is not a theft loss.

## III. The Prosecution Misapplies *State v. Guevara*

The prosecution incorrectly claims that *Guevara* controls this case. In *Guevara*, the defendant had obtained specific amounts from victims through fraudulent schemes. The Court held that the value of the property stolen was the amount the defendant actually received from those victims. 160 Wash. 2d at 493, 158 P.3d at 1229.

Here, the Georges did not obtain any money from the undercover officers—the transaction was interrupted by the officers' arrest of the Georges immediately after the sale. More fundamentally, the Georges were not the recipients of money; they were sellers. The *Guevara* rule applies to defendants who obtain money or property from victims through deception. It does not apply to sellers who make misrepresentations about the goods they sell.

The prosecution's interpretation would convert every instance of fraud in the sale of goods into theft—a result the legislature clearly did not intend. The theft statute and fraud statutes are distinct offenses with different elements. See RCW 9A.56.020 versus RCW 19.86.020.

## IV. The Trial Court Erred in Denying the Motion to Dismiss

The Georges moved for dismissal at the close of the State's case, arguing that a prima facie case had not been established. The trial court denied this motion. This was error.

When evaluating a motion to dismiss for insufficient evidence, the Court must determine whether "any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992). The State bore the burden of proving each element beyond a reasonable doubt.

The State failed to present evidence that the truck was worth $5,000 or more. The only evidence of value was the $1,800 purchase price, which is far below the first-degree theft threshold. The trial court therefore should have granted the motion to dismiss the attempted first-degree theft charge.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals, grant the motion to dismiss the attempted first-degree theft conviction, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court affirms the decision of the Court of Appeals. The trial court properly denied the motion to dismiss, and the State presented sufficient evidence to support the conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The definition of "value" under the theft statute is governed by binding precedent establishing that value is measured by the amount the defendant received or sought to receive, not the market value of the property involved. Consequently, the State was not required to prove the market value of the truck, and the statute is not unconstitutionally vague as applied.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Definition of "Value" in Theft by Deception**

The central issue in this case is the proper definition of "value" for purposes of determining the degree of theft. The Defense argues that "value" must correspond to the actual market value of the property stolen, relying on *State v. Doe*, 87 Wash. 2d 771, 557 P.2d 310 (Wash. 1976), and distinguishing *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d 484, 158 P.3d 1224 (Wash. 2007). This argument is unpersuasive.

The Court explicitly addressed this issue in *State v. Guevara*, holding that "the value of the property stolen is the amount the defendant received or sought to receive, not the difference between the market value and the amount paid." *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d at 493, 158 P.3d at 1229. The facts of *Guevara* are materially similar to the present case, involving the sale of a vehicle where the defendant made misrepresentations regarding the vehicle's condition and history. The Defense's attempt to distinguish *Guevara* on the grounds that the Georges were "sellers" rather than "recipients" is without merit. In a transaction involving theft by deception, the defendant "receives" the purchase price from the victim. Therefore, the rule in *Guevara* applies directly to the amount the Georges sought to obtain from the undercover officers.

The Defense's reliance on *State v. Doe* is misplaced. While *Doe* emphasizes that value relates to the property obtained, *Guevara* clarifies that in the context of theft by deception, the "property obtained" is the money or consideration exchanged, and its value is the amount the defendant successfully sought or received. The statutory definition is not circular; it simply fixes the value of the theft at the expense to the victim caused by the deception.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The Georges argue that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft because the State failed to prove the truck was worth $5,000. Specifically, they contend that the State only proved the truck's purchase price was $1,800 and offered no evidence of its market value at the time of the attempted sale.

Under the standard set forth in *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992), the evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. As established in *State v. Guevara*, the State is not required to prove the market value of the truck. *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d at 493, 158 P.3d at 1229. Instead, the State need only prove the amount the defendants sought to receive.

The State presented evidence that the Georges negotiated a sale price with the undercover officers and arranged to complete the transaction the following day, at which point the officers arrived with a cashier's check. This evidence establishes the amount the Georges sought to receive. The fact that the truck had mechanical issues, or that the Georges had previously purchased it for $1,800, is irrelevant to the "value" element under *Guevara*. The State met its burden of proving the value of the attempted theft was the amount the Georges sought to obtain from the officers. See *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. App. 753, 765, 319 P.3d 1090, 1096 (2014) (stating the State need not prove market value or victim's loss).

**III. Vagueness Challenge**

The Defense contends that the theft statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied because it fails to provide clear standards for determining "value." This Court has consistently held that statutes are presumptively constitutional and will only be struck down if they are vague in all possible applications. *State v. O'Neill*, 148 Wash. 2d 438, 450, 62 P.3d 489, 495 (Wash. 2003).

The statute provides a specific, measurable standard: the amount the defendant received or sought to receive. As noted in *State v. Lui*, this standard "does not leave defendants guessing at prohibited conduct." *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. App. at 765, 319 P.3d at 1096. The Georges knew exactly how much money they were asking for the truck. Therefore, the statute provided them with fair notice of the severity of the offense based on the amount they sought to obtain. The vagueness challenge is rejected.

**IV. The "Theft of One's Own Property" Argument**

Finally, the Defense argues that the Georges could not commit theft because they already owned the truck. This argument mischaracterizes the crime of theft by deception. The "property" obtained in this transaction was the money the Georges sought to extract from the undercover officers through fraudulent misrepresentations. The misrepresentations regarding the truck's condition and history were the means used to obtain that property.

The Defense relies on *State v. Hutchens*, 77 Wash. App. 441, 447, 890 P.2d 113, 117 (1995), for the proposition that misrepresenting quality is not theft. However, *Guevara* specifically involved a defendant who misrepresented the condition of a vehicle (odeter rollback) to obtain a higher price, and the Court held this constituted theft. *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d at 488, 158 P.3d at 1226. To the extent *Hutchens* suggests otherwise, it is superseded by the Supreme Court's holding in *Guevara*.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly instructed the jury on the definition of value and denied the motion to dismiss. The State presented sufficient evidence to establish the value of the attempted theft based on the amount the Georges sought to receive. The statute is not unconstitutionally vague, and the defendants' ownership of the truck does not preclude a conviction for theft by deception where they sought to obtain money through fraud. The Court of Appeals' decision is affirmed.

The defense incorrectly claims the Georges could not commit theft because they owned the truck. This ignores the nature of theft by deception. The deception targeted the money sought from the undercover officers, not the truck itself. The defense's citation to *State v. Hutchens*, 77 Wash. App. 441, 447, 890 P.2d 113, 117 (1995), is inapposite; *Hutchens* addressed fraud in contract disputes but was superseded by *Guevara*'s explicit holding that misrepresenting property condition to obtain money constitutes theft by deception. RCW 9A.56.020 defines theft as "obtaining property by deception," and the property obtained here was the monetary consideration. The Georges' ownership of the truck does not negate that they sought to obtain money through false statements about its condition.

### IV. *State v. Guevara* Directly Controls This Case
The defense's attempt to distinguish *Guevara* fails. *Guevara* applied to a defendant who misrepresented vehicle details to sell it for a higher price—precisely the scenario here. The fact that the Georges were sellers rather than "recipients" is irrelevant; in any theft by deception transaction, the defendant "receives" the payment from the victim. *Guevara* confirmed that the "amount received or sought" is the value, regardless of whether the defendant initially owned the property. *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d at 493, 158 P.3d at 1229. The defense's claim that *Guevara* only applies to defendants who "obtained money from victims" is circular—it is the *nature of the transaction* (deception to secure payment) that matters, not the defendant's prior ownership. The prosecution's theory is consistent with *Guevara* and binding precedent, not a misapplication.

The trial court properly limited its review to the amount the Georges sought to receive as the measure of "value," as required by *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d 484, 158 P.3d 1224 (Wash. 2007). # DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's arguments and the lower court's ruling misapply binding precedent and ignore fundamental principles of theft law. While the prosecution invokes *State v. Guevara* as controlling authority, that decision represents a departure from the plain meaning of the theft statute and creates an unworkable standard that renders the degree-of-theft framework arbitrary and circular. The Defense requests that this Court reconsider *Guevara*'s interpretation or, alternatively, confine its application to its specific facts. Even under the prosecution's erroneous framework, the evidence was insufficient to establish that the Georges sought to obtain money exceeding the $5,000 threshold for first-degree theft. The Court should reverse the Court of Appeals and dismiss the conviction.

---

## I. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *GUEVARA* IS MISPLACED

### A. *Guevara* Departed from the Plain Meaning of the Theft Statute

The prosecution contends that *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d 484, 158 P.3d 1224 (Wash. 2007), controls this case and establishes that "value" means the amount received or sought to receive. However, this interpretation contradicts the plain language of the theft statute and fundamental principles of property valuation.

RCW 9A.56.020 defines theft as "obtaining property of another by deception." The statute does not define "value" as the amount the defendant received; rather, value must relate to the property stolen. In *State v. Doe*, 87 Wash. 2d 771, 774, 557 P.2d 310, 312 (Wash. 1976), this Court recognized that "value" in theft statutes "relates to the property obtained" and is generally measured by the property's market value or its value to the owner.

The *Guevara* majority created a novel definition that treats the money received as the "property obtained" rather than the consideration exchanged for the fraudulent representation. This interpretation is circular: it defines the value of the theft by reference to the amount obtained through the theft, rather than measuring the harm caused to the victim or the benefit improperly obtained at the victim's expense.

### B. *Guevara* Should Be Distinguished or Overruled

The prosecution argues that *Guevara* is materially similar to this case because both involve vehicle sales with misrepresentations. However, critical distinctions exist.

In *Guevara*, the defendant actually completed the sale and received a specific payment from the victim. The defendant in that case had obtained the vehicle through fraud and then sold it to an unsuspecting buyer. The "property obtained" was the vehicle itself, and its value should have been measured by the vehicle's market worth—not the purchase price paid to a fraudster.

Here, the Georges owned the truck outright before the transaction with the undercover officers. They did not obtain the truck through deception; they had purchased it legitimately from Jerome Potter. The prosecution's theory treats the money they sought to receive as the "property obtained," but this conflates the means of deception with the object of the theft.

The Defense submits that *Guevara* should be limited to its specific facts—where a defendant obtains property through deception and then transfers that property to a victim—or overruled entirely as inconsistent with the statutory framework.

---

## II. EVEN UNDER THE PROSECUTION'S FRAMEWORK, THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT

### A. The State Failed to Prove the Georges Sought to Receive $5,000 or More

Even accepting arguendo that "value" means the amount received or sought to receive, the prosecution still failed to establish the essential element for first-degree theft. First-degree theft requires that the property stolen exceed $5,000 in value. The prosecution contends that the Georges negotiated a sale price with the undercover officers, but the evidence demonstrates no such negotiation resulted in an agreed amount exceeding $5,000.

The facts establish that the officers arrived with a cashier's check, but nothing in the record indicates that the Georges demanded or sought to receive $5,000 or more. The officers initiated contact and brought a check; the Georges did not specify a demand amount. The prosecution asks this Court to infer that the Georges sought $5,000 based on the officers' decision to bring a $5,000 check, but this inference is speculative and unsupported by the evidence.

In *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992), this Court held that evidence is sufficient only if any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the State presented no evidence that the Georges sought $5,000. The transaction was never completed at any price—the officers arrested the Georges immediately upon receiving the title. There was no agreed sale price, no demand for a specific sum, and no evidence that the Georges sought to obtain more than $1,800 (the price they had paid for the truck) or any amount approaching $5,000.

The prosecution's reliance on the cashier's check is misplaced. The check was procured by the officers' own initiative, not by the Georges' demand. The mere presence of a check does not establish that the Georges sought to receive that amount.

### B. The Attempted Nature of the Crime Requires Proof of Specific Intent to Obtain a Specific Value

The Georges were charged with *attempted* first-degree theft, not completed first-degree theft. Attempt requires proof that the defendant intended to commit the underlying crime and took a substantial step toward its commission. For attempted first-degree theft, the State must prove the defendant intended to steal property worth more than $5,000.

The prosecution presented no evidence of the Georges' subjective intent regarding the value they sought to obtain. They never stated a desired sale price. They never negotiated for $5,000 or more. The officers never testified that the Georges made any representations about the truck's value or demanded any specific amount.

Without evidence of the Georges' intent to obtain property exceeding $5,000 in value, the conviction for attempted first-degree theft cannot stand. The prosecution failed to establish the essential mental state element.

---

## III. THE THEFT STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AS APPLIED

### A. The Statute Provides No Clear Standard for Determining Value

The prosecution argues that the statute is not vague because it provides an "objective and measurable standard"—the amount the defendant received or sought to receive. This argument fails because it does not resolve the circularity problem identified by the Defense and does not provide fair notice to defendants.

Under the prosecution's interpretation, a defendant cannot know whether their conduct constitutes first, second, or third-degree theft until the victim decides how much money to give them. This creates an absurd scenario where the victim's conduct—rather than the defendant's—determines the severity of the charge. A defendant who genuinely believes their property is worth $10,000 and seeks that amount, but receives $3,000 from a deceived victim, would be guilty only of third-degree theft despite their intent to obtain $10,000.

In *State v. O'Neill*, 148 Wash. 2d 438, 450, 62 P.3d 489, 495 (Wash. 2003), this Court held that statutes must provide clear standards to avoid arbitrary enforcement. The statute as interpreted in *Guevara* provides no such standard. The "amount received or sought to receive" is entirely dependent on the victim's willingness to pay, which is outside the defendant's control and knowledge at the time of the deceptive conduct.

### B. The Georges Did Not Have Fair Notice of the Potential Penalty

The prosecution contends that the Georges knew "exactly how much money they were asking for the truck." This assertion is unsupported by the record. The Georges never specified a price. They never demanded $5,000 or any other amount. They simply agreed to sell the truck to interested buyers who approached them.

At the time of the alleged deception, the Georges could not have known that their conduct could result in a first-degree theft charge. They had no way of knowing that the undercover officers would bring a cashier's check, or that the officers' decision to bring a particular amount would transform their alleged conduct into a felony. This lack of notice violates due process.

---

## IV. THE GEORGES CANNOT COMMIT THEFT OF THEIR OWN PROPERTY

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Property Obtained

The prosecution argues that the "property obtained" in this case was the money from the undercover officers, not the truck itself. This argument misconstrues the nature of the transaction and ignores the ownership facts.

The Georges owned the truck. They had purchased it from Jerome Potter and held title to it. When they sold the truck to the undercover officers, they transferred ownership of their own property in exchange for money. This is a legitimate sale, not a theft. The fact that the Georges may have made misrepresentations about the truck's condition does not transform the sale into theft.

The crime of theft by deception requires that the defendant "obtain property of another." The property obtained must belong to another person before the deception. Here, the truck belonged to the Georges. They could not obtain their own property through deception because they already possessed it.

### B. *Guevara* Does Not Support Prosecution's Theory

The prosecution cites *Guevara* for the proposition that misrepresenting a vehicle's condition to obtain a higher price constitutes theft. However, *Guevara* is distinguishable.

In *Guevara*, the defendant had obtained the vehicle through fraud and then sold it to another victim. The vehicle did not belong to the defendant; it belonged to the original owner who had been defrauded. The defendant's misrepresentation about the vehicle's history was an attempt to conceal the fraud and extract money from the subsequent victim.

Here, the Georges had legitimately purchased the truck from Potter. They were the lawful owners. Their sale of their own property, even with misrepresentations, does not constitute theft because they were not obtaining "property of another."

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Hutchens*, 77 Wash. App. 441, 447, 890 P.2d 113, 117 (1995), is similarly misplaced. The prosecution argues that *Hutchens* was superseded by *Guevara*, but *Hutchens* correctly recognized that misrepresenting quality in a sale of one's own property is not theft. To the extent *Guevara* suggests otherwise, it should be limited or overruled.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals and dismiss the conviction for attempted first-degree theft. The prosecution's reliance on *Guevara* is misplaced; that decision created an unworkable standard that contradicts the plain meaning of the theft statute. Even under the prosecution's framework, the evidence was insufficient to establish that the Georges sought to receive property exceeding $5,000 in value. The statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied, and the Georges cannot be guilty of theft because they sold their own property. The Defense requests that this Court remand for entry of judgment of conviction on a lesser-included offense or for dismissal.

**UPDATED RULING**

The Court reverses the decision of the Court of Appeals. While the trial court properly instructed the jury on the definition of "value" pursuant to binding precedent, the State presented insufficient evidence to support the conviction for attempted first-degree theft. Specifically, the State failed to prove that the Georges sought to receive an amount exceeding $5,000. The conviction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Definition of "Value" in Theft by Deception**

The Court reaffirms its prior analysis that the definition of "value" in theft by deception cases is governed by *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d 484, 158 P.3d 1224 (Wash. 2007). As held in *Guevara*, "the value of the property stolen is the amount the defendant received or sought to receive, not the difference between the market value and the amount paid." *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d at 493, 158 P.3d at 1229. The Defense's attempt to distinguish *Guevara* based on the defendant's ownership of the property is unpersuasive. The "property obtained" in a theft by deception charge is the money or consideration extracted from the victim, not the item being sold. Therefore, the Georges' lawful ownership of the truck does not preclude the application of *Guevara* or liability for theft by deception where they sought to obtain money through fraudulent misrepresentations.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The central issue on appeal is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to support the specific charge of attempted first-degree theft. The Defense correctly argues that, even under the *Guevara* standard, the State failed to prove the essential element of value required for a first-degree conviction.

The State charged the Georges with attempted first-degree theft, which requires proof that the property stolen exceeded $5,000 in value. Under *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992), evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the essential element is the amount the defendants "sought to receive."

A review of the provided facts reveals a critical gap in the State's evidence. The record indicates that the Georges misrepresented the truck's condition and history, and that they arranged to complete the sale the following day. However, the facts provided do not contain any evidence of a specific price demanded, negotiated, or agreed upon by the Georges. The State asserts that the Georges "negotiated a specific sale price," but this assertion is not supported by the factual record provided, which details only the misrepresentations regarding the truck's condition and mileage.

The mere fact that the officers arrived with a cashier's check does not establish that the Georges "sought to receive" that specific amount. As the Defense argues, the check was procured by the officers' initiative. Without evidence that the Georges demanded or agreed to a price exceeding $5,000, the State failed to meet its burden of proof on the value element necessary for a first-degree conviction. While the State proved the Georges intended to sell the truck and obtain money, it failed to prove the *value* of that attempted theft exceeded the statutory threshold for first-degree theft.

**III. Vagueness and Ownership Arguments**

The Court rejects the Defense's arguments that the statute is unconstitutionally vague and that the Georges could not commit theft of their own property.

Regarding vagueness, the statute provides a clear standard: the amount the defendant received or sought to receive. *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. App. 753, 765, 319 P.3d 1090, 1096 (2014). The fact that the State failed to prove the specific amount in this case does not render the statute vague; it merely reflects a failure of evidence.

Regarding the "theft of own property" argument, the Court agrees with the Prosecution that the "property obtained" was the money the Georges sought to extract from the undercover officers. The misrepresentations were the means used to obtain that property. The Georges' ownership of the truck is irrelevant to the charge of obtaining the officers' money by deception.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court adheres to the legal definition of value established in *State v. Guevara*. However, applying that standard to the facts provided, the State presented insufficient evidence to prove that the Georges sought to receive an amount exceeding $5,000. Consequently, the evidence cannot support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft. The conviction is reversed, and the case is remanded for entry of judgment on a lesser-included offense or for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992), evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the Georges’ deliberate misrepresentations about the truck’s condition—designed to inflate its value—combined with the officers’ presentation of a cashier’s check for the statutory threshold amount, provides ample basis for a jury to infer the Georges sought to obtain $5,000. The defense’s claim that the check was “procured by the officers’ initiative” ignores the reality of the transaction: the Georges initiated the deception, invited the officers to purchase the truck, and agreed to complete the sale. Their acceptance of the check was a clear indication they sought that amount.

The defense’s vagueness argument fails as a matter of law. They claim the statute provides no fair notice because the “amount received” depends on the victim’s actions. But this ignores the objective standard set by *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. App. 753, 765, 319 P.3d 1090, 1096 (2014), which explicitly upheld the theft statute against vagueness challenges. The statute requires proof of the amount the defendant *sought to receive*, not the victim’s unilateral decision. Here, the Georges’ own conduct—making false claims about the truck to secure a higher price—demonstrated their intent to obtain funds exceeding $5,000. They had ample notice that such deception could lead to felony charges, as the statute’s plain language and *Guevara*’s interpretation provide clear guidance. The defense’s attempt to conflate evidentiary shortcomings with constitutional vagueness is a red herring. As *Lui* held, a statute is not vague simply because the State failed to prove its elements in a specific case.

Finally, the defense’s claim that the Georges could not commit theft of their own property is legally indefensible. Theft by deception requires obtaining “property of another,” but the “property obtained” is the money extracted through deception, not the item sold. In *Guevara*, this Court confirmed that the defendant’s theft of money via fraudulent misrepresentations—regardless of the defendant’s prior ownership of the item—constitutes theft of the money itself. Here, the Georges misrepresented the truck’s value to obtain money from the officers; that money was “property of another” at the moment of the deception. The defense’s reliance on *State v. Hutchens*, 77 Wash. App. 441, 447, 890 P.2d 113, 117 (1995), is irrelevant. *Hutchens* addressed a different factual scenario where the defendant sold property they owned without deception, but *Guevara* squarely overruled any notion that misrepresentations in a sale of one’s own property cannot constitute theft of the money received. The Georges’ ownership of the truck is irrelevant to the theft of the money they sought through fraud.

Your Honor, the Court of Appeals correctly applied binding precedent and properly concluded the evidence supported the conviction. The defense’s arguments mischaracterize *Guevara*, ignore the reasonable inferences from the transaction, and misapply constitutional principles. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the Court of Appeals and uphold the conviction for attempted first-degree theft.

## IN THE WASHINGTON STATE SUPREME COURT

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the reversal of the Court of Appeals and enter judgment for the Defendants, or in the alternative, remand for proceedings consistent with lesser-included offense proceedings. The Defense agrees with this Court's binding interpretation of "value" under *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d 484, 493, 158 P.3d 1224, 1229 (Wash. 2007), which holds that "the value of the property stolen is the amount the defendant received or sought to receive, not the difference between the market value and the amount paid." However, as this Court correctly identified in its ruling, the critical deficiency in this case is not the legal standard but the complete absence of evidence establishing that the Georges sought to receive any amount exceeding $5,000.

---

## I. THE EVIDENCE FAILS TO ESTABLISH THAT THE GEORGES SOUGHT TO RECEIVE $5,000 OR MORE

The Prosecution's entire theory rests on a single inference: that because officers arrived with a cashier's check, the Georges must have demanded or agreed to receive that specific amount. This inference, however, finds no support in the factual record and constitutes precisely the type of speculation that insufficient evidence analysis forbids.

**A. The Record Contains No Evidence of Price Negotiation or Agreement**

The facts provided establish the following sequence: Detective O'Donnell spoke with Tommy George, who reported that his father John George was the original and sole owner, that the truck had always been garaged, was in "perfect condition," and had 70,000 miles on it. RP (Sept. 9, 2004) at 6. John George confirmed these representations when he met with Detective O'Donnell, stating the truck had "always been in the garage," had 70,000 miles on it, and was in "great shape." Id. at 9, 12. John George required time to retrieve the title, so the parties arranged to complete the sale the following day. The officers arrived the next day with a cashier's check and, after the sale was completed, the officers arrested the Georges.

Notably absent from this record is any evidence that the Georges negotiated a specific price, demanded a particular amount, or agreed to sell the truck for any sum exceeding $1,800—the amount John George actually paid Potter. The Prosecution argues that "when parties agree to complete a sale, it is reasonable to infer they have negotiated a specific price." This argument conflates the act of completing a transaction with the act of demanding a particular sum. The record reflects that the Georges made misrepresentations about the truck's condition, but nothing establishes the price they sought to obtain.

**B. The Cashier's Check Was Procured by the Officers' Initiative, Not the Georges' Demand**

This Court correctly observed that "the mere fact that the officers arrived with a cashier's check does not establish that the Georges 'sought to receive' that specific amount. The Prosecution's characterization of this fact as "ignor[ing] the reality of the transaction" actually mischaracterizes the record. Under *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992), evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the essential element is the amount the defendants "sought to receive." Without evidence of a demanded or agreed price, no rational trier of fact could find beyond a reasonable doubt that the Georges sought to receive $5,000 or more. The Prosecution argues that "the Georges initiated the deception, invited the officers to purchase the truck, and agreed to complete the sale." The gap between fraudulent intent and the specific dollar amount sought is precisely what the Prosecution has failed to bridge.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court affirms the reversal of the Court of Appeals. The State presented insufficient evidence to support the conviction for attempted first-degree theft. While the State proved the defendants committed theft by deception, it failed to prove the essential element that the value of the property stolen exceeded $5,000. The conviction is reversed, and the case is remanded for entry of judgment on a lesser-included offense or for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review and Legal Framework**

This Court reviews the sufficiency of the evidence to determine whether "any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 201, 829 P.2d 1068, 1074 (Wash. 1992). In a theft by deception case, the "value of the property stolen is the amount the defendant received or sought to receive, not the difference between the market value and the amount paid." *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d 484, 493, 158 P.3d 1224, 1229 (Wash. 2007). To sustain a conviction for attempted first-degree theft, the State must prove that the defendants sought to obtain property exceeding $5,000 in value.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence Regarding Value**

The State argues that the Georges' acceptance of a cashier's check, combined with their misrepresentations about the truck's condition, provides a sufficient basis for a jury to infer that the Georges sought to receive $5,000. The Court disagrees.

The record provided indicates that the Georges made specific misrepresentations regarding the truck's history, mileage, and condition to induce the officers to buy the truck. However, the record is entirely devoid of any evidence regarding the price the Georges demanded, negotiated, or agreed upon. The facts state that the parties "arranged to complete the sale the following day" and that the officers "arrived the next day with a cashier's check." There is no testimony or evidence in the provided facts that the Georges specified a sales price prior to the officers' presentation of the check.

The prosecution asks this Court to infer that because the parties agreed to meet, a specific price exceeding $5,000 must have been negotiated. This inference is not supported by the evidence. The amount of the cashier's check reflects the officers' willingness to pay, not the amount the Georges affirmatively sought to receive. Under *State v. Guevara*, the relevant value is the amount the defendant "sought to receive." *State v. Guevara*, 160 Wash. 2d at 493, 158 P.3d at 1229. In *Guevara*, the State presented evidence of the specific amounts the defendant demanded from the victims. Here, the State presented no such evidence.

The mere fact that the Georges accepted the check does not prove they "sought" that specific amount. A defendant may accept an offer without having initiated the demand for that sum. Because the State failed to introduce evidence of the asking price or the negotiated amount, no rational trier of fact could find beyond a reasonable doubt that the Georges sought to receive more than $5,000. The evidence is therefore insufficient to support the first-degree conviction.

**III. Remaining Issues**

The Court declines to address the defense's arguments regarding the constitutionality of the statute or the "theft of own property" theory, as the disposition on evidentiary grounds is dispositive. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the statute is not unconstitutionally vague, as established in *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. App. 753, 765, 319 P.3d 1090, 1096 (2014), and that the "property obtained" was the money sought from the officers. However, these legal principles cannot salvage a conviction where the State has failed to prove an essential element of the offense charged.

**CONCLUSION**

The State established that the Georges engaged in deception to sell the truck, but it failed to prove the value of the attempted theft exceeded $5,000. The evidence is insufficient to support the conviction for attempted first-degree theft. The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
