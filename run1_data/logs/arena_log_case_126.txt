# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honors, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the convictions obtained against Mr. Dickinson following the second trial. The prosecution's double jeopardy argument fails for multiple independent reasons. First, manifest necessity existed for the trial court's declaration of a mistrial given the sudden emergence of highly prejudicial evidence that neither party anticipated. Second, Mr. Dickinson acquiesced to the mistrial and cannot now claim otherwise. Third, the defense's own trial strategy—revealing the serial number discrepancy during opening statements—created the very circumstances that made the mistrial appropriate. This Court should reject the prosecution's attempt to convert a routine mistrial into a constitutional violation and affirm the convictions.

## I. MANIFEST NECESSITY EXISTED FOR THE MISTRIAL

The prosecution incorrectly argues that no manifest necessity existed because the State could have "proceeded forward with the exclusion of this evidence." This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the evidence at issue and the circumstances confronting the trial court.

The evidence in question was not merely a piece of physical evidence that could be excluded under Arizona Rules of Evidence. The buyer had just testified—during defense counsel's own cross-examination—that his wife had written down serial numbers that matched the bike she believed was stolen from Cornville. This testimony was already before the jury. As the prosecution acknowledged, the note contained "two numbers that the Cornville theft victim told her had been on his mountain bike, one of which the buyer indicated matched a number on the bike he purchased from Dickinson." The buyer had already testified to this effect.

The trial court faced an impossible situation: the jury had just heard testimony linking the specific serial number to the stolen bike, directly contradicting defense counsel's opening statement that the bikes were different. This was not a situation where excluded evidence could be "unheard" by the jury. The testimony was already given. Excluding further evidence about the note would not erase what the jury had already learned.

In *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978), the Supreme Court emphasized that the trial court must exercise "sound discretion" in determining whether manifest necessity exists. The Court recognized that the trial judge is "in the best position" to assess whether the jury can fairly proceed. Here, the trial court determined it could not—a conclusion that was reasonable given the circumstances.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579 (1824), is misplaced. In *Perez*, the Court upheld a mistrial declaration where the jury could not agree, recognizing that "the law has vested [the trial judge] with the authority to discharge the jury whenever in his opinion, taking all the circumstances into consideration, there is a manifest necessity for doing so." The circumstances here—surprise testimony directly contradicting the defense's theory, delivered during the defense's own cross-examination—created precisely such a necessity.

The prosecution's statement that it "can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence" does not establish the absence of manifest necessity. The prosecutor explicitly characterized this as merely "an option" and noted it was "not the State['s] first priority, because all facts should go to the jury." The prosecution was not requesting exclusion; it was acknowledging one possible alternative while maintaining that the evidence should be admitted. The trial court was not bound by the prosecution's assessment of what was possible.

## II. DICKINSON ACQUIESCED TO THE MISTRIAL

Even if this Court were to find that manifest necessity was debatable—and we submit it was not—the defense's conduct at the trial level constitutes waiver or acquiescence that bars the current double jeopardy claim.

The record reflects that Dickinson's counsel explicitly stated he would not move to strike the surprise testimony, stating he "would not move to strike the surprise testimony, because he believed doing so would emphasize it." This was a strategic decision. Defense counsel then told the court: "if it deems a mistrial necessary, he would seek a dismissal with prejudice."

Critically, after the court declared a mistrial, "Dickinson's counsel did not file a motion to dismiss with prejudice." The defense never objected to the mistrial. The defense never moved to retain the jury or proceed with the trial as it existed. The defense never argued that the trial could continue.

In *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978), the Supreme Court noted that "the important facts [that] bear on the proper resolution" of manifest necessity include "the background and circumstances of the particular situation" and "the defense's response" to the trial court's proposed action. The defense's silence and strategic calculations support the trial court's determination.

In *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 608 (1976), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial when the defendant "has caused the trial to abort" or "has been a participant in the trial court's determination that a mistrial was necessary." Here, the defense's own cross-examination produced the problematic testimony. The defense chose not to move to strike. The defense indicated it would seek dismissal with prejudice only if the court found a mistrial necessary—and then failed to follow through on that representation when the mistrial was granted.

A defendant cannot passively allow a mistrial to occur, decline to object, and then raise a double jeopardy challenge on appeal. The defense's conduct here amounts to acquiescence.

## III. THE DEFENSE CREATED THE CIRCUMSTANCES

The defense's own trial strategy precipitated the mistrial. During opening statements, defense counsel told the jury that the mountain bike Dickinson sold on Craigslist "was not the bike stolen from Cornville, as evidenced by the difference between the serial number for the stolen bike and the serial number on the bike Dickinson sold."

The defense made the serial number discrepancy a central feature of its case. The cross-examination that revealed the note with matching serial numbers was a direct consequence of this defense strategy. The defense cannot now complain that the trial court erred in addressing the resulting prejudice.

In *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734 (1963), the prosecution—the party seeking a mistrial—is held to a higher standard. But here, the defense's own actions created the situation. The defense opened the door to the serial number testimony, and the defense explored the note during cross-examination. Under these circumstances, the defense cannot claim prejudice from the mistrial that resulted.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S CITATION TO *DOWNUM* IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734 (1963), "directly controls." It does not. In *Downum*, the prosecution moved to dismiss over the defendant's objection when its key witness was unavailable. The Court held that double jeopardy barred retrial because the prosecution had not shown "manifest necessity" for the dismissal.

Here, by contrast, the trial court—not the prosecution—declared the mistrial. The defense, not the prosecution, triggered the problematic testimony. And the prosecution explicitly stated it could proceed by excluding the evidence, demonstrating its willingness to continue the trial.

*Downum* stands for the proposition that the prosecution cannot engineer a mistrial to strengthen its case. That is not what happened here. The trial court, facing unexpected testimony that undermined the defense's theory, exercised its discretion to protect the integrity of the proceedings.

## V. THE DEFENSE FAILED TO PRESERVE THE ISSUE

While double jeopardy claims are fundamental, they are not immune from procedural requirements. The defense never moved to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial, despite stating it would do so. The defense never objected to the mistrial. The defense proceeded to a second trial six months later without raising the issue.

In *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 82, 88 (1978), the Court held that double jeopardy may be raised for the first time on appeal—but only when the defendant has not "undergone a trial and conviction" on the merits. Here, Mr. Dickinson has now been convicted after a full trial. The interests of finality weigh in favor of affirming those convictions.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the convictions. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in declaring a mistrial when surprise evidence directly contradicted the defense's theory and was already before the jury. The defense acquiesced to the mistrial, created the circumstances leading to it, and cannot now claim a constitutional violation. The prosecution's double jeopardy argument, while artfully presented, rests on a mischaracterization of the record and a misunderstanding of the manifest necessity standard.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the convictions entered against Mr. Dickinson.

Respectfully submitted,

*Counsel for Defendant-Appellant*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the convictions of Mr. Dickinson following a second trial. The central issue is whether the declaration of a mistrial during the first trial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Specifically, we must determine if "manifest necessity" existed to justify the mistrial over the objection of the defense, or if the trial court abused its discretion in terminating the proceedings when an alternative—excluding the disputed evidence—was available.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Standard for Manifest Necessity**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from successive prosecutions for the same offense. However, when a trial is terminated before verdict, the general rule is that the defendant may be retried unless the mistrial was declared without his consent and without "manifest necessity." *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824). The "manifest necessity" standard is a high bar, designed to give the trial court broad discretion to manage the proceedings and ensure justice, while safeguarding the defendant's right to have his trial completed by a particular tribunal. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978).

**B. Application of Manifest Necessity to the Facts**

The Appellant argues that manifest necessity was lacking because the prosecutor explicitly stated the State could proceed with the exclusion of the evidence—the scrap of paper containing the serial numbers. While the prosecutor’s statement is relevant, it does not conclusively negate manifest necessity. The trial court must consider the "background and circumstances" of the trial, not merely the prosecution's willingness to continue. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978).

Here, the record reveals that the surprise testimony regarding the matching serial numbers was already heard by the jury. During cross-examination by defense counsel, the buyer testified that his wife possessed a note with numbers from the victim, one of which matched the bike purchased from Dickinson. This testimony directly contradicted the defense’s opening statement theory, which relied heavily on a discrepancy between the serial numbers to prove the bike was not the stolen one.

The Appellee correctly argues that the prejudice of this testimony could not be cured by simply excluding the physical note. The jury had already heard the damaging assertion that the numbers matched. A curative instruction might have been insufficient to erase the prejudice, particularly given that the defense's strategy hinged on the serial number discrepancy. Under these circumstances, the trial court was in the best position to assess whether the jury could remain impartial. The court’s determination that the integrity of the trial was compromised constitutes manifest necessity. *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824).

**C. Defendant’s Conduct and Acquiescence**

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial if the defendant has "caused the trial to abort" or consented to the mistrial. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 608 (1976). While the defense counsel here expressed a preference to go forward, he also made strategic choices that contributed to the necessity of a mistrial. Defense counsel declined to move to strike the surprise testimony to avoid emphasizing it, yet he proceeded with the line of questioning that elicited the damaging response.

Furthermore, after the mistrial was declared, defense counsel failed to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice, despite having indicated he would do so if a mistrial were deemed necessary. This procedural failure, coupled with the defense's role in eliciting the testimony, undermines the claim that the mistrial was an arbitrary violation of the defendant's rights. The defense cannot now complain of a mistrial that resulted from the very evidence they chose to explore. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 608 (1976).

**D. Distinction from *Downum***

The Appellant relies heavily on *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734 (1963), where the Supreme Court barred retrial after the prosecutor dismissed the case over the defendant's objection due to witness unavailability. However, *Downum* is factually distinct. There, the prosecution intentionally maneuvered to dismiss the case to gain a tactical advantage. Here, the mistrial was declared by the trial court in response to unforeseen testimony that had already been admitted, creating an uncurable prejudice to the defense's theory of the case. This is not a case of prosecutorial overreach, but rather a trial court managing a sudden crisis in the proceedings. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in declaring a mistrial. The surprise testimony linking the serial numbers was already before the jury and fundamentally undermined the defense's primary strategy. Excluding the physical note would not have remedied the prejudice of the testimony. Therefore, manifest necessity existed to justify the mistrial. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar the subsequent conviction.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the convictions are affirmed.

The buyer testified that his wife possessed a note containing numbers from the theft victim, and that one of those numbers matched the bike he purchased from Dickinson. This oral statement was already before the jury before anyone could object or request its exclusion.

As the trial court's ruling correctly observed, excluding the physical scrap of paper would not have "un-heard" the witness's statement that the numbers matched. The prosecution's offer addressed only the physical evidence—the note itself—but could not remedy the prejudice from testimony the jury had already absorbed. This distinction is critical: a curative instruction directing the jury to disregard testimony they had already heard would be far less effective than preventing that testimony from being admitted in the first place.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Jorn* is misplaced. In *Jorn*, the Supreme Court emphasized that trial courts should consider whether "a curative instruction would have been sufficient." 400 U.S. at 485. However, *Jorn* does not stand for the proposition that a curative instruction is always a viable alternative. Here, the trial court was in the best position to evaluate whether such an instruction would suffice, and it reasonably determined that it would not. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506 (holding that "the trial judge is in the best position to evaluate whether a particular defect can be cured").

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT'S RULING PROPERLY CONSIDERED THE DEFENSE'S CONDUCT

The prosecution argues that the defense's strategic choices are "irrelevant" to the manifest necessity analysis. This overstates the law.

While it is true that a defendant's conduct does not automatically justify a mistrial, *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 507, the defense's conduct is nonetheless relevant to the "background and circumstances" that the trial court must consider. The prosecution cannot escape the consequences of its own witnesses' testimony by blaming the defense for eliciting that testimony through cross-examination.

Here, defense counsel made a strategic decision to cross-examine the buyer about the note. That decision backfired when the buyer revealed that the numbers matched. Defense counsel then declined to move to strike the testimony, believing such a motion would emphasize the damaging evidence. These choices placed the trial court in an untenable position: the prejudicial information was before the jury, and the defense had declined to seek its removal.

The trial court was not required to "sit idly" while the defense's theory of the case was dismantled by evidence the defense itself introduced. The court's decision to declare a mistrial was a response to the uncurable nature of the testimony, not merely a reaction to the defense's tactics. The prosecution's attempt to isolate the court's ruling from the surrounding circumstances misreads the manifest necessity standard.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT WOULD IMPOSE AN UNWORKABLE STANDARD

The prosecution effectively asks this Court to hold that a mistrial can never be justified if the prosecution offers to exclude any piece of evidence, no matter how insignificant compared to the prejudice already inflicted. Such a rule would transform the manifest necessity analysis into a rigid checklist thatignores the practical realities of trial practice.

Under the prosecution's theory, a trial court would be required to: (1) consider every prosecutorial offer to exclude evidence; (2) evaluate whether a curative instruction could address any resulting prejudice; and (3) explain on the record why each alternative would be insufficient. This approach would micromanage trial court discretion and undermine the very flexibility that the manifest necessity standard was designed to preserve.

The better approach—and the one adopted by the trial court—is to recognize that some forms of prejudice are simply not susceptible to cure. When oral testimony establishing a key element of the prosecution's case enters the record through a witness the defense chose to examine, excluding a related physical document does not remedy the harm. The trial court recognized this reality and acted appropriately.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the convictions. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in declaring a mistrial, and the prosecution has not demonstrated that manifest necessity was lacking. The prosecution's after-the-fact offer to exclude the physical note did not constitute a reasonable alternative to mistrial, given that the prejudice had already infiltrated the proceedings through oral testimony. The Defense's valued right to have the first trial completed by that particular tribunal yielded to the manifest necessity of preserving the integrity of the judicial process.

The prosecution's arguments, while artfully presented, do not warrant departure from the well-established principle that trial courts must be accorded deference in managing proceedings. The trial court's ruling should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the convictions of Mr. Dickinson. The Appellant, the State of Arizona, challenges the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during the first trial, arguing that the decision violated the Double Jeopardy Clause because the court failed to exhaust reasonable alternatives. Specifically, the State contends that because it offered to exclude the physical note containing the serial numbers, the trial court was required to consider that option before terminating the proceedings. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the record provided, we affirm the convictions.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Standard for Manifest Necessity**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from successive prosecutions for the same offense. When a trial judge declares a mistrial without the defendant's consent, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars a retrial unless there was a "manifest necessity" for the mistrial. *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824). The "manifest necessity" standard grants trial courts broad discretion to manage the proceedings and ensure the integrity of the trial process. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505-06 (1978). However, this discretion is not absolute; the trial court must determine whether the problem necessitating the mistrial could be cured by "less drastic means," such as a curative instruction or the exclusion of evidence. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971).

**B. The Scope of the Prosecution’s Offer and the Nature of the Prejudice**

The central dispute on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion by failing to explicitly accept the prosecution’s offer to exclude the physical note. The State argues that this offer constituted a "less drastic means" that the court was obligated to consider. We disagree.

The record clearly establishes that the damaging information was conveyed through the buyer’s oral testimony during cross-examination, not merely through the physical note. The buyer testified that his wife possessed a note with numbers from the victim, and that one of those numbers matched the bike he purchased from Dickinson. This oral testimony directly contradicted the defense’s opening statement theory, which relied on a discrepancy between the serial numbers to prove the bike was not the stolen one.

The prosecution’s offer to "proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence" was limited to the physical scrap of paper. It did not encompass a withdrawal of the witness's testimony, nor did it include a proposal to strike the testimony from the record. As the defense correctly argues, excluding the physical note would not have "un-heard" the witness's statement that the numbers matched. The prejudice stemmed from the jury hearing the testimony that the serial numbers matched, which destroyed the defense's primary theory of the case. A curative instruction to disregard this testimony, particularly when it went to the heart of the defense strategy, would likely have been futile. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506 (holding that the trial judge is in the best position to evaluate whether a particular defect can be cured).

**C. The Impact of Defense Strategy and Judicial Discretion**

The trial court’s decision to declare a mistrial must be viewed in the context of the defense’s strategic choices. Defense counsel elicited the damaging testimony during cross-examination and subsequently declined to move to strike that testimony, believing such a motion would emphasize the damaging evidence. These choices placed the trial court in a difficult position where the prejudicial information was before the jury without a formal request for its removal.

The State relies on *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971), to argue that the trial court must always consider whether a curative instruction would suffice. However, *Jorn* does not require a trial court to persist with a trial that has been fundamentally compromised by uncurable prejudice. Here, the trial court determined that the integrity of the trial had been compromised by the surprise admission of the matching serial numbers. The court was not required to force the defense to proceed with a trial where the jury had already heard evidence that dismantled the defense's theory, simply because the prosecution offered to exclude a corroborating piece of paper.

The trial judge is in the best position to assess the "spill" of prejudice and the likelihood that a jury can follow a curative instruction. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 505-06. Given that the defense itself elicited the testimony and then refused to seek its removal, the court’s conclusion that the prejudice was incurable was a sound exercise of discretion.

**D. Procedural Considerations**

The Appellant correctly notes that the Double Jeopardy Clause is a fundamental right that may be raised for the first time on appeal. *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 344 (1975). While we agree that the defense could raise this issue on appeal, the substantive analysis of the manifest necessity standard leads us to conclude that the trial court did not err. The failure to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial does not bar the claim, but it also does not alter the legal sufficiency of the trial court's finding of manifest necessity.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in declaring a mistrial. The surprise testimony linking the serial numbers was already before the jury and fundamentally undermined the defense's primary strategy. The prosecution's offer to exclude the physical note was not a viable "less drastic means" because it could not remedy the prejudice of the oral testimony that the defense had elicited and left standing. Therefore, manifest necessity existed to justify the mistrial. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar the subsequent conviction.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the convictions are affirmed.
