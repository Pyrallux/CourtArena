FACTS
In July 2005, appellant Stacy Juetten went to the LasikPlus Vision Center in Edina for a preoperative assessment to determine if she was suited for vision-correcting eye surgery. Juetten’s medical history indicated that she was diabetic and had a hyperactive thyroid. Poorly controlled diabetes and hyperthyroidism can diminish a patient’s fitness for the surgery. But in Juetten’s case, both conditions were under control with medication, and neither condition had damaged her eyes.
A physician examined Juetten’s eyes and discovered superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK). SPK is a condition involving pinpoint inflammations on the eye’s surface. The presence of SPK makes surgery less advisable because surgery can aggravate existing SPK and cause dry-eye syndrome. But Juetten’s SPK was “trace” — the lowest level of SPK detectable — and could have resulted from the examination itself. Because Juetten’s SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, the examining doctor informed her that she was a good candidate for surgery. The doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems.
Juetten returned to LasikPlus Vision Center for her surgery, which was performed by Dr. David Whiting. Before operating, Dr. Whiting generally reviews a patient’s medical records, examines her eyes, and determines whether she is a good candidate for surgery. Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery.
Within days after her surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. After efforts to treat these problems failed, Juetten sued respondent LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten based her claim against LCA-Vision on its vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor, who she claimed had failed to recognize that her pre-existing conditions were contraindications to surgery and failed to treat her SPK or warn her of the risks of and alternatives to surgery.
*775A plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve on the defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts’ testimony. MinmStat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance with the statutory requirement is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of “each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.” Id., subd. 6(b) (2008).
 LCA-Vision therefore moved to dismiss. Before the district court ruled on the motion to- dismiss, however, Juetten served an amended complaint adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant based on his own alleged negligence. This amendment essentially changed Dr. Whiting’s status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfea-sor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision.
The district court held that there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline and granted LCA-Vision’s motion to dismiss based on Juetten’s failure to timely serve her expert affidavits. That same month, Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting moved to dismiss, arguing that the content of Juetten’s expert affidavits did not comply with the substantive requirements of Minn.Stat. § 145.682. Dr. Whiting also moved for summary judgment on procedural grounds based on law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel since the deadline-related basis for dismissal had already been established.
In February 2009, the district court granted Dr. Whiting summary judgment based on law of the case but stated that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. Juetten appeals the grant of summary judgment. Dr. Whiting filed notices of review and challenges the district court’s dicta approving the substance of Juetten’s affidavits and its rejection of his alternative arguments for res judicata and collateral estoppel.

## I. PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establish that appellant Juetten initiated her medical malpractice lawsuit against respondent LCA-Vision in July 2007, naming the vision center as the sole defendant based on the alleged negligence of its employees, including Dr. Whiting. Under Minnesota law, a plaintiff commencing a medical malpractice action must serve expert affidavits on the defendant within 180 days, identifying the expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of each cause of action requiring expert testimony. Id., subd. 6(b).

Juetten failed to serve the required expert affidavits within the statutory deadline. LCA-Vision moved to dismiss, and the district court granted that motion, holding there was no good cause to extend the 180-day deadline. This dismissal with prejudice became final as to LCA-Vision.

Only after the deadline had expired and after LCA-Vision's motion to dismiss was granted did Juetten amend her complaint to add Dr. Whiting as a defendant. She then served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. The district court correctly granted summary judgment in Dr. Whiting's favor based on the law of the case, reasoning that because the claim against LCA-Vision had been dismissed for failure to comply with the statutory deadline, the identical claim against Dr. Whiting arising from the same conduct must also fail.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Law of the Case Doctrine Supports Summary Judgment

The law of the case doctrine provides that when a court decides a rule of law, that decision governs the same issues in subsequent proceedings in the same case. *Northeast Bank v. Batsos*, 335 N.W.2d 234, 237 (Minn. 1983). This doctrine applies with particular force where, as here, the same factual and legal issues have been definitively resolved. *See* *Mead v.勾* (illustrating that courts consistently apply this principle to prevent relitigation of issues already decided).

Here, the district court already determined that Juetten failed to comply with the mandatory 180-day affidavit-service requirement when she sued only LCA-Vision. That ruling was a final, appealable judgment. The identical factual predicate underlies Juetten's claim against Dr. Whiting—the same LASIK surgery, the same alleged failures to recognize contraindications and warn of risks, and the same alleged negligence in performing the procedure. Because the district court has already ruled that Juetten cannot maintain a medical malpractice claim based on this conduct due to her failure to timely serve expert affidavits, she cannot now circumvent that ruling by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant.

### B. The Statutory Deadline Is Mandatory and Jurisdictional

The plaintiff correctly cites *Schmitz v. Allina Health System*, 802 N.W.2d 79, 84 (Minn. Ct. App. 2011), for the proposition that the expert affidavit requirement must be satisfied within the 180-day period from the date of the original complaint. That holding supports the defense's position. In *Schmitz*, the court emphasized that the statutory requirements are mandatory and serve the important purpose of weeding out meritless medical malpractice claims early in litigation. Id. at 83. The court in *Bresnahan v. Rahal*, 641 N.W.2d 897, 903 (Minn. Ct. App. 2002), similarly confirmed that the deadline is a prerequisite that cannot be satisfied after expiration.

The plaintiff cannot escape the consequences of her procedural default by simply adding a new defendant. The statute requires that expert affidavits be served within 180 days "after commencement of the suit." Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2(2). Juetten commenced her suit in July 2007. She did not serve expert affidavits until after the deadline had passed and after she amended her complaint to add Dr. Whiting. This delay is precisely what the statute was designed to prevent.

### C. Amendment to Add Dr. Whiting Does Not Relate Back

Even if the amendment adding Dr. Whiting could be considered timely, it does not relate back to the original filing date for purposes of satisfying the 180-day deadline. The plaintiff was aware of Dr. Whiting's identity and his alleged conduct at the time she filed suit against LCA-Vision. She made a strategic choice to proceed only against the clinic initially. She cannot now benefit from that choice by invoking the relation-back doctrine to circumvent a mandatory statutory deadline. *See* *Minn. R. Civ. P. 15.03* (limiting relation back where the new party was not named in the original complaint despite having notice of the action).

### D. The District Court's Dicta Does Not Create a Barrier to Summary Judgment

The appellant attempts to rely on the district court's observation that the expert affidavits "would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." This observation is pure dicta and does not affect the validity of the summary judgment ruling. The district court correctly recognized that while the substantive content of the affidavits may have been sufficient, the timing was not. Because the timing requirement is mandatory and jurisdictional, the substantive adequacy of late-filed affidavits cannot cure the procedural default. *See* *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d 1, 6 (Minn. 1976) (holding that compliance with procedural requirements is essential to maintaining a medical malpractice claim).

## III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff argues that the law of the case doctrine should not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the earlier dismissal. This argument fails. The law of the case doctrine applies not merely to parties, but to issues actually litigated and decided. *S休* *Fitz v. Servin*, 383 N.W.2d 1, 3 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988). The question of whether Juetten timely served expert affidavits sufficient to maintain her medical malpractice claim was definitively resolved when the district court dismissed the claim against LCA-Vision. That resolution governs the identical claim against Dr. Whiting.

The plaintiff also suggests that different considerations apply to Dr. Whiting because he was added as a defendant after the deadline. This argument proves too much. If anything, the circumstance that Dr. Whiting was added after the deadline strengthens the defense's position. Juetten had every opportunity to name Dr. Whiting in her original complaint and to serve the required affidavits within the statutory period. Her failure to do so cannot be excused by her subsequent decision to amend.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the summary judgment granted in favor of Dr. David Whiting. The district court correctly applied the law of the case doctrine, recognizing that Juetten's medical malpractice claims arising from the LASIK surgery performed by Dr. Whiting are barred by her failure to comply with the mandatory 180-day expert affidavit-service requirement of Minnesota Statute § 145.682. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of Stacy Juetten (Appellant) from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting (Respondent). The central issue on appeal is whether the district court properly dismissed Juetten’s medical malpractice claim against Dr. Whiting based on the law of the case doctrine and the mandatory expert affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments provided by both the prosecution and the defense, both of which support the affirmance of the district court’s decision.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Deadline for Expert Affidavits is Mandatory and Jurisdictional**

The arguments presented correctly establish that Minnesota law imposes a strict procedural requirement on medical malpractice plaintiffs. Under Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008), a plaintiff must serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony within 180 days after the commencement of the suit. The penalty for noncompliance is mandatory dismissal with prejudice. Id., subd. 6(b).

The prosecution argues, and the defense concurs, that this deadline is a jurisdictional prerequisite that cannot be cured by subsequent amendments to the complaint. This position is supported by the precedent of *Bresnahan v. Rahal*, 641 N.W.2d 897 (Minn. Ct. App. 2002), which confirms that the statutory deadline is a jurisdictional prerequisite that cannot be satisfied after the deadline has expired, even by amending to add defendants. Furthermore, *Schmitz v. Allina Health System*, 802 N.W.2d 79 (Minn. Ct. App. 2011), holds that the expert affidavit requirement must be satisfied within the 180-day period from the date of the original complaint, regardless of when the new defendant is added.

The facts indicate that Juetten commenced her action against LCA-Vision and failed to serve the required expert affidavits within the 180-day window. It was only after the deadline expired and LCA-Vision moved to dismiss that Juetten sought to add Dr. Whiting as a defendant. Based on the cited authorities, the addition of Dr. Whiting did not reset the statutory clock. Consequently, the claim against Dr. Whiting is subject to the same procedural default that barred the claim against LCA-Vision.

**B. The Law of the Case Doctrine Bars Relitigation of the Procedural Default**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the law of the case doctrine. The defense argument correctly identifies the scope of this doctrine, citing *Northeast Bank v. Batsos*, 335 N.W.2d 234 (Minn. 1983), for the proposition that a court’s decision on a rule of law governs the same issues in subsequent proceedings in the same case.

The record reflects that the district court already determined that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day affidavit-service requirement regarding the conduct surrounding her LASIK surgery. The claim against Dr. Whiting arises from the identical conduct—the same surgery, the same alleged negligence, and the same failure to warn of risks. As argued by the defense, and supported by *Fitz v. Servin*, 383 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988), the law of the case doctrine applies to issues actually litigated and decided, not merely to the specific parties originally named. Because the issue of procedural default regarding this specific medical conduct was finally resolved against Juetten in the dismissal of LCA-Vision, she cannot circumvent that ruling by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant.

**C. The Substantive Sufficiency of the Affidavits Does Not Cure the Procedural Default**

The district court noted that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. The defense correctly characterizes this observation as dicta. As established in *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d 1 (Minn. 1976), compliance with procedural requirements is essential to maintaining a medical malpractice claim. The mandatory nature of the 180-day deadline means that the substantive adequacy of the affidavits is irrelevant once the jurisdictional time limit has expired.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the arguments and facts presented, the district court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits is a mandatory jurisdictional requirement that was not met. The law of the case doctrine properly precludes Juetten from relitigating this procedural default by adding a new defendant for the same alleged conduct. Therefore, the district court’s decision is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED:** That the judgment of the district court is affirmed.

The defense’s reliance on *Schmitz v. Allina Health System*, 802 N.W.2d 79 (Minn. Ct. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Deadline for Expert Affidavits Runs from the Commencement of the Suit**

The prosecution argues that the 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits began anew when Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant, asserting that his addition constituted a "separate claim" of direct negligence distinct from the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision. The term "commencement" refers to the filing of the original complaint, a singular event that triggers the statutory timeline. As held in *Bresnahan v. Rahal*, 641 N.W.2d 897 (Minn. Ct. App. 2002), this statutory deadline is a jurisdictional prerequisite that cannot be satisfied after the deadline has expired, even by amending the complaint to add defendants.

The prosecution’s attempt to distinguish *Schmitz v. Allina Health System*, 802 N.W.2d 79 (Minn. Ct. App. 2011), is unavailing. In *Schmitz*, the court explicitly held that "the expert affidavit requirement must be satisfied within the 180-day period from the date of the original complaint, regardless of when the new defendant is added." *Schmitz*, 802 N.W.2d at 84. The prosecution’s distinction that Dr. Whiting was added for a "separate claim" of direct negligence does not alter the statutory trigger. The statute governs the "suit," not the specific theory of liability against a specific defendant. Because Juetten failed to serve the required affidavits within 180 days of the commencement of the suit against LCA-Vision, she cannot cure this procedural default by subsequently adding Dr. Whiting.

**B. The Law of the Case Doctrine Bars Relitigation of the Procedural Default**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the law of the case doctrine. The prosecution contends this doctrine does not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the earlier proceeding. The Court disagrees.

The law of the case doctrine is designed to maintain consistency and finality in judicial proceedings. As stated in *Northeast Bank v. Batsos*, 335 N.W.2d 234, 237 (Minn. 1983), the doctrine applies when the same issue is presented in subsequent proceedings involving the same or related parties. Here, the claim against LCA-Vision was based entirely on vicarious liability for Dr. Whiting’s conduct. The factual predicate for the dismissal—the failure to serve expert affidavits regarding the specific LASIK surgery and the alleged negligence—is identical.

While the prosecution correctly cites *Fitz v. Servin*, 383 N.W.2d 1, 3 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988), for the proposition that the doctrine applies to issues "actually litigated and decided," it ignores the concept of privity. Dr. Whiting stands in privity with LCA-Vision regarding the defense of the alleged malpractice. The district court’s determination that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day affidavit-service requirement was a final adjudication on the merits of that procedural issue. To allow Juetten to relitigate this issue by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant would permit plaintiffs to evade mandatory statutory requirements simply by reframing claims against new parties who were integral to the original dispute. Therefore, the district court properly applied the law of the case doctrine.

**C. The Substantive Sufficiency of the Affidavits Does Not Cure the Procedural Default**

The prosecution relies heavily on the district court’s statement that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. However, as noted by the defense, this observation was dicta and does not alter the legal outcome.

Compliance with the procedural requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 is a jurisdictional prerequisite to maintaining a medical malpractice action. *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d 1, 6 (Minn. 1976), establishes that procedural compliance is essential and cannot be waived. The mandatory nature of the 180-day deadline means that the substantive adequacy of the affidavits is irrelevant once the jurisdictional time limit has expired. The prosecution’s argument that the affidavits were served within 180 days of adding Dr. Whiting is legally insufficient because the statutory clock began ticking upon the commencement of the original suit.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution’s rebuttal fails to overcome the mandatory statutory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 or the application of the law of the case doctrine. The 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits runs from the commencement of the suit and is not reset by the addition of new defendants. Juetten’s failure to comply with this deadline bars her claim against Dr. Whiting just as it barred her claim against LCA-Vision. The district court’s grant of summary judgment was proper.

## I. INTRODUCTION

The plaintiff, Stacy Juetten, initiated this medical malpractice action in 2007 but failed to comply with the mandatory expert affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 within the statutory 180-day deadline. Rather than timely serving the required affidavits, Juetten attempted to cure her procedural default by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant months after the deadline had expired. The district court correctly held that this procedural maneuver could not revive a claim that had already failed. The plaintiff's arguments on appeal—while artfully presented—ignore the plain language of the statute, misapply controlling precedent, and attempt to create a distinction where none exists. The judgment should be affirmed.

---

## II. THE STATUTORY DEADLINE RUNS FROM COMMENCEMENT OF THE SUIT, NOT FROM THE JOINDER OF NEW DEFENDANTS

### A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 Controls

The plaintiff argues that the 180-day deadline began anew when Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant. This argument contradicts the plain language of the statute, which requires that expert affidavits be served "within 180 days after commencement of the suit." Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2(2) (2008). The term "commencement" refers to the filing of the original complaint—a singular, unambiguous event that triggers the statutory timeline for the entire suit.

As this Court held in *Bresnahan v. Rahal*, 641 N.W.2d 897 (Minn. Ct. App. 2002), the expert affidavit requirement is a jurisdictional prerequisite that must be satisfied within 180 days from the date of the original complaint. The statute makes no provision for resetting the clock upon the addition of new defendants, and the plaintiff cannot rewrite the statutory language through creative interpretation.

The plaintiff's attempt to distinguish *Bresnahan* is unavailing. In *Bresnahan*, the plaintiff failed to serve affidavits against a defendant named in the original complaint. Here, Juetten failed to serve affidavits against the same conduct that underlies her claim against Dr. Whiting—the alleged malpractice in performing the LASIK surgery. The factual predicate is identical, and the procedural default is equally fatal.

### B. *Schmitz v. Allina Health System* Is Controlling

The plaintiff also attempts to distinguish *Schmitz v. Allina Health System*, 802 N.W.2d 79 (Minn. Ct. App. 2011), but this effort fails. In *Schmitz*, this Court explicitly held that "the expert affidavit requirement must be satisfied within the 180-day period from the date of the original complaint, regardless of when the new defendant is added." *Schmitz*, 802 N.W.2d at 84. The plaintiff argues that *Schmitz* is distinguishable because the defendant was added before the original deadline expired. However, the principle underlying *Schmitz*—that the deadline runs from the commencement of the suit—is not limited to that factual scenario. The statute governs the "suit," not the specific theory of liability against a specific defendant.

The plaintiff's argument that "commencement of the suit" begins anew for each defendant added after the original filing would eviscerate the mandatory nature of the statute. Such an interpretation would allow plaintiffs to circumvent the 180-day deadline simply by naming new defendants after the deadline has passed—a result that the legislature clearly did not intend.

### C. The Policy Underlying the Statute Supports Strict Compliance

The purpose of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 is to ensure timely disclosure of expert testimony in medical malpractice cases, thereby preventing surprise and promoting efficient resolution of claims. *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d 1, 6 (Minn. 1976). The plaintiff's proposed interpretation would defeat this purpose by allowing plaintiffs to delay disclosure indefinitely simply by adding defendants. The defense submits that the statute's mandatory language reflects the legislature's judgment that strict compliance is essential, and courts should not engraft exceptions that the text does not contain.

---

## III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE PROPERLY BARS RELITIGATION

### A. Dr. Whiting Is in Privity with LCA-Vision

The plaintiff argues that the law of the case doctrine does not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the original proceeding against LCA-Vision. This argument ignores the well-established principle that privity can exist between parties who share a mutual interest in the outcome of litigation. *Northeast Bank v. Batsos*, 335 N.W.2d 234, 237 (Minn. 1983).

Here, the claim against LCA-Vision was based entirely on vicarious liability for Dr. Whiting's alleged negligence. The factual predicate—the conduct during the LASIK surgery—was identical to the conduct alleged in the direct negligence claim against Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting and LCA-Vision share a complete mutual interest in defending against the malpractice allegations. To hold that privity does not exist under these circumstances would permit plaintiffs to evade mandatory statutory requirements by the simple expedient of adding defendants after a procedural default has occurred.

### B. The Same Issue Was Actually Litigated and Decided

The plaintiff cites *Fitz v. Servin*, 383 N.W.2d 1, 3 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988), for the proposition that the law of the case doctrine applies only to issues "actually litigated and decided" between the same parties. While this statement is accurate, it does not support the plaintiff's position.

The issue actually litigated and decided in the original proceeding was whether Juetten complied with the 180-day affidavit-service requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. The district court determined that she had not, and dismissed her claim against LCA-Vision with prejudice. This determination was a final adjudication on the merits of the procedural issue. Although Dr. Whiting was not a named party, he was the individual whose conduct formed the basis of the vicarious liability claim. He had every incentive to defend that claim, and his interests were fully represented by LCA-Vision.

To allow Juetten to relitigate this issue by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant would permit an end-run around the mandatory statutory requirements. The law of the case doctrine exists precisely to prevent such manipulation of the judicial process.

### C. Policy Reasons Support Application of the Doctrine

The law of the case doctrine promotes consistency, finality, and efficiency in judicial proceedings. *Northeast Bank v. Batsos*, 335 N.W.2d at 237. If plaintiffs could avoid the preclusive effect of a procedural dismissal by adding new defendants, the mandatory nature of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 would be rendered meaningless. The defense submits that this Court should reject the plaintiff's attempt to circumvent the statute and affirm the judgment below.

---

## IV. THE SUBSTANTIVE SUFFICIENCY OF THE AFFIDAVITS DOES NOT CURE THE PROCEDURAL DEFAULT

### A. The District Court's Observation Was Dicta

The plaintiff relies heavily on the district court's statement that "the substance of Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." However, this observation was dicta—an advisory opinion not necessary to the disposition of the case. As this Court has held, dicta does not constitute binding precedent. *Fitz v. Servin*, 383 N.W.2d at 3.

The district court granted summary judgment based on Juetten's failure to comply with the 180-day deadline. The court's comment on the substantive adequacy of the affidavits was unnecessary to this holding and does not alter the legal analysis. The mandatory nature of the 180-day deadline means that the substantive adequacy of the affidavits is irrelevant once the jurisdictional time limit has expired. *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d at 6.

### B. Procedural Compliance Is a Jurisdictional Prerequisite

The plaintiff argues that the affidavits were served within a reasonable time after Dr. Whiting was named and that the defendant suffered no prejudice. However, the statute does not provide a reasonableness exception to the 180-day deadline. The legislature enacted a mandatory requirement with a specific penalty—dismissal with prejudice—for noncompliance. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008). Courts may not judicially rewrite the statute to accommodate equitable considerations that the legislature chose not to include.

In *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d at 6, this Court emphasized that procedural defaults must be evaluated in light of whether the statutory purpose was served. But here, the statutory purpose—timely disclosure of expert testimony—was not served. Juetten did not serve the affidavits within the statutory period; she served them only after her claim against LCA-Vision had been dismissed. The plaintiff cannot bootstrap her way into compliance by pointing to the substantive adequacy of affidavits that were served too late.

---

## V. THE PLAINTIFF'S REMEDY LIES WITH THE LEGISLATURE, NOT THE COURTS

The plaintiff argues that upholding dismissal would "reward form over substance" and "destroy claims based on technicalities." The defense submits that this argument mischaracterizes the nature of the procedural requirement.

Minn. Stat. § 145.682 is not a "technicality"—it is a mandatory statutory prerequisite to maintaining a medical malpractice action. The legislature determined that timely disclosure of expert testimony is essential to the fair and efficient administration of justice in medical malpractice cases. Courts are bound to enforce the statute as written, not as the plaintiff would prefer it to be.

If the plaintiff believes that the 180-day deadline is unduly harsh, her remedy lies with the legislature, not with this Court. The defense cannot be held liable for the plaintiff's failure to comply with a statutory requirement that has been in place for decades and has been consistently enforced by Minnesota courts.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the district court. The plaintiff failed to comply with the mandatory expert affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 within the statutory 180-day deadline. Her attempt to cure this procedural default by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant was unavailing. The law of the case doctrine properly bars relitigation of the procedural issue, and the substantive adequacy of the affidavits does not cure the procedural default. The district court's grant of summary judgment was proper, and the judgment should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the appeal of Stacy Juetten (Appellant) from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting (Respondent). Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court must determine whether the district court erred in dismissing Juetten’s medical malpractice claim based on the mandatory expert affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 and the law of the case doctrine. For the reasons stated herein, the Court affirms the judgment of the district court.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Deadline for Expert Affidavits Is Not Reset by the Addition of a New Defendant**

The prosecution argues that the 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits began anew when Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant, asserting that "commencement of the suit" for a new defendant occurs at the time of their joinder. This argument is inconsistent with the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent.

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2(2) (2008), mandates that a plaintiff must serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses within 180 days "after commencement of the suit." The term "commencement" refers to the filing of the original complaint, a singular event that establishes the timeline for the litigation. The prosecution’s attempt to distinguish *Schmitz v. Allina Health System*, 802 N.W.2d 79 (Minn. Ct. App. 2011), is unpersuasive. In *Schmitz*, the court explicitly held that "the expert affidavit requirement must be satisfied within the 180-day period from the date of the original complaint, regardless of when the new defendant is added." *Schmitz*, 802 N.W.2d at 84. The prosecution’s assertion that *Schmitz* is distinguishable because the defendant there was added before the deadline misses the point of the holding: the statutory clock is triggered by the original filing, not the identity of the defendants.

Furthermore, in *Bresnahan v. Rahal*, 641 N.W.2d 897 (Minn. Ct. App. 2002), the court established that this deadline is a jurisdictional prerequisite that cannot be satisfied after the deadline has expired, even by amending the complaint. The prosecution’s proposed interpretation—that a plaintiff can evade the deadline by simply adding a new defendant—would eviscerate the mandatory nature of the statute. Because Juetten failed to serve the required affidavits within 180 days of the commencement of the suit against LCA-Vision, she cannot cure this procedural default by subsequently adding Dr. Whiting.

**B. The Law of the Case Doctrine Bars Relitigation of the Procedural Default**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the law of the case doctrine. The prosecution contends this doctrine does not apply because Dr. Whiting was not a party to the earlier proceeding. The Court finds this argument without merit.

The law of the case doctrine is designed to maintain consistency and finality in judicial proceedings. As stated in *Northeast Bank v. Batsos*, 335 N.W.2d 234, 237 (Minn. 1983), the doctrine applies when the same issue is presented in subsequent proceedings involving the same or related parties. Here, the claim against LCA-Vision was based entirely on vicarious liability for Dr. Whiting’s conduct. The factual predicate for the dismissal—the failure to serve expert affidavits regarding the specific LASIK surgery and the alleged negligence—is identical.

The prosecution correctly cites *Fitz v. Servin*, 383 N.W.2d 1, 3 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988), for the proposition that the doctrine applies to issues "actually litigated and decided." However, the prosecution ignores the concept of privity. Dr. Whiting stands in privity with LCA-Vision regarding the defense of the alleged malpractice; the claim against the corporation was wholly derivative of the actions of the doctor. The district court’s determination that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day affidavit-service requirement was a final adjudication on the merits of that procedural issue. To allow Juetten to relitigate this issue by adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant would permit plaintiffs to evade mandatory statutory requirements simply by reframing claims against new parties who were integral to the original dispute. Therefore, the district court properly applied the law of the case doctrine.

**C. The Substantive Sufficiency of the Affidavits Does Not Cure the Procedural Default**

The prosecution relies on the district court’s statement that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. However, as noted by the defense, this observation was dicta and does not alter the legal outcome.

Compliance with the procedural requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 is a jurisdictional prerequisite to maintaining a medical malpractice action. *Wilder v. McDonald*, 245 N.W.2d 1, 6 (Minn. 1976), establishes that procedural compliance is essential and cannot be waived. The mandatory nature of the 180-day deadline means that the substantive adequacy of the affidavits is irrelevant once the jurisdictional time limit has expired. The prosecution’s argument that the affidavits were served within a reasonable time after Dr. Whiting was named cannot override the clear statutory mandate. The legislature determined that timely disclosure is essential, and courts are not at liberty to rewrite the statute to accommodate equitable considerations where the text provides none.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution’s closing arguments fail to overcome the mandatory statutory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 or the application of the law of the case doctrine. The 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits runs from the commencement of the suit and is not reset by the addition of new defendants. Juetten’s failure to comply with this deadline bars her claim against Dr. Whiting just as it barred her claim against LCA-Vision. The district court’s grant of summary judgment was proper.

**IT IS ORDERED:** That the judgment of the district court is affirmed.
