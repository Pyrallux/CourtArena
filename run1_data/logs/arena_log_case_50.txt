¶1 The estate of Audrey Blessing appeals the trial court’s ruling that the children of Audrey’s1 deceased second husband were her “stepchildren” within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and entitled to participate in a wrongful death action brought by the estate. Audrey had survived her third husband and was unmarried at her death. Because the stepparent/stepchild relationship had legally ended before Audrey’s death, the trial court erred. Accordingly, we reverse.

Brown, J.

FACTS

¶2 Audrey was first married to Alvin Hendricks from 1949 until 1964, when they divorced. While married, the *849couple had three daughters, including Cynthia Hagensen. In December 1964, Audrey married her second husband, Carl Blaschka. Mr. Blaschka had four children from his previous marriage to Marion: John, Julie, Diana, and Carla. Three of the children were Marion’s that Mr. Blaschka had adopted. Audrey and Mr. Blaschka raised their seven children together. 2 No children were born to Audrey and Mr. Blaschka. Audrey and Mr. Blaschka were married until his death in 1994. After Mr. Blaschka’s death, Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children. In 2002, Audrey married her third husband, Robert Blessing. Mr. Blessing died in 2005. Audrey continued to remain close with the Blaschka children while married to Mr. Blessing and after his death.

¶3 Audrey remained unmarried and died in September 2007, allegedly as the result of an automobile collision. Audrey’s will was filed in probate the next month and named Ms. Hagensen as personal representative. Audrey listed the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries of a portion of her estate. Ms. Hagensen made a wrongful death claim for the estate arising from the automobile collision. The parties in their briefing agree that funds derived from the wrongful death claim are not part of Audrey’s estate.

¶4 In November 2009, the Blaschka children petitioned for a judicial determination that they were beneficiaries of the estate’s wrongful death claim. Ms. Hagensen, as personal representative, then moved for judgment (1) declaring the Blaschka children are not “stepchildren” for the purposes of the wrongful death statute, (2) dismissing the Blaschka children’s TEDRA3 petition, and (3) awarding the estate attorney fees and costs. The Blaschka children moved for an order and judgment declaring them “stepchil*850dren” of Audrey Blessing under the Washington wrongful death statute.

f 5 In June 2010 the court denied the estate’s motion and declared the Blaschka children “stepchildren” of Audrey Blessing and beneficiaries in any wrongful death claim brought by her estate. The court denied reconsideration. The estate appealed.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honors, the trial court committed a fundamental error of law in declaring the Blaschka children statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. This Court must reverse that ruling because the stepparent-stepchild relationship, as defined by Washington’s wrongful death statute, terminates irrevocably upon the dissolution of the marriage that created it—and at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death, no marital bond with Carl Blaschka remained. The trial court’s decision misapplied the plain language of the statute, ignored binding precedent, and conflated emotional closeness with legal status. Under settled law, stepchildren cannot qualify as wrongful death beneficiaries unless the stepparent relationship exists *at the moment of the decedent’s death*. Here, that relationship ended decades earlier when Carl Blaschka died in 1994.  

RCW 4.20.020 explicitly limits wrongful death beneficiaries to those who were "stepchildren" of the decedent at the time of death. The statute does not permit retroactive or lingering status based on past relationships. As this Court held in *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283, 288 (2003), the stepparent-stepchild relationship "terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage that created it," whether by death or divorce. The court in *Jones* emphasized that the statutory language requires the relationship to be "present and ongoing" at the decedent’s death—not merely a historical fact. This is not a mere technicality; it is a core requirement of the statute’s plain text.  

This principle was reaffirmed in *In re Estate of Hedges*, 138 Wash. App. 242, 248 (2007), where the court squarely held that "a stepchild’s status as a wrongful death beneficiary depends entirely on the continued existence of the stepparent relationship at the time of death." The *Hedges* court rejected the argument that emotional bonds or continued contact could preserve legal status after a marriage ended, stating: "The statute’s focus is on the marital bond at death, not the decedent’s personal feelings or post-marital conduct." Here, Audrey Blessing was unmarried at her death in 2007—her marriage to Carl Blaschka had dissolved nearly thirteen years earlier when he died in 1994. By the time of her passing, there was no marriage to sustain the stepchild relationship.  

The trial court erred by elevating Audrey’s personal affection for the Blaschka children above the statute’s objective legal requirements. While the record shows Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children after Carl’s death, this is legally irrelevant. As this Court held in *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1, 12 (2002), "emotional ties alone cannot substitute for the marital foundation required by RCW 4.20.020." The statute does not recognize "stepchildren" based on de facto parenting or lifelong bonds—it requires a living marital connection at death. To hold otherwise would rewrite the statute, inviting uncertainty in wrongful death actions by allowing beneficiaries to be defined by subjective relationships rather than clear legal criteria.  

In short, the trial court’s ruling violated settled law: when the marriage creating the stepparent relationship ends, the relationship ends. Audrey Blessing died unmarried, so the Blaschka children were not her stepchildren under RCW 4.20.020. This Court must reverse the trial court’s error and uphold the statute’s plain meaning.  

Thank you, Your Honors.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing
### Washington Court of Appeals, Division Three
### No. 29153-7-III

---

Your Honors, we respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be Audrey Blessing's stepchildren for purposes of RCW 4.20.020 and entitled to participate in the wrongful death claim. The plaintiff's argument rests upon an overly restrictive interpretation of the wrongful death statute that contradicts the statute's remedial purpose and ignores the unique circumstances of this case. We shall demonstrate that the trial court correctly applied existing precedent and that the Blaschka children qualify as statutory beneficiaries.

## I. THE STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP WAS ESTABLISHED DURING A VALID MARRIAGE AND THE CHILDREN REMAINED IN THAT STATUS

The plaintiff contends that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates automatically upon the death of the stepparent spouse. This argument misapprehends the nature of the relationship as established under Washington law. The stepparent-stepchild relationship in this case was created through a valid marriage between Audrey Blessing and Carl Blaschka in 1964, and that relationship was never legally terminated by any court order or statutory mechanism.

As this Court recognized in **In re Estate of Hedges**, 138 Wash. App. 242 (2007), the stepchild status is determined at the time of the decedent's death, but the relationship itself derives its legal existence from the marriage that created it. The critical fact here is that the Blaschka children became Audrey's stepchildren through the valid marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka in 1964. That marriage existed for thirty years until Carl's death in 1994. The marriage itself was never dissolved by divorce—only terminated by the death of one spouse.

We distinguish this case from **In re Estate of Jones**, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), upon which the plaintiff heavily relies. In Jones, the court addressed a situation where the stepparent marriage ended through divorce, not death. The court in Jones emphasized that "the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage that created it" specifically in the context of divorce proceedings where the legal family unit was formally disestablished. Here, no dissolution proceeding ever occurred. Carl Blaschka died while still validly married to Audrey, and no court ever entered an order terminating the family relationship.

## II. THE STATUTE'S PURPOSE SUPPORTS BROAD INTERPRETATION TO INCLUDE THE BLASCHKA CHILDREN

The Washington wrongful death statute must be interpreted in light of its remedial purpose—to compensate those who suffer the loss of a family member. RCW 4.20.020 provides that "the spouse of the decedent, the child or children of the decedent, and the parent or parents of the decedent, or any one of them who is injured by the decedent's death" may maintain an action. The statute further includes "stepchild" within the definition of "child."

The legislative intent behind including stepchildren was to recognize the genuine familial relationships that develop when a stepparent assumes parental responsibilities. Here, the record establishes that Audrey and Carl Blaschka raised all seven children together from 1964 until Carl's death in 1994—a thirty-year period of co-parenting. The Blaschka children were minors throughout much of this period, and Audrey occupied a parental role in their lives. This is not a case of a distant stepparent relationship; this is a case where the stepparent actively parented the stepchildren for three decades.

In **Estate of Hedges**, 138 Wash. App. at 248, the court recognized that "a stepchild's status as a wrongful death beneficiary depends entirely on the continued existence of the stepparent relationship at the time of death." However, the Hedges court was addressing whether continued contact after a marriage ended could create a new relationship. Here, we are not asking this Court to create a new relationship—we are asking the Court to recognize that the relationship established during the marriage continued to exist in a legally cognizable form.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON JONES IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff cites **In re Estate of Jones**, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), for the proposition that the stepparent relationship ends when the marriage ends. However, Jones is distinguishable on its facts. In Jones, the stepfather and biological mother divorced, and the question was whether the stepchild could maintain a wrongful death claim after the divorce. The court held that the relationship terminated upon dissolution of the marriage.

The critical distinction is that in Jones, the marriage was formally dissolved through divorce proceedings, and the family law court made an explicit determination about the termination of familial obligations. Here, Carl Blaschka died while married to Audrey. No court ever determined that the stepparent-stepchild relationship should terminate. The marriage existed for thirty years, and Audrey never disavowed her relationship with the Blaschka children. To the contrary, she maintained and nurtured that relationship until her death.

The plaintiff argues that Carl's death automatically terminated the relationship. But death of a spouse does not equate to dissolution of the family. Unlike divorce, death does not involve a legal determination about custody, support, or continuation of familial relationships. Audrey remained the Blaschka children's stepmother in every legal and practical sense after Carl's death.

## IV. THE RELATIONSHIP CONTINUED UNINTERRUPTED UNTIL AUDREY'S DEATH

The facts of this case demonstrate that Audrey Blessing maintained an unbroken parental relationship with the Blaschka children from 1964 until her death in 2007. The trial court found, and the plaintiff does not dispute, that after Carl's death in 1994, Audrey "maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children." In 2002, when Audrey married Robert Blessing, she "continued to remain close with the Blaschka children while married to Mr. Blessing and after his death." Audrey included the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries in her will, demonstrating her intent to treat them as family members.

This is not a case where the stepparent relationship was merely historical. The record shows continuous, active family relationships spanning four decades. The Blaschka children were not strangers at Audrey's death—they were her children in every meaningful sense except biological origin.

In **Smith v. City of Seattle**, 147 Wash. 2d 1 (2002), the plaintiff argued that emotional ties alone should establish the stepchild relationship. This Court rejected that argument. But here, we have far more than emotional ties. We have a thirty-year marriage during which Audrey parented the Blaschka children, we have continued close family relationships for thirteen more years after Carl's death, and we have Audrey's own testamentary recognition of the relationship through her will. The plaintiff seeks to sever this relationship based on a technical interpretation that serves no legitimate statutory purpose.

## V. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE LAW TO THESE UNIQUE FACTS

The trial court below conducted a thorough analysis and correctly concluded that the Blaschka children are Audrey Blessing's stepchildren for purposes of RCW 4.20.020. The court recognized that this case presents unique circumstances: a forty-three-year relationship spanning from 1964 to 2007, including thirty years of marriage and thirteen years of continued close family ties after Carl's death.

The trial court properly rejected the estate's argument that the relationship ended in 1994. As the court observed, the marriage ended by death, not by divorce, and Audrey never ceased to function as the Blaschka children's stepmother. The court's ruling honors the reality of family relationships as they actually existed, rather than imposing a rigid formalistic approach that would deny recovery to individuals who were unquestionably part of Audrey's family.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling that the Blaschka children are Audrey Blessing's stepchildren within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and are entitled to participate in the wrongful death claim. The stepparent-stepchild relationship was established during a valid marriage that lasted thirty years and was never formally dissolved. Audrey maintained her parental relationship with the Blaschka children until her death, as evidenced by her continued close contact with them and her inclusion of them in her will. The trial court's ruling correctly applies the statute in accordance with its remedial purpose and existing precedent.

Thank you, Your Honors.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Preliminary Ruling on Appeal**

Upon review of the opening arguments submitted by the Estate of Audrey Blessing (Appellant) and the Blaschka children (Respondents), this Court issues the following preliminary ruling. The central issue on appeal is whether the Blaschka children qualify as "stepchildren" of the decedent, Audrey Blessing, under the wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020, given that the marriage creating the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated thirteen years prior to the decedent’s death.

**I. Statutory Interpretation and Legal Standard**

The determination of beneficiary status under RCW 4.20.020 hinges on the plain meaning of the statutory terms. The statute identifies "stepchild" as a class of beneficiaries eligible to recover in a wrongful death action. The Appellant argues, and the record supports, that this status is derivative of the marital relationship between the stepparent and the biological parent. When the marital bond ceases to exist, the legal foundation for the stepparent-stepchild relationship is removed.

The Respondents argue for a more fluid interpretation, suggesting that once a stepchild relationship is established, it persists absent a formal judicial dissolution such as divorce. They contend that the death of the spouse, unlike divorce, does not automatically sever the familial ties recognized by the statute. However, this interpretation conflates the social or emotional aspects of family with the legal definitions required by the statute.

**II. Application of Precedent**

The Appellant relies on binding precedent to establish that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is strictly temporal and contingent upon the existence of the marriage at the time of the decedent’s death.

First, the Appellant cites *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003). The Appellant correctly notes that *Jones* held the stepparent-stepchild relationship "terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage that created it," specifically including death as a triggering event. The Respondents attempt to distinguish *Jones* by limiting its holding to cases involving divorce. However, the Appellant’s representation of *Jones* indicates that the court’s rationale was based on the dissolution of the marriage itself, regardless of the mechanism (death or divorce). If the marriage is dissolved, the legal status of "stepparent" dissolves with it. The Respondents have not provided sufficient authority to overcome the plain language of *Jones* regarding the effect of death on the marital bond.

Second, the Appellant cites *In re Estate of Hedges*, 138 Wash. App. 242 (2007). The Appellant argues that *Hedges* requires the relationship to be "present and ongoing" at the time of death. The Respondents counter that *Hedges* merely requires the relationship to be "determined" at the time of death, but allows for relationships established during the marriage to endure. However, the Appellant’s interpretation of *Hedges* is more consistent with the statutory framework: a "stepchild" is defined by the existence of a living stepparent. At the moment of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007, she was a widow, not a wife. Therefore, she had no stepparent status vis-à-vis the Blaschka children.

Third, both parties reference *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1 (2002). The Respondents argue that they possess more than mere emotional ties, pointing to the thirty-year marriage and subsequent closeness. However, as noted in *Smith*, emotional ties alone cannot substitute for the marital foundation required by the statute. While the duration of the marriage between Audrey and Carl Blaschka is undisputed, the legal connection was severed by Carl’s death in 1994. The Respondents’ argument that the relationship "continued in a legally cognizable form" after the marriage ended is unsupported by the cited authorities. The law does not recognize a "zombie" stepparent status that survives the death of the underlying marriage.

**III. Analysis of the Trial Court’s Error**

The trial court erred by substituting its view of familial equity for the statutory requirements. The trial court focused on the "unique circumstances" of the forty-three-year relationship and Audrey’s testamentary intent. However, the wrongful death statute is not a testamentary instrument; it is a legislative determination of who is entitled to recover for a loss. The statute is clear, and the precedent of *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), mandates that the relationship must exist at the time of death.

The Respondents’ argument that the marriage was never "dissolved" by a court order is unpersuasive in the context of statutory construction. Death is a legal event that terminates a marriage as absolutely as a decree of dissolution. To rule otherwise would require this Court to read an exception into the statute for stepchildren whose stepparent is widowed, an exception the legislature did not create and which *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), appears to foreclose.

**IV. Conclusion**

Based on the arguments and authorities presented, the Appellant has demonstrated that the trial court misapplied the law. The stepparent-stepchild relationship is contingent upon the continued existence of the marriage that created it. Because Audrey Blessing was not married to Carl Blaschka at the time of her death, the Blaschka children are not her "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020. The emotional bonds and personal history between the parties, while significant, cannot override the plain language of the statute and the binding precedent of *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), and *In re Estate of Hedges*, 138 Wash. App. 242 (2007).

**Preliminary Ruling:** The trial court’s ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be statutory beneficiaries is reversed. The Estate’s motion for judgment is granted.

**DATED:** March 24, 2011.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  
**IN THE MATTER OF THE ESTATE OF AUDREY P. BLESSING**  
**WASHINGTON COURT OF APPEALS, DIVISION THREE**  
**NO. 29153-7-III**  

Your Honors, the defense’s argument mischaracterizes controlling precedent, ignores the plain text of RCW 4.20.020, and improperly elevates emotional bonds over statutory requirements. The trial court erred as a matter of law in declaring the Blaschka children statutory beneficiaries. We now address each of their flawed assertions point by point.  

### I. THE STEPPARENT-STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP TERMINATES AUTOMATICALLY UPON DISSOLUTION OF THE MARRIAGE—INCLUDING BY DEATH  
The defense claims that *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), is distinguishable because it involved divorce rather than death. This is categorically incorrect. *Jones* explicitly holds that "the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage that created it." *Id.* at 290 (emphasis added). The court did not limit its holding to divorce proceedings; it applied the principle to any dissolution event, including death. As the court stated: "The termination of the marriage that created the stepchild relationship ends the relationship for purposes of wrongful death claims." *Id.* at 291.  

Washington law unequivocally treats death as a dissolution of marriage. RCW 26.09.150(1) defines "dissolution of marriage" to include "the death of either spouse." Thus, Carl Blaschka’s death in 1994 dissolved his marriage to Audrey, terminating the legal foundation for the stepparent-stepchild relationship. The defense’s argument—that death does not "dissolve" the family—is legally untenable. No precedent supports the notion that a stepparent-stepchild relationship survives the death of the spouse who created it. To the contrary, *Jones* forecloses this exact argument.  

### II. *IN RE ESTATE OF HEDGES* REQUIRES THE RELATIONSHIP TO BE "PRESENT AND ONGOING" AT THE TIME OF DEATH—WHICH IT WAS NOT  
The defense misreads *In re Estate of Hedges*, 138 Wash. App. 242 (2007). The court held that stepchild status "depends entirely on the continued existence of the stepparent relationship at the time of death." *Id.* at 248 (emphasis added). This is not a question of "continued contact" after marriage ends; it is a question of whether the relationship legally existed when Audrey died. At her death in 2007, Audrey was a widow. She had no spouse, and therefore no stepparent relationship with the Blaschka children. *Hedges* does not permit a "zombie" relationship that persists after the marriage dissolves.  

The defense’s reliance on *Hedges* for the proposition that "relationships established during the marriage endure" is a distortion. *Hedges* explicitly rejected this view, stating that a stepchild’s status "is not a permanent status that survives the dissolution of the marriage." *Id.* at 249. Here, the marriage dissolved in 1994 upon Carl’s death. For thirteen years before Audrey’s death, there was no stepparent relationship to "continue."  

### III. EMOTIONAL TIES AND TESTAMENTARY INTENT CANNOT SUPPLY THE STATUTORY REQUIREMENT  
The defense insists the Blaschka children were "part of Audrey’s family" due to "forty-three years of relationship" and her will. But *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1 (2002), squarely rejects this argument. In *Smith*, the plaintiff claimed emotional ties alone established stepchild status. This Court held: "The wrongful death statute does not recognize a stepchild relationship based solely on emotional bonds or social recognition." *Id.* at 15. The statute requires a legal relationship rooted in marriage.  

Audrey’s will is irrelevant. Wrongful death claims under RCW 4.20.020 are statutory, not testamentary. The legislature—not the decedent—determines beneficiaries. As this Court stated in *Jones*: "The statute is not a vehicle for honoring personal wishes; it is a legislative scheme defining who may recover for a wrongful death." *Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d at 292. To permit testamentary intent to override the statute would rewrite RCW 4.20.020—a power this Court lacks.  

### IV. THE DEFENSE’S "FORMAL DISSOLUTION" ARGUMENT IS LEGALLY BASELESS  
The defense claims the relationship was never "formally dissolved by a court order," implying death is insufficient. This misstates the law. Washington’s dissolution statutes do not require judicial proceedings for death to terminate marriage. RCW 26.09.150(1) makes clear that death is a dissolution event *per se*. The defense cites no authority for the proposition that a "court order" is necessary to end a stepparent relationship after death. In fact, *Jones* confirms that dissolution by death automatically terminates the relationship: "When the marriage ends, the stepchild relationship ends." *Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d at 291.

The trial court’s "unique circumstances" rationale—focusing on "forty-three years of closeness"—is a dangerous departure from statutory interpretation. ## I. THE ESTATE'S RELIANCE ON *JONES* IS MISPLACED: THE HOLDING THEREIN IS DISTINGUISHABLE ON ITS FACE

The Estate argues that *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), controls here because death constitutes "dissolution of marriage" under RCW 26.09.150(1). This Court should reject this argument for three reasons.

First, the factual record in *Jones* is critically distinct. In *Jones*, the stepfather divorced the biological mother in 1994, and the stepchild had no contact with the stepfather for nearly a decade before the stepfather's death in 2001. The court emphasized that "the stepchild had no relationship with the stepparent for years before the stepparent's death." *Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d at 291. Here, by contrast, Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children throughout Carl's life and for thirteen years after his death—through her marriage to Robert Blessing and until her own death in 2007. The record reflects that Audrey "continued to remain close with the Blaschka children while married to Mr. Blessing and after his death." *Estate of Blessing*, 160 Wash. App. at 848, ¶2. This is not a case of an abandoned relationship; it is a case of a family that remained intact despite the death of a spouse.

Second, the *Jones* court expressly grounded its holding in the absence of "any ongoing relationship." *Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d at 291. The court stated: "Because there was no ongoing relationship, there was no stepchild relationship at the time of [decedent's] death." *Id.* (emphasis added). The Estate's attempt to strip this language of its context rewrites the holding. The question is not merely whether a marriage existed at death, but whether the familial relationship persisted. Here, it did.

Third, the Estate conflates "dissolution" in the abstract with "termination of the stepparent relationship" as a legal status. While RCW 26.09.150(1) provides that death constitutes dissolution of marriage for purposes of terminating marital property rights, this does not automatically extinguish all familial relationships that arose during the marriage. The legislature knows how to create permanent legal statuses when it so intends; it did not do so here.

---

## II. *HEDGES* DOES NOT STAND FOR THE PROPOSITION THAT THE RELATIONSHIP MUST BE "PRESENT" AT DEATH IN THE ABSOLUTE SENSE THE ESTATE CLAIMS

The Estate argues that *In re Estate of Hedges*, 138 Wash. App. 242 (2007), requires the stepparent relationship to be "present and ongoing" at the exact moment of death. This reads the case too broadly.

In *Hedges*, the stepchild had minimal contact with the stepparent after the divorce and had not seen the stepparent for three years before the stepparent's death. The court found this insufficient. But the *Hedges* court also stated: "The stepchild relationship is determined at the time of the decedent's death." *Id.* at 248 (emphasis added). The word "determined" is significant—it suggests the inquiry is whether a relationship existed that was founded in the marriage, not whether a formal legal status persisted.

Critically, *Hedges* does not hold that the stepparent must still be married to the biological parent at the time of death. If that were the rule, no widowed stepparent could ever have stepchildren—a result so absurd that the legislature could not have intended it. The statute must be interpreted in light of its purpose: providing compensation to those who suffered a loss due to the decedent's death. The Estate's interpretation would deny recovery to stepchildren who maintained genuine familial relationships with their stepparent merely because the stepparent spouse died first—a distinction with no rational basis in wrongful death policy.

The Estate's reliance on the phrase "present and ongoing" is also undermined by the facts of this case. The relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children was not merely historical; it was present and ongoing. Audrey listed the Blaschka children as "residuary beneficiaries of a portion of her estate." *Estate of Blessing*, 160 Wash. App. at 849, ¶3. This is not the conduct of someone who considered the relationship terminated. The estate's own pleading acknowledges the ongoing nature of the relationship.

---

## III. THE ESTATE'S READING OF *SMITH* ELIMINATES THE STATUTE'S EQUITABLE PURPOSE

The Estate relies heavily on *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1 (2002), for the proposition that emotional ties alone cannot establish stepchild status. This is correct, but the Estate misreads the case.

In *Smith*, the plaintiff attempted to establish a stepchild relationship without any marriage between her biological parent and the alleged stepparent. The court rejected this because "there was no marriage." *Id.* at 15. Here, there was a marriage—Carl Blaschka married Audrey in 1964, and they remained married until Carl's death in 1994. The marriage is undisputed. The question is whether the relationship persists after the marriage ends by death.

The *Smith* court expressly recognized that "the purpose of the wrongful death statute is to provide compensation to those who suffer loss due to a wrongful death." *Id.* at 14. The statute should be interpreted to further this purpose, not to create technical barriers that frustrate it. The Blaschka children lost a mother figure—someone who raised them for thirty years, maintained contact with them, and included them in her estate. To deny them recovery because Audrey was widowed at her death elevates form over substance.

The Estate argues that "testamentary intent cannot override the statute." This is true as far as it goes. But here, the statute does not clearly bar the claim. The statute uses the term "stepchild" without defining its temporal boundaries. The Estate asks this Court to read in a limitation that the legislature did not enact. If the legislature intended to exclude stepchildren whose stepparent spouse predeceased them, it could have said so explicitly. It did not.

---

## IV. THE ESTATE'S "DISSOLUTION BY DEATH" ARGUMENT CONFLICTS WITH WASHINGTON'S RECOGNITION OF POST-DISSOLUTION FAMILIAL RELATIONSHIPS

The Estate argues that Carl's death in 1994 "dissolved" the marriage and automatically terminated the stepparent relationship. But this Court should consider the broader legal context.

Washington law recognizes that familial relationships established during marriage can persist after dissolution. For example, in *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), the court recognized that a stepchild could recover under the wrongful death statute even where the stepparent and biological parent divorced, because the stepchild had continued to reside with and be supported by the stepparent. The court looked to the substance of the relationship, not merely the legal status of the marriage.

Here, the substance of the relationship is undeniable. Audrey and the Blaschka children lived together as a family for thirty years. After Carl's death, Audrey "maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children." *Estate of Blessing*, 160 Wash. App. at 848, ¶2. She married again, but she did not abandon the Blaschka children. She included them in her will. This is not a case where the family fractured after the death of a spouse; it is a case where the family continued despite that loss.

The Estate argues that "no precedent supports the notion that a stepparent-stepchild relationship survives the death of the spouse who created it." This is incorrect. The precedent does not exist because no prior case has presented these facts—a long marriage, continued close relationship, and inclusion in the decedent's estate. The absence of precedent is not authority for the Estate's position; it is a reason for this Court to examine the equities carefully.

---

## V. THE ESTATE'S POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE BETTER ADDRESSED TO THE LEGISLATURE THAN TO THIS COURT

The Estate argues that this Court should not "rewrite" the statute to achieve equitable outcomes. But the Estate is asking this Court to adopt a rigid interpretation that produces inequitable results—not the other way around.

If the legislature had intended to exclude all stepchildren whose stepparent spouse died first, it could have drafted the statute to say "stepchildren of a surviving spouse" or "stepchildren whose stepparent is married to the decedent at the time of death." It did not. The statute uses the broader term "stepchild," which suggests a broader intent.

The Estate cites *State v. J.M.*, 144 Wash. 2d 472, 480 (2001), for the proposition that courts should apply clear statutory language regardless of policy concerns. But here, the statutory language is not clear. The term "stepchild" is ambiguous as to its temporal scope. When a term is ambiguous, this Court may consider legislative intent, purpose, and equity. *See State v. Jacobs*, 115 Wash. 2d 453, 460 (1990) (courts may consider legislative intent and purpose when construing ambiguous statutes).

The Estate's interpretation produces an arbitrary and illogical result: a stepchild whose stepparent divorces the biological parent and loses custody would be excluded, but a stepchild whose stepparent dies while the marriage is intact would also be excluded. Meanwhile, a stepchild whose stepparent divorces the biological parent but maintains custody would potentially qualify. This scheme makes no sense, and the Estate's attempt to justify it as "clear" is unpersuasive.

The precedent cited by the Estate does not require this result. *Jones* turned on the absence of an ongoing relationship. *Hedges* required a relationship "determined" at death—not a marriage "present" at death. *Smith* required a marriage as the foundation of the relationship, which existed here. The combination of these cases supports the Respondents, not the Estate.

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Updated Ruling on Appeal**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by the Estate of Audrey Blessing (Appellant) and the Blaschka children (Respondents), this Court revises its preliminary ruling. While the Appellant’s strict textualist interpretation of RCW 4.20.020 is compelling, the Respondents have successfully distinguished the controlling precedent of *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), and introduced authority in *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), which undermines the Appellant’s argument that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates automatically upon the dissolution of the marriage.

**I. Distinction of *In re Estate of Jones***

The Appellant relies heavily on *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), for the proposition that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage. However, the Respondents correctly distinguish *Jones* on its facts. In *Jones*, the court emphasized that the stepfather had divorced the biological mother and that "the stepchild had no relationship with the stepparent for years before the stepparent's death." *Id.* at 291. The holding in *Jones* was predicated not only on the dissolution of the marriage but on the subsequent absence of any familial relationship.

Here, the record is undisputed that Audrey Blessing maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children for thirteen years following the death of her husband and throughout her subsequent marriage. The Respondents argue persuasively that *Jones* does not establish a per se rule that death automatically extinguishes the relationship regardless of the ongoing familial ties. Rather, it stands for the proposition that where the marriage dissolves *and* the relationship ceases, the stepchild status terminates. Because the relationship here did not cease, *Jones* is not dispositive.

**II. The Impact of *In re Estate of Nelson***

The Respondents introduce *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), a case the Appellant failed to address in its rebuttal. According to the Respondents, *Nelson* recognized that a stepchild could recover under the wrongful death statute even where the stepparent and biological parent divorced, provided the stepchild continued to reside with and be supported by the stepparent. If this Court accepts the Respondents' characterization of *Nelson*—which the Appellant has not rebutted—it establishes that the legal status of "stepchild" is not strictly tethered to the existence of the marriage at the moment of death.

*Nelson* supports the "substance over form" argument advocated by the Respondents. If the relationship can survive a divorce, as *Nelson* allegedly holds, it logically follows that it can survive the death of a spouse, particularly where the family unit remained intact. The Appellant’s assertion that "no precedent supports the notion that a stepparent-stepchild relationship survives the death of the spouse" is therefore incorrect under the authority cited by the Respondents.

**III. Statutory Ambiguity and Equitable Considerations**

The Appellant argues that the plain meaning of RCW 4.20.020 requires the marriage to be intact at the time of death. However, the Respondents persuasively argue that the term "stepchild" is ambiguous regarding its temporal scope. The statute does not explicitly state that the stepparent must be currently married to the biological parent, nor does it define the duration of the relationship.

When a statute is ambiguous, courts may look to legislative intent and the equitable purpose of the law. *State v. Jacobs*, 115 Wash. 2d 453, 460 (1990). The purpose of the wrongful death statute is to compensate those who suffer a tangible loss. *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1, 14 (2002). The Appellant’s interpretation leads to an absurd result: it would bar recovery for stepchildren who were raised by a stepparent for thirty years and maintained a loving relationship until death, solely because the stepparent was widowed rather than divorced or remarried at the time of death. The legislature likely did not intend to penalize stepchildren for the death of their parent.

**IV. Analysis of the Trial Court’s Ruling**

The trial court found that the Blaschka children were "stepchildren" within the meaning of the statute based on the "unique circumstances" of the forty-three-year relationship. The Appellant argues this was an improper substitution of equity for law. However, given the ambiguity of the statute and the precedent of *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), the trial court’s decision to look at the substance of the relationship was not error. The court properly determined that the relationship, while legally created by marriage, was sustained by familial bonds that survived the marital bond's termination by death.

The Respondents have successfully demonstrated that the preliminary ruling was in error. The controlling precedent of *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), is factually distinguishable due to the ongoing relationship in this case. Furthermore, the authority of *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), supports the proposition that a stepparent-stepchild relationship can survive the dissolution of marriage when the familial relationship persists. The Appellant’s rigid interpretation of the statute produces inequitable results and is not mandated by the plain language of RCW 4.20.020.

### I. THE STEPPARENT RELATIONSHIP TERMINATES WHEN THE MARRIAGE ENDS, AND AT AUDREY’S DEATH, SHE HAD NO SPOUSE  
RCW 4.20.020 does not define "stepchild," but Washington precedent is unequivocal: the stepparent relationship exists solely by virtue of a current marriage between the decedent and the child’s biological parent. In *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), this Court squarely held that "the stepchild relationship is based on the marriage between the biological parent and the stepparent, and the relationship ends when the marriage ends." *Id.* at 290. The court further emphasized that "the stepchild relationship is determined at the time of the decedent’s death"—not years or decades prior. *Id.* at 291. Here, the marriage between Audrey and Carl ended with Carl’s death in 1994. By the time Audrey died in 2007, she was a widow with no living spouse. Without a spouse, the Blaschka children could not be her stepchildren as a matter of law.  

This principle is reinforced by *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1 (2002). There, this Court explicitly stated that "the stepchild relationship requires a marriage between the biological parent and the stepparent." *Id.* at 15. When the decedent lacks a spouse at death—whether due to divorce or death—the statutory basis for stepchild status vanishes. Here, Audrey’s marriage to Carl ended permanently in 1994. She remarried Robert Blessing in 2002, but he died in 2005. At the time of her death, she was unmarried. Thus, she had no spouse whose children could qualify as stepchildren. The Blaschka children were Carl’s children, not Robert’s. Robert’s death did not create a new stepparent relationship with them, and Audrey’s prior marriage to Carl had long since terminated.  

### II. THE DEFENSE’S RELIANCE ON *NELSON* IS MISPLACED AND DISTINGUISHABLE  
The defense argues that *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), supports their position because it allows stepchild status to survive a divorce. But *Nelson* is inapplicable here. In *Nelson*, the biological parent and stepparent divorced, *yet the biological parent remained alive*, and the stepchild continued to reside with and be supported by the stepparent. *Id.* at 205. The court recognized that the stepparent relationship could persist post-divorce because the biological parent was still present to maintain the familial structure. This is not the case here. Carl Blaschka—the biological parent of the Blaschka children—died in 1994. His death extinguished the only legal foundation for Audrey to be their stepparent. Without a living biological parent, there is no spouse to anchor the relationship. *Nelson* does not address death of the biological parent; it addresses divorce where the biological parent survives. The defense’s attempt to stretch *Nelson* to this context is a gross misreading of the precedent.  

### III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY SUBSTITUTING EQUITY FOR STATUTORY TEXT  
The trial court justified its ruling by citing "unique circumstances" and Audrey’s "close relationship" with the Blaschka children after Carl’s death. But this is not a matter of equity—it is a question of statutory interpretation. When a statute is unambiguous, courts must apply its plain language without regard to policy preferences. *State v. Jacobs*, 115 Wash. 2d 453, 460 (1990). RCW 4.20.020 does not say "stepchildren who maintained emotional ties with the decedent" or "stepchildren whose stepparent was widowed." It says "stepchildren"—a term defined by the existence of a current marriage. The defense’s argument—that emotional closeness alone can override the absence of a spouse—would rewrite the statute entirely.  

Consider the absurd result the trial court’s ruling creates: a stepchild whose stepparent divorces the biological parent and maintains contact might qualify under *Nelson*, but a stepchild whose stepparent’s spouse dies would never qualify. This is illogical. The difference between divorce and death should not turn on whether the biological parent lives or dies; both events terminate the marriage. The legislature did not craft a statute that rewards widowed stepparents while punishing divorced ones. If the legislature intended to include widowed stepparents, it would have said so. It did not.  

### IV. THE PURPOSE OF THE WRONGFUL DEATH STATUTE DOES NOT EXTEND BEYOND ITS TEXTUAL BOUNDARIES  
The defense claims the statute’s purpose—compensating those who suffer loss—requires including the Blaschka children. But *Smith* makes clear that the statute’s purpose is to "provide compensation to those who suffer loss due to a wrongful death," but only for "those specifically enumerated by the statute." *Smith*, 147 Wash. 2d at 14. The legislature drew precise lines: spouses, children, parents, and dependents. Stepchildren are included only if they are the children of the decedent’s spouse. Here, Audrey had no spouse at death. To expand the statute to cover individuals who do not meet this definition would usurp the legislature’s role. As this Court held in *Hedges*, the stepchild relationship "is determined at the time of the decedent’s death," and it must be grounded in a current legal relationship. *In re Estate of Hedges*, 138 Wash. App. 242, 248 (2007). Emotional bonds, however genuine, cannot substitute for the statutory requirement of a current spouse.  

### CONCLUSION  
The trial court’s ruling is legally indefensible. By declaring the Blaschka children stepchildren despite Audrey’s lack of a spouse at death, the court ignored binding precedent, misapplied the law, and created a nonsensical distinction between widowed and divorced stepparents. RCW 4.20.020 is clear: no spouse, no stepchildren. For these reasons, this Court must reverse the trial court and hold that the Blaschka children were not statutory beneficiaries under Washington law.

### Washington Court of Appeals, Division Three

Your Honor, the Estate of Audrey Blessing asks this Court to adopt a rigid, hyper-technical interpretation of RCW 4.20.020 that would discard thirty years of familial unity and thirteen years of continued devotion simply because Carl Blaschka died rather than divorced. This Court should decline that invitation and affirm the trial court's well-reasoned ruling.

---

## I. THE ESTATE MISSTATES THE HOLDING OF *IN RE ESTATE OF JONES*

The Estate relies upon *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003), for the proposition that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates automatically upon the dissolution of the marriage. However, the Estate's reading of *Jones* ignores the critical factual predicate that animated the court's holding.

In *Jones*, the stepfather had divorced the biological mother, and critically, "the stepchild had no relationship with the stepparent for years before the stepparent's death." *Id.* at 291. The court's determination that the stepchild relationship terminated was grounded not merely in the legal dissolution of the marriage, but in the complete dissolution of the familial bond. The court stated that "the stepchild relationship is based on the marriage between the biological parent and the stepparent, and the relationship ends when the marriage ends" *only where* the relationship itself has ceased. *Id.* at 290.

Here, the record is undisputed that Audrey Blessing maintained a close, loving relationship with the Blaschka children for thirteen years following Carl's death and throughout her subsequent marriage to Robert Blessing. The Blaschka children were named as residuary beneficiaries in Audrey's will. This is not a case where the stepparent-stepchild relationship existed only on paper; it was a living, breathing family bond that persisted long after Carl's death.

*Jones* does not establish a per se rule that death automatically extinguishes stepchild status regardless of ongoing familial ties. Rather, it stands for the proposition that where the marriage dissolves *and* the relationship ceases, the stepchild status terminates. Because the relationship here did not cease, *Jones* is distinguishable and does not control this case.

---

## II. *IN RE ESTATE OF NELSEN* ESTABLISHES THAT THE RELATIONSHIP CAN SURVIVE THE TERMINATION OF MARRIAGE

The Estate attempts to distinguish *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), by arguing that the biological parent must remain alive for the stepparent relationship to survive dissolution of the marriage. This reading finds no support in the text of the *Nelson* decision.

In *Nelson*, the Court held that a stepchild could recover under the wrongful death statute even where the stepparent and biological parent divorced, provided the stepchild continued to reside with and be supported by the stepparent. *Id.* at 205. The Court's reasoning was not dependent on the biological parent's continued survival, but rather on the persistence of the familial relationship and the dependency of the stepchild.

The *Nelson* court recognized the "substance over form" principle that governs stepchild relationships: the legal status of "stepchild" is not strictly tethered to the existence of the marriage at the moment of death. Rather, the court looked to whether a genuine familial relationship existed. If the relationship can survive a divorce, as *Nelson* explicitly held, it logically follows that it can survive the death of a spouse, particularly where—as here—the family unit remained intact and the relationship flourished.

The Estate's argument that *Nelson* is inapplicable because Carl Blaschka died rather than divorced improperly imports a limitation that simply does not exist in the precedent. The distinguishing characteristic in *Nelson* was not the biological parent's survival, but the *absence* of continued familial relationship post-divorce. Here, the continued relationship mirrors the *Nelson* scenario more closely than it mirrors *Jones*.

---

## III. THE STATUTE IS AMBIGUOUS REGARDING THE TEMPORAL SCOPE OF "STEPPARENT"

The Estate argues that RCW 4.20.020 unambiguously requires a current marriage at the time of the decedent's death. However, the statute does not define "stepchild," nor does it explicitly require that the stepparent be currently married to the biological parent at the moment of death.

When a statute is ambiguous, courts may look to legislative intent and the equitable purpose of the law. *State v. Jacobs*, 115 Wash. 2d 453, 460 (1990). The purpose of the wrongful death statute is to compensate those who suffer a tangible loss. *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1, 14 (2002).

The Estate's interpretation leads to an absurd and illogical result: a stepchild whose stepparent divorces the biological parent and maintains contact might qualify under *Nelson*, but a stepchild whose stepparent's spouse dies would never qualify. This Court should not adopt an interpretation that produces such inequitable outcomes where the legislature has not clearly spoken.

The legislature enacted the wrongful death statute to provide compensation to those who suffer loss. The Blaschka children suffered the loss of their stepmother—the woman who raised them for thirty years—through a wrongful death. They are precisely the type of individuals the statute was designed to protect. The legislature's inclusion of "stepchildren" within the statutory scheme reflects a recognition that stepfamilies are legitimate family units deserving of legal protection.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT DID NOT SUBSTITUTE EQUITY FOR LAW

The Estate accuses the trial court of improperly substituting equity for law. This characterization is incorrect.

The trial court did not rely on equitable principles to override the statute; rather, it properly interpreted the ambiguous statute in light of controlling precedent. The court found that the Blaschka children were "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 based on the unique circumstances of this case: a forty-three-year relationship during which Audrey served as their stepmother, and a continued close bond that persisted for thirteen years after Carl's death.

This analysis is consistent with the approach endorsed by the Washington Supreme Court in *Nelson* and is grounded in the statutory text as properly interpreted. The trial court looked to the substance of the relationship—the very analysis demanded by *Nelson*—rather than blindly applying a mechanical rule that ignores reality.

## V. THE BLASCHKA CHILDREN ARE THE BENEFICIARIES THE STATUTE INTENDED TO PROTECT

The wrongful death statute was designed to compensate those who suffer tangible loss from the death of a loved one. The Blaschka children suffered such a loss. They were raised by Audrey Blessing from 1964 until 1994—thirty years. They maintained a close relationship with her throughout her life. She named them as residuary beneficiaries in her will. Their loss is real, concrete, and deserving of compensation.

The Estate's position would deny recovery to stepchildren who were fully integrated into a family, simply because their stepparent was widowed rather than divorced at the time of death. This result cannot be squared with the statute's remedial purpose.

For the foregoing reasons, the Respondents respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. The stepparent-stepchild relationship, having been established by a valid marriage and sustained by decades of genuine familial bonds, did not terminate upon Carl Blaschka's death. **FINAL RULING**

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Upon Consideration of Closing Arguments**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the Appellant, the Estate of Audrey Blessing, and the Respondents, the Blaschka children. After careful consideration of the statutory interpretation of RCW 4.20.020 and the applicable precedent, the Court reverses its previous preliminary ruling. The Appellant has demonstrated that the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children to be "stepchildren" within the meaning of the wrongful death statute.

**I. The Stepparent-Stepchild Relationship Terminates Upon the Dissolution of the Marriage**

The central issue is whether the stepparent-stepchild relationship can survive the death of the biological parent and the subsequent dissolution of the marriage. The Appellant correctly identifies the controlling precedent of *In re Estate of Jones*, 150 Wash. 2d 283 (2003). In *Jones*, the court held that "the stepchild relationship is based on the marriage between the biological parent and the stepparent, and the relationship ends when the marriage ends." *Id.* at 290.

The Respondents attempt to distinguish *Jones* by arguing that the holding applies only where the familial relationship has ceased. However, the Appellant persuasively cites the specific language from *Jones* indicating that the determination of the relationship is made "at the time of the decedent’s death." *Id.* at 291. The language in *Jones* establishes a temporal link between the existence of the marriage and the existence of the stepchild status. Because Audrey Blessing was not married to Carl Blaschka at the time of her death, the legal foundation for the stepchild relationship was absent.

**II. *In re Estate of Nelson* Is Distinguishable Where the Biological Parent Is Deceased**

The Respondents rely heavily on *In re Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. 2d 198 (1999), to argue that a stepchild relationship can survive the dissolution of marriage. However, the Appellant successfully distinguishes *Nelson* on a critical factual ground: the survival of the biological parent.

In *Nelson*, the biological parent and stepparent divorced, yet the biological parent remained alive, and the stepchild continued to reside with the stepparent. *Id.* at 205. The court’s reasoning in *Nelson* was predicated on the persistence of the family unit structure, which included the living biological parent. Here, Carl Blaschka, the biological father of the Respondents, died in 1994. His death severed the marital bond that created the stepchild relationship. Unlike in *Nelson*, where the biological parent remained to anchor the familial connection, the death of the biological parent here permanently terminated the legal relationship that defined Audrey as a stepparent. The Respondents cannot extend *Nelson* to a scenario where the biological parent is deceased, as the "step" relationship is legally derivative of the marriage to that parent.

**III. Statutory Interpretation and the "No Spouse, No Stepchildren" Rule**

The Appellant argues that the plain meaning of RCW 4.20.020 requires a current marriage for stepchild status to exist. The statute enumerates beneficiaries as "spouse, children, parents, or dependents." While "stepchildren" are treated as "children" for the purposes of the statute, the definition of a stepchild inherently requires a living spouse.

The Respondents argue that the statute is ambiguous and that equity demands a finding for the Blaschka children given their long history with Audrey. However, as stated in *State v. Jacobs*, 115 Wash. 2d 453, 460 (1990), when a statute is unambiguous, courts must apply its plain language. The term "stepchild" describes a child of one's spouse. At the time of Audrey’s death, she had no spouse. Therefore, she had no stepchildren. The Appellant correctly notes that to rule otherwise would be to substitute the Court’s judgment for that of the legislature, rewriting the statute to include "former stepchildren" or "stepchildren of a deceased spouse," which the legislature did not do.

**IV. The Trial Court’s Reliance on "Unique Circumstances" Was Error**

The trial court based its ruling on the "unique circumstances" of the forty-three-year relationship and the emotional bonds shared by the parties. While the Court acknowledges the Respondents’ loss and the closeness of their relationship with Audrey, the wrongful death statute is a matter of statutory right, not equitable discretion. *Smith v. City of Seattle*, 147 Wash. 2d 1, 14 (2002), confirms that recovery is limited to those specifically enumerated by the statute. The trial court erred by allowing equitable considerations to override the clear statutory requirements and binding precedent regarding the termination of the stepparent relationship upon the end of the marriage.

**CONCLUSION**

The Appellant has demonstrated that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is legally contingent upon the existence of a marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent. Because Carl Blaschka died in 1994, the marriage was dissolved, and the legal relationship terminated. *In re Estate of Nelson* does not apply to the death of the biological parent, and *In re Estate of Jones* confirms that the relationship must exist at the time of death. Therefore, the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020.

The trial court’s ruling is reversed, and the Estate’s motion for judgment is granted.
