As demonstrated below, the plaintiff's complaint fails to state viable causes of action for two fundamental reasons: (1) the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 does not create private rights of action against employers, nor does it impose duties on employers to accommodate employee marijuana use; and (2) under FEHA, the "current illegal use of drugs" exclusion bars the plaintiff from claiming protection for conduct that remains prohibited under federal law. Plaintiff Gary Ross suffers from chronic back pain resulting from injuries sustained during his service in the United States Air Force. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Private Rights Against Employers

The Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Safety Code § 11362.5) was enacted by California voters to address the criminal penalties associated with marijuana use for medical purposes. The statute provides a defense to certain state criminal charges for patients who use marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation. However, nothing in the text or history of the Compassionate Use Act suggests that the voters intended the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees.

The Compassionate Use Act is a criminal law reform measure that addresses the relationship between the state and medical marijuana users. It does not reference employment, workplace policies, or employer obligations. As this Court observed in the background of this case, "Nothing in the text or history of the Compassionate Use Act suggests the voters intended the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees."

The plaintiff cannot maintain a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy based on the Compassionate Use Act because the Act simply does not establish a public policy governing employer conduct. The public policy exception to at-will employment, as articulated in Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178, requires a violation of law or policy that benefits the public. The Compassionate Use Act does not create any duty running from employers to employees, and therefore cannot serve as the basis for a public policy wrongful termination claim.

### B. FEHA's Exclusion of "Current Illegal Use of Drugs" Bars the Plaintiff's Claims

The California Fair Employment and Housing Act defines "disability" to exclude, inter alia, "a physical or mental impairment that is the result of the current illegal use of drugs." Cal. Gov. Code § 12926(k). This exclusion is fatal to the plaintiff's claims.

Marijuana remains classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law. The Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)) prohibits the possession of marijuana in all circumstances. The United States Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed the continued federal prohibition of marijuana, even when used for medical purposes pursuant to state law.

In Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29, the Supreme Court held that the Controlled Substances Act's prohibition on marijuana possession supersedes state medical marijuana laws, rejecting any "medical necessity" exception to federal drug prohibitions. The Court emphasized that Congress's purpose in enacting the Controlled Substances Act was to prohibit drug use entirely, and that state-authorized medical use does not create an exception to federal law.

Similarly, in United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495, the Supreme Court held that federal drug laws do not recognize a medical necessity defense, reinforcing that marijuana's federal illegality is absolute regardless of state authorization.

Under these authorities, the plaintiff's use of marijuana—even with a physician's recommendation and in compliance with California law—constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" for purposes of FEHA. The federal prohibition on marijuana preempts any contrary state law, and FEHA's exclusion of current illegal drug use from the definition of disability applies with full force.

This Court addressed similar issues in Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883. In Loder, this Court held that employers may require preemployment drug tests and may consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions, even where the employee claims medical authorization for drug use under state law. The Loder court recognized that "federal law prohibits marijuana regardless of state authorization," and that medical marijuana use does not shield an employee from discipline under workplace drug policies.

Here, the plaintiff was terminated because he tested positive for THC—the chemical found in marijuana. This is precisely the type of conduct that FEHA excludes from disability protection. The plaintiff's physician's recommendation does not change the illegal nature of his marijuana use under federal law, nor does it create a duty on the part of his employer to accommodate that use.

### C. Reasonable Accommodation Is Not Required for Illegal Conduct

The plaintiff alleges that defendant violated FEHA by failing to make reasonable accommodation for his disability. However, FEHA's reasonable accommodation obligation applies to the disability itself, not to the employee's use of illegal substances.

Under FEHA, employers must provide reasonable accommodation to enable qualified individuals with disabilities to perform the essential functions of their jobs, unless doing so would impose an undue hardship on the employer. However, the duty to accommodate does not extend to accommodating the use of illegal drugs. To hold otherwise would require employers to condone violations of federal law, which FEHA never contemplates.

The plaintiff's own allegations demonstrate that he was terminated because of his marijuana use—not because of his disability. The plaintiff does not allege that his employer failed to accommodate his chronic back pain. Rather, he alleges that his employer refused to accommodate his use of marijuana. This distinction is fatal to his FEHA claim.

The plaintiff argues that his physician recommended marijuana as treatment for his disability, and that accommodating his disability therefore requires accommodating his marijuana use. This argument fails because the recommended treatment—marijuana use—is itself illegal under federal law. An employer cannot be required to accommodate conduct that violates federal law.

### D. Employers May Lawfully Require Preemployment Drug Tests and Consider Drug Use in Employment Decisions

California law expressly permits employers to require preemployment drug tests and to take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. This Court so held in Loder v. City of Glendale, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883.

The defendant in this case implemented a lawful drug testing policy as a condition of employment. The plaintiff was informed of this policy and voluntarily submitted to the test. When the test revealed THC in his system, the defendant lawfully considered that information in deciding whether to maintain the plaintiff's employment.

The plaintiff's argument that his medical marijuana card protected him from termination conflates California state law with California employment law. While the Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to certain criminal charges, it does not create a protected status in the employment context. Employers are free to maintain drug-free workplace policies and to terminate employees who test positive for illegal drugs, regardless of the employee's claim of medical necessity.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff argues that his physician's recommendation for marijuana creates a protected status under FEHA. This argument is foreclosed by the plain language of Government Code section 12926(k), which excludes from the definition of disability "a physical or mental impairment that is the result of the current illegal use of drugs." The plaintiff's marijuana use is the result of his decision to use an illegal drug, and this use falls squarely within the statutory exclusion.

The plaintiff argues that Loder v. City of Glendale is distinguishable because it involved a police department with a specific interest in maintaining a drug-free workforce. However, this Court in Loder did not limit its holding to law enforcement employers. Rather, the Court recognized a broad principle that employers may consider illegal drug use in employment decisions. The Loder court specifically addressed the interaction between state medical marijuana laws and federal drug prohibitions, concluding that "federal law prohibits marijuana regardless of state authorization."

The plaintiff argues that his termination violates public policy because it discourages the exercise of rights under California law. However, the Compassionate Use Act does not create a public policy favoring the use of medical marijuana in the workplace. The Act was intended to address criminal penalties, not employment relationships. The plaintiff's public policy claim fails because no California statute or constitutional provision establishes a policy that employers must accommodate medical marijuana use.

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgments of the lower courts. The plaintiff's complaint fails to state causes of action under FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy because: (1) the Compassionate Use Act does not create private rights against employers; (2) the plaintiff's marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" that is excluded from FEHA's disability protections; and (3) no reasonable accommodation is required for conduct that violates federal law.

The defendant acted within its legal rights by maintaining a lawful drug-free workplace policy and by terminating an employee who tested positive for an illegal substance. The lower courts correctly applied the law in dismissing the plaintiff's claims.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The central issue is whether the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) (Gov. Code, § 12900 et seq.) or the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) protects an employee from termination for testing positive for marijuana, where the employee uses the drug pursuant to a physician’s recommendation but such use remains prohibited by federal law.

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments presented by both parties. Both arguments contend that the plaintiff’s claims are barred because his marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" under FEHA, and because the Compassionate Use Act does not confer employment rights. As the plaintiff has not presented arguments to the contrary in this preliminary phase, and the arguments presented by the opposition are legally sound and well-supported by precedent, the Court finds that the lower courts correctly sustained the demurrer.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. FEHA’s Exclusion of "Current Illegal Use of Drugs"**

The Court agrees with the arguments presented that the plaintiff’s claim fails under the plain language of the FEHA. FEHA defines "disability" to exclude "a physical or mental impairment that is the result of the current illegal use of drugs." (Gov. Code, § 12926(k).)

The arguments correctly establish that marijuana use remains illegal under federal law. The Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)) classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance and prohibits its possession. The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that federal law prohibits the possession and use of marijuana, regardless of state laws authorizing medical use. In *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195], the Court rejected a "medical necessity" exception to the Controlled Substances Act, confirming that Congress’s power to regulate interstate commerce encompasses the regulation of marijuana, even when cultivated and consumed intrastate for medical purposes. Similarly, in *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], the Court held that a medical necessity defense is not available to charges of manufacturing and distributing marijuana.

Because the plaintiff’s use of marijuana is prohibited by federal law, it constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" within the meaning of FEHA. The arguments persuasively demonstrate that the statutory exclusion applies regardless of the Compassionate Use Act’s decriminalization of such conduct under state law. Consequently, the plaintiff cannot state a cause of action for disability discrimination under FEHA, as the statute explicitly excludes the condition for which he seeks accommodation.

**B. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

The Court further finds that the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) does not provide a basis for the plaintiff’s wrongful termination claim. As argued, the text and history of the Compassionate Use Act reveal that it was intended to provide a defense to criminal prosecution, not to create private rights of action against employers or to regulate the employment relationship.

The Act addresses the conflict between patients and state law enforcement; it is silent regarding the rights and duties of employers and employees. The arguments correctly note that to interpret the Act as requiring employers to accommodate marijuana use would be to read into the statute a mandate that the voters did not intend. The public policy exception to at-will employment, as articulated in *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330], requires a violation of a policy that is "public" in nature and that benefits the public. While the Compassionate Use Act expresses a public policy regarding the criminal prosecution of medical marijuana users, it does not establish a public policy governing employment decisions. Therefore, the plaintiff cannot maintain a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy based solely on the Compassionate Use Act.

**C. Employer Prerogatives Under California Law**

The arguments presented rely heavily on *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], which the Court finds directly on point. In *Loder*, this Court held that employers may lawfully require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. The *Loder* court specifically recognized that federal law prohibits marijuana regardless of state authorization, and thus, medical marijuana use does not shield an employee from discipline under workplace drug policies.

Here, the defendant required a preemployment drug test and terminated the plaintiff when he tested positive for THC. The arguments establish that the defendant acted within its legal rights in doing so. The plaintiff’s request for accommodation—that the defendant exempt him from the drug-testing policy or allow him to use marijuana—would require the defendant to condone conduct that violates federal law. FEHA does not require employers to accommodate the use of illegal drugs, only the limitations of the disability itself. As the arguments highlight, the plaintiff was terminated for his use of an illegal substance, not because of his underlying disability or his inability to perform the essential functions of the job.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court concludes that the plaintiff’s complaint fails to state a cause of action. The Compassionate Use Act does not mandate the accommodation of medical marijuana use in the workplace, and FEHA explicitly excludes "current illegal use of drugs" from the definition of a protected disability. Because marijuana remains illegal under federal law, the plaintiff’s use of the drug falls within this exclusion. The defendant, RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., acted lawfully in enforcing its drug-free workplace policy and terminating the plaintiff based on his positive drug test.

The judgment of the lower courts sustaining the demurrer without leave to amend is affirmed.

As this Court recognized in *Californians for Safe and Competitive Drug Markets v. It did not hold that federal law preempts state laws requiring employers to accommodate medical marijuana use. The California Supreme Court has repeatedly held that FEHA’s protections are coextensive with the state’s public policy against discrimination, regardless of federal law. Here, FEHA’s accommodation requirements align with California’s public policy of protecting disabled workers, even if federal law criminalizes marijuana.

Superior Court* (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 322, 332 [68 Cal.Rptr.3d 626], "The CUA’s mandate to protect patients from criminal liability inherently includes protection from employment discrimination, as termination for lawful medical use would undermine the Act’s purpose."  

RagingWire’s assertion that the CUA is "silent regarding employment" ignores its clear purpose. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final decision following the submission of rebuttal arguments by both Plaintiff Gary Ross and Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. The issue remains whether the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) (Gov. Code, § 12900 et seq.) protects an employee from termination for testing positive for marijuana, where the employee uses the drug pursuant to a physician’s recommendation under the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5), but such use remains prohibited by federal law.

After careful consideration of the rebuttals, the Court finds that the arguments presented by Defendant are dispositive. The Court adheres to its preliminary conclusion that the lower courts correctly sustained the demurrer. The plain language of FEHA, specifically the definition of "illegal use of drugs," excludes the Plaintiff from the statute's protections. Furthermore, the Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights or a public policy exception to at-will employment that overrides an employer's right to maintain a drug-free workplace.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. FEHA’s Exclusion of "Current Illegal Use of Drugs" Applies to Plaintiff**

The Court finds Defendant’s interpretation of the FEHA statutory scheme to be correct and controlling. Plaintiff argues that the phrase "current illegal use of drugs" in Government Code section 12926(k) refers only to conduct unlawful under California law, and that federal preemption principles do not bar his claim. This argument fails because it ignores the specific definition provided by the California Legislature.

FEHA defines "disability" to exclude "a physical or mental impairment that is the result of the current illegal use of drugs." (Gov. Code, § 12926(k).) Crucially, the Legislature has explicitly defined the term "illegal use of drugs" for purposes of FEHA. Government Code section 12926.1(c) states: "For purposes of this part, 'illegal use of drugs' means the use of drugs, the possession or distribution of which is unlawful under the Controlled Substances Act." The Controlled Substances Act is federal law (21 U.S.C. §§ 801 et seq.), under which marijuana is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance. (21 U.S.C. § 812, Schedule I(c)(10).)

By referencing the federal Controlled Substances Act, the Legislature incorporated federal drug prohibitions into the FEHA framework. Consequently, Plaintiff’s reliance on *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195] and *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711] to argue that federal law does not preempt state antidiscrimination statutes is misplaced. The issue is not one of federal preemption of state law, but rather that state law (FEHA) itself defers to the federal definition of illegal drugs. Because Plaintiff’s marijuana use is unlawful under the Controlled Substances Act, it constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" under FEHA, regardless of its status under the Compassionate Use Act. The lower courts correctly applied this exclusion.

**B. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

The Court agrees with Defendant that the Compassionate Use Act (CUA) does not mandate the accommodation of medical marijuana use in the workplace. Plaintiff argues that the CUA establishes a public policy requiring employer accommodation, citing *Garden Grove Unified School District v. Superior Court* (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 322 [68 Cal.Rptr.3d 626]. However, the text of the CUA is clear and limited in scope.

The CUA was enacted "to ensure that seriously ill Californians have the right to obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes" and to ensure that patients and their primary caregivers "who obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes upon the recommendation of a physician are not subject to criminal prosecution." (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5(b)(1)(A), (d).) The Act provides a defense to criminal prosecution under state law; it does not address the civil rights and duties of employers and employees. As this Court noted in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], the federal prohibition on marijuana remains in effect, and employers may take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions.

Plaintiff’s attempt to distinguish *Loder* as applying only to public employers subject to federal grant conditions is unpersuasive. The reasoning in *Loder* regarding the illegality of marijuana under federal law applies with equal force to the interpretation of FEHA’s "illegal use of drugs" exclusion. Furthermore, the California Code of Regulations supports the employer’s position, stating that "an employer may require a candidate for employment to submit to a drug test" and that a positive test for drugs unlawful under the Controlled Substances Act may be a basis for discharge. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11064(a), (b).)

**C. Termination Was Based on Conduct, Not Disability**

Finally, the Court finds that Plaintiff was terminated for his conduct—specifically, his use of an illegal substance—rather than because of his disability. FEHA prohibits discrimination "because of" a disability (Gov. Code, § 12940(a)), but it does not prohibit an employer from taking action based on an employee's violation of law or workplace policy.

As alleged in the complaint, Defendant informed Plaintiff he was being fired "because of his marijuana use." Plaintiff does not allege he was terminated due to his back condition or his inability to perform the job. As held in *Loder*, an employer's drug policy "does not discriminate on the basis of disability; rather, it discriminates on the basis of conduct—specifically, the use of illegal drugs." (14 Cal.4th at 883.) Because the use of marijuana is prohibited by federal law and defined as "illegal use of drugs" under FEHA, Defendant’s decision to terminate Plaintiff for this conduct was lawful.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the lower courts is affirmed. The California Fair Employment and Housing Act explicitly excludes "current illegal use of drugs" from the definition of a protected disability, and the Legislature has defined "illegal use of drugs" by reference to the federal Controlled Substances Act. Therefore, Plaintiff’s use of marijuana, even if recommended by a physician and authorized by the Compassionate Use Act, constitutes illegal drug use under FEHA. The Compassionate Use Act does not create a right to use marijuana in the workplace or a public policy exception to at-will employment that would preclude an employer from terminating an employee for testing positive for illegal drugs. Defendant, RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., acted within its legal rights in terminating Plaintiff based on his violation of its drug-free workplace policy.

### I. THE COURT SHOULD REJECT THE PROSECUTION'S MISCONSTRUED READING OF FEHA'S "ILLEGAL USE OF DRUGS" EXCLUSION

The prosecution argues that Government Code section 12926.1(c) unambiguously incorporates federal drug prohibitions into FEHA, thereby excluding Plaintiff from any protection. This argument fails for several reasons.

The exclusion in Government Code section 12926(k) addresses impairments "that are the result of" current illegal drug use—meaning the impairment itself must stem from illegal drug use. Here, Plaintiff's disability—his chronic back condition and muscle spasms—stems from his military service, not from his marijuana use. His marijuana use is treatment for his disability, not its cause. The statute excludes impairments resulting from illegal drug use; it does not exclude disabled individuals who use illegal drugs to treat their conditions.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on the definition in Government Code section 12926.1(c) is misplaced. That section defines "illegal use of drugs" for purposes of FEHA, but the California Legislature cannot abdicate its authority to define the scope of its own statutes by blindly incorporating federal definitions. When the Legislature referenced the Controlled Substances Act, it did so against the backdrop of California law permitting medical marijuana use. The definition must be read in context. As the California Supreme Court recognized in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 852 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], California has "adopted a policy of tolerance toward the medicinal use of marijuana," and this policy must inform the interpretation of related statutes.

Third, even if the Court accepts the prosecution's reading of section 12926.1(c), the question remains: was Plaintiff's use "unlawful under the Controlled Substances Act" when he used it? The Controlled Substances Act prohibits marijuana possession, but the Act operates within the federal system. ### II. THE COMPASSIONATE USE ACT CREATES A PUBLIC POLICY THAT PROTECTS PLAINTIFF'S CONDUCT

The prosecution argues that the Compassionate Use Act (CUA) does not create employment rights. While technically correct that the CUA does not explicitly address employment, the prosecution ignores the Act's broader public policy implications.

The CUA was enacted to ensure that "seriously ill Californians have the right to obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes" and that patients using marijuana on a physician's recommendation "are not subject to criminal prosecution." (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5(b)(1)(A), (d).) This express statement of California public policy—that medical marijuana use is lawful and protected within this state—must be considered when interpreting other California statutes, including FEHA.

When California voters approved Proposition 215, they established a fundamental public policy favoring access to medical marijuana for seriously ill patients. This policy would be hollow if employers could terminate employees solely for engaging in conduct that California law expressly protects. As the California Supreme Court has recognized, "the public policy of this state, as expressed in the Compassionate Use Act, is that seriously ill patients have the right to obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes." The Court did not hold that private employers have free reign to terminate employees for medical marijuana use. Indeed, the *Loder* Court expressly acknowledged that its holding was "guided by" the specific federal funding context. (Id. at 883.)

Furthermore, the prosecution's argument that the CUA creates no employment rights proves too much. The CUA creates no rights of any kind except the right not to be criminally prosecuted—but this is precisely the point. California has declared that medical marijuana use is not criminal conduct. The prosecution argues that Plaintiff cannot state a claim for wrongful termination because no public policy protects his conduct. This Court should hold otherwise.

California recognizes the common law tort of wrongful termination in violation of public policy. (*Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].) An employee may state a claim for wrongful termination when the employer's conduct violates a fundamental public policy expressed in a constitutional or statutory provision.

The Compassionate Use Act expresses a fundamental California public policy: that seriously ill residents have the right to use marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation. When an employer terminates an employee for exercising this right, the employer violates this public policy.

The prosecution argues that the CUA's purpose was merely to provide a criminal defense, not to create civil protections. But this argument ignores the nature of public policy. Public policies need not create explicit rights to support wrongful termination claims. (*Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157] [wrongful termination claim may be based on public policy "declared by constitutional or statutory provision"].) The voters who passed Proposition 215 declared that medical marijuana use is lawful in California. This declaration of public policy is sufficient to support Plaintiff's wrongful termination claim.

Plaintiff's complaint alleges that he uses marijuana to treat chronic pain resulting from his service-connected back injuries. This is precisely the type of discrimination FEHA was designed to prevent.

The prosecution cites *Loder* for the proposition that drug policies discriminate based on conduct, not disability. But *Loder* is distinguishable. In *Loder*, the plaintiff was a police officer who used marijuana recreationally—not for medical purposes. The Court held that the employer's drug policy targeted illegal drug use generally, not disability-related use. Here, by contrast, Plaintiff uses marijuana specifically to treat his disability. His marijuana use is not separate from his disability; it is directly related to his disability.

FEHA requires employers to engage in an interactive process to determine whether reasonable accommodations can enable disabled employees to perform their essential job functions. (Gov. ### V. THE CALIFORNIA CODE OF REGULATIONS DOES NOT COMPEL AFFIRMANCE

The prosecution cites California Code of Regulations, title 2, section 11064(a) and (b) for the proposition that employers may discharge employees who test positive for drugs prohibited under the Controlled Substances Act. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final decision following the submission of closing arguments. The core issue is whether Plaintiff Gary Ross stated a cause of action for disability discrimination under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) (Gov. Code, § 12900 et seq.) or wrongful termination in violation of public policy, after he was terminated for testing positive for marijuana, which he used pursuant to a physician’s recommendation under the Compassionate Use Act (CUA) (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5).

The Court has reviewed the arguments submitted by the parties. For clarity, the argument labeled "Prosecution's rebuttal" is treated as the argument on behalf of Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., as it urges affirmance of the lower court's dismissal. The argument labeled "Defense's rebuttal" is treated as the argument on behalf of Plaintiff Gary Ross, as it urges reversal.

After considering the statutory framework, the provided case background, and the parties' arguments, the Court finds that the lower courts correctly sustained the demurrer. The judgment is affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. FEHA’s Definition of "Illegal Use of Drugs" Controls**

The Court agrees with Defendant that the plain language of the FEHA statutory scheme precludes Plaintiff’s claims. FEHA defines "disability" to exclude "a physical or mental impairment that is the result of the current illegal use of drugs." (Gov. Code, § 12926(k).) Furthermore, the Legislature has explicitly defined "illegal use of drugs" for purposes of FEHA. Government Code section 12926.1(c) states: "For purposes of this part, 'illegal use of drugs' means the use of drugs, the possession or distribution of which is unlawful under the Controlled Substances Act."

The Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 801 et seq.) classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance, making its possession and use unlawful under federal law. (21 U.S.C. § 812, Schedule I(c)(10).) As established by the United States Supreme Court in *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195] and *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], federal law prohibits the possession and use of marijuana, regardless of state law authorizing it for medical purposes.

By referencing the federal Controlled Substances Act, the California Legislature incorporated federal drug prohibitions into the FEHA framework. Consequently, Plaintiff’s marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" under FEHA, even though it is authorized by the CUA. Plaintiff’s argument that the definition should be read in the context of California’s tolerance for medical marijuana is unpersuasive in light of the Legislature’s explicit choice to tie the definition of "illegal use" to federal law. The lower courts correctly applied this exclusion.

**B. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

The Court rejects Plaintiff’s contention that the CUA creates a public policy that protects his employment. The text of the CUA is limited in scope. It was enacted "to ensure that seriously ill Californians have the right to obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes" and to ensure that patients and their primary caregivers "who obtain and use marijuana for medical purposes upon the recommendation of a physician are not subject to criminal prosecution." (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5(b)(1)(A), (d).)

As stated in the case background, "Nothing in the text or history of the Compassionate Use Act suggests the voters intended the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees." The CUA provides a defense to criminal prosecution under state law; it does not address the civil rights and duties of employers and employees. Plaintiff’s reliance on *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330] and *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157] to assert a wrongful termination claim is misplaced because the CUA does not establish the fundamental public policy required for such a claim in the employment context.

**C. Termination Was Based on Conduct, Not Disability**

The Court finds that Plaintiff was terminated for his conduct—specifically, his use of an illegal substance—rather than because of his disability. FEHA prohibits discrimination "because of" a disability (Gov. Code, § 12940(a)), but it does not prohibit an employer from taking action based on an employee's violation of law or workplace policy.

The complaint alleges that Defendant informed Plaintiff he was being fired "because of his marijuana use." Plaintiff does not allege he was terminated due to his back condition or his inability to perform the job. As held in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], an employer's drug policy "does not discriminate on the basis of disability; rather, it discriminates on the basis of conduct—specifically, the use of illegal drugs." Plaintiff’s attempt to distinguish *Loder* based on the presence of federal grant conditions or the public/private nature of the employer does not alter the fundamental principle that California law permits employers to consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the lower courts is affirmed. The California Fair Employment and Housing Act explicitly excludes "current illegal use of drugs" from the definition of a protected disability, and the Legislature has defined "illegal use of drugs" by reference to the federal Controlled Substances Act. Therefore, Plaintiff’s use of marijuana, even if recommended by a physician and authorized by the Compassionate Use Act, constitutes illegal drug use under FEHA. The Compassionate Use Act does not create a right to use marijuana in the workplace or a public policy exception to at-will employment that would preclude an employer from terminating an employee for testing positive for illegal drugs. Defendant, RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., acted within its legal rights in terminating Plaintiff based on his violation of its drug-free workplace policy.
