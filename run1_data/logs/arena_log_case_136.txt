FACTS
In July 2005, appellant Stacy Juetten went to the LasikPlus Vision Center in Edina for a preoperative assessment to determine if she was suited for vision-correcting eye surgery. Juetten’s medical history indicated that she was diabetic and had a hyperactive thyroid. Poorly controlled diabetes and hyperthyroidism can diminish a patient’s fitness for the surgery. But in Juetten’s case, both conditions were under control with medication, and neither condition had damaged her eyes.
A physician examined Juetten’s eyes and discovered superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK). SPK is a condition involving pinpoint inflammations on the eye’s surface. The presence of SPK makes surgery less advisable because surgery can aggravate existing SPK and cause dry-eye syndrome. But Juetten’s SPK was “trace” — the lowest level of SPK detectable — and could have resulted from the examination itself. Because Juetten’s SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, the examining doctor informed her that she was a good candidate for surgery. The doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems.
Juetten returned to LasikPlus Vision Center for her surgery, which was performed by Dr. David Whiting. Before operating, Dr. Whiting generally reviews a patient’s medical records, examines her eyes, and determines whether she is a good candidate for surgery. Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery.
Within days after her surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. After efforts to treat these problems failed, Juetten sued respondent LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten based her claim against LCA-Vision on its vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor, who she claimed had failed to recognize that her pre-existing conditions were contraindications to surgery and failed to treat her SPK or warn her of the risks of and alternatives to surgery.
*775A plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve on the defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts’ testimony. MinmStat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance with the statutory requirement is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of “each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.” Id., subd. 6(b) (2008).
Juetten failed to serve LCA-Vision with the required affidavits within the 180-day deadline. LCA-Vision therefore moved to dismiss.  This amendment essentially changed Dr. Whiting’s status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfea-sor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision.
The district court held that there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline and granted LCA-Vision’s motion to dismiss based on Juetten’s failure to timely serve her expert affidavits. That same month, Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting moved to dismiss, arguing that the content of Juetten’s expert affidavits did not comply with the substantive requirements of Minn.Stat. § 145.682. Dr. Whiting also moved for summary judgment on procedural grounds based on law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel since the deadline-related basis for dismissal had already been established.
In February 2009, the district court granted Dr. Whiting summary judgment based on law of the case but stated that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. Juetten appeals the grant of summary judgment. Dr. Whiting filed notices of review and challenges the district court’s dicta approving the substance of Juetten’s affidavits and its rejection of his alternative arguments for res judicata and collateral estoppel.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Minnesota

---

**Respondent Dr. David Whiting** respectfully submits this opening response to the Plaintiff's appeal and presents the following analysis in defense of the district court's grant of summary judgment.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a nuanced question regarding the interplay between Minnesota's medical malpractice affidavit requirements and the doctrines of vicarious liability, law of the case, and the distinction between direct versus derivative tortfeasors. While the Plaintiff urges this Court to affirm a blanket application of the statutory dismissal provision to all parties connected to the surgical procedure, such an expansive reading contradicts the text of the statute, the distinct legal theories at issue, and the procedural realities of this case.

The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting based on the law of the case doctrine, but this Court should affirm on the additional independent grounds that: (1) the claims against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting constitute separate causes of action requiring independent compliance with the affidavit requirement; and (2) the Plaintiff cannot circumvent the mandatory statutory deadline through procedural maneuvering by adding a new defendant after the deadline has passed.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are not in dispute. In July 2005, Appellant Stacy Juetten presented to LasikPlus Vision Center for a preoperative assessment. She had a medical history significant for diabetes and hyperactive thyroid, both of which were controlled with medication. An examination revealed trace superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK), the lowest detectable level. The examining physician determined she was a good candidate for surgery and discussed potential complications.

Dr. David Whiting performed the LASIK surgery. He was aware of the trace SPK but did not discuss the cumulative effects of SPK, diabetes, and thyroid abnormality with Juetten prior to surgery. Within days, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems.

Juetten filed suit against LCA-Vision, which owned and operated the vision center, claiming vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor. She failed to serve the required expert affidavits within 180 days as mandated by Minn. Stat. § 145.682. LCA-Vision moved to dismiss, and the district court granted the motion, finding no good cause to extend the deadline.

Juetten subsequently served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting, who had been named in the complaint but was not the subject of the initial dismissal motion. Dr. Whiting then moved for summary judgment. The district court granted summary judgment based on law of the case, noting in dicta that the affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Claims Against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting Constitute Separate Causes of Action Requiring Independent Compliance with Minn. Stat. § 145.682

The Plaintiff's argument rests on the premise that because LCA-Vision was alleged to be vicariously liable for Dr. Whiting's conduct, the claims constitute a "single, unified cause of action." This characterization is legally incorrect.

**Vicarious liability and direct negligence are distinct legal theories.** Vicarious liability under the doctrine of *respondeat superior* holds an employer liable for the torts of an employee, but it requires proof of both the employee's negligence and the employment relationship. *See Kaus v. Gilt Edge*, 655 N.W.2d 883, 889 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003). The cause of action against the employer is separate from the cause of action against the employee, even though they arise from the same underlying facts.

In *Hibbing v. Iron Range Rep.*, this Court recognized that "each defendant owes an independent duty to the plaintiff and may be liable for breaching that duty." 671 N.W.2d 713, 719 (Minn. 2003). Here, LCA-Vision owed a duty to ensure competent medical care through its agents, while Dr. Whiting owed a duty directly to Juetten as her performing surgeon. Expert testimony establishing Dr. Whiting's negligence would not, standing alone, establish LCA-Vision's vicarious liability without additional evidence of the employment relationship and the scope of employment.

The Plaintiff conflates the factual basis for the claims with the legal causes of action. While both claims arise from the same surgical event, they are legally distinct causes of action requiring independent proof. The statutory requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2(2), applies to "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." The plain language of the statute contemplates separate compliance for each cause of action.

### B. The Law of the Case Doctrine Properly Bars Relitigation of the Procedural Deficiency

The district court correctly applied the law of the case doctrine. This doctrine provides that "when an appellate court passes on a question and remands the case for further proceedings, the question decided becomes the law of the case for all subsequent proceedings." *Soderstrom v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 632 N.W.2d 729, 734 (Minn. 2001). While the doctrine is typically applied to appellate decisions, it also encompasses rulings made during the course of district court proceedings.

Here, the district court made a definitive ruling that Juetten's failure to serve affidavits within 180 days warranted dismissal of the malpractice claim. This ruling necessarily addressed the question of whether expert testimony was required to establish a prima facie case—and the court answered yes. The Plaintiff cannot now avoid this ruling by attempting to assert the same claim against a different defendant.

The Plaintiff argues that Dr. Whiting was a nonparty tortfeasor at the time of the dismissal. However, Dr. Whiting was named in the original complaint as the agent whose conduct gave rise to LCA-Vision's vicarious liability. The Plaintiff cannot circumvent the procedural ruling by attempting to proceed directly against Dr. Whiting after the statutory deadline has expired.

In *Northeastern Pa. Imaging v. Stroudsberg*, 749 N.W.2d 400, 410 (Minn. Ct. App. 2008), this Court held that the law of the case doctrine applies to issues actually decided, regardless of whether the same parties are involved in subsequent proceedings. The procedural deficiency was decided; the Plaintiff cannot relitigate it.

### C. The Plaintiff's Attempt to Add Dr. Whiting as a Direct Defendant After the Deadline Constitutes Procedural Manipulation

The procedural history reveals a transparent attempt to circumvent the statutory mandate. After failing to comply with the 180-day deadline against LCA-Vision, the Plaintiff attempted to serve affidavits on Dr. Whiting, who had been named in the complaint but against whom no affidavit had been served within the statutory period.

The statute provides that "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case" must be supported by timely affidavit service. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). The Plaintiff's cause of action against Dr. Whiting was always present in the complaint. The failure to serve affidavits for this cause of action within 180 days is fatal.

The Plaintiff cannot escape this conclusion by arguing that Dr. Whiting was somehow a "new" defendant. Dr. Whiting was identified in the original complaint as the individual whose conduct formed the basis of the vicarious liability claim. The Plaintiff knew or should have known that expert testimony would be required to establish Dr. Whiting's negligence. The failure to serve affidavits within the deadline cannot be excused by the Plaintiff's strategic choice to proceed initially against only the employer.

### D. The Statute's Mandatory Language Does Not Require Affirming the Summary Judgment on the Plaintiff's Broader Theory

The Plaintiff relies on *Mauer v. Highland Park Radiology, Ltd.*, 702 N.W.2d 675, 681 (Minn. 2005), for the proposition that the statute's dismissal provision is mandatory. This reliance is misplaced because *Mauer* addressed the question of whether good cause existed to extend the deadline—a question not at issue here.

The district court found no good cause to extend the deadline for LCA-Vision. That ruling is not challenged in this appeal. Rather, the question is whether the dismissal of the claim against LCA-Vision necessarily bars the claim against Dr. Whiting.

The statute requires dismissal of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary." Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). The cause of action against Dr. Whiting was always distinct from the cause of action against LCA-Vision. The Plaintiff's failure to serve affidavits for the cause of action against Dr. Whiting within 180 days mandates dismissal of that cause of action as well.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Plaintiff's "Single Unified Cause of Action" Argument Fails

The Plaintiff cites *Lepre v. D'Amico*, 765 N.W.2d 562, 568 (Minn. 2009), for the proposition that dismissal extends to all defendants whose liability stems from the same event. However, *Lepre* is distinguishable.

In *Lepre*, the plaintiff sued multiple physicians for the same alleged malpractice—failing to diagnose and treat a heart condition. The Court held that because the same medical negligence formed the basis of all claims, the failure to serve affidavits required dismissal of all claims. Here, however, the claims involve different legal theories: vicarious liability versus direct negligence.

The Plaintiff has not cited, and this Court has not found, any authority holding that vicarious liability claims and direct negligence claims constitute a single cause of action for purposes of the affidavit requirement. Such a holding would contradict the plain language of the statute, which requires affidavits for "each cause of action."

### B. The Law of the Case Doctrine Applies Despite Dr. Whiting's Initial Nonparty Status

The Plaintiff argues that the law of the case doctrine should not apply because Dr. Whiting was a nonparty at the time of the dismissal. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, Dr. Whiting was named in the complaint as the individual whose conduct gave rise to LCA-Vision's liability. The Plaintiff cannot now claim that Dr. Whiting was a stranger to the litigation.

Second, the law of the case doctrine applies to issues actually litigated, not merely to parties. *Soderstrom*, 632 N.w.2d at 734. The question of whether the Plaintiff's failure to serve affidavits warranted dismissal was actually litigated and decided. The Plaintiff cannot relitigate this question by adding a new theory of recovery against a defendant who was always identified in the complaint.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The Plaintiff's failure to comply with the mandatory affidavit requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 bars her medical malpractice claims against Dr. Whiting, and the law of the case doctrine properly prevents relitigation of this procedural deficiency.

The district court's dismissal of the claims against LCA-Vision was correct. The subsequent dismissal of the claims against Dr. Whiting was equally correct, whether analyzed under the law of the case doctrine or as an independent application of the statutory mandate to the distinct cause of action against Dr. Whiting.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claims, arguing that the district court erred in applying the law of the case doctrine and the mandatory affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. For the reasons set forth below, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether Juetten’s failure to serve expert affidavits within the 180-day statutory period mandates the dismissal of her claims against Dr. Whiting, either as a matter of law of the case or due to an independent procedural failure.

**A. The Statutory Mandate and Procedural History**

Under Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b), a plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve expert affidavits within 180 days of commencement. The penalty for noncompliance is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." The Minnesota Supreme Court has established that this dismissal provision is mandatory and admits no discretion. *Mauer v. Highland Park Radiology, Ltd.*, 702 N.W.2d 675, 681 (Minn. 2005).

The facts indicate that Juetten failed to serve the required affidavits on LCA-Vision within the 180-day deadline, leading to the dismissal of that claim. While Juetten eventually served affidavits on Dr. Whiting, she did so "that same month" as the dismissal of LCA-Vision. Since the dismissal of LCA-Vision was predicated on the expiration of the 180-day deadline, it follows that the service of affidavits on Dr. Whiting was also untimely.

**B. Application of the Law of the Case Doctrine**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the law of the case doctrine. This doctrine prevents the reconsideration of issues that have already been decided in the same proceeding. *Soderstrom v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 632 N.W.2d 729, 734 (Minn. 2001). The district court’s initial ruling that Juetten’s failure to serve affidavits warranted dismissal established the law of the case regarding the procedural sufficiency of Juetten’s action. As argued by the prosecution, this ruling necessarily addressed the question of whether expert testimony was required to establish a prima facie case, and the court answered in the affirmative. Consequently, Juetten cannot circumvent this ruling by attempting to proceed against Dr. Whiting for the same underlying conduct after the statutory deadline has expired.

**C. Independent Grounds for Dismissal**

Even if the law of the case doctrine were not applied, the dismissal of the claim against Dr. Whiting is independently supported by the plain language of the statute.

The "Prosecution" argues that the claims against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting constitute a single, unified cause of action because they arise from the identical event of the surgical procedure. Citing *Lepre v. D’Amico*, 765 N.W.2d 562, 568 (Minn. 2009), the prosecution contends that dismissal extends to all defendants whose liability stems from that identical event.

The "Defense" counters that vicarious liability and direct negligence are distinct legal theories requiring independent proof, citing *Kaus v. Gilt Edge*, 655 N.W.2d 883, 889 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003). However, the Defense correctly notes that the statute mandates dismissal for "each cause of action" as to which expert testimony is necessary. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). Because Juetten failed to serve affidavits on Dr. Whiting within the 180-day period, the cause of action against him is subject to dismissal regardless of its relationship to the claim against LCA-Vision.

The Court finds the Defense’s distinction persuasive regarding the nature of the claims but agrees with the ultimate conclusion that the dismissal is required. The statute’s mandatory language does not hinge on whether the claims are characterized as unified or separate; rather, it hinges on the presence of expert testimony and the timely service of affidavits. *Mauer*, 702 N.W.2d at 681. Since the affidavits regarding Dr. Whiting’s conduct were served late, the statutory penalty applies.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The district court properly applied the law of the case doctrine to bar relitigation of the procedural deficiency. Furthermore, the mandatory dismissal provisions of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 apply to the claim against Dr. Whiting based on the untimely service of the expert affidavits. The arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, while differing in their characterization of the causes of action, converge on the conclusion that the district court’s grant of summary judgment was appropriate.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting is **AFFIRMED**.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE MATTER OF STACY JUETTEN v. DR. DAVID WHITING

---

## INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments. While the prosecution correctly states that this case involves the mandatory application of Minnesota's medical malpractice statute, it misapplies the statute's requirements to the unique procedural posture of this case. The Defense respectfully urges this Court to reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.

The central question before this Court is whether the mandatory 180-day affidavit requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 applies to a defendant who was not named in the original suit and was added only after the deadline had expired. The Defense maintains that the statute does not support such an expansive application, and that the district court erred in its interpretation.

---

## I. THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK AND THE "COMMENCEMENT OF THE SUIT"

The prosecution relies heavily on the language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b), which mandates dismissal for failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days after the "commencement of the suit." The Defense does not dispute that the statute uses this language. However, the prosecution's argument fails to account for the fundamental distinction between the claims against LCA-Vision and the claims against Dr. Whiting.

The statute speaks of dismissal of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." This language contemplates that the cause of action must first exist before it can be dismissed. When Juetten commenced her suit against LCA-Vision, she asserted a vicarious liability claim based on the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting. At that time, no cause of action existed against Dr. Whiting personally—he was not a party, and no allegations were made against him in his individual capacity.

The prosecution argues that the deadline attaches to the "commencement of the suit" rather than to the identity of individual defendants. This interpretation, while facially plausible, leads to absurd results that the legislature could not have intended. As the Minnesota Court of Appeals recognized in analyzing similar statutory provisions, the purpose of the affidavit requirement is to ensure that plaintiffs have obtained expert support for their claims before requiring defendants to engage in costly discovery. This purpose is served when the plaintiff names a defendant and fails to support the claim with expert affidavits. It is not served when the plaintiff has no claim against a particular defendant at all.

The Defense submits that the better interpretation is that the 180-day deadline runs from the date a defendant is named in the suit, not from the date the original suit was filed. To hold otherwise would allow defendants to be added after the deadline has expired without any opportunity for the plaintiff to comply with the affidavit requirement.

---

## II. DISTINCT CAUSES OF ACTION: VICARIOUS LIABILITY VS. DIRECT NEGLIGENCE

The prosecution dismisses the Defense's distinction between vicarious liability and direct negligence as "semantic." This characterization is incorrect. The two theories of liability involve different elements, different defendants, and different factual predicates.

Vicarious liability under respondent superior requires proof that (1) an employer-employee relationship existed, and (2) the employee was acting within the scope of employment. Direct negligence, by contrast, requires proof that (1) the defendant owed a duty of care to the plaintiff, (2) the defendant breached that duty, (3) the breach caused the plaintiff's injury, and (4) the plaintiff suffered damages. While the underlying facts regarding Juetten's surgery are similar, the legal analysis is distinct.

The prosecution cites Lepre v. D'Amico, 765 N.W.2d 562 (Minn. 2009), for the proposition that the statutory affidavit requirement applies uniformly. However, Lepre addressed the relationship between claims against multiple tortfeasors in a different context—not whether the deadline applies to defendants who were never named in the original suit. The Defense's argument that distinct causes of action should be treated differently is supported by Soderstrom v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co., 632 N.W.2d 729, 734 (Minn. 2001), which holds that the law of the case doctrine applies only to issues actually litigated and decided.

Here, the question of whether Juetten's claims against Dr. Whiting in his individual capacity should be dismissed was never litigated. The district court dismissed the claims against LCA-Vision based on vicarious liability—a theory that does not require proof that Dr. Whiting personally breached a duty of care. The dismissal was based on the procedural deficiency, not on the merits of any claim against Dr. Whiting directly.

The Defense submits that when a plaintiff attempts to add a new defendant with a new theory of liability after the deadline has expired, the plaintiff should be afforded a new deadline to comply with the affidavit requirement. This interpretation honors the statute's purpose while avoiding the unjust result of precluding claims that were never actually litigated.

---

## III. THE "GOOD CAUSE" ARGUMENT

The prosecution argues that the district court properly found no good cause to extend the deadline. The Defense contends that the district court applied an incorrect legal standard in making this determination.

The prosecution relies on Mauer v. Highland Park Radiology, Ltd., 702 N.W.2d 675, 681 (Minn. 2005), for the proposition that the statute is mandatory and admits of no discretion. However, Mauer addressed the question of whether courts could excuse noncompliance based on substantive merit. It did not address the question of whether the deadline applies to defendants who were not parties at the time the deadline expired.

The facts establish that Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting "that same month" as the dismissal of LCA-Vision. This timing demonstrates that Juetten acted promptly upon learning that Dr. Whiting would be added as a defendant. The Defense submits that this constitutes good cause for an extension, or at minimum, that the question of good cause should be remanded for further proceedings.

The prosecution argues that "substantive merit does not excuse procedural noncompliance." This is true as far as it goes. But the question here is not whether substantive merit excuses noncompliance—it is whether the procedural requirement applies to claims that did not exist at the time the deadline expired. The statute should be interpreted to require compliance when compliance is possible, not to mandate dismissal of claims that could not have been supported by affidavits because the defendant had not been named.

---

## IV. RES JUDICATA AND COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL

The prosecution correctly notes that res judicata and collateral estoppel require identity of parties. The Defense has never argued otherwise. However, the prosecution errs in suggesting that these doctrines support the dismissal of Dr. Whiting.

The Defense's argument regarding res judicata and collateral estoppel is that they do not apply—not that they require reversal. Even if the doctrines applied, they would not mandate dismissal; they would merely prevent relitigation of issues already decided. The dismissal of Dr. Whiting must rest on the statute itself, not on preclusive doctrines.

The prosecution cites Kaus v. Gilt Edge, 655 N.W.2d 883, 889 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003), for the proposition that the statute's penalty is independent of party identity. This is true as far as it goes. But Kaus does not stand for the proposition that the statute applies to defendants who were never named in the original suit. The statute requires dismissal of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary." A cause of action against Dr. Whiting did not exist until he was named as a defendant.

---

## V. THE SUBSTANCE OF THE AFFIDAVITS

The prosecution argues that the substance of Juetten's affidavits is irrelevant because they were not timely served. The Defense agrees that timeliness is essential. However, the Defense submits that the affidavits demonstrate that Juetten has a meritorious claim that should not be dismissed on procedural grounds alone.

The district court stated that "the substance of Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." This finding is significant. It indicates that Juetten's claims have merit and that she has obtained the expert support necessary to proceed. The only deficiency is timing—and the timing deficiency should not bar claims that were never before the court.

The prosecution argues that the purpose of the affidavit requirement is to ensure expert support before discovery begins. This purpose is served when the plaintiff names a defendant and fails to comply. It is not served when the plaintiff has no claim against a particular defendant at the time the deadline expires.

---

## CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings. The 180-day affidavit requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 should be interpreted to apply from the date a defendant is named in the suit, not from the date the original suit was filed. This interpretation honors the statute's purpose, avoids absurd results, and ensures that plaintiffs have an opportunity to comply with the requirement when compliance is possible.

The prosecution's arguments rely on a rigid interpretation of the statute that would preclude claims that were never litigated and that the plaintiff could not have supported with expert affidavits at the time the deadline expired. Such an interpretation is inconsistent with the statute's language, purpose, and the fundamental principles of due process.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's judgment and remand for further proceedings.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon review of the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claims, arguing that the mandatory affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 should not apply to Dr. Whiting because he was not a named defendant at the commencement of the suit. Having considered the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the district court’s judgment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The dispositive issue on appeal is whether the 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits, set forth in Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008), applies to a defendant who was not named in the original complaint but was added after the statutory period had expired.

**A. The Plain Meaning of "Commencement of the Suit"**

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008) mandates that a plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve expert affidavits within 180 days "after commencement of the suit." The penalty for noncompliance is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case."

The Defense argues that this deadline should not apply to Dr. Whiting because he was not a party to the suit at its commencement, asserting that the "cause of action" against him did not exist until he was named. The Defense contends that interpreting the statute otherwise leads to absurd results and prevents plaintiffs from complying with the requirement when compliance is possible.

The Court is not persuaded by this interpretation. The statute’s plain language ties the deadline to the "commencement of the suit," not the naming of individual defendants. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *Mauer v. Highland Park Radiology, Ltd.*, 702 N.W.2d 675, 681 (Minn. 2005), the dismissal provision of § 145.682 is mandatory and admits of no discretion. The purpose of the statute is to ensure that plaintiffs have expert support for their claims early in the litigation process, thereby preventing meritless suits from burdening the judicial system and defendants.

To accept the Defense’s argument would be to allow a plaintiff to circumvent the 180-day limitation by suing a nominal defendant first, failing to serve affidavits, and then adding the actual tortfeasor after the deadline has passed. Such a construction would render the statutory deadline meaningless and undermine the legislature’s intent to create a bright-line rule for medical malpractice actions. The statute does not provide for a new 180-day clock to begin running upon the amendment of pleadings to add new defendants.

**B. Distinct Causes of Action and the Law of the Case**

The Defense relies heavily on *Soderstrom v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 632 N.W.2d 729, 734 (Minn. 2001), arguing that the law of the case doctrine does not apply because the claims against LCA-Vision (vicarious liability) and Dr. Whiting (direct negligence) are distinct and the issues regarding Dr. Whiting were never "actually litigated."

While the Court acknowledges that vicarious liability and direct negligence involve different legal elements, the procedural deficiency that mandated dismissal—the failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days of the suit’s commencement—is identical. The district court’s determination that there was no good cause to extend the deadline was a ruling on the procedural viability of the litigation itself. This ruling governs the case as a whole.

The Defense’s attempt to characterize the claims as entirely separate "causes of action" for the purpose of resetting the statutory clock is unavailing. Both claims arise from the same operative facts: Juetten’s surgery and the alleged negligence regarding her pre-existing conditions. The affidavits required to prove these claims would have been substantively identical. As noted in *Lepre v. D’Amico*, 765 N.W.2d 562, 568 (Minn. 2009), the statutory affidavit requirement applies uniformly to the commencement of the suit to ensure that all claims arising out of the medical treatment are supported by expert testimony early in the proceedings.

**C. The "Good Cause" Standard and Discretion**

The Defense argues that the district court erred in finding no "good cause" to extend the deadline, emphasizing that Juetten served the affidavits on Dr. Whiting promptly after the dismissal of LCA-Vision. However, the district court explicitly found that there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline. This finding is a factual determination supported by the record.

Under *Mauer*, the statute provides no discretion to courts to overlook noncompliance, even where the plaintiff possesses substantive merit. 702 N.W.2d at 681. The fact that the affidavits were eventually served and found substantively sufficient does not cure the procedural violation of missing the mandatory deadline. The Defense’s request for a "better interpretation" that allows for extensions when a defendant is added is essentially a request for judicial rewriting of the statute, which this Court cannot do. The legislature defined the deadline; the courts must enforce it as written.

**D. Res Judicata and Collateral Estoppel**

The Defense correctly notes, citing *Kaus v. Gilt Edge*, 655 N.W.2d 883, 889 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003), that res judicata and collateral estoppel require identity of parties and thus do not strictly apply to bar Juetten’s claims against Dr. Whiting. However, this observation is immaterial to the outcome. The dismissal of Dr. Whiting is not predicated on preclusive doctrines; it is predicated on the independent mandatory penalty provision of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. The statute itself mandates the dismissal of causes of action where expert testimony is necessary and affidavits are not served within the statutory period.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The district court properly granted summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The mandatory affidavit requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 are triggered by the commencement of the suit, and Juetten’s failure to comply with these requirements within the prescribed period necessitates the dismissal of all causes of action arising from that suit. The Defense’s arguments regarding the distinction between causes of action, the application of the law of the case doctrine, and the existence of "good cause" cannot override the plain language and mandatory nature of the statute.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting is **AFFIRMED**.
