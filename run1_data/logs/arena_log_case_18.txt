# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the trial court committed a manifest abuse of discretion in entering default judgment against the defendant pursuant to Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019. While this Court has recognized that default judgment is a proper sanction for discovery violations in certain circumstances, the circumstances presented here do not warrant such an extreme remedy. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court and remand for further proceedings.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY IMPOSING THE MOST SEVERE SANCTION AVAILABLE WITHOUT PROPERLY CONSIDERING LESS SEVERE ALTERNATIVES

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court has consistently held that the choice of an appropriate sanction for discovery violations rests within the sound discretion of the trial court. However, this discretion is not unlimited. In *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001), this Court emphasized that default judgment is an extreme remedy that should be imposed only after careful consideration of multiple factors, including the nature and severity of the violation, the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party, the prejudice to the opposing party, the possibility of curing that prejudice through lesser sanctions, and the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded.

The defense does not dispute that the defendant, Tyrone Diefenderfer, failed to appear for multiple scheduled depositions over a period of several years. However, the record demonstrates that these failures were not the product of willful bad faith or deliberate obstruction. Rather, the record reflects that defendant's counsel repeatedly represented that he was unable to locate the defendant. This is a critical distinction. In *D'Elia v. C.R. Laurence Co.*, 564 Pa. 524, 769 A.2d 505 (Pa. 2001), this Court recognized that sanctions must be tailored to the nature and severity of the violation and that the trial court must consider whether lesser sanctions might adequately address the discovery violation while avoiding the harsh consequence of a default judgment.

Here, the trial court failed to adequately consider or implement lesser sanctions. Throughout the seven-year period of litigation, the plaintiff never sought an order compelling the defendant's attendance at deposition under penalty of contempt, nor did he seek an order precluding the defendant from offering certain evidence at trial. The record reflects that the plaintiff could have pursued additional judicial interventions that would have addressed the discovery violations without the extreme measure of entering default judgment.

The defense further notes that the plaintiff himself contributed to the delay in obtaining the defendant's deposition. The initial deposition scheduled for August 13, 2007, was not attended by the defendant, yet the plaintiff waited until August 27, 2008—over a year later—to secure new counsel for the defendant. This delay undermines the plaintiff's claim that he diligently pursued discovery and suggests that the plaintiff himself bears some responsibility for the inability to obtain the defendant's deposition testimony before his death.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE IRREPARABLE PREJUDICE SUFFICIENT TO JUSTIFY DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The plaintiff contends that the defendant's death rendered the prejudice irreparable and that no lesser sanction could restore the lost evidence. This argument, while superficially appealing, ignores the fundamental reality that the defendant's testimony was not the only evidence available to establish the cause and origin of the fire.

The facts of this case establish that the cause and ignition source of the fire was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove. These findings were made by fire investigators and were not dependent upon the defendant's deposition testimony. The plaintiff had access to the fire investigation reports, the physical evidence from the scene, and the testimony of fire personnel who investigated the incident and recovered the victim's body.

The defendant was not the only potential source of information regarding the circumstances of the fire. The landlords and owners of the premises, defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, had knowledge of the defendant's arrangement to perform maintenance in exchange for rent, and they could have provided relevant testimony regarding the condition of the premises, the stove, and the kerosene container. The plaintiff never sought to depose these witnesses or to obtain their testimony regarding the cause of the fire.

Furthermore, the defendant did provide an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire, in which he denied that he was the cause of the fire. While this statement was not under oath and was not subject to cross-examination, it nonetheless provides some insight into the defendant's version of events. The plaintiff chose not to pursue this statement or to seek to introduce it at trial, yet now claims that the defendant's death has caused irreparable prejudice.

The plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that the defendant's deposition testimony was essential to establishing liability. The fire investigation reports, the physical evidence, and the testimony of other witnesses were available to the plaintiff and could have been used to establish the cause of the fire. The trial court therefore erred in finding that the prejudice to the plaintiff was irreparable and that no lesser sanction could address that prejudice.

## III. THE ENTRY OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT IS CONTRARY TO THE EVIDENCE REGARDING THE CAUSE OF THE FIRE

The defendant's third issue on appeal challenges the trial court's implicit finding that the defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The record contains no evidence to support such a finding.

The fire investigation determined that the fire originated from an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The defendant was asleep on a couch in the first floor living room at the time the fire started. He was not in the kitchen, and there is no evidence that he placed the pot on the stove or that he was responsible for the ignition of the fire. The rapid progression and expansion of the fire was attributed to kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove—a condition for which the defendant bears no responsibility.

The plaintiff has produced no expert testimony, fire investigation evidence, or other proof establishing that the defendant's conduct was negligent or that it was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The mere fact that the defendant was present in the residence at the time of the fire is insufficient to establish negligence. Under Pennsylvania law, a plaintiff must prove that the defendant's conduct was the actual and proximate cause of the plaintiff's injuries. The plaintiff has failed to meet this burden, yet the trial court entered default judgment against the defendant as if negligence had been established.

This Court should not allow the procedural mechanism of a discovery sanction to serve as a substitute for proof of liability. The purpose of discovery sanctions is to enforce the discovery rules and to prevent a party from gaining an unfair advantage by concealing relevant evidence. It is not intended to be used as a shortcut to establishing liability when the underlying evidence does not support such a finding. The trial court's entry of default judgment in this case effectively presumed negligence without any evidentiary basis, which is contrary to the fundamental principles of our legal system.

## IV. THE DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT DOES NOT RISE TO THE LEVEL OF WILLFUL BAD FAITH REQUIRED FOR DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The plaintiff has characterized the defendant's conduct as willful and in bad faith, arguing that his repeated failures to appear for depositions demonstrate a deliberate intent to obstruct the discovery process. However, the record does not support this characterization.

As noted above, the defendant's counsel repeatedly represented that he was unable to locate the defendant. While this may not excuse the defendant's failures to appear, it does distinguish this case from those in which a party deliberately and willfully refuses to comply with discovery orders. In *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001), this Court emphasized that the willfulness of the violation is a critical factor in determining whether default judgment is appropriate. A party who cannot be located cannot be said to have willfully refused to comply with discovery orders.

Moreover, the defendant did not actively conceal himself or flee from the jurisdiction. The private investigator hired by the plaintiff was able to locate the defendant, which suggests that the defendant was not deliberately evading service. The defendant's subsequent refusal to submit to a videotaped deposition, while regrettable, does not rise to the level of willfulness required to justify default judgment. The defendant may have had legitimate concerns about being videotaped, and the plaintiff could have accommodated these concerns by agreeing to a non-videotaped deposition.

The plaintiff also failed to seek court intervention when the defendant refused to be videotaped. Rather than moving for a court order compelling the deposition or addressing the defendant's concerns about videotaping, the plaintiff simply allowed the matter to languish. This failure to pursue available remedies undermines the plaintiff's claim that the defendant's conduct was so egregious as to warrant the ultimate sanction of default judgment.

## V. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO BALANCE THE EQUITIES AND CONSIDER THE IMPACT OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT ON THE DEFENDANT'S ESTATE

The defendant died in January 2012, and the defendant administrator has been substituted in his place. The entry of default judgment against the defendant has significant consequences for the defendant's estate, including potential liability for damages that may not have been established had the case proceeded to trial on the merits.

This Court has recognized that default judgment is a drastic remedy that should be used sparingly and only when the interests of justice require it. In this case, the trial court failed to adequately consider the impact of default judgment on the defendant's estate, particularly in light of the lack of evidence establishing the defendant's negligence or causal responsibility for the fire.

The plaintiff has filed a motion for judgment in his favor based on the discovery sanction, effectively seeking a determination of liability without a trial on the merits. This Court should not permit the discovery sanction to serve as a substitute for a full and fair adjudication of the plaintiff's claims. The trial court abused its discretion by entering default judgment without adequate consideration of the equities and without sufficient evidence to establish liability.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, and remand this matter for further proceedings. The trial court committed a manifest abuse of discretion in entering default judgment as a discovery sanction, as it failed to properly consider lesser sanctions, failed to find that the defendant's conduct was willful or in bad faith, failed to identify evidence of irreparable prejudice, and failed to establish that the defendant was negligent or causally responsible for the fire. The interests of justice require that the plaintiff be required to prove his claims at trial rather than to obtain a default judgment based on discovery violations that do not warrant such an extreme remedy.

Respectfully submitted,

*Defense Counsel*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**PAPALAS, J.**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the orders of the trial court entering default judgment against the Defendant, Tyrone Diefenderfer, as a sanction for discovery violations. The Appellant, Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, argues that the trial court abused its discretion by imposing the most severe sanction available without adequately considering lesser alternatives and without finding that the Defendant acted in bad faith. After careful review of the record and the arguments presented by both parties, we must determine whether the trial court’s decision constitutes an abuse of discretion under the governing precedent.

**I. Standard of Review**

The authority to impose sanctions for discovery violations rests within the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court may not reverse such a decision absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. In *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001), the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania established that while trial courts have broad discretion, the entry of default judgment is an "extreme remedy" that requires a careful balancing of several factors. These factors include: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing that prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded. *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001). Furthermore, as noted in *D'Elia v. C.R. Laurence Co.*, 564 Pa. 524, 769 A.2d 505 (Pa. 2001), sanctions must be tailored to the nature of the violation, and courts must consider whether lesser sanctions would adequately address the misconduct.

**II. Analysis of the *Pappas* Factors**

**A. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The record reveals a "dismal record of non-compliance" spanning nearly five years. The Defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions in August 2007, October 2008, November 2008, November 2010, and December 2010. The Appellant argues that the Defendant’s conduct was not willful because his counsel repeatedly represented an inability to locate him. While the inability to locate a client may explain some delays, it does not entirely excuse the pattern of obstruction. Once the Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant via a private investigator in early 2009, the Defendant actively refused to be videotaped, leading to the cancellation of the April 2009 deposition. This refusal, following years of evasion, demonstrates a deliberate disregard for the court’s discovery processes. The subsequent cancellations in late 2010 and the failure to appear in January 2011 further compound the severity of the violation. The trial court was justified in finding that the cumulative effect of these actions was egregious.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The Appellant contends that the prejudice to the Plaintiff is not irreparable because the cause of the fire was established by physical evidence (an unattended pot and leaking kerosene) and that other witnesses, such as the landlords, were available. However, this argument misunderstands the nature of the prejudice. The Defendant was the sole eyewitness to the incident. While physical evidence may establish the mechanical cause of the fire, it cannot establish the Defendant’s negligence or his role in the events leading up to the ignition. The Defendant’s testimony was indispensable to the Plaintiff’s ability to prove liability.

The Defendant’s death in January 2012 rendered the prejudice permanent and irreparable. No lesser sanction, such as monetary fines or an adverse inference jury instruction, can cure the loss of the sole eyewitness testimony. As held in *D'Elia v. C.R. Laurence Co.*, 564 Pa. 524, 769 A.2d 505 (Pa. 2001), where the violation results in the irreversible loss of critical evidence, the trial court’s decision to impose the ultimate sanction is within its discretion. The Appellant’s suggestion that the Plaintiff could have deposed the landlords or relied on the unsworn statement to the state trooper does not mitigate the prejudice, as these sources cannot substitute for the Defendant's sworn testimony regarding his own conduct and state of mind.

**C. Importance of the Precluded Evidence**

The importance of the Defendant’s testimony cannot be overstated. As the only person present in the residence other than the decedent, the Defendant possessed unique knowledge regarding the events of that evening. His absence from the record leaves a gap that cannot be filled by any other evidence. The trial court correctly identified that the preclusion of this testimony was fatal to the Plaintiff’s case, thereby justifying the entry of judgment against the Defendant.

**D. Consideration of Lesser Sanctions and Plaintiff’s Diligence**

The Appellant argues that the trial court failed to consider lesser sanctions and that the Plaintiff’s own lack of diligence contributed to the delay. The record shows that the Plaintiff filed motions for contempt and sanctions in May 2009 and May 2010. While the Plaintiff may not have sought a court order compelling attendance under penalty of contempt at every juncture, the Plaintiff’s efforts to locate the Defendant—including hiring a private investigator—demonstrate sufficient diligence. The trial court was aware of the procedural history and implicitly determined that lesser sanctions, which had been threatened and partially sought, were insufficient to compel compliance. Given the Defendant's continued refusal to cooperate and subsequent death, the trial court reasonably concluded that anything short of default judgment would be futile.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court’s decision to enter default judgment against the Defendant was based on a clear pattern of discovery violations spanning several years, culminating in the irreversible loss of the sole eyewitness testimony due to the Defendant’s death. Applying the balancing test set forth in *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001), the trial court properly weighed the severity of the violations, the willfulness of the conduct, and the irreparable prejudice to the Plaintiff. The court did not abuse its discretion in determining that lesser sanctions were inadequate to address the harm caused by the Defendant’s noncompliance.

Accordingly, the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.

**BY THE COURT,**

/s/ Papalas, J.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO ADEQUATELY CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS

The plaintiff asserts that the trial court properly considered lesser sanctions and found them inadequate. However, the record does not support this conclusion. However, this argument conflates two separate concepts: (1) whether the defendant violated discovery rules, and (2) whether the violation warranted the extreme sanction of default judgment.

The defense acknowledges that the defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions. However, the appropriate remedy for such violations need not be the termination of the litigation. Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019 provides a range of sanctions, including:

- Monetary penalties
- An order that the matters embraced in the discovery order or other court orders be taken as established
- An order prohibiting the offending party from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses
- An order striking out pleadings or parts thereof
- An order staying proceedings until compliance is achieved
- An order dismissing the action or any part thereof
- An order rendering a judgment by default against the offending party

The plaintiff cites *D'Elia v. C.R. Laurence Co.*, 564 Pa. 524, 769 A.2d 505 (Pa. 2001), for the proposition that default judgment is appropriate when the violation results in the irreversible loss of critical evidence. The plaintiff cannot retroactively justify the default judgment by pointing to the defendant's death in January 2012. **PAPALAS, J.**

**OPINION**

This matter returns before the Court upon consideration of the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, and the Defense, Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer. The Appellant challenges the trial court’s entry of default judgment as a sanction for discovery violations, arguing that the court failed to adequately consider lesser sanctions, that the Defendant’s conduct was not willful, and that the Plaintiff failed to exhaust all legal remedies. After a thorough review of the arguments and the record, we hold that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering the default judgment.

**I. Standard of Review**

As previously stated, the authority to impose sanctions for discovery violations rests within the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court may not reverse such a decision absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001). In determining whether the extreme sanction of default judgment is appropriate, a trial court must engage in a balancing test considering: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing that prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded. *Pappas*, 564 Pa. at 475.

**II. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Finding Lesser Sanctions Inadequate**

The Appellant argues that the trial court failed to explicitly analyze lesser sanctions in its orders and that remedies such as monetary fines or adverse inference instructions would have been sufficient. We disagree.

While the trial court’s orders may not have contained a detailed itemization of every potential alternative sanction, the court’s determination that default judgment was the "most appropriate sanction" implies a finding that lesser measures were inadequate. *D'Elia v. C.R. Laurence Co.*, 564 Pa. 524, 769 A.2d 505 (Pa. 2001), instructs that sanctions must be tailored to the nature of the violation. Here, the violation resulted in the permanent unavailability of the sole eyewitness to the incident due to the Defendant’s death.

The Appellant suggests that the trial court should have considered monetary sanctions or adverse inferences. However, monetary sanctions cannot compensate the Plaintiff for the inability to prove his case. Similarly, an adverse inference jury instruction, while a potent tool, is speculative. It asks a jury to guess what the missing testimony might have been. Where, as here, the missing testimony is the *only* direct evidence regarding the Defendant’s conduct and state of mind immediately preceding the fire, an adverse inference is not a substitute for the evidence itself. The trial court reasonably concluded that the irreversible loss of this critical evidence rendered lesser sanctions futile. *Pappas*, 564 Pa. at 475.

**III. The Defendant’s Conduct Constituted Willful Non-Compliance**

The Appellant contends that the Defendant’s conduct was not willful because his counsel was unable to locate him for a significant period and because his refusal to be videotaped was a specific procedural objection rather than a blanket refusal to testify. This argument lacks merit.

The record establishes a "long uncontroverted history of defendant’s noncompliance." While the Defendant’s inability to be located may explain some delays, it does not excuse the cumulative effect of years of evasion. Crucially, once the Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant in 2009, the Defendant actively refused to comply with the noticed deposition by objecting to the videotaping. Under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4017.1, a party may be compelled to appear at a videotaped deposition. The Defendant’s unilateral refusal to be videotaped, without a court order excusing him from that requirement, constituted a willful violation of the discovery process.

Furthermore, the subsequent cancellations in late 2010 and early 2011, attributed to the Defendant being "out of town," continued the pattern of obstruction. The trial court was well within its discretion to view this sequence of events—not merely the initial inability to locate the Defendant—as evidence of willful disregard for the court’s authority and the Plaintiff’s rights. *Pappas*, 564 Pa. at 475.

**IV. The Plaintiff Suffered Irreparable Prejudice**

The Appellant argues that the Plaintiff failed to establish prejudice because the Defendant denied responsibility in an unsworn statement to police and because physical evidence established the cause of the fire. This argument misapprehends the nature of the prejudice.

The physical evidence (the unattended pot and leaking kerosene) establishes the mechanical cause of the fire but does not establish the Defendant’s negligence. To prevail in a negligence action, the Plaintiff must prove that the Defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing the harm. As the sole eyewitness, the Defendant was the only person who could testify to his actions, such as whether he turned on the stove, placed the pot there, or knew of the leaking kerosene.

The Appellant’s reliance on the Defendant’s unsworn statement to a state trooper is misplaced. An unsworn statement is not testimony; it is not subject to cross-examination, and it cannot stand as proof at trial. The loss of the Defendant’s sworn testimony left the Plaintiff unable to prove an essential element of his case. This constitutes irreparable prejudice. *D'Elia*, 564 Pa. at 505.

**V. The Plaintiff’s Diligence and Procedural Compliance**

The Appellant asserts that the Plaintiff failed to exhaust available remedies, such as seeking a bench warrant or agreeing to a non-videotaped deposition. We find these arguments unpersuasive.

The Plaintiff demonstrated sufficient diligence by hiring a private investigator to locate the Defendant after counsel’s inability to do so, and by filing multiple motions for contempt and sanctions. The Plaintiff is not required to pursue every conceivable theoretical remedy before seeking sanctions for ongoing violations. The Defendant’s refusal to be videotaped was the violation; the Plaintiff was not obligated to abandon his right to a videotaped deposition to accommodate the Defendant’s objection.

Regarding the Appellant’s claim that an evidentiary hearing was required under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019(c), the record indicates that the history of noncompliance was "uncontroverted." The facts of the missed depositions and the Defendant’s refusal to appear were not in dispute; only the reasons for those failures were contested. Where the facts of the violation are clear, a trial court may act on the motion papers without an evidentiary hearing. The trial court did not err in proceeding to judgment based on the undisputed record of noncompliance.

**VI. Conclusion**

The trial court carefully considered the "dismal record of non-compliance" spanning several years. The court’s decision to enter default judgment was not a punishment for the Defendant’s death, but a remedy for the discovery violations that, combined with the Defendant’s death, irreparably prejudiced the Plaintiff’s ability to prosecute his claim. The trial court properly applied the *Pappas* factors and determined that lesser sanctions were inadequate to cure the prejudice. We find no abuse of discretion.

Accordingly, the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.

**PAPALAS, J.**

**OPINION**

This matter returns before the Court upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, and the Defense, Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer. The Appellant challenges the trial court’s entry of default judgment as a sanction for discovery violations, reiterating arguments that the court failed to adequately consider lesser sanctions, that the Defendant’s conduct was not willful, and that the Plaintiff failed to exhaust all legal remedies. After a thorough review of the rebuttal arguments and the record, we hold that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering the default judgment.

**I. Standard of Review**

As previously stated, the authority to impose sanctions for discovery violations rests within the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court may not reverse such a decision absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. *Pappas v. Bazak*, 564 Pa. 462, 769 A.2d 475 (Pa. 2001). In determining whether the extreme sanction of default judgment is appropriate, a trial court must engage in a balancing test considering: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing that prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded. *Pappas*, 564 Pa. at 475.

**II. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Finding Lesser Sanctions Inadequate**

The Appellant argues, citing *Marshall v. J. C. Penney Co.*, 283 Pa. Super. 1, 423 A.2d 413 (Pa. Super. 1980), that the trial court failed to explicitly analyze lesser sanctions in its orders and that remedies such as monetary fines or adverse inference instructions would have been sufficient. We disagree.

While the trial court’s orders may not have contained a detailed itemization of every potential alternative sanction, the court’s determination that default judgment was the "most appropriate sanction" implies a finding that lesser measures were inadequate. *D'Elia v. C.R. Laurence Co.*, 564 Pa. 524, 769 A.2d 505 (Pa. 2001), instructs that sanctions must be tailored to the nature of the violation. Here, the violation resulted in the permanent unavailability of the sole eyewitness to the incident due to the Defendant’s death.

The Appellant suggests that the trial court should have considered monetary sanctions or adverse inferences. However, monetary sanctions cannot compensate the Plaintiff for the inability to prove his case. Similarly, an adverse inference jury instruction, while a potent tool, is speculative. It asks a jury to guess what the missing testimony might have been. Where, as here, the missing testimony is the *only* direct evidence regarding the Defendant’s conduct and state of mind immediately preceding the fire, an adverse inference is not a substitute for the evidence itself. The trial court reasonably concluded that the irreversible loss of this critical evidence rendered lesser sanctions futile. *Pappas*, 564 Pa. at 475.

**III. The Defendant’s Conduct Constituted Willful Non-Compliance**

The Appellant contends that the Defendant’s conduct was not willful because his counsel was unable to locate him for a significant period and because his refusal to be videotaped was a specific procedural objection rather than a blanket refusal to testify. This argument lacks merit.

The record establishes a "long uncontroverted history of defendant’s noncompliance." While the Defendant’s inability to be located may explain some delays, it does not excuse the cumulative effect of years of evasion. Crucially, once the Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant in 2009, the Defendant actively refused to comply with the noticed deposition by objecting to the videotaping. Under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4017.1, a party may be compelled to appear at a videotaped deposition. The Defendant’s unilateral refusal to be videotaped, without a court order excusing him from that requirement, constituted a willful violation of the discovery process.

Furthermore, the subsequent cancellations in late 2010 and early 2011, attributed to the Defendant being "out of town," continued the pattern of obstruction. The trial court was well within its discretion to view this sequence of events—not merely the initial inability to locate the Defendant—as evidence of willful disregard for the court’s authority and the Plaintiff’s rights. *Pappas*, 564 Pa. at 475.

**IV. The Plaintiff Suffered Irreparable Prejudice**

The Appellant argues that the Plaintiff failed to establish prejudice because the Defendant denied responsibility in an unsworn statement to police and because physical evidence established the cause of the fire. This argument misapprehends the nature of the prejudice.

The physical evidence (the unattended pot and leaking kerosene) establishes the mechanical cause of the fire but does not establish the Defendant’s negligence. To prevail in a negligence action, the Plaintiff must prove that the Defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing the harm. As the sole eyewitness, the Defendant was the only person who could testify to his actions, such as whether he turned on the stove, placed the pot there, or knew of the leaking kerosene.

The Appellant’s reliance on the Defendant’s unsworn statement to a state trooper is misplaced. An unsworn statement is not testimony; it is not subject to cross-examination, and it cannot stand as proof at trial. The loss of the Defendant’s sworn testimony left the Plaintiff unable to prove an essential element of his case. This constitutes irreparable prejudice. *D'Elia*, 564 Pa. at 505.

**V. The Plaintiff’s Diligence and Procedural Compliance**

The Appellant asserts that the Plaintiff failed to exhaust available remedies, such as seeking a bench warrant or agreeing to a non-videotaped deposition. We find these arguments unpersuasive.

The Plaintiff demonstrated sufficient diligence by hiring a private investigator to locate the Defendant after counsel’s inability to do so, and by filing multiple motions for contempt and sanctions. The Plaintiff is not required to pursue every conceivable theoretical remedy before seeking sanctions for ongoing violations. The Defendant’s refusal to be videotaped was the violation; the Plaintiff was not obligated to abandon his right to a videotaped deposition to accommodate the Defendant’s objection.

Regarding the Appellant’s claim that an evidentiary hearing was required under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019(c), the record indicates that the history of noncompliance was "uncontroverted." The facts of the missed depositions and the Defendant’s refusal to appear were not in dispute; only the reasons for those failures were contested. Where the facts of the violation are clear, a trial court may act on the motion papers without an evidentiary hearing. The trial court did not err in proceeding to judgment based on the undisputed record of noncompliance.

**VI. Conclusion**

The trial court carefully considered the "dismal record of non-compliance" spanning several years. The court’s decision to enter default judgment was not a punishment for the Defendant’s death, but a remedy for the discovery violations that, combined with the Defendant’s death, irreparably prejudiced the Plaintiff’s ability to prosecute his claim. The trial court properly applied the *Pappas* factors and determined that lesser sanctions were inadequate to cure the prejudice. We find no abuse of discretion.

Accordingly, the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.
