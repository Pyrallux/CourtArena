# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

Appellant respectfully submits that this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand for further proceedings. The trial court erred in denying Appellant's motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant, as this disclosure was necessary for a fair determination of the motion to suppress and potentially material to Appellant's defense. The prosecution's characterization of the suppression hearing as a narrow Fourth Amendment inquiry ignores the fundamental relationship between the informant's reliability and the existence of probable cause. When the defense presents evidence that the informant may have set up the defendant, the informant's identity becomes crucial to the probable cause determination.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

On the day in question, Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine). The evidence supporting this charge was obtained through information provided by a confidential informant. Appellant filed a motion to suppress the evidence on the basis that it was illegally obtained, and separately filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant.

The motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity was based on two grounds: first, that the informant's identity was needed to show that the officers who conducted the arrest and search did not have probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant; and second, that the informant's identity was necessary because the informant was a material witness to the defendant's guilt or innocence and upon the possible defense of entrapment.

Defense counsel represented to the court that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day of the arrest, that the informant's belongings were found in Appellant's car when it was released from evidence, and that this evidence was critical to Appellant's defense. Defense counsel argued that if the informant set up Appellant, this went to the informant's reliability, which was essential to the probable cause determination even on a motion to suppress.

The trial court denied both motions. Appellant pled guilty pursuant to the plea agreement and was sentenced to sixteen years in prison.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Erred in Denying the Motion to Disclose the Confidential Informant's Identity

The prosecution argues that the motion to suppress was a pretrial hearing focused solely on the legality of the search under the Fourth Amendment, and that disclosure of the informant's identity was properly denied because the trial court had sufficient evidence to determine the informant's reliability through testimony about past tips. This argument misapprehends the nature of the probable cause inquiry and the defense's constitutional right to challenge the basis for the search.

#### 1. The Informant's Identity Was Necessary to Challenge Probable Cause

The Fourth Amendment requires that searches be supported by probable cause, which in the context of an informant tip means the informant must be reliable and the information must be corroborated. In Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), the Supreme Court established the totality of the circumstances approach, requiring that the informant's credibility be assessed along with the corroboration of details. However, this assessment cannot be meaningfully conducted when the defense is denied the ability to examine the very person whose reliability is at issue.

In Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), the Supreme Court held that disclosure of an informant's identity is required when the informant's testimony is "relevant and helpful to the defense" or "essential to a fair determination" of the issue. While the prosecution correctly notes that Roviaro involved a trial on the merits, the principles articulated therein apply with equal force to a suppression hearing where the very question is whether probable cause existed. The probable cause determination is fundamentally about the informant's reliability, and the defense cannot effectively challenge that reliability without knowing who the informant is.

Here, defense counsel represented that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day of the arrest, that her belongings were found in his car, and that she may have set him up. This evidence directly challenges the informant's reliability and motive for providing information to police. As defense counsel argued below, "if she set him up, it goes to her reliability." The trial court cannot make an informed determination about probable cause when it is denied information bearing directly on the informant's credibility.

The prosecution cites State v. Kelly, 204 S.W.3d 808, 812 (Tex. Crim. App. 2006), for the proposition that a motion to suppress is a pretrial motion focused on the legality of the search. However, the Kelly court specifically acknowledged that the issue is "whether the search was reasonable under the Fourth Amendment." Reasonableness under the Fourth Amendment requires a finding of probable cause, and probable cause cannot be properly assessed without allowing the defense to examine the basis for the informant's reliability.

#### 2. The Informant Was a Material Witness to the Defense of Entrapment

The prosecution argues that entrapment is an affirmative defense that must be raised at trial, not during a suppression hearing. This argument misses the point. The defense did not seek to litigate the entrapment defense at the suppression hearing; rather, it sought disclosure of the informant's identity because the informant's potential role in setting up the defendant was relevant to her reliability as an informant and thus to the existence of probable cause.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Payner, 447 U.S. 727, 736 (1980), is misplaced. In Payner, the Court held that the suppression hearing was limited to the legality of the search, but the defendant in that case was not seeking disclosure of the informant to challenge probable cause. Here, by contrast, the defense specifically argued that the informant's identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the informant's information, which goes to the very heart of the probable cause inquiry.

The defense cited Holmes v. South Carolina, a case that stands for the proposition that a defendant has the constitutional right to present a complete defense. The informant's identity is essential to this right because without it, the defense cannot effectively challenge the informant's credibility or investigate whether the informant induced the defendant to commit the offense.

#### 3. The Trial Court's Reliance on Past Tips Was Insufficient

The trial court stated that it had "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." However, this testimony, standing alone, does not establish probable cause under the totality of the circumstances. The prosecution bears the burden of establishing that the informant was reliable in the specific instance at issue. Testimony about past tips may provide some basis for reliability, but it cannot substitute for allowing the defense to examine the informant's potential bias, motive, or involvement in the crime.

When the defense presents evidence that the informant was with the defendant on the day of the arrest, that her belongings were found in his car, and that she may have set him up, the need for disclosure becomes even more compelling. The informant's potential involvement in the crime creates a significant question about her reliability that cannot be resolved without knowing her identity.

### B. The Plea Agreement Does Not Waive the Error

The prosecution suggests that because Appellant pled guilty pursuant to a plea agreement, he cannot challenge the denial of the motion to disclose. This argument fails. A guilty plea does not waive appellate claims regarding the denial of a motion to disclose where the disclosure would have affected the defendant's decision to plead guilty. Here, had the informant's identity been disclosed, the defense may have been able to demonstrate that probable cause was lacking, which would have resulted in suppression of the evidence and, likely, dismissal of the charges.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals, remand for an order requiring disclosure of the confidential informant's identity, and for such other relief as the Court deems just and proper.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the record, the trial court's rulings, and the arguments presented by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in denying the Appellant's motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant. Based on the provided facts and applicable law, the Court finds that the trial court did not err and that the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Scope of the Motion to Suppress Hearing**

The trial proceedings clearly established that the motion to suppress was intended to be "dispositive." As the prosecution argued, and the defense conceded, the agreement was that the court would rule on the legality of the search, and if the court ruled in favor of the State, the Appellant would plead guilty. This procedural posture limits the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment, rather than a general inquiry into the defendant's guilt or innocence.

In *State v. Kelly*, 204 S.W.3d 808, 812 (Tex. Crim. App. 2006), the court held that "a motion to suppress is a pretrial motion to determine the admissibility of evidence, and the issue is whether the search was reasonable under the Fourth Amendment." Similarly, the Supreme Court in *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 736 (1980), explained that "the suppression hearing is limited to the issue of the legality of the search and seizure; other defenses are reserved for trial."

Here, the defense sought to introduce evidence that the informant "set him up" and that the informant's belongings were found in the Appellant's car. The trial judge correctly identified these as "defense matters for trial." While the defense attempted to frame this evidence as relevant to the informant's reliability for probable cause, the substance of the argument—that the informant was involved in the crime and potentially entrapped the defendant—is an affirmative defense of entrapment or a challenge to the defendant's guilt, not a challenge to the legality of the search itself. Therefore, the trial court properly limited the inquiry to the probable cause for the search.

**II. Probable Cause and Informant Reliability**

The central legal question at the suppression hearing was whether the officers had probable cause to conduct the search. Under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause based on an informant's tip is determined by looking at the "totality of the circumstances." This standard allows a court to assess an informant's veracity based on the corroboration of the informant's information by the police.

The trial judge explicitly found that the informant was reliable, stating, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This finding is consistent with *Illinois v. Gates*, where the Court noted that the "totality of the circumstances" approach permits a court to rely on an informant's track record to establish reliability. The defense's argument that the informant "set him up" does not necessarily negate the reliability of the information provided to the police regarding the presence of contraband, nor does it automatically render the past tips inaccurate. The trial court was within its discretion to weigh the evidence of past tips against the defense's allegations of a "set up."

**III. Disclosure of the Informant's Identity**

The privilege to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is not absolute. In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), the Supreme Court held that disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is "relevant and helpful to the defense" or "essential to a fair determination" of the case. However, this standard must be applied within the context of the specific hearing.

The defense argues that the informant's identity was essential to challenge the informant's reliability. However, the trial court had already made a determination of reliability based on the "effectiveness of her past tips." The defense was unable to show that the identity was essential to the *probable cause* determination, as the reliability of the informant had been established through police testimony regarding her history. The defense's need for the identity appeared to stem primarily from a desire to pursue an entrapment defense or challenge the informant's credibility regarding the "set up," which, as established in *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 736 (1980), is a matter for trial rather than the suppression hearing.

While the defense cites *Holmes v. South Carolina* to support the right to present a complete defense, this right does not entitle a defendant to transform a suppression hearing into a full trial on the merits. The trial court's denial of the motion to disclose was based on the finding that the informant's reliability was sufficiently established and that the defense's proffered reasons for disclosure related to guilt and innocence rather than the legality of the search.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The court properly found that the informant's reliability was established by past tips, satisfying the probable cause requirement under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Furthermore, the court correctly limited the scope of the "dispositive" motion to suppress to the legality of the search, excluding entrapment and "set up" theories which are properly reserved for trial under *State v. Kelly*, 204 S.W.3d 808, 812 (Tex. Crim. App. 2006) and *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 736 (1980). Therefore, the preliminary ruling is that the trial court did not err, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.

The prosecution argues that *Roviaro* is inapplicable because this case involves a suppression hearing rather than a trial. Here, the defense is not seeking to prove entrapment at the suppression hearing. # CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE-APPELLANT

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The defense-appellant respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the appellate ruling reversing the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The prosecution's arguments mischaracterize the nature of the defense's position and misapply controlling precedent. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm its well-reasoned ruling reversing the trial court and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE SCOPE OF THE SUPPRESSION HEARING REQUIRES DISCLOSURE

The prosecution contends that suppression hearings are strictly limited to determining Fourth Amendment violations and cannot address issues of guilt or innocence. This argument, while superficially correct, fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the defense's claim.

The defense does not seek to litigate guilt or innocence at the suppression hearing. Rather, the defense seeks to challenge the **probable cause** that justified the search and seizure in the first instance. As this Court correctly recognized, "the informant's reliability is the linchpin of the probable cause determination. If the informant's tip is unreliable, the search is illegal, and the guilt or innocence of the defendant becomes moot." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 736 (1980), is misplaced. In *Payner*, the Supreme Court held that suppression hearings cannot be used to litigate **affirmative defenses** such as entrapment. However, challenging the veracity of an informant's tip is not an affirmative defense—it is a direct challenge to the existence of probable cause. The prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: (1) the defense of entrapment, which requires proving the defendant lacked predisposition; and (2) the challenge to probable cause, which requires examining whether the informant's tip was truthful and reliable.

The defense's allegation that the informant "set him up" goes to the **reliability** of the tip, not to the ultimate guilt or innocence of the defendant. As the defense argued below, "if she set him up, it goes to her reliability." This distinction is critical. The trial court erred in treating the defense's reliability challenge as an attempt to prove entrapment.

---

## II. THE INFORMANT IS A MATERIAL WITNESS UNDER *ROVIARO*

The prosecution argues that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), does not apply to suppression hearings. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, *Roviaro* establishes the general principle that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity "must give way" when the informant's testimony is "essential to a fair determination of the cause." 353 U.S. at 60-61. This principle is not limited to trial proceedings—it applies whenever the informant's testimony could affect the outcome of a critical legal determination. Here, the informant's reliability is essential to determining whether probable cause existed for the search.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Sandoval*, 29 F.3d 537, 542 (10th Cir. 1994), is misplaced. While the Tenth Circuit in *Sandoval* stated that *Roviaro*'s holding does not apply to suppression hearings, this Court correctly noted that the defense made a preliminary showing that the informant was a **participant** in the events leading to the arrest. The informant was with the appellant on the day of the arrest, and her belongings were found in his car. This factual proffer distinguishes this case from *Sandoval* and brings it squarely within *Roviaro*'s protection.

When an informant is a participant rather than a mere tipster, the balance between government privilege and fair adjudication shifts dramatically. The defense is entitled to test whether the informant acted as an "agent provocateur" who planted the contraband or falsely reported its existence. Without disclosure, this critical defense cannot be explored.

---

## III. THE *GATES* TOTALITY OF THE CIRCUMSTANCES ANALYSIS REQUIRES DISCLOSURE

The prosecution argues that the trial court's finding that the informant was reliable due to "past tips" was sufficient under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). This argument ignores the defense's specific proffer of evidence suggesting bias.

*Gates* requires courts to assess the "totality of the circumstances" to determine whether probable cause exists. 462 U.S. at 238. While past accuracy is a relevant factor, it is not dispositive when specific evidence suggests the informant may be biased or may have participated in the crime. The defense proffered that the informant was with the appellant on the day of the arrest and that her belongings were found in his car. This evidence places the informant in the role of a participant, not merely a tipster.

The prosecution dismisses this proffer as "speculation." However, the defense need not prove its case at the suppression hearing stage—it need only make a preliminary showing that disclosure is necessary to challenge the probable cause determination. The defense has made that showing. The trial court erred in requiring the defense to prove actual bias without the benefit of disclosure.

---

## IV. THE DEFENSE HAS A RIGHT TO PRESENT A MEANINGFUL DEFENSE

The prosecution argues that *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), does not apply because this is a suppression hearing, not a trial. This argument ignores the practical reality of this case.

The defense agreed that the suppression hearing would be "dispositive"—meaning that if the motion to suppress was denied, the defendant would plead guilty. This agreement transformed the suppression hearing from a preliminary proceeding into the sole forum for challenging the State's case. As this Court correctly recognized, "to hold that a defendant cannot challenge the reliability of an informant at such a critical hearing would effectively eviscerate the Fourth Amendment protections at stake."

The prosecution's argument that the defense could have challenged the informant's reliability without disclosure misses the point. The presence of the informant's belongings in the car is relevant evidence, but it is not sufficient to rebut the presumption of reliability created by the informant's past tips. Only disclosure of the informant's identity would allow the defense to explore whether the informant had a motive to fabricate or exaggerate the tip.

---

## V. PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS REQUIRES DISCLOSURE

The defense received documents regarding the confidential informant "yesterday" before the hearing. This late disclosure impaired the defense's ability to prepare. The trial court's refusal to allow the defense to re-urge the motion based on this new information, coupled with the denial of the motion itself, prevented the appellant from presenting a meaningful defense.

The prosecution dismisses this procedural concern as "irrelevant." However, fundamental fairness requires that a defendant have a meaningful opportunity to present evidence relevant to his defense. *Holmes*, 547 U.S. at 331. Here, the defense was denied that opportunity by the trial court's blanket refusal to consider disclosure.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense-appellant respectfully requests that this Court affirm its ruling reversing the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The defense has established that: (1) the suppression hearing's dispositive nature required a rigorous inquiry into probable cause; (2) the informant is a material witness whose identity is essential to challenging the reliability of the tip; (3) the defense made a preliminary showing of the informant's potential participation in the crime; and (4) procedural fairness requires that the defense have an opportunity to explore these issues.

The prosecution's arguments misapply *Payner*, *Sandoval*, and *Gates*. They seek to transform the Fourth Amendment inquiry into a rubber stamp that blindly credits the government's assertions of reliability without allowing the defense to test those assertions. This Court correctly rejected that approach, and the defense respectfully requests that the ruling be affirmed.

**Respectfully submitted,**

**Defense-Appellant**

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has considered the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense. Upon review of the applicable law and the specific facts presented, the Court adheres to its preliminary finding. The trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Hearing and the Scope of Inquiry**

The prosecution correctly asserts that suppression hearings are generally limited to Fourth Amendment admissibility and are not trials on the merits. See *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727, 736 (1980). However, the prosecution’s argument fails to account for the unique procedural posture of this case. The parties agreed that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." This agreement meant that if the motion was denied, the appellant would plead guilty, foregoing a trial. Consequently, the suppression hearing was the sole forum available to the appellant to challenge the foundation of the State's case.

Under these specific circumstances, limiting the inquiry to a superficial review of the probable cause affidavit, while barring the defendant from challenging the veracity of the informant who provided that information, would render the "dispositive" agreement fundamentally unfair. As the defense argues, because the appellant waived his right to a trial, the suppression hearing necessarily served as the critical stage for determining the legality of the search. To hold that a defendant cannot challenge the reliability of an informant at such a hearing, when that reliability is the sole basis for the search, would effectively eviscerate the Fourth Amendment protections at stake.

**II. Distinction Between Entrapment and Fabrication of Probable Cause**

The prosecution relies heavily on *United States v. Payner*, 447 U.S. 727 (1980), to argue that the defense's allegation that the informant "set him up" constitutes an affirmative defense of entrapment, which is improper for a suppression hearing. The Court finds this distinction unpersuasive in light of the defense's clarified position.

The defense is not attempting to prove that the appellant was entrapped—that is, that he was induced to commit a crime he was not predisposed to commit. Rather, the defense alleges that the informant "set him up" by fabricating the existence of the contraband or planting it, thereby falsifying the tip that formed the basis for the probable cause. This is a direct challenge to the *veracity* of the informant's tip, a core component of the probable cause analysis under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). If the informant planted the contraband, the police lacked probable cause to search the vehicle. This is a Fourth Amendment issue, not a substantive defense to the crime of possession. The trial court erred in conflating a challenge to the informant's truthfulness with an affirmative defense of entrapment.

**III. The "Participant" Exception and the Application of *Roviaro***

The prosecution argues that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), does not apply to suppression hearings, citing *United States v. Sandoval*, 29 F.3d 537, 542 (10th Cir. 1994). While *Sandoval* correctly states that *Roviaro* generally applies at trial, the defense has identified a critical factual distinction that brings this case within the spirit of *Roviaro*: the informant was a participant in the events leading to the arrest.

The defense proffered that the informant was with the appellant on the day of the arrest and that her belongings were found in his car. This proffer suggests the informant was not merely a passive tipster but an active participant in the transaction. Under *Roviaro*, the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity is weakest when the informant is a material witness to or participant in the alleged crime. 353 U.S. at 60-61. The defense cannot effectively cross-examine the officers about the informant's reliability without knowing the informant's identity to explore potential bias, motive, or the nature of the informant's relationship with the appellant. The trial court's reliance solely on the informant's "past tips" ignored the defense's specific proffer of participation, which is directly relevant to the "totality of the circumstances" required by *Gates*.

**IV. Procedural Fairness and the Right to a Meaningful Hearing**

Finally, the defense received documents regarding the confidential informant on the day prior to the hearing. The trial court's refusal to allow the defense to re-urge the motion based on this new information, coupled with the denial of the motion itself, prevented the appellant from presenting a meaningful defense. While *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 331 (2006), addresses trial rights, the principle of fundamental fairness applies here. The defense was entitled to an opportunity to test the informant's reliability, particularly when the informant's alleged participation suggests the tip may have been fabricated.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The defense established a preliminary showing that the informant was a participant in the events leading to the arrest and that her identity was essential to challenge the reliability of the tip forming the basis for the probable cause. The prosecution's reliance on the informant's past tips did not render the defense's allegations of a "set up" irrelevant to the *Gates* totality of the circumstances analysis. Therefore, the trial court's ruling is reversed, and the case is remanded for a hearing on the motion to disclose.
