COURT ARENA LOG - Case 36
FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1
Applying these standards, and based on the paltry evidence before us, defendants have not presented a compelling case of prejudice. To begin with, they cite only newspaper articles and editorials. There is no *108evidence whatsoever of prejudiced radio or television coverage or public sentiment generally. But see Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723; Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1487. Furthermore, the newspaper articles, while prone to hyperbole, are not inflammatory. Granted, some of the headlines describe the defendants of a “prostitution ring” or “sex ring.” But, the articles make an effort to point out that the defendants have not been found guilty; rather, they are alleged to have committed the crimes. In fact, most of the articles contain an attempt to reproduce testimony and arguments from court, heard in a public forum open to all. That is, they contain information that an average citizen would be privy to were he to go to court himself. Finally, the defendants reproduced only six articles, a far cry from the type of overwhelming saturation which can lead to a presumption of prejudice. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

Your Honor, the defendants have utterly failed to meet the stringent legal standard required to justify a change of venue. As the trial court correctly held, the defendants have not presented a single shred of evidence demonstrating the "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" pretrial publicity necessary to overcome the presumption of a fair trial. Their motion rests on speculative assertions and a paltry record that falls far short of the exceptional circumstances required under binding precedent. This Court should affirm the denial of their motion for change of venue, as the defendants have not carried their heavy burden of proof.

The law demands that a showing of presumed prejudice—reserved only for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings"—be proven through "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985); *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). Yet the defendants rely exclusively on six newspaper articles—none of which are inflammatory, sensationalized, or indicative of community-wide bias. As the trial court observed, these articles consistently note the defendants' presumption of innocence and merely report on public court proceedings to which any citizen could attend. This is precisely the type of routine, non-prejudicial reporting that does not constitute grounds for venue change. See *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963) (reversing conviction only where a televised confession was broadcast "several times to tens of thousands of people" in the community, creating an "atmosphere of preconceived ideas"). Here, the defendants cite no broadcast media coverage, no evidence of public sentiment beyond isolated newspaper snippets, and no proof that the articles "pervasively poisoned" the jury pool. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537 (rejecting presumed prejudice where newspaper coverage was "not so pervasive and inflammatory" as to render a fair trial impossible).

Nor can the defendants establish actual prejudice. That standard requires showing "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured," *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987), but this inquiry is premature without voir dire. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999) (actual prejudice "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire"). No juror interviews have occurred, so the defendants' claim is purely theoretical and unsupported by any factual foundation. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725–28 (1961) (requiring "careful scrutiny of the voir dire" to assess juror impartiality).

The defendants' ancillary claim of "cultural bias against foreign-born defendants" is equally meritless. They cite no evidence—statistical, testimonial, or otherwise—that systemic prejudice against Chinese or Samoan nationals exists in the community, let alone that it has tainted the jury pool. The trial court properly rejected this speculation as unsupported by the record. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (due process requires "a tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power," but bare allegations of cultural bias without proof cannot satisfy this standard). The defendants' reliance on unsubstantiated rhetoric about a "culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here" is precisely the kind of generalized, emotional appeal that courts consistently reject in venue motions. *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 802 (1975) (reversing venue change only where "the totality of the circumstances" shows "an atmosphere of prejudice so pervasive that a fair trial is impossible").

Moreover, even if prejudice were proven—a fact the defendants cannot show—a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa. As the trial court correctly noted, the Territory has a "single jury roll" of all registered voters, and "transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same." *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (confirming jury trials are "practical and not anomalous" in small island communities). The defendants' citation to *A.S.C.A. § 46.0601* is misplaced; this statute authorizes physical relocation for convenience, not a change of venue to circumvent alleged prejudice. *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (holding change of venue is "unsuitable" in territories with limited geographic and demographic scope).

In sum, the defendants have not adduced evidence of "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" publicity, nor have they demonstrated any cultural bias affecting the jury pool. Their motion is grounded in conjecture, not law. The trial court's denial was correct, consistent with *Irvin*, *Rideau*, and *Coleman*, and fully supported by the record. This Court should uphold that decision and ensure the defendants receive a fair trial before an impartial jury in the proper venue.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE HIGH COURT OF AMERICAN SAMOA

** Defendants-Appellants,

** Plaintiff-Respondent.

## DEFENSE OPENING BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF APPEAL FROM DENIAL OF MOTION FOR CHANGE OF VENUE

---

May it please the Court:

The defense respectfully submits that this Court should reverse the trial court's denial of the defendants' motions for change of venue. While the prosecution correctly articulates the legal standards governing venue changes, it misapplies those standards to the unique circumstances of this case. The defense acknowledges the heavy burden inherent in demonstrating presumed prejudice, but contends that the trial court applied an incorrectly heightened standard and failed to properly consider the cumulative effect of the publicity alongside the insular nature of the American Samoa community. Furthermore, the defense maintains that the trial court erred in its characterization of the available remedy and in its premature dismissal of the cultural bias claim without adequate consideration of the community context.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT APPLIED AN INCORRECTLY HEIGHTENED STANDARD FOR PRESUMED PREJUDICE

The prosecution correctly states that presumed prejudice requires an exceptional showing of oversaturation. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). However, both the prosecution and the trial court fail to properly account for the qualitative nature of the publicity in this case and the quantitative limitations of the American Samoa community.

The trial court emphasized that the defendants submitted only six newspaper articles and characterized this as "a far cry from the type of overwhelming saturation" required. *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at 108. This quantitative analysis, while relevant, ignores the critical fact that American Samoa possesses a dramatically smaller population and media market than the jurisdictions examined in cases like *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), and *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963). In *Rideau*, the Supreme Court reversed the conviction where the community was exposed to a televised confession broadcast "several times to tens of thousands of people." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727. The relevant metric is not merely the absolute number of articles but rather the ratio of publicity to population and the penetrative effect of that publicity within the community.

The trial court acknowledged that "our island community is small" and that "we have but one jury roll." *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at 109. Yet the court failed to integrate this acknowledged fact into its prejudice analysis. In a community where all registered voters constitute the jury pool, even limited publicity achieves a penetration rate vastly exceeding that in larger jurisdictions. The six newspaper articles, when considered against the total population of potential jurors, may well constitute the type of saturating coverage that renders the presumption of prejudice appropriate.

The trial court further noted that "there is no evidence whatsoever of prejudiced radio or television coverage or public sentiment generally." *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at 107. This observation, however, overlooks the practical reality that American Samoa has limited broadcast media outlets. The absence of electronic media coverage does not diminish the impact of print coverage in an island community where newspapers may be the primary source of information and where the small population ensures that even limited reporting reaches a substantial percentage of potential jurors.

Additionally, the trial court's finding that "the newspaper articles, while prone to hyperbole, are not inflammatory" understates the prejudicial nature of the coverage. *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at 107. The articles described the defendants as operating a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring." These terms carry inherently pejorative connotations that presuppose criminal enterprise rather than the individual acts allegedly committed. While the articles noted the defendants had "not been found guilty," this formalistic acknowledgment does not neutralize the prejudicial impact of the sensationalized terminology. The Supreme Court has recognized that inflammatory publicity need not explicitly declare guilt to prejudice a jury; the cumulative effect of repeated references to criminal activity creates an atmosphere prejudicial to the defendant. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 351 (1966).

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ITS TREATMENT OF CULTURAL BIAS

The prosecution dismisses the defendants' cultural bias claim as "unsupported" and "meritless." However, the trial court did not actually rule on the merits of this claim; rather, it failed to address it at all in any meaningful way. The court merely noted counsel's assertion and proceeded to other matters without analyzing whether the claimed bias warranted relief.

The defense contends that in a community as small and interconnected as American Samoa, the presence of cultural attitudes toward foreign-born individuals is a proper subject for judicial consideration in the venue analysis. The prosecution's argument that "no evidence" of systemic prejudice exists conflates the requirement of proof with the impossibility of proof at the pre-voir dire stage. In a small island community, prejudice may be diffuse, embedded in social attitudes rather than expressed in overt statements, and thus incapable of being documented through traditional evidentiary means.

The defense does not claim that every community member harbors prejudice against Chinese nationals or those born outside American Samoa. Rather, the defense submits that the totality of circumstances—including the nature of the publicity, the small and interconnected community, and the known presence of xenophobic sentiment in some segments of the population—creates a reasonable doubt as to whether an impartial jury can be seated. The trial court erred in requiring the defendants to prove actual community bias through statistical or testimonial evidence at this stage, rather than considering whether the circumstances raise a reasonable doubt about jury impartiality.

The prosecution cites *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 802 (1975), for the proposition that "bare allegations of cultural bias without proof cannot satisfy this standard." However, *Murphy* involved a situation where the defendant failed to demonstrate any connection between the claimed prejudice and the actual jury pool. Here, by contrast, the defendants have pointed to specific circumstances—the nature of the publicity, the small community, and the documented presence of xenophobic letters to the editor—that collectively support their claim. The trial court should have considered these circumstances together rather than dismissing each element in isolation.

## III. THE REMEDY ANALYSIS WAS FLAWED

The trial court stated that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives" and that "a change of venue is not constitutionally required." *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at 109-110. This analysis conflates the question of whether prejudice exists with the question of what remedy is appropriate.

The defense does not dispute that a change of venue may be impractical in American Samoa due to the single jury roll. However, the trial court's remedy analysis prematurely assumes that the defendants have failed to demonstrate prejudice, when in fact the court should have conducted a more thorough inquiry into the nature and extent of the publicity and its potential impact on the jury pool.

Furthermore, even if this Court agrees that a traditional change of venue is unavailable, the trial court's alternative suggestions—voir dire questionnaires and individual voir dire—do not fully address the prejudice problem. These remedies are designed to identify and exclude prejudiced jurors after they have already been exposed to prejudicial publicity. They do not prevent the initial tainting of the jury pool. In a community as small as American Samoa, potential jurors who have read the inflammatory articles may not even recognize their own prejudice, or may be reluctant to admit it during voir dire. *See Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725-28 (1961) (noting that juror assertions of impartiality may be unreliable where pre-trial publicity has been extensive).

The defense submits that if this Court finds the publicity sufficient to raise a presumption of prejudice, the appropriate remedy may include postponement of the trial until the publicity has dissipated, expanded voir dire procedures, or potentially a change of venue to the extent practicable under the circumstances. The trial court's categorical statement that "a change of venue is not constitutionally required" improperly limits the remedial options without first making a finding on the prejudice question.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON PRECEDENT IS MISPLACED

The prosecution relies heavily on *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), for the proposition that six newspaper articles cannot constitute presumptive prejudice. However, *Coleman* is distinguishable on its facts. In *Coleman*, the Eleventh Circuit found no presumed prejudice despite extensive coverage because the coverage was "not so pervasive and inflammatory" as to render a fair trial impossible. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. The court specifically noted that the coverage, while extensive, did not include the type of confession or admission that was present in *Rideau*.

This case presents a different situation. Here, the publicity includes not only routine reporting of allegations but also the publication of xenophobic letters to the editor expressing hostility toward foreign-born defendants. The trial court acknowledged that such letters represent "a very limited section of society," *Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue* at 108, but this acknowledgment does not negate the potential impact on potential jurors who share those views. The combination of sensationalized headlines describing a "prostitution ring" with the publication of explicitly prejudiced letters creates a cumulative effect more prejudicial than the mere reporting of court proceedings.

The prosecution also cites *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269 (D. Vt. 1993), for the proposition that presumed prejudice requires "oversaturation." However, *Washington* itself acknowledged that presumed prejudice can be shown "without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. The trial court in this case failed to properly assess whether the atmosphere created by the publicity—combined with the publication of biased letters and the small community context—belies any claim of impartiality.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motions for change of venue and remand for further proceedings consistent with this analysis. The defense acknowledges the difficulty of proving presumed prejudice and does not ask this Court to find prejudice based on speculation alone. However, the unique circumstances of this case—particularly the small community, the limited jury pool, the nature of the publicity, and the presence of explicitly prejudiced communications in the media—distinguish it from cases where courts have rejected venue change motions.

Alternatively, if this Court is unwilling to find presumed prejudice at this stage, the defense requests that the Court remand with instructions to conduct a more thorough voir dire process, including expanded questioning about media exposure and attitudes toward foreign-born individuals, and to reconsider the venue motion following voir dire if the responses indicate widespread prejudice.

The defendants are entitled to a trial before an impartial jury. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 722. The trial court erred in denying their motion without fully considering the circumstances that distinguish this case from those in larger jurisdictions. This Court should reverse and remand.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to** the review of the defendants' appeal from the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling based on the provided facts and the opening arguments of the prosecution and defense.

**I. Standard for Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants bear a heavy burden to demonstrate that pre-trial publicity was so pervasive and prejudicial that it presumed a tainted jury pool, rendering a fair trial impossible. The Supreme Court has held that such a showing is "rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985); see also *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963). To establish presumed prejudice, the defendants must show "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490.

**II. Analysis of Pre-Trial Publicity**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by focusing on the quantitative number of articles (six) rather than the "penetrative effect" of those articles in American Samoa's small population. While the defense correctly notes that the *ratio* of publicity to population is a relevant factor, the trial court’s finding that the coverage was not "inflammatory" is supported by the record.

The defense contends that the use of terms like "prostitution ring" is inherently pejorative and creates an atmosphere of prejudice. However, the trial court found that the articles consistently noted the defendants' presumption of innocence and reported on public court proceedings. Reporting the nature of the charges, even with colorful language, does not rise to the level of "inflammatory" publicity required to overturn a venue decision, particularly where the press is fulfilling its duty to report on public court proceedings. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (requiring a "tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power").

The defense relies heavily on *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where the Supreme Court reversed a conviction because a televised confession was broadcast to the community. Unlike *Rideau*, where the media presented the defendant's confession as fact prior to trial, the articles here reported on allegations and court proceedings. The distinction between reporting on allegations and broadcasting a confession is critical. The trial court correctly distinguished this case from *Rideau* and *Coleman*, finding the coverage "paltry" and lacking the sensationalism necessary to presume prejudice. *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993).

**III. Analysis of Cultural Bias**

The defense argues that the trial court failed to adequately address the claim of cultural bias against foreign-born defendants, asserting that xenophobic letters to the editor contribute to a prejudicial atmosphere. The trial court characterized these letters as "narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions" protected by the First Amendment but not representative of the community at large.

To establish presumed prejudice based on community bias, the defendants must show that the "totality of circumstances" indicates an "atmosphere of prejudice so pervasive that a fair trial is impossible." *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 802 (1975). The defense has not provided statistical or testimonial evidence that the jury pool is saturated with xenophobic sentiment. The existence of isolated letters to the editor, while distasteful, does not establish the pervasive community bias required to deny the defendants a fair trial. The trial court did not err in finding that the defense failed to meet its high burden of proof on this issue.

**IV. Analysis of Remedy and Change of Venue**

The defense contends that the trial court erred in its remedy analysis by conflating the finding of prejudice with the availability of a remedy. The defense argues that even if a change of venue is impractical, the court should have considered other remedies such as postponement.

The trial court correctly observed that a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in American Samoa because the Territory utilizes a single jury roll of all registered voters. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (finding jury trials practical in small island communities). Transferring the trial to a different physical location within the Territory would not change the composition of the jury pool. *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (finding change of venue unsuitable in territories with limited scope).

Because the defendants failed to demonstrate presumed prejudice, the trial court was not required to employ drastic remedial measures. The court's offer to allow a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire, including inquiries about media exposure and cultural bias, is a sufficient procedural safeguard to identify any actual prejudice that may exist. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725-28 (1961) (holding that actual prejudice is discerned through voir dire).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motions for change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate that the pre-trial publicity was so pervasive or inflammatory as to presume prejudice, nor did they establish that cultural bias has tainted the jury pool. The impracticality of a change of venue in American Samoa does not deprive the defendants of a fair trial, particularly where the court has implemented robust voir dire procedures to detect actual prejudice.

**The Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed.**

The defendants bear the "heavy burden" of demonstrating that pre-trial publicity was so pervasive and prejudicial that it presumed a tainted jury pool. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The defense argues that the trial court erred by focusing on the quantitative number of articles (six) rather than the qualitative impact of those articles in American Samoa's small population, citing *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 727 (1961), for the proposition that the "atmosphere in the community" is the relevant inquiry.

While the defense correctly identifies that the "totality of circumstances" includes community size and the penetrative effect of media, this does not lower the threshold for presumed prejudice. As the prosecution notes, the standard requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The defense's argument regarding the "ratio of publicity to population" is a valid consideration, but it cannot substitute for the requisite showing that the *content* of the publicity was inherently inflammatory. The trial court found that the articles, while prone to hyperbole, reported on public court proceedings and consistently noted the defendants' presumption of innocence. This finding aligns with the requirement that the atmosphere created must "belie any claim of impartiality." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The defense has not demonstrated that the trial court's assessment of the content's non-inflammatory nature was erroneous.

**II. Distinction from *Rideau* and the Nature of the Publicity**

The defense contends that the prosecution's distinction of *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), is unsupported because *Rideau* established the broader principle that publicity which "pervades" the proceedings can render a fair trial impossible. However, the defense overextends the holding of *Rideau*. In *Rideau*, the defendant's confession was televised multiple times to a massive audience, presenting the defendant's guilt as a foregone conclusion. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726-27.

Here, the media reported on allegations and court arguments. The defense argues that the use of terms like "prostitution ring" is inherently pejorative and creates prejudice regardless of disclaimers of innocence. While such terminology is undeniably negative, reporting the specific nature of criminal charges is a standard function of a free press. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966). The trial court determined that the articles did not amount to the "highly sensationalized" coverage required to presume prejudice. The defense's reliance on the "virtual monopoly" of media in American Samoa to amplify the effect of this coverage is noted, but a monopoly on factual reporting of court proceedings does not equate to the "carnival atmosphere" condemned in *Sheppard*. The prosecution correctly argues that the absence of a televised confession or factually inaccurate reporting distinguishes this case from the "exceptional" circumstances of *Rideau*.

**III. Cultural Bias and Community Sentiment**

The defense argues that the trial court failed to properly weigh the xenophobic letters to the editor as evidence of broader cultural tension, asserting that the "totality of circumstances" supports a finding of presumed prejudice. The prosecution counters that isolated letters, without statistical evidence of systemic bias, are insufficient to meet the *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), standard.

This Court agrees with the prosecution's assessment. To establish presumed prejudice based on community bias, the defendants must show an "atmosphere of prejudice so pervasive that a fair trial is impossible." *Murphy*, 421 U.S. at 802. The trial court characterized the letters as "narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions" representing a "very limited section of society." The defense has not provided evidence that these views are pervasive throughout the jury pool. While the defense argues that the compact social structure of American Samoa allows these views to permeate more easily, speculation about the reach of isolated letters does not constitute proof of the "pervasive community bias" required to overturn the trial court's discretion. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding that the letters were protected speech that did not taint the entire jury pool.

**IV. Sufficiency of Voir Dire and Alternative Remedies**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by relying solely on voir dire to cure prejudice, citing *Sheppard* for the proposition that jurors may be unconscious of their bias. The defense further contends that the court failed to consider alternative remedies, such as postponement, as mandated by *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987).

The defense's remedy argument is predicated on the assumption that presumed prejudice has been established. Because the trial court found no presumed prejudice, it was not obligated to employ the "strong measures" required in *Sheppard*. *Sheppard*, 384 U.S. at 363. The trial court's offer of a detailed voir dire questionnaire is the appropriate mechanism for discerning *actual* prejudice, which is the standard applicable when presumed prejudice is not found. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725-28 (1961).

Regarding *Moreno*, the defense argues that the court should have considered postponement. However, *Moreno* stands for the proposition that when a change of venue is impractical, courts must explore alternatives *if prejudice is found*. *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. Since the trial court found no prejudice, it was not required to implement alternative remedies. The prosecution correctly notes that a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in American Samoa due to the single jury roll, *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977), and postponement would not alter the composition of that roll. The trial court's decision to await the results of voir dire before considering further remedies was a proper exercise of judicial economy and discretion.

**Conclusion**

The defense's rebuttal raises thoughtful concerns regarding the unique dynamics of a small island community and the potential impact of media monopolies. However, the defense has not demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion in finding that the pre-trial publicity did not rise to the level of "presumed prejudice." The coverage, while extensive relative to the population, was factual reporting of court proceedings rather than the "highly sensationalized" coverage required to overturn a venue decision. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1490. Furthermore, the trial court's proposed voir dire procedures are sufficient to identify any actual prejudice that may exist. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 725-28.

**The Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed.**

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to** the review of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, this Court issues its final ruling regarding the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue.

**I. The Standard for Presumed Prejudice and the "Totality of Circumstances"**

The defendants bear the "heavy burden" of demonstrating that pre-trial publicity was so pervasive and prejudicial that it presumed a tainted jury pool. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The defense argues that the "totality of circumstances" in American Samoa—a small population of approximately 55,000 with a media monopoly—requires adjusting the quantitative standard for "oversaturation." Specifically, the defense contends that six newspaper articles constitute "oversaturation" when viewed as a ratio to the population size.

While the defense correctly identifies that community size is a factor in the *totality of circumstances* analysis, *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490, this consideration does not lower the qualitative threshold for presumed prejudice. The Supreme Court has reserved the finding of presumed prejudice for "exceptional cases" where the publicity creates an atmosphere that "belies any claim of impartiality." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963).

The defense's reliance on *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 727 (1961), for the proposition that the "atmosphere in the community" is dispositive, is accurate but misapplied. In *Irvin*, the Court found presumed prejudice based on a "wave of public passion" generated by a series of murders and extensive, inflammatory media coverage. Here, the trial court found that the six articles at issue, while prone to hyperbole, reported on public court proceedings and consistently noted the defendants' presumption of innocence. The defense argues that the terminology "prostitution ring" is inherently pejorative and overrides these disclaimers. However, reporting the specific nature of criminal charges is a standard function of a free press and does not equate to the "highly sensationalized" coverage required to presume prejudice. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966). The "ratio of publicity to population" argument, while creative, cannot transform factual reporting of court proceedings into the "carnival atmosphere" condemned in *Sheppard*. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the volume and nature of the coverage did not meet the high standard for presumed prejudice.

**II. Distinction from *Rideau* and the Nature of the Publicity**

The defense contends that *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), supports a finding of presumed prejudice because the publicity "pervaded" the small community. The prosecution correctly distinguishes *Rideau* on its facts. In *Rideau*, the defendant's uncorroborated confession was televised multiple times to the community, presenting the defendant's guilt as a foregone conclusion. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726-27.

The defense argues that the cumulative effect of repetitive newspaper coverage in American Samoa creates an "equivalent prejudice." This Court disagrees. The *Rideau* exception turns on the *nature* of the publicity—the broadcast of a judicially coerced confession—not merely its reach. The media coverage here consisted of reports on allegations and court arguments, not a televised confession or a judicial determination of guilt. The defense's attempt to extend *Rideau* to cover standard reporting of charges in a small community is unpersuasive. The trial court properly distinguished this case from the "exceptional" circumstances of *Rideau*.

**III. Cultural Bias and Community Sentiment**

The defense argues that the trial court failed to properly weigh the xenophobic letters to the editor as evidence of broader cultural tension, asserting that in a small community, a "limited section" of society represents a significant portion of the jury pool. The prosecution counters that isolated letters, without statistical evidence of systemic bias, are insufficient to meet the *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), standard.

This Court agrees with the prosecution. To establish presumed prejudice based on community bias, the defendants must show an "atmosphere of prejudice so pervasive that a fair trial is impossible." *Murphy*, 421 U.S. at 802. The trial court characterized the letters as "narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions" representing a "very limited section of society." The defense's argument that this "limited section" permeates the jury pool is speculative. While the defense correctly notes that requiring statistical proof of bias in a small territory is difficult, the burden remains on the defendant to show that the bias is pervasive, not merely that it *might* be. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The record does not support a finding that the views expressed in the letters are so widespread as to taint the entire jury pool. The trial court's finding that the letters were protected speech that did not render the trial fundamentally unfair was not an abuse of discretion.

**IV. Sufficiency of Voir Dire and Alternative Remedies**

The defense argues that the trial court's proposed voir dire questionnaire—limited to "yes or no" responses—is inadequate to cure the prejudice, citing *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 363 (1966), for the proposition that jurors may be unconscious of their bias. The defense further contends that the trial court erred by failing to consider alternative remedies, such as postponement, as mandated by *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987).

The defense's remedy argument is predicated on the assumption that presumed prejudice has been established. Because the trial court found no presumed prejudice, it was not obligated to employ the "strong measures" required in *Sheppard*. *Sheppard*, 384 U.S. at 363. The trial court's offer of a voir dire questionnaire is the appropriate mechanism for discerning *actual* prejudice, which is the standard applicable when presumed prejudice is not found. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 725-28 (1961).

Regarding the defense's critique of the questionnaire format, the trial court's order explicitly stated that it would "take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary." This reservation of authority to conduct individual voir dire addresses the defense's concern that "yes or no" questions are insufficient to uncover bias. *Irvin* establishes that voir dire is the primary tool for assessing juror impartiality, and the trial court's plan provides sufficient flexibility to do so.

Regarding *Moreno*, the defense argues that the court should have considered postponement. However, *Moreno* stands for the proposition that when a change of venue is impractical, courts must explore alternatives *if prejudice is found*. *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. Since the trial court found no prejudice, it was not required to implement alternative remedies. The prosecution correctly notes that a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in American Samoa due to the single jury roll, *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977), and postponement would not alter the composition of that roll. The trial court's decision to await the results of voir dire before considering further remedies was a proper exercise of judicial economy and discretion.

**Conclusion**

The defense's closing statement raises valid concerns regarding the unique dynamics of a small island community and the potential impact of media monopolies. However, the defense has not demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion in finding that the pre-trial publicity did not rise to the level of "presumed prejudice." The coverage, while extensive relative to the population, was factual reporting of court proceedings rather than the "highly sensationalized" coverage required to overturn a venue decision. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1490. Furthermore, the trial court's proposed voir dire procedures, including the possibility of individual questioning, are sufficient to identify any actual prejudice that may exist. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 725-28.

**The Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed.**
