Your Honor, members of this Court, today we address a fundamental question of California employment law: whether the plaintiff has stated a valid cause of action under the Fair Employment and Housing Act for failure to accommodate his use of marijuana. The answer is unequivocally no. The plaintiff’s claim fails as a matter of law because the California Fair Employment and Housing Act does not require employers to accommodate current illegal drug use, and marijuana remains illegal under federal law—regardless of any state medical authorization. This Court must affirm the lower courts’ rulings sustaining the demurrer, as the plaintiff’s allegations cannot support a FEHA claim when the conduct in question is prohibited by federal statute.

The plaintiff alleges he suffers from a disability and used marijuana pursuant to a physician’s recommendation under the Compassionate Use Act of 1996. However, the critical fact is that federal law explicitly prohibits marijuana possession, even for medical purposes. As the United States Supreme Court has definitively held, "marijuana is a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law," and "Congress has the authority to regulate the intrastate, noncommercial cultivation and possession of marijuana" under the Commerce Clause. *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195]. This prohibition is absolute: "federal law continues to prohibit the drug’s possession, even by medical users." *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711]. Consequently, the plaintiff’s marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" under the plain meaning of California law, which excludes such conduct from FEHA’s protections.

The Fair Employment and Housing Act explicitly does not protect individuals engaged in current illegal drug use. While the statute itself defines disability to exclude "current illegal use of drugs," this principle has been consistently applied by California courts. Most directly, *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200] established that "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." This precedent is dispositive here: the defendant’s decision to terminate the plaintiff for testing positive for THC—a chemical exclusively associated with marijuana—was lawful because marijuana is illegal under federal law. The plaintiff’s assertion that his use was "medical" does not alter this reality; federal illegality renders the use "illegal" for employment purposes under California law. As *Loder* confirms, employers have no duty to accommodate conduct that violates federal drug prohibitions, regardless of any state-level medical justification.

The plaintiff’s claim also fails because FEHA’s accommodation requirement applies only to disabilities that do not involve current illegal drug use. The plaintiff’s allegations—that his disability "does not affect his ability to do the essential functions of the job"—are irrelevant. The law does not require employers to adjust policies to permit conduct that is federally prohibited. To hold otherwise would create a direct conflict between state and federal law, undermining the uniformity of federal drug enforcement. This Court has long recognized that employers may enforce drug-free workplace policies without violating FEHA when the drug in question is illegal under federal law. *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200] makes this clear: the defendant’s refusal to grant an exemption from its drug-testing policy was not a failure to accommodate under FEHA—it was a lawful exercise of its right to enforce federal drug laws.

In sum, the plaintiff has not and cannot state a cause of action under FEHA because his marijuana use is illegal under federal law, and FEHA provides no protection for current illegal drug use. The lower courts correctly sustained the demurrer, and this Court should affirm. We urge you to uphold the rule that federal law prevails in employment contexts involving federally prohibited substances, ensuring employers may maintain safe and lawful workplaces without fear of FEHA liability. ## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plaintiff's FEHA Claims Fail as a Matter of Law Because His Marijuana Use Constitutes "Current Illegal Use of Drugs" Excluded from FEHA Protection

The central question before this Court is whether the FEHA requires an employer to accommodate an employee's use of marijuana for medical purposes, even though such use violates federal law. The answer is no.

The FEHA prohibits employment discrimination against "qualified individuals with a disability" but explicitly excludes from the definition of "disability" any "condition involving the use of illegal drugs or alcohol." The statute provides that " '[q]ualified individual with a disability' means an individual with a disability who can, with or without reasonable accommodation, perform the essential functions of the job." However, this protection does not extend to "current illegal use of drugs."

The critical fact, which plaintiff cannot escape, is that marijuana remains classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law. As the United States Supreme Court held in *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29, "Congress has the authority to regulate the intrastate, noncommercial cultivation and possession of marijuana" under the Commerce Clause, and the federal Controlled Substances Act "reflects a federal policy of drug prohibition." The Court explicitly stated that "federal law continues to prohibit the drug's possession, even by medical users." *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495.

This Court addressed the precise issue in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883, holding that "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." The *Loder* Court explicitly rejected the argument that employers must accommodate illegal drug use, finding that the FEHA's protections do not extend to such conduct.

The plaintiff's reliance on the Compassionate Use Act is misplaced. While the Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to certain California state criminal charges, it does not and cannot legalize marijuana use under federal law. As the United States Supreme Court recognized in *Raich*, state legislation authorizing medical use of marijuana "does not itself authorize the manufacture, possession, or distribution of marijuana" because such conduct remains prohibited by federal law. *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. at 29.

Therefore, plaintiff's marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" under both federal and state law, and such use is excluded from FEHA's protections. The defendant did not violate the FEHA by terminating the plaintiff for testing positive for THC.

### B. The Defendant Did Not Fail to Accommodate the Plaintiff's Disability

The plaintiff cannot invoke the accommodation provisions while simultaneously engaging in conduct—marijuana use—that constitutes a federal crime.

Second, even if the plaintiff were entitled to accommodation, the law does not require employers to accommodate illegal conduct. The plaintiff asks this Court to order employers to permit marijuana use in the workplace, despite federal prohibition. This would create an impossible situation: an employer would be forced to choose between violating federal law (by permitting marijuana possession) or violating state law (by failing to accommodate). The FEHA does not require such a choice.

The plaintiff alleges that his disability does not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of his job. However, this allegation is irrelevant to the question before this Court. The issue is not whether the plaintiff can perform his job—it is whether his marijuana use is protected conduct. It is not. As *Loder* makes clear, employers may enforce drug-free workplace policies without violating the FEHA when the drug in question is illegal under federal law.

### C. The Plaintiff's Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy Claim Fails for the Same Reasons

As this Court recognized, "the Compassionate Use Act does not require employers to tolerate or accommodate marijuana use in the workplace." The public policy underlying the Compassionate Use Act is to prevent the criminal prosecution of patients who use marijuana for medical purposes—it is not a policy that guarantees employment to medical marijuana users.

Moreover, the public policy favoring compliance with federal law outweighs any state-law policy favoring medical marijuana use. Employers cannot be required to violate federal law to accommodate employees. The plaintiff's wrongful termination claim thus fails.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The central issue presented is whether the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) (Gov. Code, § 12900 et seq.) or the public policy exception to at-will employment protects an employee from termination for using marijuana pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5), when such use remains prohibited by federal law. Based on the arguments presented and the controlling legal authority, the Court concludes that the plaintiff cannot state a cause of action for disability discrimination or wrongful termination.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a judgment entered after a demurrer is sustained without leave to amend, we conduct a de novo review of the complaint to determine whether it states a cause of action. We treat the demurrer as admitting the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations of material fact, but not its contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. *Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241]. The question is whether the facts alleged, under any reasonable intendment, constitute a cause of action.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Plaintiff’s FEHA Claim Fails Because Marijuana Use Constitutes “Current Illegal Use of Drugs”**

The plaintiff alleges that he was terminated in violation of the FEHA, which prohibits employment discrimination against qualified individuals with a disability. However, the FEHA’s protections are not absolute. Specifically, the statute excludes from the definition of disability any condition involving the “current illegal use of drugs.” The arguments presented correctly highlight that marijuana remains illegal under federal law, and this federal prohibition controls the determination of whether the drug use is “illegal” for purposes of FEHA.

The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that Congress has the authority to prohibit the possession and cultivation of marijuana under the Commerce Clause, even when such activity is authorized by state law for medical purposes. *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195]. The Court further stated that “federal law continues to prohibit the drug’s possession, even by medical users.” *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711]. Because the plaintiff’s use of marijuana is prohibited by the federal Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)), it constitutes “current illegal use of drugs.”

This Court’s precedent in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200] is directly on point. In *Loder*, we held that “an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions.” The plaintiff in *Loder* argued that his drug use was not illegal because he had a prescription, but we rejected that argument, finding that the drug’s status under federal law rendered the use illegal. Similarly, the plaintiff here argues that his use was authorized by the Compassionate Use Act. However, as established in *Raich* and *Oakland Cannabis*, state law cannot authorize what federal law forbids. Therefore, the plaintiff’s use of marijuana falls squarely within the FEHA’s exclusion for current illegal drug use. The defendant had no duty to accommodate conduct that is federally prohibited, and the termination based on the positive drug test was lawful.

**B. The Plaintiff’s Wrongful Termination Claim Fails Because No Public Policy Protects the Conduct**

The plaintiff also alleges a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. To state such a claim, a plaintiff must identify a public policy that protects his conduct and that his termination violates that policy. *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

The plaintiff relies on the public policy embodied in the Compassionate Use Act. However, the Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to criminal prosecution under state law; it does not create a fundamental public policy protecting employment for medical marijuana users. The Act does not require employers to accommodate marijuana use in the workplace, nor does it prohibit employers from maintaining drug-free policies. As argued, the public policy favoring compliance with federal drug laws outweighs any state-law policy favoring medical marijuana use in the employment context. Because the plaintiff was engaged in conduct prohibited by federal law, his termination does not violate any clear, mandatory, and fundamental public policy.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The plaintiff’s allegations, even if accepted as true, do not state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The FEHA does not protect employees engaged in current illegal drug use, and marijuana use remains illegal under federal law despite the Compassionate Use Act. Furthermore, the Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy prohibiting employers from terminating employees for such use. The lower courts correctly sustained the demurrer without leave to amend.

The judgment is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The central issue is whether the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) (Gov. Code, § 12900 et seq.) or the public policy exception to at-will employment protects an employee from termination for using marijuana pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. **II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a judgment entered after a demurrer is sustained without leave to amend, we conduct a de novo review of the complaint to determine whether it states a cause of action. We treat the demurrer as admitting the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations of material fact, but not its contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. *Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241]. The question is whether the facts alleged, under any reasonable intendment, constitute a cause of action.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Plaintiff’s FEHA Claim Fails Because Marijuana Use Constitutes “Current Illegal Use of Drugs”**

The plaintiff alleges that he was terminated in violation of the FEHA, which prohibits employment discrimination against qualified individuals with a disability. However, the FEHA’s protections are expressly limited by the exclusion of any condition involving the “current illegal use of drugs.” (Gov. Code, § 12926, subd. (m).)

The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that Congress has the authority to prohibit the possession and cultivation of marijuana under the Commerce Clause, even when such activity is authorized by state law for medical purposes. *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195]. The Court further stated that “federal law continues to prohibit the drug’s possession, even by medical users.” *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711]. Because the plaintiff’s use of marijuana is prohibited by the federal Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)), it constitutes “current illegal use of drugs.”

This Court’s precedent in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200] is directly on point. In *Loder*, we held that “an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions.” The plaintiff’s reliance on the Compassionate Use Act is unavailing because, as established in *Raich* and *Oakland Cannabis*, state law cannot authorize what federal law forbids.

In their rebuttal, Plaintiff’s counsel correctly acknowledges that the plaintiff’s use of marijuana constitutes “current illegal use of drugs” under federal law and that this exclusion removes him from the category of “qualified individuals with a disability” entitled to accommodation. Consequently, the defendant had no duty to accommodate conduct that is federally prohibited, and the termination based on the positive drug test was lawful.

**B. The Plaintiff’s Wrongful Termination Claim Fails Because No Public Policy Protects the Conduct**

Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

Because the plaintiff was engaged in conduct prohibited by federal law, his termination does not violate any clear, mandatory, and fundamental public policy.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The plaintiff’s allegations, even if accepted as true, do not state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The FEHA does not protect employees engaged in current illegal drug use, and marijuana use remains illegal under federal law despite the Compassionate Use Act. Furthermore, the Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy prohibiting employers from terminating employees for such use. # CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENDANT-APPELLEE RAGINGWIRE TELECOMMUNICATIONS, INC.

---

## INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, Members of the Panel:

Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. respectfully submits this closing statement in response to plaintiff Gary Ross's appeal of the superior court's judgment sustaining our demurrer without leave to amend. For the reasons set forth below and in our prior briefing, we submit that the plaintiff's complaint fails to state cognizable causes of action under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, and that the judgment should be affirmed.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The material facts, as alleged in the plaintiff's complaint and admitted for purposes of demurrer, establish the following:

Gary Ross suffers from a disability—chronic back pain with strain and muscle spasms resulting from injuries sustained during his service in the United States Air Force. He receives governmental disability benefits and is a qualified individual with a disability under the FEHA.

In September 1999, approximately two years before his employment with RagingWire, plaintiff began using marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation, allegedly to treat his chronic pain. This recommendation was obtained under the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5).

On September 10, 2001, RagingWire offered plaintiff a position as lead systems administrator. As a condition of employment, RagingWire required all new employees to submit to preemployment drug testing. Plaintiff was informed of this requirement before accepting the position.

Before taking the drug test on September 14, 2001, plaintiff provided the testing clinic with a copy of his physician's recommendation for marijuana use. He began employment on September 17, 2001.

Later that week, the clinic notified plaintiff that he had tested positive for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the chemical compound found in marijuana.

On September 20, 2001, RagingWire informed plaintiff he was being suspended as a result of the positive drug test. Plaintiff provided RagingWire with a copy of his physician's recommendation and explained that he used marijuana for medical purposes to treat his chronic back pain.

On September 21, 2001, RagingWire's board of directors met to discuss the matter. On September 25, 2001, RagingWire's chief executive officer informed plaintiff he was being terminated because of his marijuana use.

The complaint alleges that plaintiff's disability and marijuana use do not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of his job, and that he has performed his job satisfactorily.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The FEHA Claim Fails as a Matter of Law Because Marijuana Use Constitutes "Current Illegal Use of Drugs"

The plaintiff's first cause of action alleges that RagingWire violated the FEHA by terminating him because of his disability and by failing to accommodate his disability. This claim fails as a matter of law because the FEHA expressly excludes from its protections any individual engaged in the "current illegal use of drugs."

California Government Code section 12926, subdivision (m) defines "disability" under the FEHA, but explicitly excludes from this definition "[a]ny condition involving the current illegal use of drugs." This statutory exclusion is unambiguous and controlling.

The critical question, therefore, is whether the plaintiff's marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs" under applicable law. The answer is unequivocally yes.

#### 1. Federal Law Prohibits Marijuana Possession Regardless of State Authorization

The Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 801 et seq.) classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance, the most restrictive category reserved for drugs with no accepted medical use and high potential for abuse. Under federal law, it is unlawful to possess marijuana. 21 U.S.C. § 844(a).

The United States Supreme Court has definitively addressed the interplay between federal and state marijuana laws. In Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195], the Court held that Congress has constitutional authority under the Commerce Clause to prohibit the possession and cultivation of marijuana, even when such activity is authorized by state law for medical purposes. The Court expressly stated that "federal law continues to prohibit the drug's possession, even by medical users."

This holding was reaffirmed in United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], where the Court held that federal law supersedes state medical marijuana laws under the Supremacy Clause. The Court rejected arguments that necessity or medical necessity provided a defense to federal criminal prohibitions.

Therefore, under controlling Supreme Court precedent, the plaintiff's use of marijuana—even pursuant to a physician's recommendation under California law—constitutes a violation of federal law. It is "current illegal use of drugs" within the meaning of Government Code section 12926, subdivision (m).

#### 2. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Change This Analysis

The plaintiff relies on the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5), which was approved by California voters. This Act provides a defense to certain California state criminal charges for persons who use marijuana for medical purposes upon a physician's recommendation.

However, the Compassionate Use Act does not—and cannot—legalize marijuana use under federal law. As the Supreme Court held in Raich and Oakland Cannabis, state law authorization does not override federal prohibition. The Compassionate Use Act provides only a state-law defense to criminal prosecution; it does not transform federally prohibited conduct into lawful activity.

This Court addressed similar considerations in Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], holding that "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." The Loder court recognized that employers have legitimate interests in maintaining drug-free workplaces and that state law cannot require employers to accommodate conduct that violates federal law.

#### 3. The Plaintiff Is Not a "Qualified Individual with a Disability" Entitled to Accommodation

Under the FEHA, employers are required to provide reasonable accommodation to "qualified individuals with a disability." However, the statutory definition of "disability" expressly excludes current illegal drug use. Because the plaintiff's marijuana use constitutes current illegal drug use under federal law, he falls outside the protected category.

The plaintiff argues that his disability—chronic back pain—entitles him to accommodation, and that his marijuana use is merely the medication he chooses to treat that disability. This argument fails because the FEHA does not require employers to accommodate the use of illegal drugs as a treatment for disability. The exclusion of current illegal drug use from the disability definition applies regardless of the reason for the drug use.

Accordingly, RagingWire had no duty to accommodate the plaintiff's marijuana use, and its decision to terminate him based on a positive drug test did not violate the FEHA.

### B. The Wrongful Termination Claim Fails Because No Public Policy Protects the Plaintiff's Conduct

The plaintiff also alleges wrongful termination in violation of public policy. To state such a claim, a plaintiff must identify a "clear, mandatory, and fundamental public policy" that protects his conduct and that his termination violates that policy. Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

The plaintiff contends that the Compassionate Use Act embodies a public policy protecting medical marijuana users from employment discrimination. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the Compassionate Use Act does not contain any language addressing employment or prohibiting employers from maintaining drug-free workplaces. The Act is confined to criminal defense provisions, providing a shield against state prosecution only. It creates no rights in the employment context.

Second, even if the Compassionate Use Act could be interpreted as expressing a public policy, that policy is outweighed by the contrary federal policy prohibiting marijuana. The Supreme Court has recognized that federal drug control laws reflect a fundamental public policy of national scope. The Supremacy Clause requires that federal law prevail over conflicting state law.

Third, the public policy against illegal drug use in the workplace is well-established. In Loder, this Court recognized the legitimacy of employer drug testing policies and the right of employers to consider illegal drug use in employment decisions. This policy reflects the fundamental interest of employers and society in maintaining drug-free workplaces.

Because the plaintiff was engaged in conduct prohibited by federal law—marijuana possession and use—no public policy protects that conduct from adverse employment consequences. His termination therefore does not give rise to a wrongful termination claim.

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The plaintiff has asserted two claims: (1) disability discrimination under the FEHA, and (2) wrongful termination in violation of public policy. Both fail as a matter of law.

### A. Rebuttal of FEHA Claim

The plaintiff argues that he is entitled to protection under the FEHA because he has a disability and used marijuana on his physician's recommendation. This argument ignores the statutory exclusion for current illegal drug use.

The FEHA's exclusion is clear and unambiguous: "Any condition involving the current illegal use of drugs" is excluded from the definition of disability. The plaintiff's marijuana use, prohibited by federal law, falls squarely within this exclusion.

The plaintiff's reliance on his physician's recommendation is unavailing. A physician's recommendation under California law does not make marijuana use legal under federal law. As the Supreme Court held in Raich and Oakland Cannabis, state authorization is irrelevant to federal prohibition.

The plaintiff also argues that RagingWire should have accommodated his disability by accepting his physician's recommendation as sufficient. However, the FEHA does not require employers to accommodate conduct that violates federal law. To hold otherwise would force employers to choose between federal compliance and state-law accommodation—a choice the law does not require.

Finally, the plaintiff argues that his job performance was satisfactory and that his marijuana use did not affect his ability to work. This argument is irrelevant. The FEHA excludes current illegal drug use regardless of whether the use affects job performance. An employer may terminate an employee for illegal drug use even if the employee is otherwise performing well.

### B. Rebuttal of Wrongful Termination Claim

The plaintiff argues that his termination violates the public policy embodied in the Compassionate Use Act. This argument fails because the Act does not create a public policy protecting employment.

The Compassionate Use Act is a criminal defense statute. It provides that certain persons using marijuana for medical purposes "shall not be subject to criminal prosecution" under California law. Health & Saf. Code § 11362.5(d). The Act contains no language regarding employment, workplace drug policies, or employer obligations.

Even assuming the Act expresses a public policy favoring medical marijuana use, that policy cannot override federal law. The Supremacy Clause mandates that federal law prevails when state and federal law conflict. Because marijuana remains illegal under federal law regardless of state authorization, no California public policy can protect conduct that federal law prohibits.

The plaintiff has not identified any "clear, mandatory, and fundamental public policy" that his termination violates. His termination was based on his violation of federal drug law—not on his disability, his race, his religion, or any other protected characteristic. The termination does not give rise to a wrongful termination claim.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the superior court, which sustained the demurrer without leave to amend.

The plaintiff cannot state a cause of action under the FEHA because his marijuana use constitutes "current illegal use of drugs," which is expressly excluded from the statute's protections. The Compassionate Use Act provides only a defense to state criminal charges; it does not make marijuana use lawful under federal law or create employment rights. Under controlling Supreme Court precedent, federal law prohibits marijuana possession even for medical users, and this prohibition supersedes state law under the Supremacy Clause.

The plaintiff also cannot state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy because no public policy protects conduct that violates federal law. The Compassionate Use Act does not create a public policy regarding employment, and even if it did, that policy would be outweighed by the fundamental federal policy against illegal drug use.

RagingWire acted lawfully in requiring preemployment drug testing and in terminating the plaintiff when he tested positive for marijuana. The plaintiff's termination was based on his violation of federal law—not on any protected characteristic. The law is clear, and the judgment should be affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The central issue is whether the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) (Gov. Code, § 12900 et seq.) or the public policy exception to at-will employment protects an employee from termination for using marijuana pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5). Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, we note that Plaintiff’s counsel has expressly conceded that the plaintiff cannot state a valid cause of action. Specifically, Plaintiff acknowledges that his marijuana use constitutes “current illegal use of drugs” under federal law and that the Compassionate Use Act does not provide a defense to the employer’s decision. Based on the undisputed facts, the controlling legal authority, and the plaintiff’s concessions, we conclude that the plaintiff cannot state a cause of action for disability discrimination or wrongful termination.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a judgment entered after a demurrer is sustained without leave to amend, we conduct a de novo review of the complaint to determine whether it states a cause of action. We treat the demurrer as admitting the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations of material fact, but not its contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. *Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241]. The question is whether the facts alleged, under any reasonable intendment, constitute a cause of action.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Plaintiff’s FEHA Claim Fails Because Marijuana Use Constitutes “Current Illegal Use of Drugs”**

The plaintiff alleges that he was terminated in violation of the FEHA, which prohibits employment discrimination against qualified individuals with a disability. However, the FEHA’s protections are expressly limited by the exclusion of any condition involving the “current illegal use of drugs.” (Gov. Code, § 12926, subd. (m).)

In his closing argument, Plaintiff’s counsel concedes that the plaintiff’s use of marijuana constitutes “current illegal use of drugs” under federal law and that this exclusion removes him from the category of “qualified individuals with a disability” entitled to accommodation. This concession is well-founded. The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that Congress has the authority to prohibit the possession and cultivation of marijuana under the Commerce Clause, even when such activity is authorized by state law for medical purposes. *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195]. The Court further stated that “federal law continues to prohibit the drug’s possession, even by medical users.” *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711]. Because the plaintiff’s use of marijuana is prohibited by the federal Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)), it constitutes “current illegal use of drugs.”

This Court’s precedent in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200] is directly on point. In *Loder*, we held that “an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions.” The plaintiff’s reliance on the Compassionate Use Act is unavailing because, as established in *Raich* and *Oakland Cannabis*, state law cannot authorize what federal law forbids. Consequently, the defendant had no duty to accommodate conduct that is federally prohibited, and the termination based on the positive drug test was lawful.

**B. The Plaintiff’s Wrongful Termination Claim Fails Because No Public Policy Protects the Conduct**

The plaintiff also alleges a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. To state such a claim, a plaintiff must identify a public policy that protects his conduct and that his termination violates that policy. *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

Plaintiff’s counsel has conceded that the wrongful termination claim fails as a matter of law. We agree. The plaintiff relies on the public policy embodied in the Compassionate Use Act. However, the Act provides a defense to criminal prosecution under state law; it does not create a fundamental public policy protecting employment for medical marijuana users. The Act does not require employers to accommodate marijuana use in the workplace, nor does it prohibit employers from maintaining drug-free policies. As argued by the defense and acknowledged by Plaintiff’s counsel, the public policy favoring compliance with federal drug laws outweighs any state-law policy favoring medical marijuana use in the employment context. Because the plaintiff was engaged in conduct prohibited by federal law, his termination does not violate any clear, mandatory, and fundamental public policy.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The plaintiff’s allegations, even if accepted as true, do not state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The FEHA does not protect employees engaged in current illegal drug use, and marijuana use remains illegal under federal law despite the Compassionate Use Act. Furthermore, the Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy prohibiting employers from terminating employees for such use. The lower courts correctly sustained the demurrer without leave to amend.

The judgment is affirmed.
