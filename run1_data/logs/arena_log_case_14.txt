## I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING PL-ETHYSMOGRAPH TEST RESULTS

The prosecution's brief fails to address the central evidentiary error that formed the basis of Lalor's motion for reconsideration. The trial court erroneously admitted the results of a pl-ethysmograph test, which measures erectile response to sexual stimuli, without proper foundation regarding the test's reliability or relevance to the issues at hand.

As this Court recognized in **State v. Post**, 2005 WI 60, 281 Wis. 2d 357, 699 N.W.2d 201, the admissibility of novel scientific evidence requires a rigorous reliability analysis under the standards established in **Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals**, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S. Ct. 2786, 125 L. Ed. 2d 469 (1993). The pl-ethysmograph has been the subject of significant scientific criticism regarding its reliability and validity. Studies have questioned whether penile plethysmography reliably measures sexual arousal patterns or predicts future behavior, particularly in the context of civil commitment proceedings where the stakes are extraordinarily high.

The defense submits that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting this evidence without conducting the required **Daubert** analysis. The admission of unreliable scientific evidence undermines the fundamental fairness of the commitment proceeding and violates Lalor's right to due process under the Fourteenth Amendment.

---

## II. THE ACTUARIAL INSTRUMENTS LACK SUFFICIENT RELIABILITY FOR CIVIL COMMITMENT DETERMINATIONS

The prosecution places great weight on the actuarial instruments relied upon by the State's experts, including the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R), the Hanson (1997) RRASOR, the Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool (MnSOST), the MnSOST-Revised, and the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (V-RAG). However, the defense expert, Dr. Lynn Maskel, testified regarding the significant problems associated with these instruments, and this testimony was improperly discounted by the trial court.

The defense submits that these actuarial instruments were not validated for the purpose of predicting future sexually violent behavior in the context of civil commitment proceedings. As this Court noted in **State v. Daniel**, 2000 WI App 144, 237 Wis. 2d 561, 614 N.W.2d 862, the reliability of risk assessment instruments must be carefully scrutinized when applied to individual cases. The instruments relied upon by the State were developed for different purposes, including treatment planning and correctional classification, and were not designed to determine whether an individual meets the statutory definition of a "sexually violent person."

Moreover, the prosecution's reliance on **Kansas v. Hendricks**, 521 U.S. 346, 117 S. Ct. 2072, 138 L. Ed. 2d 501 (1997), is misplaced. While the Supreme Court in **Hendricks** upheld the constitutionality of Kansas's Sexually Violent Predator Act, the Court specifically noted that the Constitution requires that the State prove the existence of a "mental disorder" that predisposes the person to commit sexually violent acts. The mere application of actuarial instruments that have not been validated for the specific purpose of civil commitment does not satisfy this requirement.

---

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR IS A SEXUALLY VIOLENT PERSON

Under Wisconsin Statute § 980.01(7), a "sexually violent person" is defined as a person who "is suffering from a mental disorder that makes the person more likely than not to engage in acts of sexual violence." The prosecution bears the burden of proving this element beyond a reasonable doubt.

The defense submits that the State failed to meet this burden. The testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields was based primarily on actuarial data and statistical probabilities rather than on a clinical determination that Lalor specifically suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts. As this Court held in **In re Commitment of Curley**, 2001 WI App 272, 248 Wis. 2d 628, 637 N.W.2d 784, the State must present evidence sufficient to prove that the individual's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future acts of sexual violence—not merely that the individual falls within a statistical category associated with elevated risk.

The trial court's findings, as set forth in the record, indicate that Lalor was convicted of sexually violent offenses in 1984 and 1992. However, the record does not contain sufficient evidence regarding Lalor's current mental state or the specific nature of his alleged mental disorder. The State's experts relied on actuarial instruments and Lalor's criminal history, but they failed to demonstrate that Lalor currently suffers from a mental disorder that creates the requisite risk of future dangerousness.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO MAKE REQUISITE FINDINGS REGARDING MENTAL DISORDER AND LACK OF CONTROL

While the prosecution correctly notes that **Kansas v. Hendricks** held that the Constitution does not require a finding of "inability to control behavior," this Court in **Laxton v. The trial court's written decision fails to make specific findings regarding the nature of Lalor's alleged mental disorder or how that disorder predisposes him to future acts of sexual violence.

The trial court found that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that he is "dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." However, the trial court failed to identify the specific mental disorder from which Lalor suffers or explain how that disorder affects his capacity to control his behavior. This failure is particularly significant given that the State's own experts relied on actuarial instruments that measure general recidivism risk rather than clinical indicators of a specific mental disorder.

The prosecution urges this Court to apply the clear error standard to the trial court's factual findings. However, the defense submits that the trial court's errors were errors of law, not fact, and therefore warrant de novo review.

However, the Court also recognized that questions of law, including the interpretation of statutory requirements and the application of constitutional standards, are reviewed de novo. ## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's rebuttal fails to address the fundamental deficiencies in the State's case and misapplies the applicable legal standards. The defense maintains that the trial court erred in admitting the pl-ethysmograph test results without proper *Daubert* analysis, erred in relying on unreliable actuarial instruments without adequate clinical foundation, and failed to make sufficient findings linking Lalor's specific mental disorder to his future dangerousness. The prosecution's attempt to minimize these errors as harmless is unavailing where the trial court's findings rest upon the same unreliable evidentiary foundation.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Prosecution's Characterization of the Evidentiary Record Is Incomplete

The prosecution asserts that the trial court "credited the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields" and that this testimony "provided clinical opinions based on comprehensive evaluations." However, the prosecution glosses over critical limitations in this testimony that warrant reversal.

The record demonstrates that Dr. Fields relied upon multiple actuarial instruments including the Hanson (1997) RRASOR, the Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool (MnSOST/1997 version), the Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool-Revised (MnSOST-R/1998 version), and the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (V-RAG). These instruments are actuarial tools that generate statistical probabilities of reoffense based on static factors such as prior convictions and demographic characteristics. As this court recognized in *In re Commitment of Curley*, 2001 WI App 272, 248 Wis. 2d 628, 637 N.W.2d 784, statistical probabilities alone are insufficient to prove that a person is a sexually violent person; rather, there must be evidence linking the individual's specific mental disorder to future dangerousness.

The prosecution argues that Curley does not hold that actuarial data alone is insufficient, but this reading ignores the essential teaching of that case. In Curley, this court emphasized that "evidence of a mental disorder alone is insufficient" and that the State must prove "that the person's mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he or she will engage in acts of sexual violence." The prosecution cannot satisfy this requirement by presenting actuarial scores without adequate clinical explanation of how Lalor's particular mental disorder specifically predisposes him to reoffend.

### B. The Defense Expert's Testimony Was Substantive, Not Merely Critical

The prosecution dismisses Dr. Maskel's testimony as "limited to exposing problems associated with those instruments" and argues that she "did not refute the core diagnosis." This characterization understates the nature of her testimony and its implications for the State's case.

The facts establish that Dr. Maskel was appointed as a defense expert specifically to assist Lalor in challenging the reliability of the testing instruments. Her testimony exposed fundamental problems with the actuarial tools upon which Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied. If the testing instruments themselves are unreliable or invalid for the purpose of predicting future sexual violence in individual cases, then the conclusions drawn from those instruments—namely, that Lalor presents a substantial probability to reoffend—are fundamentally compromised.

The prosecution's argument that the trial court was entitled to "credit the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields over the criticisms raised by Dr. Maskel" conflates credibility with reliability. Even crediting Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields as sincere and truthful witnesses, their testimony remains premised on actuarial instruments that Dr. Maskel demonstrated were problematic. The trial court cannot credit its way to reliability where the underlying evidence is fundamentally unsound.

### C. The Pl-Ethysmograph Evidence Requires Further Analysis

The prosecution contends that any error in admitting the pl-ethysmograph test results was harmless because the trial court's written decision "explicitly states its findings were based solely on the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields" with "no reference to the pl-ethysmograph."

This argument proves too much. If the trial court did not rely upon the pl-ethysmograph results, then the question becomes why the defense was unable to exclude this evidence through proper objection. The prosecution's harmlessness argument assumes that the evidence was properly considered and then rejected, when the more likely scenario is that the evidence was admitted over objection but not explicitly discussed in the written decision.

Even assuming the trial court did not rely upon the pl-ethysmograph results, the defense is entitled to a clean appellate record. The admission of unreliable scientific evidence, even if not ultimately relied upon, undermines the integrity of the proceedings and creates the appearance of impropriety. As the Supreme Court held in *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S. Ct. 2786, 125 L. Ed. 2d 469 (1993), the trial court serves as a gatekeeper responsible for ensuring that expert testimony rests on reliable foundations.

The prosecution cites *State v. Post*, 2005 WI 60, 281 Wis. 2d 357, 699 N.W.2d 201, as irrelevant, but this citation actually supports the defense's position. In *Post*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized the importance of proper *Daubert* analysis in sexual offender cases. The prosecution cannot escape the need for such analysis by arguing that the evidence was not ultimately relied upon.

### D. The Prosecution Misconstrues the Required Mental Disorder Finding

The prosecution argues that the trial court was not required to make "explicit findings regarding Lalor's ability to control his behavior" and that the findings made "precisely track the statutory definition in Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7)."

While it is true that *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 117 S. Ct. 2072, 138 L. Ed. 2d 501 (1997), held that the Constitution does not require a finding of volitional impairment, this does not mean that any showing of mental disorder is sufficient. The statute requires that the mental disorder "create a substantial probability that he or she will engage in acts of sexual violence." This requires some explanation of the causal mechanism—how does this particular mental disorder, in this particular individual, create this substantial probability?

The trial court's finding that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that his "mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence" is conclusory. It does not identify what the mental disorder is, how it manifests in Lalor's case, or why it specifically predisposes him to sexual violence. This is insufficient under *In re Commitment of Curley*, which requires evidence linking the mental disorder to future dangerousness.

The prosecution's reliance on *Laxton v. State*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 182, 646 N.W.2d 783, is misplaced. In *Laxton*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the statute does not require a finding of "inability to control" as a constitutional minimum. However, *Laxton* does not hold that conclusory findings of mental disorder are sufficient. The statute still requires that the disorder create a substantial probability of future violence, and the trial court must explain how it reaches this conclusion.

### E. The Prosecution's Reliance on Actuarial Tools Is Misplaced

The prosecution argues that "actuarial tools may be used alongside clinical judgment in commitment proceedings" citing *State v. Daniel*, 2000 WI App 144, 237 Wis. 2d 561, 614 N.W.2d 862. However, *Daniel* does not stand for the proposition that actuarial tools alone can satisfy the State's burden.

In *Daniel*, this court held that "the use of actuarial instruments in conjunction with clinical judgment is permissible in commitment proceedings." The key phrase is "in conjunction with clinical judgment." The clinical judgment component requires the expert to explain how the individual's specific mental disorder, considered in light of the actuarial data, creates a substantial probability of future violence. Without such clinical explanation, the actuarial data alone is insufficient.

The facts of this case demonstrate that the State's experts relied heavily, if not exclusively, on actuarial instruments. Dr. Fields specifically relied on the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG—each an actuarial tool generating statistical probabilities. While the prosecution argues that these were used alongside clinical judgment, the record does not demonstrate adequate clinical analysis explaining how Lalor's specific mental disorder specifically predisposes him to reoffend.

### F. The Beyond a Reasonable Doubt Standard Was Not Satisfied

The prosecution argues that "the State met its burden of proving that Lalor is a sexually violent person under Wisconsin law." The defense maintains that the State failed to meet the beyond a reasonable doubt standard required for civil commitment.

The trial court found that Lalor had been convicted of sexually violent offenses in 1984 and 1992, that he "suffers from a mental disorder," and that his "mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." However, the trial court did not identify what mental disorder Lalor suffers from, how that disorder was diagnosed, or specifically how it predisposes him to sexual violence.

The prosecution argues that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields provided "clinical opinions based on comprehensive evaluations." However, the facts indicate that these evaluations consisted primarily of administering actuarial instruments. The prosecution has not pointed to, and the record does not contain, a specific clinical diagnosis of Lalor's mental disorder with an explanation of how that disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffense.

Given the serious liberty interests at stake in civil commitment proceedings, the beyond a reasonable doubt standard requires more than actuarial probabilities and conclusory assertions of mental disorder. The State must prove, through reliable evidence, that the individual suffers from a specific mental disorder that creates a substantial probability of future sexual violence. The State has not met this burden here.

The standard of review applicable to this appeal is well settled. Appellate review of a trial court's findings of fact in a civil commitment case is governed by the clearly erroneous standard. In *State v. Williams*, 2005 WI App 189, 286 Wis. 2d 302, 702 N.W.2d 801, the court held that "appellate review of civil commitment findings is for clear error, not de novo." This standard requires this court to defer to the trial court's credibility determinations and its weighing of the evidence. Because the trial court's findings were based on the unchallenged testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields regarding Lalor's mental disorder and risk of reoffense, and not on the pl-ethysmograph results, any error in admitting the test was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of reoffending. He argues that the experts relied too heavily on actuarial instruments rather than clinical diagnosis, and that the defense expert, Dr. Maskel, exposed fundamental problems with those instruments. The defense relies on *In re Commitment of Curley*, 2001 WI App 272, 248 Wis. 2d 628, 637 N.W.2d 784, for the proposition that statistical probabilities alone are insufficient.

Your Honor, the defense's argument fundamentally misapprehends the constitutional requirements for civil commitment under Wisconsin's Sexually Violent Person statute. They assert that the trial court erred by failing to expressly find that Lalor is "unable to control his behavior," but this claim is foreclosed by binding precedent from both the United States Supreme Court and this court. The Constitution does not demand such a finding, and the trial court's findings fully satisfied the statutory and constitutional standards.

In *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 117 S. Ct. 2072, 138 L. Ed. 2d 501 (1997), the Supreme Court squarely rejected the notion that civil commitment of sexually violent predators requires a finding of "inability to control." The Court explicitly held: "We have never required a State to prove that the person is unable to control his dangerous behavior to commit him as a sexually violent predator under civil commitment laws." This decision established that the Due Process Clause permits civil commitment where the State proves two elements: (1) the person suffers from a mental disorder, and (2) that disorder predisposes the person to engage in future acts of sexual violence. The Court emphasized that "the Constitution does not require a specific finding of volitional impairment" to satisfy substantive due process.

This court confirmed *Hendricks*'s application in *Laxton v. State*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 182, 646 N.W.2d 783. We held that "Wisconsin's Sexually Violent Person statute does not require a finding of volitional impairment to satisfy substantive due process." The statute, Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7), requires only that the person "suffers from a mental disorder that predisposes the person to engage in acts of sexual violence" and "is likely to engage in acts of sexual violence." The trial court's findings precisely tracked this statutory language: it found Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that this disorder "creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." These findings, supported by the expert testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, fully complied with both the statute and *Hendricks*.

The defense's attempt to repackage their argument as a "conclusory findings" challenge is legally irrelevant. They cannot transform a constitutional requirement that does not exist into a substantive defect. *Hendricks* and *Laxton* make clear that the Constitution imposes no duty on the trial court to articulate a finding of "inability to control." The trial court's findings were legally sufficient because they addressed the only requirements the Constitution and statute impose: the existence of a mental disorder and its causal link to future dangerousness. To require more would impose an unconstitutional burden on the State—something *Hendricks* explicitly forbids.

The State met its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person under Wisconsin law. The trial court's findings were legally adequate, and its failure to make a volitional impairment finding—because no such finding is required—did not violate Lalor's substantive due process rights. This court should affirm the commitment order.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Matter of the Commitment of Thomas Lalor

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and in support of Mr. Lalor's appeal. While the prosecution correctly cites *Kansas v. Hendricks* and *Laxton v. State* regarding the absence of a constitutional requirement for a volitional impairment finding, the defense does not rest its appeal on the absence of such a finding. Rather, the defense contends that the trial court's findings are insufficient because they fail to establish the requisite causal link between Mr. Lalor's alleged mental disorder and his predicted future dangerousness, and because the evidence presented was legally insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability of future sexual violence.

---

## I. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS ARE INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH THE REQUISITE CAUSAL LINK

The prosecution correctly notes that *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 117 S. Ct. 2072, 138 L. Ed. 2d 501 (1997), and *Laxton v. State*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 182, 646 N.W.2d 783, hold that the Constitution does not require a finding of volitional impairment. However, this does not end the inquiry. Even accepting that no specific finding of "inability to control" is required, the State still bears the burden of proving that the person "suffers from a mental disorder" that "creates a substantial probability" of future acts of sexual violence. Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7).

The trial court's written findings, as described in paragraph 9 of the facts, state only that "Lalor suffers from a mental disorder" and that "his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." These findings track the statutory language but fail to identify the specific mental disorder from which Mr. Lalor suffers or explain the mechanism by which this disorder predisposes him to sexual violence.

In *State v. Daniel*, 2000 WI App 144, 237 Wis. 2d 561, 614 N.W.2d 862, this court recognized that expert testimony regarding actuarial tools may be used in conjunction with clinical judgment. However, the court also emphasized that the evidence must establish the required link between the mental disorder and future dangerousness. The trial court's findings, which merely parrot the statutory language without identifying the specific disorder or explaining the causal mechanism, are conclusory and fail to satisfy this requirement.

The prosecution argues that the findings are supported by the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields. However, the mere existence of testimony supporting a conclusion does not cure deficient findings. The trial court was required to make explicit findings identifying the mental disorder and explaining how it creates a substantial probability of reoffense. The failure to do so renders the commitment order unsupported by adequate findings of fact, as this court held in *In re Commitment of Curley*, 2001 WI App 272, 248 Wis. 2d 628, 637 N.W.2d 784.

---

## II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT

The prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Lalor is a sexually violent person. Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). This burden requires more than mere statistical probabilities or actuarial scores.

The defense does not dispute that Mr. Lalor has prior convictions for sexual offenses. However, past conduct alone is insufficient to justify civil commitment. As this court recognized in *In re Commitment of Curley*, 2001 WI App 272, 248 Wis. 2d 628, 637 N.W.2d 784, "statistical probabilities alone are insufficient" to establish that a person suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of future dangerousness.

The prosecution relies on the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields. Dr. Moore's evaluation assigned Mr. Lalor a score of five out of six on a risk assessment, indicating, in Dr. Moore's opinion, a substantial probability to reoffend. Dr. Moore's PCL-R evaluation was consistent with the label of psychopathy. Dr. Fields concluded that Mr. Lalor's "mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act."

However, the defense expert, Dr. Maskel, testified regarding the problems associated with the testing instruments employed by Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields. While the prosecution characterizes this testimony as merely "criticizing" the instruments, Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed fundamental flaws in the actuarial tools relied upon by the State's experts. The trial court was entitled to weigh this testimony, but it could not simply disregard it.

The prosecution argues that Dr. Fields relied on actuarial data "alongside consultations and a review of Department of Corrections records." However, the record does not indicate what specific clinical findings supported her conclusion beyond the actuarial data. The prosecution cannot satisfy its burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt by relying on actuarial instruments that the defense expert testified were fundamentally flawed.

In *State v. Williams*, 2005 WI App 189, 286 Wis. 2d 302, 702 N.W.2d 801, this court held that appellate review of civil commitment findings is for clear error. However, the clear error standard does not permit this court to uphold findings that are not supported by sufficient evidence. The trial court's findings rest on the testimony of experts who relied on unreliable actuarial instruments, and the defense expert exposed the limitations of those instruments. Under these circumstances, the evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Lalor suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of future dangerousness.

---

## III. THE ADMISSION OF PL-ETHYSMOGRAPH TEST RESULTS WAS ERROR

Although the prosecution argues that any error in admitting the pl-ethysmograph test results was harmless because the trial court's written decision did not rely on them, the defense maintains that the admission of these results requires reversal.

In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S. Ct. 2786, 125 L. Ed. 2d 469 (1993), the Supreme Court established that trial courts must serve as gatekeepers for scientific evidence, ensuring that expert testimony is both relevant and reliable. In *State v. Post*, 2005 WI 60, 281 Wis. 2d 357, 699 N.W.2d 201, this court applied *Daubert* principles to expert testimony in criminal proceedings.

The trial court admitted the pl-ethysmograph test results without conducting a *Daubert* analysis. This was error. The prosecution's argument that the error was harmless because the trial court's written decision did not reference the test ignores the reality that the trial court heard the evidence and may have been influenced by it, even if not explicitly referenced in the written decision. The harmless error doctrine cannot salvage a proceeding tainted by the admission of unreliable scientific evidence.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this court reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for a new trial. The trial court's findings are insufficient because they fail to identify the specific mental disorder or explain the causal mechanism by which the disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffense. The evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Lalor suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of future sexual violence, particularly in light of the defense expert's testimony exposing the limitations of the actuarial instruments relied upon by the State's experts. Finally, the trial court erred in admitting the pl-ethysmograph test results without conducting a *Daubert* analysis.

The prosecution has not met its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Lalor is a sexually violent person under Wisconsin law. The commitment order should be reversed.

**DECISION**

The appeal of Thomas Lalor from the judgment of the circuit court committing him as a sexually violent person under Wis. Based on the provided facts and arguments, this court concludes that the trial court's decision must be affirmed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of a trial court's findings of fact in a civil commitment case is governed by the clearly erroneous standard. In *State v. Williams*, 2005 WI App 189, 286 Wis. 2d 302, 702 N.W.2d 801, the court held that "appellate review of civil commitment findings is for clear error, not de novo." This court must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations and its weighing of conflicting expert testimony. Questions of law, including the interpretation of statutory requirements and the sufficiency of findings, are subject to de novo review.

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF FINDINGS AND VOLITIONAL IMPAIRMENT**

Lalor argues that the trial court's findings are conclusory because they merely parrot the statutory language—stating that he "suffers from a mental disorder" and that this disorder "creates a substantial probability" of reoffense—without identifying the specific disorder or explaining the causal mechanism. The defense contends that this failure violates due process and renders the findings insufficient.

The State correctly responds that the Constitution does not require a finding of volitional impairment or a detailed explanation of the "mechanism" of control. In *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 117 S. Ct. 2072, 138 L. Ed. 2d 501 (1997), the Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause permits civil commitment where the State proves that the person suffers from a mental disorder and has difficulty controlling his behavior, but it does not require a finding of total "inability to control." This court applied *Hendricks* in *Laxton v. State*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 182, 646 N.W.2d 783, confirming that Wisconsin's Sexually Violent Person statute does not require a finding of volitional impairment.

The trial court's written findings tracked the language of Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7), finding that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and is "dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." While the defense argues these findings are conclusory, they are legally sufficient. The findings are supported by the underlying testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, which provided the specificity the defense seeks. Dr. Moore testified regarding Lalor's psychopathy, and Dr. Fields testified regarding his mental disorders. The trial court was not required to write a detailed opinion explaining the psychological mechanics of the disorder where the findings adopted the statutory language and were supported by the record. The defense's attempt to transform the absence of a "volitional impairment" finding into a "conclusory findings" error is unpersuasive.

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE AND ACTUARIAL INSTRUMENTS**

Lalor contends that the evidence was insufficient to prove him a sexually violent person beyond a reasonable doubt because the State's experts relied on actuarial instruments that were fundamentally flawed, as exposed by defense expert Dr. Maskel. The defense relies on *In re Commitment of Curley*, 2001 WI App 272, 248 Wis. 2d 628, 637 N.W.2d 784, for the proposition that statistical probabilities alone are insufficient.

The trial court found that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder and that this disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts. These findings are supported by the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields. Dr. Moore's evaluation indicated a substantial probability to reoffend and was consistent with psychopathy. Dr. Fields concluded that Lalor's mental disorders create a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts.

However, the record indicates that Dr. Fields did not rely solely on actuarial data. As noted in the facts, Dr. Fields relied on actuarial data alongside consultations and a review of Department of Corrections records. In *State v. Daniel*, 2000 WI App 144, 237 Wis. 2d 561, 614 N.W.2d 862, this court recognized that actuarial tools may be used in conjunction with clinical judgment. Because the experts here used clinical judgment in addition to actuarial scores, *Curley* is distinguishable.

The defense expert's testimony was limited to criticizing the testing instruments. The trial court was entitled to weigh this criticism against the clinical opinions offered by Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields. Under the clear error standard, this court must defer to the trial court's determination that the State's experts were credible and that their testimony established the required link between Lalor's mental disorder and his future dangerousness. The defense's disagreement with the weight given to the actuarial data does not render the trial court's findings clearly erroneous.

These findings are based on the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields. The written decision contains no reference to the pl-ethysmograph test.

While the defense correctly notes that trial courts serve as gatekeepers for scientific evidence, the harmless error doctrine applies where the admitted evidence does not affect the outcome of the case. Because the trial court's findings were based on the unchallenged testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields regarding Lalor's mental disorder and risk of reoffense, and not on the pl-ethysmograph results, any error in admitting the test was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.
