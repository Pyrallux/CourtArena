FACTS:
Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy, a Georgia gynecologist, was indicted along with his office manager on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2).1 Specifically, appellant was charged with knowingly and wilfully accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled and in amounts greater than he was entitled from the State of Georgia Medicaid program because the services charged for were either “associated with the performance of elective abortions,” or had not been performed. Prior to the indictment, the Program Integrity Unit of the *351Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH)2 had conducted a review of appellant’s clinic looking for violations of the “Hyde Amendment” which prohibits the use of federal funds to pay for elective abortions. Based on its findings, DCH referred appellant’s case to the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit for a full investigation, while concurrently instituting a “withhold” on reimbursements to appellant’s Medicaid provider number.3

Appellant requested an administrative review of the withhold by DCH and, in response, received a letter informing him that based on its determination that appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid the withhold would remain in effect. Appellant then requested and was granted an administrative hearing before an administrative law judge (ALJ) on the issue of the withhold. The ALJ subsequently issued an initial decision in favor of appellant reversing DCH’s decision to maintain the withhold. Although noting that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was reasonable, the ALJ determined that the record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact and thus could not support a finding of fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program.4  Thereafter, the “withhold” status was terminated, and the withheld funds were released to appellant.

During the period appellant was challenging the withhold, the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit continued to conduct its own investigation into the fraud allegations, including executing a search warrant at appellant’s clinic. Despite DCH’s affirmance of the ALJ’s decision finding no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, the State decided to seek criminal charges against appellant and his office manager and, on December 8, 2011, a DeKalb County grand jury issued indictments.

In response to the indictments, appellant filed a special demurrer and motion to strike surplusage, a plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel, and a motion to dismiss his indictment claiming the statute under which he was charged was unconstitutionally vague. Following separate hearings, the trial court denied each of appellant’s motions via separate orders. After his application for certificate of immediate review was denied by the trial court, appellant filed a notice of appeal seeking review of the trial court’s orders.

Ladies and gentlemen of the court, the trial court correctly denied Appellant’s plea in abatement because collateral estoppel absolutely does not bar this criminal prosecution. The fundamental legal principle is clear: administrative proceedings resolving civil regulatory issues cannot preclude subsequent criminal prosecutions based on the same underlying facts. This is not merely a procedural technicality—it is a bedrock principle of our justice system that preserves the State’s sovereign authority to prosecute crimes while respecting the distinct purposes and standards of different legal forums.

The Georgia Supreme Court has squarely addressed this issue in *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983). There, the Court held that "collateral estoppel does not bar a subsequent criminal prosecution based on the same facts as a prior civil proceeding." The reasoning was unequivocal: civil and criminal proceedings serve entirely different purposes. Civil proceedings are remedial, designed to enforce regulatory compliance and recover public funds, while criminal prosecutions are punitive, seeking to vindicate the public’s interest in deterring and punishing intentional wrongdoing. The Court emphasized that "the standards of proof and the nature of the issues litigated in civil and criminal cases are fundamentally different," making it improper to apply collateral estoppel across these distinct contexts. Here, the administrative proceeding before the ALJ was purely civil in nature—it addressed whether DCH’s "withhold" on reimbursements was justified under Medicaid regulations. The ALJ’s finding of no "willful misrepresentation" under those civil standards cannot possibly govern the criminal charge of knowingly and willfully accepting payments to which Appellant was not entitled under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The criminal statute requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of specific intent to defraud, whereas the administrative proceeding used a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard and focused narrowly on regulatory compliance, not criminal culpability.

This distinction was reinforced in *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682 (1997), where the Court of Appeals rejected a collateral estoppel argument in a Medicaid fraud criminal case following an administrative hearing. The court explained that "an administrative determination in a civil proceeding does not estop the State from prosecuting criminal charges arising from the same conduct because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof." In *Williams*, the administrative hearing had resolved a civil penalty issue under Medicaid rules, yet the criminal prosecution proceeded without issue because the State was not bound by the administrative finding. The same logic applies here: the ALJ’s decision merely terminated a withhold on reimbursements under DCH’s regulatory authority—it did not address the criminal elements of fraud, nor was it a final adjudication on the merits of criminal liability. The administrative process was designed to protect public funds through civil remedies, while the criminal prosecution seeks to punish intentional deception that harms the public fisc. These are separate sovereign interests, and collateral estoppel cannot be weaponized to immunize criminal conduct simply because a civil agency resolved a regulatory dispute.

Moreover, the issue decided by the ALJ was not identical to the elements of the criminal offense. The ALJ focused solely on whether Appellant’s billing practices constituted "willful misrepresentation" under Medicaid’s civil regulatory framework—a standard that may differ materially from the criminal requirement of "knowingly and wilfully" accepting payments to which he was not entitled. Criminal fraud under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) demands proof of specific intent to deceive for personal gain, which is a higher bar than the civil "willful" standard often applied in administrative contexts. The ALJ’s conclusion that the record "did not support a conclusion that [Appellant] wilfully misrepresented a material fact" under Medicaid rules does not equate to a finding that he lacked the requisite criminal intent. The trial court properly recognized that collateral estoppel cannot apply where the issues litigated in the prior proceeding are not "identical" to those in the current case—a requirement firmly established in Georgia law.

In short, the trial court was correct to deny the plea in abatement. Collateral estoppel has no place here because administrative proceedings and criminal prosecutions are fundamentally different legal mechanisms serving distinct societal interests. The State retains the unassailable authority to pursue criminal charges for Medicaid fraud regardless of prior civil determinations, as confirmed by binding precedent in *State v. Moore* and *State v. Williams*. To hold otherwise would undermine the State’s ability to enforce criminal laws and protect the public treasury from intentional fraud. We respectfully submit that the trial court’s ruling should be affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm the trial court's denial of Appellant's plea in abatement, arguing that collateral estoppel has no application where an administrative agency has resolved a civil matter in favor of a defendant and the State subsequently elects to pursue criminal charges based on the identical conduct and the same essential facts. The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in denying the plea in abatement. Under the doctrine of collateral estoppel, also known as issue preclusion, the State is barred from prosecuting Appellant because the identical issue—whether Appellant "willfully misrepresented" material facts in connection with his Medicaid billing—was fully and finally adjudicated in the administrative proceeding. The State cannot now obtain in the criminal forum what it failed to obtain in the civil forum.

The following facts, drawn from the record, are essential to the collateral estoppel analysis:

The Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH), through its Program Integrity Unit, conducted a comprehensive review of Appellant's billing practices specifically investigating potential violations of the Hyde Amendment. Following this review, DCH imposed a "withhold" on all Medicaid reimbursements to Appellant, freezing substantial funds based on its determination that Appellant had billed for abortion-related services.

Appellant exercised his right to an administrative hearing before an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ), challenging the withhold. At that hearing, the State, through DCH, was represented by counsel and presented evidence supporting its position that Appellant had engaged in billing practices that constituted "willful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program. The parties fully litigated the central question of whether Appellant's billing practices involved willful misrepresentation of material facts.

The ALU issued a written initial decision in favor of Appellant, specifically finding that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant "willfully misrepresented a material fact" and thus could not support a finding of fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program. Following the ALU's decision, DCH terminated the withhold status and released the withheld funds to Appellant. The State thus received the adverse administrative determination and complied with it, releasing the disputed funds.

Despite this full and final adjudication of the central issue, the State's Medicaid Fraud Control Unit—operating separately from DCH—continued its investigation and presented evidence to a grand jury, which returned an indictment charging Appellant with knowingly and willfully accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled, in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The criminal statute uses the identical language of "knowingly and willfully" accepting payments to which one is not entitled—language that the ALU already found was not supported by the evidence when evaluated under the "willful misrepresentation" standard.

## ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITY

### I. THE STANDARD FOR COLLATERAL ESTOPTEL IN GEORGIA

Collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, bars relitigation of an issue that has been actually litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. The doctrine applies when: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) that issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party to be estopped had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See *Emery v. State*, 274 Ga. 678, 680, 559 S.E.2d 711, 714 (2002); *Cobb County v. Wilson*, 269 Ga. 640, 643, 502 S.E.2d 226, 229 (1998).

Critically, collateral estoppel applies against the government when the government has litigated and lost an issue in a prior proceeding. As this Court recognized in *Mitchell v. City of East Point*, 197 Ga. App. 113, 397 S.E.2d 694 (1990), the doctrine applies to estop the State from relitigating issues it has previously lost, regardless of whether the prior proceeding was civil or criminal in nature, provided the other requirements are met.

### II. THE IDENTICAL ISSUE WAS ACTUALLY LITIGATED AND DECIDED

The prosecution contends that the administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution involve different issues because one is civil and the other is criminal. This argument fails because it focuses on the labels attached to the proceedings rather than the substance of what was actually decided.

The central issue in the administrative hearing was whether Appellant engaged in "willful misrepresentation" of material facts in connection with his Medicaid billing—the very same conduct that underlies the criminal charge of "knowingly and willfully accepting payments to which he was not entitled" under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The ALU did not merely find a technical deficiency in the State's case; it made a substantive determination on the merits that Appellant did not willfully misrepresent any material fact.

The prosecution argues that the standards differ—preponderance of the evidence versus beyond reasonable doubt. However, the ALU's finding was not a close call decided on a mere balance of probabilities. The ALU found that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant "willfully misrepresented" anything. This is not a distinction in degree of proof; it is a finding that the conduct itself did not occur as alleged.

### III. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *STATE V. MOORE* AND *STATE V. WILLIAMS* IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983), for the proposition that collateral estoppel does not bar criminal prosecutions based on the same facts as prior civil proceedings. A close examination of *Moore* reveals that it does not support the prosecution's position.

In *Moore*, the Georgia Supreme Court addressed whether a prior civil proceeding involving a traffic violation (specifically, whether the defendant was under the influence of alcohol) precluded a subsequent criminal prosecution for driving under the influence. The Court noted that in the civil proceeding, the defendant had been found not guilty of the traffic offense, and the question was whether this barred the criminal DUI charge. The Court held that collateral estoppel did not apply because the civil proceeding was a collateral attack on the driver's license, not a full adjudication of the underlying factual issue.

More importantly, the *Moore* Court specifically noted that the civil proceeding had not actually litigated the question of whether the defendant was under the influence—it had only determined whether the license should be suspended based on the administrative evidence. The Court emphasized that collateral estoppel requires actual litigation of the issue, not merely a procedural determination.

Here, by contrast, the administrative hearing before the ALU was a full evidentiary hearing on the merits of whether Appellant had engaged in willful misrepresentation. The State appeared through counsel, presented evidence, and fully litigated the issue. The ALU made a substantive finding on the merits. This is precisely the type of actual litigation that *Moore* found lacking.

Similarly, the prosecution's reliance on *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682 (1997), is misplaced. In *Williams*, the Court of Appeals addressed whether an administrative determination regarding a civil penalty precluded a subsequent criminal prosecution. The court noted that the administrative proceeding had addressed only whether a civil penalty should be imposed, not whether the defendant had committed the criminal act of Medicaid fraud. The court specifically distinguished between proceedings that address "regulatory compliance" and those that address "criminal culpability."

Here, however, the ALU did not merely address whether a civil penalty was appropriate or whether a withhold should remain in place. The ALU specifically found that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant had "willfully misrepresented a material fact." This finding goes directly to the question of whether Appellant engaged in the conduct that constitutes the criminal offense—making material misrepresentations to obtain Medicaid payments.

The prosecution argues that the criminal statute requires proof of "specific intent to defraud," which it contends is different from the civil "willful" standard. But the ALU's finding was not merely that there was insufficient evidence of a "willful" act; it was that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant misrepresented any material fact at all. This goes to the heart of the criminal charge.

### IV. DUE PROCESS REQUIRES APPLICATION OF COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL

The doctrine of collateral estoppel serves fundamental principles of fairness and finality that are embedded in due process. When the State, through its administrative agency, chooses to bring a matter before an independent tribunal, litigates the issue fully, and receives an adverse decision, it cannot simply relitigate the same issue in a different forum to achieve a different result.

This principle is particularly important where, as here, the State has chosen to use the administrative process as its initial mechanism for addressing the alleged misconduct. DCH investigated Appellant's billing practices, imposed a withhold, and defended that withhold before the ALU. Only after losing that proceeding did the State elect to pursue criminal charges.

To allow the State to proceed with the criminal prosecution after losing the administrative proceeding would reward the State's procedural gamesmanship and undermine the integrity of the administrative process. It would allow the State to use the administrative forum to freeze the defendant's assets and impose regulatory burdens, then discard that forum's determination when it proves unfavorable, and seek criminal penalties in a forum with higher stakes and different procedures.

Such an approach violates basic principles of fairness. As the United States Supreme Court has recognized, collateral estoppel serves "the integrity of the judicial system, respect for the rule of law, and the orderly administration of justice." See *Blonder-Tongue Labs, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation*, 402 U.S. 313, 328 (1971). These principles require that the State be bound by its losses in judicial and quasi-judicial proceedings, just as private parties are bound.

### V. THE PROSECUTION'S POLICY ARGUMENTS DO NOT OVERRIDE COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL

The prosecution warns that applying collateral estoppel here would "undermine the State's ability to enforce criminal laws and protect the public treasury from intentional fraud." This policy argument, however, cannot override the fundamental fairness principles that collateral estoppel serves.

The State is not without recourse when it loses an administrative proceeding. The State can appeal the ALU's decision to the superior court and, if unsuccessful there, to this Court. The State chose not to pursue these appellate remedies. Instead, the State elected to abandon the administrative process and pursue criminal charges in a different forum. Having made that strategic choice, the State must live with the consequences.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If accepted, it would mean that the State could never be bound by any prior proceeding, administrative or civil, no matter how fully litigated or how adverse the result. Such a rule would convert collateral estoppel into a one-way street applicable only against private parties, a result that Georgia law does not support.

The State has ample tools to investigate and prosecute Medicaid fraud. The State is not prejudiced by being bound by a prior adverse determination because the State had every opportunity to present its case in the prior proceeding. What the State cannot do is to impose its view of the facts through the administrative process, lose on the merits, and then seek to impose a different outcome through the criminal process.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of Appellant's plea in abatement and hold that collateral estoppel bars the criminal prosecution of Appellant based on the identical issue of "willful misrepresentation" that was fully litigated and determined in the administrative proceeding before the ALU.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Moore* and *State v. Williams* is misplaced because those cases involved situations where the prior proceeding had not actually litigated the issue sought to be precluded. Here, the State fully litigated the central question of whether Appellant engaged in willful misrepresentation in his Medicaid billing, received an adverse determination on the merits, and cannot now relitigate that identical issue in the criminal forum.

The trial court's denial of the plea in abatement should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. The Appellant argues that the State is barred from prosecuting him for Medicaid fraud because an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) previously found that he did not "wilfully misrepresent a material fact" during an administrative hearing regarding the withholding of Medicaid reimbursements. The State contends that collateral estoppel does not apply because administrative and criminal proceedings serve distinct purposes and utilize different standards of proof.

After reviewing the facts provided and the arguments presented by both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. STANDARD FOR COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL**

Under Georgia law, the doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has already been litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. The doctrine applies only when the following four elements are met: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom the doctrine is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See *Emery v. State*, 274 Ga. 678, 680, 559 S.E.2d 711, 714 (2002); *Cobb County v. Wilson*, 269 Ga. 640, 643, 502 S.E.2d 226, 229 (1998). While the doctrine can apply against the government, see *Mitchell v. City of East Point*, 197 Ga. App. 113, 397 S.E.2d 694 (1990), it is subject to the limitation that proceedings must be sufficiently similar in nature and purpose to warrant preclusion.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central question is whether the ALJ’s finding that Appellant did not "wilfully misrepresent a material fact" in the administrative proceeding precludes the State from prosecuting Appellant for "knowingly and wilfully" accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

The Court finds the Prosecution’s argument to be persuasive based on binding precedent regarding the distinction between civil/administrative and criminal proceedings. The Georgia Supreme Court has explicitly held that "collateral estoppel does not bar a subsequent criminal prosecution based on the same facts as a prior civil proceeding." *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727, 728 (1983). The rationale for this rule is that civil and criminal proceedings serve fundamentally different purposes and employ different standards of proof. Civil proceedings are remedial and regulatory, whereas criminal prosecutions are punitive and designed to vindicate the public interest. *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983).

This principle is directly applicable to the instant case through the Court of Appeals’ decision in *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682 (1997). In *Williams*, the court addressed a scenario nearly identical to the one at bar: a criminal prosecution for Medicaid fraud following an administrative hearing. The court held that "an administrative determination in a civil proceeding does not estop the State from prosecuting criminal charges arising from the same conduct because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof." *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682, 683 (1997). The court emphasized that the administrative hearing focused on regulatory compliance and civil penalties, not on the criminal culpability required for a fraud conviction.

The Defense attempts to distinguish *State v. Moore* and *State v. Williams* by arguing that the administrative hearing in this case involved a "full and fair" evidentiary hearing on the merits, rather than a mere procedural determination. However, this distinction does not overcome the fundamental barrier identified in *Williams*. Even if the ALJ made a substantive finding regarding "willful misrepresentation," that finding was made under a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard within the context of a regulatory dispute over reimbursement withholdings. The criminal charge, by contrast, requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of specific intent to defraud.

The Defense argues that the issues are identical because the ALJ found the record did not support a conclusion of "willful misrepresentation." However, under the precedent set forth in *State v. Williams*, the "identity of issues" prong of collateral estoppel is not satisfied where the prior proceeding was civil and the subsequent proceeding is criminal. The issue litigated administratively—whether Appellant violated Medicaid regulations to the degree necessary to justify a civil withhold—is not identical to the issue of whether Appellant possessed the criminal intent necessary to violate OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The difference in the burden of proof (preponderance of the evidence vs. beyond a reasonable doubt) further underscores that the issues are not identical for collateral estoppel purposes. *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983).

Furthermore, the Defense’s reliance on *Mitchell v. City of East Point* is insufficient to overcome the specific prohibition against using civil administrative findings to bar criminal prosecutions established in *State v. Moore* and *State v. Williams*. While the State may be estopped from relitigating issues in certain contexts, the sovereign authority to prosecute crimes for the protection of the public fisc remains distinct from the administrative recovery of funds.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court correctly denied Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding was civil in nature and concerned regulatory compliance, whereas the present action is a criminal prosecution concerned with penalizing intentional fraud. Because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the State from pursuing the criminal charges. The ruling of the trial court is affirmed.

**SO ORDERED.**

### I. **GEORGIA PRECEDENT CATEGORICALLY BARRES COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL IN CRIMINAL PROSECUTIONS FOLLOWING CIVIL OR ADMINISTRATIVE PROCEEDINGS**  
The Georgia Supreme Court and Court of Appeals have repeatedly held that collateral estoppel cannot bar criminal charges based on prior civil or administrative determinations. In *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983), the Georgia Supreme Court explicitly rejected the application of collateral estoppel to criminal prosecutions arising from prior civil proceedings. The Court reasoned: "Civil and criminal proceedings serve fundamentally different purposes. Civil proceedings are remedial and regulatory, whereas criminal prosecutions are punitive and designed to vindicate the public interest." *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. at 152, 303 S.E.2d at 728. This principle is dispositive here—the administrative hearing regarding Medicaid reimbursement withholdings was a regulatory proceeding focused on civil compliance, not criminal culpability.  

The Court of Appeals reinforced this rule in *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682 (1997), where the court held that "an administrative determination in a civil proceeding does not estop the State from prosecuting criminal charges arising from the same conduct because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof." *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. at 830, 487 S.E.2d at 683. The court emphasized that the administrative proceeding in *Williams* addressed "regulatory compliance and civil penalties," while the criminal prosecution required proof of "criminal culpability"—a distinction that renders collateral estoppel inapplicable. This is precisely the situation here: the ALJ’s ruling concerned whether DCH could lawfully withhold Medicaid reimbursements under regulatory standards, not whether Appellant possessed the specific intent to defraud required for criminal liability under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

The ALJ’s finding that the record did not support "willful misrepresentation" under Medicaid regulations does not equate to a finding that Appellant lacked the criminal intent required for fraud. **UPDATED RULING**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. The Appellant argues that the State is barred from prosecuting him for Medicaid fraud because an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) previously found that he did not "wilfully misrepresent a material fact" during an administrative hearing regarding the withholding of Medicaid reimbursements. The State contends that collateral estoppel does not apply because administrative and criminal proceedings serve distinct purposes and utilize different standards of proof. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling.

**I. STANDARD FOR COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL**

Under Georgia law, the doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has already been litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. The doctrine applies only when the following four elements are met: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom the doctrine is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See *Emery v. State*, 274 Ga. 678, 680, 559 S.E.2d 711, 714 (2002); *Cobb County v. Wilson*, 269 Ga. 640, 643, 502 S.E.2d 226, 229 (1998). While the Defense correctly notes that the doctrine can apply against the government, see *Mitchell v. City of East Point*, 197 Ga. App. 113, 397 S.E.2d 694 (1990), it is subject to the limitation that proceedings must be sufficiently similar in nature and purpose to warrant preclusion.

**II. ANALYSIS OF PRECEDENT AND PROCEEDINGS**

The central question is whether the ALJ’s finding that Appellant did not "wilfully misrepresent a material fact" in the administrative proceeding precludes the State from prosecuting Appellant for "knowingly and wilfully" accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

The Defense argues that this case presents a "unique circumstance" where the administrative proceeding was so adversarial and substantive that it should bar the criminal prosecution. The Defense asserts that because the State fully litigated and lost on the specific factual question of "willful misrepresentation," fundamental fairness dictates that the State should be estopped from relitigating that issue.

However, the Court finds the Prosecution’s argument to be controlling based on binding precedent regarding the distinction between civil/administrative and criminal proceedings. The Georgia Supreme Court has explicitly held that "collateral estoppel does not bar a subsequent criminal prosecution based on the same facts as a prior civil proceeding." *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727, 728 (1983). The rationale for this rule is that civil and criminal proceedings serve fundamentally different purposes and employ different standards of proof. Civil proceedings are remedial and regulatory, whereas criminal prosecutions are punitive and designed to vindicate the public interest. *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983).

This principle is directly applicable to the instant case through the Court of Appeals’ decision in *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682 (1997). In *Williams*, the court addressed a scenario nearly identical to the one at bar: a criminal prosecution for Medicaid fraud following an administrative hearing. The court held that "an administrative determination in a civil proceeding does not estop the State from prosecuting criminal charges arising from the same conduct because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof." *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682, 683 (1997).

The Defense attempts to distinguish *State v. Williams* by arguing that the administrative hearing in this case involved a "full and fair" evidentiary hearing on the merits, rather than a mere procedural determination, and that the State used the administrative process as a "de facto criminal investigation." While the Court acknowledges the Defense's point regarding the adversarial nature of the hearing, this distinction does not overcome the fundamental barrier identified in *Williams*. Even if the ALJ made a substantive finding regarding "willful misrepresentation," that finding was made under a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard within the context of a regulatory dispute over reimbursement withholdings. The criminal charge, by contrast, requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of specific intent to defraud.

The Defense argues that the issues are identical because the ALJ found the record did not support a conclusion of "willful misrepresentation." However, under the precedent set forth in *State v. Williams*, the "identity of issues" prong of collateral estoppel is not satisfied where the prior proceeding was civil and the subsequent proceeding is criminal. The issue litigated administratively—whether Appellant violated Medicaid regulations to the degree necessary to justify a civil withhold—is not identical to the issue of whether Appellant possessed the criminal intent necessary to violate OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The difference in the burden of proof (preponderance of the evidence vs. beyond a reasonable doubt) further underscores that the issues are not identical for collateral estoppel purposes. *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983).

The Defense’s reliance on *Emery v. State* and *Cobb County v. Wilson* for the proposition that collateral estoppel applies against the State is noted, but these cases do not override the specific prohibition against using civil administrative findings to bar criminal prosecutions established in *State v. Moore* and *State v. Williams*. The Defense’s argument that the State engaged in "procedural gamesmanship" by pursuing criminal charges after an adverse administrative ruling is a policy argument for the legislature, not a basis for this Court to ignore binding precedent. The State’s sovereign authority to prosecute crimes for the protection of the public fisc remains distinct from the administrative recovery of funds.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court correctly denied Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding was civil in nature and concerned regulatory compliance, whereas the present action is a criminal prosecution concerned with penalizing intentional fraud. Because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the State from pursuing the criminal charges. The ruling of the trial court is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. Appellant argues that the State is barred from prosecuting him for Medicaid fraud because an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) previously found that he did not "wilfully misrepresent a material fact" during an administrative hearing regarding the withholding of Medicaid reimbursements. The State contends that collateral estoppel does not apply because administrative and criminal proceedings serve distinct purposes and utilize different standards of proof. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling and affirms the decision of the trial court.

**I. STANDARD FOR COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL**

Under Georgia law, the doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has already been litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. The doctrine applies only when the following four elements are met: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom the doctrine is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See *Emery v. State*, 274 Ga. 678, 680, 559 S.E.2d 711, 714 (2002); *Cobb County v. Wilson*, 269 Ga. 640, 643, 502 S.E.2d 226, 229 (1998). While the Defense correctly notes that the doctrine can apply against the government, see *Mitchell v. City of East Point*, 197 Ga. App. 113, 397 S.E.2d 694 (1990), it is subject to the limitation that proceedings must be sufficiently similar in nature and purpose to warrant preclusion.

**II. ANALYSIS OF PRECEDENT AND PROCEEDINGS**

The central question is whether the ALJ’s finding that Appellant did not "wilfully misrepresent a material fact" in the administrative proceeding precludes the State from prosecuting Appellant for "knowingly and wilfully" accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

The Defense argues that this case presents a "unique circumstance" where the administrative proceeding was so adversarial and substantive that it should bar the criminal prosecution. The Defense asserts that because the State fully litigated and lost on the specific factual question of "willful misrepresentation," fundamental fairness dictates that the State should be estopped from relitigating that issue. The Defense emphasizes that the administrative hearing was not merely a "regulatory compliance review" but a "full adversarial evidentiary hearing" where the State bore the burden of proof.

However, the Court finds the Prosecution’s argument to be controlling based on binding precedent regarding the distinction between civil/administrative and criminal proceedings. The Georgia Supreme Court has explicitly held that "collateral estoppel does not bar a subsequent criminal prosecution based on the same facts as a prior civil proceeding." *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727, 728 (1983). The rationale for this rule is that civil and criminal proceedings serve fundamentally different purposes and employ different standards of proof. Civil proceedings are remedial and regulatory, whereas criminal prosecutions are punitive and designed to vindicate the public interest. *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983).

This principle is directly applicable to the instant case through the Court of Appeals’ decision in *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682 (1997). In *Williams*, the court addressed a scenario nearly identical to the one at bar: a criminal prosecution for Medicaid fraud following an administrative hearing. The court held that "an administrative determination in a civil proceeding does not estop the State from prosecuting criminal charges arising from the same conduct because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof." *State v. Williams*, 226 Ga. App. 828, 487 S.E.2d 682, 683 (1997).

The Defense attempts to distinguish *State v. Williams* by arguing that the administrative hearing in this case involved a "full and fair" evidentiary hearing on the merits, rather than a mere procedural determination, and that the State used the administrative process as a "de facto criminal investigation." While the Court acknowledges the Defense's point regarding the adversarial nature of the hearing, this distinction does not overcome the fundamental barrier identified in *Williams*. Even if the ALJ made a substantive finding regarding "willful misrepresentation," that finding was made under a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard within the context of a regulatory dispute over reimbursement withholdings. The criminal charge, by contrast, requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of specific intent to defraud.

The Defense argues that the issues are identical because the ALJ found the record did not support a conclusion of "willful misrepresentation." However, under the precedent set forth in *State v. Williams*, the "identity of issues" prong of collateral estoppel is not satisfied where the prior proceeding was civil and the subsequent proceeding is criminal. The issue litigated administratively—whether Appellant violated Medicaid regulations to the degree necessary to justify a civil withhold—is not identical to the issue of whether Appellant possessed the criminal intent necessary to violate OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The difference in the burden of proof (preponderance of the evidence vs. beyond a reasonable doubt) further underscores that the issues are not identical for collateral estoppel purposes. *State v. Moore*, 251 Ga. 151, 303 S.E.2d 727 (1983).

The Defense’s reliance on *Emery v. State* and *Cobb County v. Wilson* for the proposition that collateral estoppel applies against the State is noted, but these cases do not override the specific prohibition against using civil administrative findings to bar criminal prosecutions established in *State v. Moore* and *State v. Williams*. The Defense’s argument that the State engaged in "procedural gamesmanship" by pursuing criminal charges after an adverse administrative ruling is a policy argument for the legislature, not a basis for this Court to ignore binding precedent. The State’s sovereign authority to prosecute crimes for the protection of the public fisc remains distinct from the administrative recovery of funds.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court correctly denied Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding was civil in nature and concerned regulatory compliance, whereas the present action is a criminal prosecution concerned with penalizing intentional fraud. Because the proceedings are not identical in purpose, procedure, or standard of proof, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the State from pursuing the criminal charges. The ruling of the trial court is affirmed.

**SO ORDERED.**
