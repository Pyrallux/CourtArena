In this case we consider, among other issues, a question previously left open (see Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] [hereafter Richmond}) regarding the validity of a taxation scheme sought to be imposed by “cities, counties and special districts” (see Cal. At issue here is the validity of a sales tax (retail transaction and use tax) imposed on sales occurring in San Diego County (hereafter the County) for the purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities (hereafter justice facilities) for the County. We conclude the tax is invalid because it was not approved by at least two-thirds of the County’s voters, as required by section 4.

In 1987, in express recognition of the County’s need for improved courtrooms and jails, the Legislature passed an act (Gov. Under the act, the Agency was charged with adopting a tax ordinance imposing a supplemental sales tax of one-half of 1 percent throughout the County for the purpose of financing the construction of justice facilities. (Id., §§ 26267, 26271-26275.) The act provided for a county wide election held for the purpose of approving the tax ordinance by simple majority vote. (Id., §§ 26271, 26273.) The act also provided that the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax. (Id., § 26283.)

*6At an election held in June 1988, the County’s voters approved the tax ordinance by a bare (50.8 percent) majority vote. Plaintiffs, being County taxpayers, filed the present suit to challenge the validity of the tax. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 863.) As pertinent here, the complaint asserted the tax violated the supermajority vote requirements of both section 4 and Government Code sections 53720-53730 (added by Prop. 62, discussed below). Prior to trial, the tax went into operation; tax revenues have been collected and accumulated pending final decision.

The Court of Appeal disagreed and reversed the trial court’s judgment declaring the tax invalid. The appellate court further concluded that application to the Agency of a similar statutory supermajority voter approval provision in Government Code section 53722 would be improper as an attempted local tax referendum.

The Court finds in favor of the prosecution. The sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is invalid because it was not approved by the requisite two-thirds supermajority vote as mandated by Article XIII A, Section 4 of the California Constitution.

By allowing the County to circumvent the two-thirds vote requirement simply by creating a dependent entity with no property tax power, the County effectively evades the constitutional constraints placed upon its own taxing power. As the prosecution argues, this structure is the precise mechanism warned against by Justice Broussard in his *Richmond* dissent: "the majority's interpretation of section 4 will create a loophole that will enable counties and cities to circumvent the two-thirds voter approval requirement by creating special districts that lack the power to levy property taxes but have the power to levy other taxes." (*Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 218 (Broussard, J., dissenting).)

To allow the Agency to stand outside the scope of Section 4 is to allow the County to do indirectly what it cannot do directly. The Agency functions as an arm of the County for the specific purpose of levying a tax; therefore, it must be held to the same constitutional standards as the County itself.

The defense contends that because the Legislature created the Agency after the *Richmond* decision and explicitly denied it property tax power, the Legislature intended for the Agency to be exempt from Section 4. California Taxpayers Association* (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520, the *Richmond* dissent's warning regarding the exploitation of such loopholes was "prophetic." The Legislature cannot utilize statutory formalities to defeat the people's constitutional mandate. If the defense's argument were accepted, the supermajority requirement of Section 4 would be rendered a nullity whenever a local government chose to create a "financing instrumentality" to levy its taxes.

The tax imposed by the Agency is a "special tax" because it is earmarked for a specific purpose: the financing of justice facilities. Under Section 4, "Cities, counties and special districts shall not impose any special tax... except [if approved by two-thirds of the voters]." The defense's reliance on the technical absence of property tax power cannot override the clear intent of Proposition 13 to prevent the circumvention of voter approval requirements. The Agency is a subterfuge for County action and is subject to the supermajority requirements of Section 4. Consequently, the tax imposed by the Agency is invalid. The judgment of the trial court declaring the tax invalid is affirmed.

Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, which explicitly held that Section 4 applies only to entities possessing the power to levy a property tax.

In *Richmond*, the California Supreme Court addressed whether Section 4 applied to a county transportation commission that had been granted authority to impose a sales tax but no property tax authority. The Court held that Section 4 was "intended to apply only to those districts which have the power to levy a property tax" and that "the language of section 4, by its terms, applies only to special districts which have the power to levy a property tax." (*Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208.) The Court explicitly stated that "section 4 does not apply to the [commission] because it has no power to levy a property tax." (*Id.* at 209.)

The Defense acknowledges that the Court of Appeal below found the Agency to be "nothing more than an empty shell through which the Board of Supervisors of the County of San Diego can exercise its discretion." **FINAL RULING**

The Court reverses the judgment of the trial court. The sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is valid. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, Article XIII A, Section 4 of the California Constitution applies only to special districts with such property tax authority. Because the Agency does not possess this authority, the supermajority vote requirement does not apply, and the tax approved by a simple majority is constitutionally valid.

**ANALYSIS**

This case requires the Court to reconcile the policy concerns raised by the prosecution regarding the circumvention of Proposition 13 with the binding legal authority established by the California Supreme Court. After careful consideration of the closing arguments, the Court finds that the defense’s reliance on *stare decisis* and the specific holding of *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 is dispositive. The Court cannot disregard the Supreme Court’s explicit limitation of Section 4 to entities with property tax power, even if doing so creates a potential loophole that the Legislature has chosen to exploit.

**I. The Binding Precedent of *Richmond* Controls the Outcome**

The prosecution urges this Court to look beyond the majority opinion in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 and adopt the reasoning of the dissent to prevent a "loophole." However, as the defense correctly argues, the majority opinion in *Richmond* is the binding law of this state. In *Richmond*, the Supreme Court explicitly addressed the argument that Section 4 should apply to all special taxes regardless of the entity's taxing authority. The Court rejected this argument, holding that "section 4 does not apply to the [commission] because it has no power to levy a property tax." (*Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 209.)

The prosecution argues that the *Richmond* majority’s interpretation is mere "judicial gloss" that contradicts the plain text of the Constitution. While the text of Section 4 does not explicitly mention "property tax power," the Supreme Court has definitively interpreted the text to include this limitation. It is not the role of this Court to second-guess that interpretation or to effectively overrule the Supreme Court by adopting a dissenting opinion. The *Richmond* majority acknowledged the potential for circumvention but concluded that "the language of section 4 compels" the result that the provision applies only to entities with property tax power. (*Id.* at 209.) This Court is bound by that compelled conclusion.

**II. The Agency Lacks Property Tax Authority and is Exempt from Section 4**

The application of *Richmond* to the present case is straightforward. The Agency was created by the Legislature under Government Code sections 26250-26285. The enabling statute explicitly states that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than" the specific sales tax authorized by the act. (Gov. Code, § 26283.) This statutory limitation places the Agency squarely within the exemption recognized in *Richmond*.

The prosecution contends that the Agency should be treated as a "special district" subject to Section 4 because it is an "empty shell" for the County. The Court of Appeal agreed with this characterization of the Agency's function. However, functional dependence does not alter the legal nature of the entity’s taxing powers. The Legislature has the constitutional authority to create local government entities and define their powers. (Cal. Const., art. XI, § 1.) By exercising this authority to create an entity with sales tax power but explicitly denying it property tax power, the Legislature has created an entity that falls outside the scope of Section 4 as defined by *Richmond*.

The prosecution relies on *People v. Kelly* (2010) 47 Cal.4th 1008 for the proposition that the Legislature cannot enact statutes contravening the Constitution. However, the defense correctly distinguishes *Kelly*. Here, the Legislature has not attempted to contravene the Constitution; rather, it has structured an agency to fit within the constitutional boundaries interpreted by the Supreme Court in *Richmond*. Because the Agency lacks property tax authority, Section 4 is not triggered, and therefore the Legislature’s authorization of a simple majority vote does not conflict with the Constitution.

**III. The "Loophole" Argument is Insufficient to Override Precedent**

The prosecution’s argument that affirming the tax would validate a "loophole" is compelling from a policy perspective. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the Agency appears to be a mechanism designed to secure voter approval for a tax that the County could not impose on itself. However, the "loophole" identified by the prosecution is not a judicial error to be corrected by this Court; it is a gap in the Constitution that the Supreme Court in *Richmond* explicitly left open.

The *Richmond* Court stated that its interpretation "may enable local governmental bodies to circumvent the two-thirds requirement" but concluded that the text of Section 4 provided no alternative. (*Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 209.) The prosecution asks this Court to ignore this holding and prioritize the dissent’s warning. This the Court cannot do. The remedy for such a loophole, if one exists, lies with the Legislature or the voters through a constitutional amendment, not with the judiciary disregarding binding precedent.

The prosecution cites *City of San Diego v. California Taxpayers Association* (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1510 to suggest that the *Richmond* dissent was "prophetic." The defense is correct that the "floodgates" scenario is speculative and, more importantly, is a consequence of the legal framework established by the Supreme Court.

**IV. The Tax is Valid Under the Simple Majority Approval**

The tax imposed by the Agency is a special tax levied for the specific purpose of financing justice facilities. Under the *Richmond* framework, because the Agency lacks property tax authority, the two-thirds supermajority requirement of Section 4 does not apply. Consequently, the tax is subject only to the approval requirements set forth by the Legislature in the enabling act. The Legislature provided for approval by a simple majority vote. (Gov. Code, §§ 26271, 26273.) The voters of San Diego County approved the tax by a 50.8 percent majority in June 1988. This approval satisfies all constitutional and statutory requirements applicable to the Agency.

The defense has demonstrated that the Agency is legally distinct from a traditional "special district" as defined by Article XIII A, Section 4 because it lacks the power to levy a property tax. The binding precedent of *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 mandates that Section 4’s supermajority requirement applies only to entities with such property tax power. While the Agency’s function as a "financing instrumentality" for the County raises policy concerns, those concerns cannot override the clear dictates of controlling precedent.
