## In the Matter of J.M.Y. v. A.F.Y. and R.R

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the interplay between Louisiana's presumption of paternity and the right of a legal father to seek biological determination of paternity when he has doubts about his biological connection to a child born during marriage. The Appellee's argument rests upon a fundamental mischaracterization of Appellant's claims and ignores the substantive due process rights at stake.

The undisputed facts establish that J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. when their son was born in 1994. Following their divorce, J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. In 2008, J.M.Y. filed proceedings seeking to establish the truth regarding biological paternity—not to shirk his support obligations, but to determine whether another individual, R.R., may bear responsibility for support obligations that J.M.Y. has alone discharged since the child's birth.

The trial court erred in granting the exceptions. This Court should reverse.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Erred in Granting the Exception of No Right of Action

The Appellee's primary argument—that J.M.Y. lacks a right of action because he never instituted disavowal proceedings—misapprehends the nature of Appellant's claims. A right of action exists when a party seeks to enforce a right granted by law. *See Smit v. Meyer*, 836 So. 2d 364, 369 (La. App. 4th Cir. 2002). The question is whether the law grants a mechanism for a legal father to seek determination of biological paternity and, upon establishing such paternity, to seek contribution or reimbursement from the biological father.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 245 provides that "[t]he child born during marriage is presumed to be the legitimate child of the husband." However, this presumption, while powerful, does not extinguish the biological truth. The Louisiana Supreme Court has recognized that the presumption of paternity serves important social purposes related to family stability, but it does not create an absolute bar to biological inquiry. *See Succession of Miller*, 731 So. 2d 977, 981 (La. 1999) (recognizing that biological truth may be established despite procedural bars in other contexts).

The Appellee's reliance on *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 512 So. 2d 1002 (La. 1987), is misplaced. In *Boudreaux*, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed the standard for disavowal proceedings under the specific statutory framework. The Court held that the presumption could be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence. However, *Boudreaux* did not address—whether expressly or implicitly—the scenario presented here: a legal father who seeks to establish biological paternity of another to determine contribution to child support.

The Appellant does not seek to disavow his legal fatherhood in the traditional sense. Rather, he seeks a determination of biological truth and, if such biology establishes R.R. as the father, contribution to support obligations. This is a distinct legal mechanism not addressed by the disavowal statute.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Granting the Exception of No Cause of Action

A cause of action exists when a party alleges facts sufficient to establish a legal claim. *See Louisiana Mobile Homes, Inc. v. City of Ruston*, 867 So. 2d 194, 199 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2004). The Appellee's argument that Article 227 creates only a duty and not a right to reimbursement misreads the substantive law.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides in pertinent part: "Fathers and mothers, by the very fact of the marriage, owe mutual aid and assistance to each other and to their children. The duty of support includes the obligation to provide for the child's needs, including food, clothing, shelter, and medical expenses."

While Article 227 imposes a duty of support on legal parents, it does not preclude a legal father from seeking contribution from a biological father when such biological paternity is established. The Appellee's argument that no legal provision permits reimbursement is incorrect.

In *Green v. Green*, 371 So. 2d 704 (La. 1979), the Louisiana Supreme Court recognized that the obligation of support may be allocated among multiple parties when circumstances warrant. Although *Green* addressed stepparent situations, its reasoning supports the principle that biological fathers may be held responsible for support when paternity is established.

The case *Jones v. Jones*, 628 So. 2d 682 (La. App. 2d Cir. 1993), relied upon by the Appellee's brief, is distinguishable. In *Jones*, the court addressed a scenario where a presumed father sought reimbursement without any pending determination of paternity. The court properly held that without disavowal, the presumed father remained the legal father. However, the instant case is distinguishable because J.M.Y. specifically seeks testing to determine whether R.R. is the biological father. If such paternity is established, the legal landscape changes fundamentally.

### C. The Constitutional Dimension

The Appellee's argument, if accepted, would create an unconstitutional situation where a man may be held legally responsible for supporting a child who is not his biological child, with no mechanism to seek contribution from the biological father. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects the right to determine one's biological lineage and to seek legal recognition of that truth. *See Michael H. v. Gerald D.*, 491 U.S. 110 (1989) (plurality opinion) (recognizing constitutional interests in biological parentage).

Louisiana law must be interpreted to permit biological truth-seeking while maintaining the stability of family relationships. The Appellant's claims, properly construed, advance this interest.

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The Appellee's brief makes several specific claims that require rebuttal:

**First**, the Appellee's claim that "Louisiana's firmly established presumption of paternity...cannot be circumvented without disavowal" overstates the holding of *Boudreaux*. The *Boudreaux* Court addressed the standard of proof required to rebut the presumption in disavowal proceedings—it did not hold that no other mechanism exists for establishing biological paternity in other legal contexts.

**Second**, the Appellee's characterization of Article 227 as creating only a duty without corresponding rights misreads the Civil Code. While Article 227 imposes the duty of support, other provisions—including Articles 229 and 231—create mechanisms for enforcement and contribution. The Appellee's narrow reading would produce absurd results, permitting a biological father to escape all responsibility simply by remaining uninvolved while a legal father bears the entire burden.

**Third**, the Appellee's reliance on the policy concern that allowing reimbursement "would create chaos in family law" is speculative and unproven. To the contrary, permitting biological fathers to be held responsible for support when paternity is established promotes fairness and accountability.

---

## IV. PRAYER FOR RELIEF

WHEREFORE, the Appellant respectfully requests that this Honorable Court:

1. Reverse the judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action;
2. Remand this matter for further proceedings to permit genetic testing to determine whether R.R. is the biological father of the child; and
3. Grant such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

The trial court erred in dismissing Appellant's claims. Louisiana law does not preclude a legal father from seeking biological determination of paternity and, upon establishing such paternity, seeking contribution from the biological father. The judgment below should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**J.M.Y. v. A.F.Y. and R.R.**
**No. 09-XXXX (La. App. 2d Cir.)**
**UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE SECOND CIRCUIT**

**DECIDED:** [Current Date]

**BEFORE:** The Appellate Court

**APPEAL:** From a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y.

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter arises from an appeal by J.M.Y., the plaintiff, following the trial court’s dismissal of his petition against A.F.Y. and R.R. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married when A.F.Y. gave birth to a son in 1994. The parties subsequently divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. In 2008, J.M.Y. filed an amended petition seeking to have R.R. submit to paternity testing and sought reimbursement from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for past and future child support payments. R.R. filed exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action, which the trial court granted. J.M.Y. appeals this dismissal.

**ANALYSIS**

On appeal, J.M.Y. argues that the trial court erred in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. He contends that he should be permitted to establish the biological paternity of R.R. and seek contribution or reimbursement for child support obligations, despite not having disavowed the child himself. We find this argument unpersuasive based on the established legal framework presented by the Appellees.

**I. The Presumption of Paternity and the Requirement of Disavowal**

Louisiana law establishes a conclusive presumption that a child born during the marriage is the legitimate child of the husband. As noted by the Appellees, the Louisiana Supreme Court held in *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 512 So. 2d 1002, 1004 (La. 1987), that “[t]he presumption of paternity is a conclusive presumption of law, which may be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence under the strict requirements of the disavowal statute.”

The Appellant attempts to distinguish *Boudreaux* by arguing it merely sets the standard of proof for disavowal proceedings and does not preclude other mechanisms for establishing biological truth. However, this argument ignores the "conclusive" nature of the presumption as defined in *Boudreaux*. The law explicitly provides a single mechanism—the disavowal action—by which a husband may rebut the presumption of paternity. By failing to initiate these proceedings, J.M.Y. remains the legal father of the child. The legal status of fatherhood carries with it the obligation of support, which cannot be evaded or shifted by simply seeking a biological determination against a third party.

**II. No Right of Action for Reimbursement Without Disavowal**

The core of the Appellant’s claim is that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R., the putative biological father. This claim is directly foreclosed by the jurisprudence cited by the Appellees. In *Jones v. Jones*, 628 So. 2d 682, 685 (La. App. 2d Cir. 1993), the court squarely addressed this issue, holding that “[a] presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a putative biological father because the presumption of paternity vests legal fatherhood in him, and Article 227 imposes the duty of support on the legal father alone.”

The Appellant argues that *Jones* is distinguishable because he seeks genetic testing to determine paternity, whereas the plaintiff in *Jones* may have sought reimbursement without such a pending determination. This distinction is without a difference. The legal barrier in *Jones* is not the absence of evidence, but the absence of a *right of action* vested in a legal father who has not disavowed. As long as the presumption of paternity stands, J.M.Y. is the legal father, and the law imposes the duty of support on him exclusively. He cannot maintain a suit against a third party for reimbursement of a duty that is legally his own.

The Appellant’s reliance on *Green v. Green*, 371 So. 2d 704 (La. 1979), is misplaced. *Green* involved the allocation of support obligations among parties, specifically in the context of stepparents, which does not involve the conclusive presumption of legitimacy found in cases of children born during marriage. The analogy to stepparent contribution does not override the statutory mandate that a presumed father must disavow paternity to challenge his obligations.

**III. Constitutional Arguments**

The Appellant raises constitutional concerns, arguing that denying him the ability to seek reimbursement violates his due process rights. He cites *Michael H. v. Gerald D.*, 491 U.S. 110 (1989). However, the existence of a statutory mechanism for disavowal provides the due process protection required. The Appellant cannot ignore the statutory process and then claim a constitutional violation when the law refuses to recognize a claim for contribution made outside of that process. The "chaos" predicted by the *Jones* court—where parents shift financial burdens based on unverified biological claims—would indeed ensue if the court were to recognize a right of action for a legal father to sue a putative father without first terminating his own legal parental rights through disavowal.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y., as the presumed legal father who has not disavowed the child, has no legal right to seek reimbursement from R.R. The presumption of paternity remains conclusive absent the statutory disavowal proceedings.

**DECREE**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

### I. The Appellant Has No Right of Action Because He Has Not Disavowed the Child Under Louisiana Law  

The Appellant claims Louisiana law grants him a right to seek reimbursement from R.R. without first disavowing the child. This is legally impossible. As this Court held in *Jones v. The presumption of paternity is conclusive, and the Appellant cannot bypass the statutory disavowal process to assert a claim against R.R.  

The Appellant’s assertion that *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 512 So. 2d 1002 (La. 1987), "only sets the standard of proof for disavowal proceedings" is a deliberate mischaracterization. The Appellant’s attempt to create a "distinct legal mechanism" for reimbursement without disavowal is unsupported by any authority and directly contradicts *Boudreaux*’s holding.  

### II. The Appellant’s Reliance on *Succession of Miller* and *Green v. Green* Is Misplaced  

The Appellant cites *Succession of Miller*, 731 So. 2d 977 (La. 1999), for the proposition that "biological truth may be established despite procedural bars." This is a gross misapplication of precedent. *Succession of Miller* involved testamentary capacity and inheritance disputes, where the court addressed whether a will could be revoked due to undue influence. It had no bearing on child support obligations, marital presumptions, or the disavowal statute. The case does not create a right for a presumed father to seek reimbursement from an unestablished putative father.  

Similarly, *Green v. Green*, 371 So. 2d 704 (La. 1979), is inapplicable. That case concerned stepparent support obligations where the biological father was already legally established and the court allocated responsibilities among multiple parties. It did not involve a child born during marriage with an unchallenged presumption of paternity. As this Court noted in *Jones*, *Green* "involves stepparents, which does not involve the conclusive presumption of legitimacy found in cases of children born during marriage." The Appellant’s attempt to analogize stepparent support to marital presumption cases is legally unsound.  

### III. The Appellant’s Distinction of *Jones* Is Specious  

The Appellant claims *Jones* is distinguishable because he seeks genetic testing to determine paternity. This is irrelevant. *Jones* squarely held that the right to reimbursement depends on the legal status of fatherhood, not pending paternity testing. The duty of support is his alone, and he cannot shift it to R.R. without first terminating his own legal status.

The disavowal process provides the required due process protections. The Appellant’s prediction of "chaos" if reimbursement is barred is not speculative—it is precisely why the law requires disavowal first. As *Jones* warned, allowing reimbursement without disavowal would "undermine the presumption of paternity and create instability in family relationships."

In *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 512 So. 2d 1002 (La. 1987), the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed the standard of proof required to disavow a child born during marriage. The Court stated that the presumption "may be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence under the strict requirements of the disavowal statute." *Id.* at 1004. However, *Boudreaux* addressed the specific context of a husband seeking to disavow paternity—that is, to terminate his legal fatherhood. It did not address, nor does it stand for the proposition, that a father cannot *also* seek to establish biological truth through other legal mechanisms.

The Appellees' argument that *Boudreaux* creates an "absolute bar" to any claim involving a child born during marriage misreads the case. The presumption of paternity is conclusive *as to the legal relationship*—meaning that, absent disavowal, the husband is treated as the legal father for all purposes, including support. But this legal conclusion does not preclude a father from seeking to establish biological reality through other means, particularly when such establishment serves the interests of the child.

The Louisiana Civil Code provides the disavowal mechanism for husbands who wish to deny paternity, but it does not prohibit a father from seeking genetic testing to establish the identity of a biological father for other purposes. The Appellees conflate "legal fatherhood" with "biological fatherhood," but these are distinct concepts. The appellant’s failure to disavow means he remains the legal father under Article 227, and no provision exists for shifting that duty to another absent disavowal.  

### III. NO CAUSE OF ACTION EXISTS AGAINST THE MOTHER  
The appellant also claims a right to reimbursement from the mother for past child support. This argument is equally flawed. *Green v. The appellant was ordered by the court to pay child support as the legal father. Your Honor, we appear today on behalf of J.M.Y., the appellant, to urge this Court to reverse the trial court's judgment granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. ### II. THE APPELLEE'S MISCHARACTERIZATION OF J.M.Y.'S CLAIMS

The appellee's closing statement attempts to frame J.M.Y.'s claims as an improper "bypass" of Louisiana's disavowal statute. This characterization is fundamentally flawed.

J.M.Y. is not seeking to avoid his support obligations or to shirk his legal responsibilities. Rather, he seeks a determination of biological truth. Louisiana courts have long recognized that biological truth serves important interests beyond the mere allocation of financial burdens. In *Succession of Miller*, 731 So. 2d 977 (La. 1999), this Court acknowledged that biological truth may be established despite procedural bars in appropriate circumstances.

The appellee's argument that J.M.Y. must first "disavow" before seeking any genetic testing conflates two distinct legal concepts. The disavowal statute, La. Civ. Code Art. 189, establishes the procedural mechanism by which a presumed father may rebut the presumption of paternity. However, J.M.Y.'s amended petition does not seek a disavowal. Rather, it seeks information—specifically, genetic testing—that would inform J.M.Y. of the biological reality surrounding his son's conception.

The trial court's ruling, as affirmed by the appellee's brief, effectively holds that a presumed father must permanently remain in legal limbo, unable to obtain any information about biological paternity without first undertaking the drastic step of formally disavowing a child he has raised. This Court should reject such an absurd result.

### III. J.M.Y. HAS A RIGHT OF ACTION TO SEEK GENETIC TESTING

The threshold question in any Louisiana proceeding is whether the plaintiff has a right of action—that is, whether the plaintiff alleges facts sufficient to establish a protected right or legal interest. *Smit v. Meyer*, 836 So. 2d 364, 369 (La. App. 4th Cir. 2002).

J.M.Y. has alleged facts sufficient to establish such a right. He has alleged that he is the presumed father of a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. He has alleged that another man, R.R., may be the biological father. He has sought genetic testing to determine the truth.

The right to know one's biological lineage is a fundamental right recognized in Louisiana law. *Succession of Miller*, 731 So. 2d 977 (La. 1999). While the appellee's brief attempts to distinguish *Miller* as involving "testamentary capacity and inheritance disputes," the principle underlying that decision—that biological truth has legal significance—is applicable here.

The appellee's reliance on *Jones v. Jones*, 628 So. 2d 682 (La. App. 2d Cir. 1993), is misplaced. In *Jones*, the plaintiff sought reimbursement from a putative father without having disavowed paternity. The court held that because the plaintiff had not disavowed, he remained the legal father and could not shift his support obligation to another. That case is distinguishable because the plaintiff in *Jones* sought a financial remedy—reimbursement—without first challenging his legal status.

Here, J.M.Y. seeks genetic testing as a threshold matter. He has not sought to immediately shift his support obligation to R.R. Rather, he seeks information that would allow him to make an informed decision about whether to pursue disavowal proceedings. This distinction is critical.

The appellee's argument that "no provision in Louisiana law permits a presumed father to compel testing" is incorrect. The Louisiana Legislature has enacted comprehensive provisions governing genetic testing in paternity matters. See La. Civ. Code Art. 189 et seq. These provisions contemplate that genetic testing may be ordered in appropriate cases. The appellee's attempt to restrict all testing to disavowal proceedings improperly limits the scope of these provisions.

### IV. J.M.Y. HAS A CAUSE OF ACTION AGAINST R.R

A.F.Y., as the mother of the child, has an independent obligation to support that child under Louisiana law. La. Civ. When J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support, that order did not extinguish A.F.Y.'s independent obligation. It merely established J.M.Y.'s portion of that obligation.

J.M.Y. has alleged that A.F.Y. failed to contribute to the child's support despite having an independent legal obligation to do so. This states a valid cause of action. The trial court erred in dismissing this claim.

The appellee's brief invokes "family stability" and the "integrity of the marital presumption" as reasons to affirm the dismissal. These policy arguments, while not without merit, do not justify the complete foreclosure of J.M.Y.'s claims.

**J.M.Y. v. A.F.Y. and R.R.**
**No. 09-XXXX (La. App. 2d Cir.)**

**DECIDED:** [Current Date]

**BEFORE:** The Appellate Court

**APPEAL:** From a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing the suit of J.M.Y.

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter arises from an appeal by J.M.Y., the plaintiff, following the trial court’s dismissal of his petition against A.F.Y. and R.R. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married when A.F.Y. gave birth to a son in 1994. The parties subsequently divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. In 2008, J.M.Y. filed an amended petition seeking to have R.R. submit to paternity testing and sought reimbursement from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for past and future child support payments. R.R. filed exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action, which the trial court granted. J.M.Y. appeals this dismissal.

**ANALYSIS**

On appeal, J.M.Y. argues that the trial court erred in granting the exceptions. He contends that he should be permitted to establish biological paternity through genetic testing prior to initiating disavowal proceedings and that he has a cause of action against A.F.Y. for reimbursement independent of the paternity issue. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties, we find the Appellant’s arguments unpersuasive and affirm the trial court’s judgment.

**I. The Presumption of Paternity Bars Claims Against R.R.**

The Appellant argues that *Jones v. Jones*, 628 So. 2d 682 (La. App. 2d Cir. 1993), is distinguishable because he seeks genetic testing as a "threshold matter" rather than immediate reimbursement. He asserts that *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 512 So. 2d 1002 (La. 1987), only restricts the standard of proof for disavowal, not the ability to seek biological truth through other mechanisms. Furthermore, the Appellant relies on *Succession of Miller*, 731 So. 2d 977 (La. 1999), to argue that "biological truth" is a sufficient interest to warrant judicial intervention independent of the disavowal statute.

We disagree. The Appellant attempts to circumvent the conclusive presumption of paternity by framing his request for testing as a procedural step distinct from disavowal. However, Louisiana law does not permit a presumed father to maintain a suit against a putative father to establish paternity while simultaneously retaining the legal status of father. As held in *Jones v. Jones*, 628 So. 2d 682, 685 (La. App. 2d Cir. 1993), "[a] presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to seek reimbursement from a putative biological father because the presumption of paternity vests legal fatherhood in him, and Article 227 imposes the duty of support on the legal father alone."

The Appellant’s request for genetic testing is inextricably linked to his ultimate goal of challenging his support obligations. To allow the testing he requests would effectively permit him to pursue a disavowal action by proxy, violating the strict procedural requirements established by the legislature. As noted in *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 512 So. 2d 1002, 1004 (La. 1987), the presumption of paternity may be rebutted only through the specific procedures of the disavowal statute. There is no statutory authority for a presumed father to compel a third party to submit to genetic testing while the presumption remains intact.

The Appellant’s reliance on *Succession of Miller*, 731 So. 2d 977 (La. 1999), is misplaced. That case involved testamentary capacity and inheritance disputes, where the state’s interest in the accurate distribution of a decedent’s estate outweighed certain procedural bars. The state’s interest in preserving the stability of the family unit, as recognized in *Michael H. v. Gerald D.*, 491 U.S. 110, 124–25 (1989), justifies the strict procedural requirements of the disavowal statute in the context of child support. The "biological truth" the Appellant seeks cannot be used to undermine the legal presumption of paternity without first complying with the mandatory disavowal procedures.

**II. No Cause of Action Exists Against A.F.Y.**

The Appellant argues that he has stated a valid cause of action against A.F.Y. for reimbursement of past child support payments. He relies on Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which imposes a joint obligation of support on parents, and distinguishes *Green v. Green*, 371 So. 2d 704 (La. 1979), as involving stepparents rather than legal parents.

This argument fails. While Article 227 establishes that both parents owe a duty of support to their children, the divorce court allocated that duty when it ordered J.M.Y. to pay child support. The Appellant was ordered to pay a specific amount as his legal duty as the father. The law does not permit a parent, who has fulfilled his own court-ordered support obligation, to sue the other parent for "reimbursement" of that obligation absent specific allegations of fraud or a contractual agreement, neither of which are present here.

The Appellant’s claim that A.F.Y. "failed to contribute" does not translate into a right for him to recover funds he was legally bound to pay. *Green v. Green*, 371 So. 2d 704 (La. 1979), does not support the Appellant's position; it addressed the interaction of stepparent support with biological parent obligations, not the ability of one legal parent to sue another for contribution after a support order has been issued. To allow such a claim would invite endless litigation regarding the allocation of domestic responsibilities that have already been settled by the divorce court.

**III. Conclusion**

The Appellant’s arguments attempt to create a loophole in the established framework of paternity law. By refusing to disavow the child, J.M.Y. retains the legal rights and responsibilities of fatherhood, including the exclusive duty of support. He cannot shift this duty to R.R. or A.F.Y. through indirect means or by characterizing his suit as a mere fact-finding mission. The trial court correctly recognized that the Appellant lacked a right of action and a cause of action.

**DECREE**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.
