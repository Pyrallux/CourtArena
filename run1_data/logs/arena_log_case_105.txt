# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The plaintiff contends that the trial court's violation of Connecticut General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b constitutes harmless error that should not warrant reversal. This argument fails because it misconstrues the nature of the statutory right at issue and asks this Court to engage in improper speculation about what the defendants "would have" done. The defense respectfully submits that the preliminary ruling reversing the judgment should be affirmed.

## I. THE PLAINTIFF'S HARMLESS ERROR ARGUMENT FAILS BECAUSE THE STATUTORY SCHEME CREATES A MANDATORY PROCEDURAL RIGHT THAT CANNOT BE DEEMED HARMLESS

The plaintiff invokes the harmless error doctrine under Connecticut General Statutes § 52-263, arguing that the procedural violation did not affect the defendants' substantial rights. This argument misunderstands the nature of the rights protected by §§ 52-216a and 52-228b.

Connecticut General Statutes § 52-216a provides: "If the trial court grants a motion for an additur, it shall order a new trial on the issue of damages unless the party against whom the verdict was rendered accepts the additur." Similarly, § 52-228b requires that "the court shall grant the motion for a new trial unless the party against whom the verdict was rendered accepts the additur." The use of the word "shall" in both statutes establishes mandatory, not directory, requirements. As the Connecticut Supreme Court held in *Maloney v. Taplin*, 154 Conn. 477, 484, 226 A.2d 514, 518 (1966), the statutory language "shall" imposes a nondiscretionary duty that must be complied with to effectuate the parties' rights.

The procedural right to choose between accepting an additur and electing a new trial is not merely a formality—it is a substantive statutory protection. The very purpose of these statutes is to give the party against whom the verdict was rendered the option to either accept the increased award or proceed to a new trial. In *Higginbotham v. Kozub*, 182 Conn. 110, 115, 421 A.2d 997, 1000 (1980), this Court explicitly held that "the trial court's failure to offer the parties the option to accept the additur or elect a new trial constitutes reversible error," emphasizing that this procedure "is not discretionary but mandatory, designed to protect the parties' statutory rights."

The plaintiff argues that the procedural error was harmless because the defendants would have rejected the additur anyway. However, this Court cannot engage in such speculation. The Connecticut Supreme Court has consistently held that a party's actual choice cannot be presumed or hypothesized. In *Johnson v. State*, 170 Conn. 495, 501, 368 A.2d 16, 19 (1976), the Court rejected a similar harmless error argument, stating that "the statutory scheme requires the trial court to give the parties the choice to accept the additur or proceed to a new trial; any deviation from this process is grounds for reversal." The Court did not engage in speculation about what the party would have chosen—the violation alone was sufficient for reversal.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF'S SPECULATION ABOUT THE DEFENDANTS' CHOICE IS INSUFFICIENT TO OVERCOME THE STATUTORY VIOLATION

The plaintiff contends that because the defendants "explicitly and repeatedly objected to the plaintiff's motion for additur," they would have chosen a new trial had the option been offered. This reasoning is fundamentally flawed.

First, objection to a motion is not equivalent to a formal election under the statute. The statutory scheme requires the trial court to make a formal offer of the additur, followed by a knowing and voluntary acceptance or rejection by the party. In *Pellegrino v. Nelson*, 36 Conn. App. 524, 531, 651 A.2d 756, 760 (1995), the Appellate Court held that "the right to elect between an additur and a new trial is a substantive right that cannot be presumed to have been waived merely by opposing the motion for additur."

Second, the plaintiff's speculation about what the defendants "would have" done is precisely the type of speculation that Connecticut courts have refused to engage in when evaluating harmless error claims. In *State v. Ricardo B.*, 272 Conn. 564, 590, 863 A.2d 720, 735 (2005), the Connecticut Supreme Court stated that "harmless error analysis cannot rest on speculation about what might have occurred under proper procedure." The Court emphasized that "the party claiming harmless error bears the burden of proving that the error did not affect the outcome, and this burden cannot be met by hypothesizing about unproven future choices."

Third, the plaintiff's argument proves too much. If accepted, this reasoning would render the statutory protection meaningless in every case where a party opposes the additur—precisely the scenario the statutes were designed to address. Such an interpretation would nullify the mandatory requirements of §§ 52-216a and 52-228b, contradicting the Connecticut Supreme Court's explicit holdings in *Higginbotham v. Kozub* and *Johnson v. State*.

## III. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON STATE V. LINARES AND OTHER HARMLESS ERROR CASES IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff cites *State v. Linares*, 232 Conn. 345, 655 A.2d 737 (1995), and *Tucker v. City of New Haven*, 234 Conn. 391, 663 A.2d 103 (1995), for the proposition that procedural errors are harmless when they cause no prejudice. These cases are distinguishable.

In *Linares*, the Court applied harmless error analysis to an evidentiary ruling, not to a statutory procedural protection. The Court noted that "the harmless error doctrine applies to errors that do not affect the substantial rights of the parties." However, the Court also recognized that certain errors are "structural" in nature and cannot be harmless. In *State v. Dotson*, 272 Conn. 309, 329, 858 A.2d 281, 293 (2005), the Court clarified that "structural errors—those that affect the framework within which the trial proceeds—are not subject to harmless error analysis."

The right to elect between an additur and a new trial is a structural procedural right. It defines the framework within which the post-verdict proceedings must occur. As the Court held in *Higginbotham v. Kozub*, 182 Conn. at 115, 421 A.2d at 1000, the trial court's failure to offer this choice "constitutes reversible error" because it deprives the party of a "substantive statutory right" that "is not discretionary but mandatory."

Similarly, in *Katz v. Katz*, 200 Conn. 139, 146, 510 A.2d 512, 516 (1986), the Court stated that "reversal is improper when procedural irregularities cause no actual prejudice to the parties." However, the Court has also held that when a procedural right is mandatory and designed to protect a party's substantive interests, the deprivation of that right constitutes prejudice per se. In *Maloney v. **FINAL RULING**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court committed reversible error by granting the plaintiff’s motion for an additur and immediately rendering judgment without providing the defendants the statutory option to reject the additur and elect a new trial. Having reviewed the closing arguments, we reaffirm our conclusion that the judgment of the trial court must be reversed.

The prosecution maintains that the trial court's failure to offer the defendants the choice between accepting the additur or electing a new trial constitutes harmless error. Specifically, the prosecution argues that because the defendants objected to the additur, they would have inevitably rejected it, and thus, the error did not affect their substantial rights. This argument is unpersuasive and is contrary to the mandatory statutory framework established by the Connecticut General Statutes and binding precedent.

Connecticut General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b establish a mandatory procedural framework governing additur. The statutes provide that if a trial court grants an additur, "it shall order a new trial on the issue of damages unless the party against whom the verdict was rendered accepts the additur." The use of the word "shall" denotes a mandatory duty. As stated in *Maloney v. Taplin*, 154 Conn. 477, 484, 226 A.2d 514, 518 (1966), statutory language of "shall" imposes a nondiscretionary duty that must be complied with to effectuate the parties' rights. The trial court's immediate entry of judgment following the grant of the additur was a direct violation of this mandatory obligation.

The prosecution's reliance on the harmless error doctrine is misplaced in this context. While Connecticut General Statutes § 52-263 provides that no judgment shall be set aside unless an error affects the substantial rights of the parties, the right to elect between an additur and a new trial is a substantive statutory protection that constitutes a structural component of the trial process. In *State v. Dotson*, 272 Conn. 309, 329, 858 A.2d 281, 293 (2005), the court clarified that "structural errors—those that affect the framework within which the trial proceeds—are not subject to harmless error analysis." The right to choose between an additur and a new trial defines the framework of the post-verdict proceedings; therefore, the deprivation of this right is structural error requiring reversal.

Furthermore, the prosecution's assertion that the error is harmless because the defendants "would have" rejected the additur is based on impermissible speculation. The defense correctly notes that a party's objection to a motion for additur is not equivalent to a formal election under the statute. In *Pellegrino v. Nelson*, 36 Conn. App. 524, 531, 651 A.2d 756, 760 (1995), the court held that "the right to elect between an additur and a new trial is a substantive right that cannot be presumed to have been waived merely by opposing the motion for additur." A party may strategically oppose an additur during litigation but ultimately accept the award to avoid the risks and costs of a new trial. Because the trial court failed to make the formal offer required by statute, the defendants were never afforded the opportunity to make this informed strategic decision.

The Connecticut Supreme Court has consistently rejected harmless error arguments that rely on speculation about a party's hypothetical choice. In *State v. Ricardo B.*, 272 Conn. 564, 590, 863 A.2d 720, 735 (2005), the court stated that "harmless error analysis cannot rest on speculation about what might have occurred under proper procedure." The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the error did not affect the outcome, a burden it cannot meet by hypothesizing about the defendants' unexercised choice.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Linares*, 232 Conn. 345, 655 A.2d 737 (1995), and *Tucker v. City of New Haven*, 234 Conn. 391, 663 A.2d 103 (1995), is inapposite. Those cases involved evidentiary rulings where the court assessed whether the admission of evidence affected the verdict. They do not govern the violation of a mandatory statutory right to elect a remedy. As held in *Higginbotham v. Kozub*, 182 Conn. 110, 115, 421 A.2d 997, 1000 (1980), "the trial court's failure to offer the parties the option to accept the additur or elect a new trial constitutes reversible error," because it deprives the party of a "substantive statutory right." This principle was reaffirmed in *Johnson v. State*, 170 Conn. 495, 501, 368 A.2d 16, 19 (1976), where the court ruled that "any deviation from this process is grounds for reversal."

The trial court's immediate entry of judgment following the grant of the additur deprived the defendants of a statutory right to choose. The error is not harmless, nor can it be cured by speculation regarding the defendants' litigation strategy. The defendants are entitled to make their election under the proper procedure.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court is reversed. The case is remanded to the trial court with directions to offer the defendants the option to accept the additur of $15,000 or, in the alternative, to elect a new trial on the issue of damages, consistent with the requirements of Connecticut General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b.
