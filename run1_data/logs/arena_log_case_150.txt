The double jeopardy clause of the Fifth Amendment—applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment—prohibits retrial after a mistrial unless the defendant consented to it or manifest necessity compelled the mistrial. *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 736 (U.S. 1963). Here, neither condition was met.  

The trial court declared a mistrial sua sponte after the prosecution’s surprise disclosure of a previously undisclosed scrap of paper containing serial numbers matching the stolen mountain bike. Crucially, Dickinson never consented to the mistrial. During the colloquy, defense counsel stated: “I prefer instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper.” He further clarified that he “would not move to strike the surprise testimony, because [he] believed doing so would emphasize it.” When asked about mistrial, he conditionally noted that “if it deemed a mistrial necessary, he would seek a dismissal with prejudice”—a statement of potential consequence, not consent. *State v. Bolding*, 148 Ariz. 483, 486 (Ariz. 1981) (holding that “a defendant’s conditional statement about seeking dismissal if a mistrial is granted does not constitute consent to the mistrial itself”).  

More fundamentally, the mistrial was not “manifestly necessary.” The Supreme Court has defined “manifest necessity” as “a real necessity” for terminating the trial—meaning no less drastic alternatives existed to address the problem. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 675–76 (U.S. 1982). Yet the prosecutor explicitly offered alternatives: “The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence. It’s not the State’s first priority, because all facts should go to the jury. But, however, it’s an option for the Court.” Defense counsel echoed this preference, insisting the trial could proceed without referencing the note. The trial court ignored these options. Instead of excluding the evidence, giving a curative instruction, or recessing to prepare, it abruptly declared a mistrial. Arizona precedent confirms that manifest necessity fails when “less severe measures could have resolved the issue.” *State v. Garcia*, 169 Ariz. 131, 135 (Ariz. 1991).  

*Downum* is directly on point: there, the Supreme Court reversed a retrial after a mistrial declared over defendant’s objection, holding that the trial court “had no alternative but to proceed with the trial as it was.” 372 U.S. at 738. Similarly, in *State v. Bolding*, the Arizona Supreme Court reversed a retrial where the mistrial was declared over defense objection despite the prosecution’s offer to exclude tainted evidence. 148 Ariz. at 487. Here, the trial court’s failure to exhaust alternatives—especially the prosecution’s own proposal to exclude the evidence—renders the mistrial unjustified.  

Because the mistrial was declared without Dickinson’s consent and without manifest necessity, double jeopardy bars retrial. *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 82, 98 (U.S. 1978) (“[T]he Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits reprosecution when a mistrial is granted over the defendant’s objection and without manifest necessity.”). For these reasons, this Court must reverse Dickinson’s convictions and sentences and remand with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Summary of Argument

The prosecution's argument that this Court must reverse Dickinson's convictions based on a double jeopardy violation fundamentally misapprehends the applicable legal standard and ignores critical facts demonstrating manifest necessity. While the defense acknowledges the prosecution's creative invocation of constitutional protections, the record clearly establishes that the trial court exercised its inherent authority to declare a mistrial when unanticipated evidence dramatically altered the trial's landscape, and Dickinson's own conduct contributed to the circumstances requiring termination of the first trial. This Court should reject the double jeopardy claim and affirm the convictions.

## I. The Trial Court Possessed Manifest Necessity to Declare a Mistrial

The prosecution correctly states that double jeopardy bars retrial after a mistrial declared without the defendant's consent and without manifest necessity. *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 736 (U.S. 1963). However, the prosecution misapplies this standard by essentially substituting its judgment for that of the trial court regarding what alternatives were available.

Manifest necessity does not require the trial court to exhaust every conceivable alternative before declaring a mistrial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 506 (U.S. 1978). Rather, the trial court must exercise "sound discretion" in determining whether continuation of the trial would result in a manifest injustice. *Id.* at 509-10. The Supreme Court has recognized that trial courts possess "inherent authority" to manage their proceedings and may declare mistrials when circumstances require. *Id.* at 505.

Here, the circumstances demonstrated manifest necessity. The surprise testimony revealed that a previously undisclosed piece of evidence—containing serial number information that could directly link Dickinson to the stolen bike—had been in the possession of a witness for three years before being disclosed just two weeks before trial. This was not a case of a minor evidentiary surprise; the serial number evidence went to the very heart of the prosecution's case and directly contradicted Dickinson's defense strategy, which relied on showing the serial numbers differed.

The prosecution's argument that exclusion of the evidence was a viable alternative ignores the practical realities facing the trial court. When evidence unexpectedly emerges during trial that fundamentally alters the strategic landscape, a trial court reasonably may determine that proceeding would prejudice the defendant who had built his defense around a contrary factual premise. *State v. Murray*, 184 Ariz. 631, 640 (Ariz. App. 1996) (holding that manifest necessity exists when unexpected evidence undermines the fairness of continuing trial).

## II. Dickinson's Conduct Contributed to the Need for a Mistrial

Even more significantly, the prosecution overlooks the critical fact that Dickinson himself created the circumstances necessitating the mistrial. Defense counsel's opening statement explicitly told the jury that the serial numbers differed—a strategic choice that directly invited the prosecution to respond with evidence about the serial numbers. When the surprise testimony emerged, it did so precisely because defense counsel had opened the door to this issue.

A defendant cannot invoke the double jeopardy clause as a sword after creating the circumstances that make a mistrial necessary. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 611 (U.S. 1976) (holding that double jeopardy does not bar retrial when the defendant "by his own conduct" created the need for mistrial). Dickinson's counsel made a deliberate tactical decision to emphasize the serial number discrepancy in opening statements. When the prosecution subsequently discovered evidence contradicting this defense theory, it was a foreseeable consequence of Dickinson's litigation strategy.

The prosecution's citation of *State v. Bolding*, 148 Ariz. 483 (Ariz. 1981) is distinguishable. In Bolding, the defendant did not open the door to the disputed evidence by making it a centerpiece of his defense. Here, by contrast, Dickinson himself made the serial number issue central to his case, and the surprise evidence directly refuted his stated defense theory.

## III. The Prosecution's Proposed Alternative Was Not a Viable Option

The prosecution's argument that manifest necessity was lacking because the prosecution offered to exclude the evidence proves too much. The decision whether to exclude evidence or declare a mistrial rests with the trial court, not the parties. *Wheat v. United States*, 486 U.S. 153, 164 (U.S. 1988). The prosecution's willingness to forgo evidence is irrelevant to whether the trial court, exercising its independent judgment, determined that a mistrial was necessary to ensure fairness.

Moreover, the prosecution's offer was conditional and ambivalent. The prosecutor stated exclusion was "not the State['s] first priority, because all facts should go to the jury." This is not a firm commitment to forego the evidence; it is at best a willingness to compromise if the court required it. A trial court cannot be expected to rely on such equivocal proposals when determining whether to proceed with a trial whose fundamental fairness has been compromised.

## IV. Even If Error Occurred, It Was Harmless

Finally, even assuming arguendo that some error occurred in declaring the mistrial, any error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Dickinson was retried before a new jury, convicted, and sentenced. The second trial was full and fair, with both sides having full opportunity to present their cases. Dickinson has not identified any evidence at the second trial that was tainted or improperly admitted.

The double jeopardy clause protects against the stress and expense of repeated prosecutions, *Ohio v. Johnson*, 467 U.S. 493, 501 (U.S. 1984), not against all procedural irregularities in earlier proceedings. Dickinson received a fair trial on the merits, and that is what the Constitution guarantees.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reject the prosecution's double jeopardy argument and affirm Dickinson's convictions and sentences. The trial court possessed manifest necessity to declare a mistrial given the dramatic unexpected evidence that directly contradicted Dickinson's stated defense, and Dickinson's own litigation choices contributed to the need for termination of the first trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial violated Dickinson’s constitutional protection against double jeopardy. Specifically, we must determine if the mistrial was declared with Dickinson’s consent or supported by "manifest necessity." *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 736 (1963). After reviewing the arguments presented by Dickinson and the State, we find that the trial court erred in declaring a mistrial. Because the mistrial was declared over Dickinson’s objection and without manifest necessity, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars his retrial.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Dickinson Did Not Consent to the Mistrial**

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial if the defendant consents to the mistrial. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 673 (1982). However, consent must be clear and unambiguous. Here, the record demonstrates that Dickinson explicitly objected to the termination of the trial. Defense counsel stated, "I prefer instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper." Furthermore, when the court broached the subject of a mistrial, counsel conditioned his response on the court’s determination of necessity, stating, "if it deemed a mistrial necessary, he would seek a dismissal with prejudice."

The State argues that Dickinson’s conduct contributed to the need for a mistrial, but this does not equate to consent. As established in *State v. Bolding*, 148 Ariz. 483, 486 (1981), a defendant’s conditional statement regarding a future motion for dismissal does not constitute consent to the mistrial itself. Dickinson’s preference to proceed with the current jury was unequivocal. Therefore, the "consent" exception to the double jeopardy bar does not apply.

**B. Manifest Necessity Was Lacking**

A mistrial declared without the defendant’s consent is permissible only if there is a "manifest necessity" for such an action. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). Manifest necessity requires a "high degree" of necessity and exists only when, considering the alternatives, "the ends of justice would be defeated if the trial were to continue." *Id.* at 506. The trial court must exhaust reasonable alternatives before declaring a mistrial. *State v. Garcia*, 169 Ariz. 131, 135 (1991).

Here, the trial court failed to exhaust reasonable alternatives. The surprise testimony regarding the scrap of paper was certainly problematic, but both parties offered solutions that did not involve terminating the trial. Dickinson’s counsel requested to proceed without reference to the note. Crucially, the prosecutor explicitly stated, "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." The State’s willingness to exclude the damaging evidence provided the trial court with a viable, less drastic alternative to a mistrial.

The State argues that the surprise evidence "fundamentally altered" the trial landscape and that the trial court has "inherent authority" to declare a mistrial to ensure fairness. While trial courts have broad discretion, that discretion is not unlimited. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 509-10. Where the prosecution itself offers to exclude the evidence causing the prejudice, it is difficult to conclude that manifest necessity existed. *State v. Garcia*, 169 Ariz. 131, 135 (1991) (holding that manifest necessity fails when less severe measures could have resolved the issue). The trial court’s abrupt declaration of a mistrial, despite the State’s offer to proceed without the evidence, was an abuse of discretion.

**C. Dickinson’s Conduct Did Not Justify the Mistrial**

The State contends that Dickinson "opened the door" to the surprise evidence by emphasizing the serial number discrepancy in his opening statement, and therefore, he cannot now claim double jeopardy. The State relies on *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 611 (1976), for the proposition that double jeopardy does not bar retrial when the defendant "by his own conduct" creates the need for a mistrial.

We find this argument unpersuasive. The "goading" exception applies when a defendant intentionally engages in misconduct to force a mistrial. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 676 (1982). Here, Dickinson’s strategy was to argue the serial numbers differed; this was a legitimate defense strategy, not an act of misconduct designed to sabotage the trial. Furthermore, Dickinson did not seek a mistrial; he fought to continue the trial. A defendant cannot be said to have "created the need" for a mistrial when he explicitly argued against it and the prosecution offered to cure the prejudice by excluding the evidence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial over Dickinson’s express objection and without manifest necessity. The State’s offer to exclude the surprise evidence provided a clear alternative to terminating the proceedings. Because the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits reprosecution under these circumstances, *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 82, 98 (1978), we must reverse Dickinson’s convictions and remand with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s motion for relief based on double jeopardy is granted. The convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded for dismissal of the charges with prejudice.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the prosecution's response and maintains that this Court should affirm its preliminary ruling granting Dickinson relief based on the Double Jeopardy Clause. The prosecution's arguments mischaracterize the record, misapply governing precedent, and fail to overcome the fundamental constitutional protections that bar retrial following an improperly declared mistrial. The prosecution's four main arguments—that manifest necessity existed, that Dickinson "opened the door," that the prosecution's offer was equivocal, and that harmless error applies—all fail as a matter of law.

## I. THE PROSECUTION'S "MANIFEST NECESSITY" ARGUMENT FAILS BECAUSE THE STATE'S OWN OFFER TO EXCLUDE THE EVIDENCE DEMONSTRATES THAT LESS SEVERE ALTERNATIVES WERE AVAILABLE

The prosecution's primary argument rests on the claim that manifest necessity existed for the mistrial. This argument fails because the prosecution itself provided the trial court with a clear alternative—exclusion of the surprise evidence—that negated any claimed necessity for terminating the trial.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that manifest necessity requires a "high degree" of necessity and exists only when, considering all alternatives, the ends of justice would be defeated if the trial were to continue. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505-06 (1978). Critically, the trial court must exhaust reasonable alternatives before declaring a mistrial. *State v. Garcia*, 169 Ariz. 131, 135 (1991).

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Murray*, 184 Ariz. 631, 640 (Ariz. App. 1996), is misplaced. In *Murray*, this Court found manifest necessity where the prosecution had not offered to exclude the prejudicial evidence. Here, by contrast, the prosecutor explicitly stated: "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." This is not a case where no exclusionary alternative existed; it is a case where the prosecution voluntarily offered the very remedy that would have cured any prejudice. The trial court's failure to accept this offered remedy or even meaningfully address it constitutes an abuse of discretion.

The prosecution argues that the exclusionary offer was not the State's "first priority," but this rhetorical acknowledgment that the State preferred to present all evidence cannot override the clear, affirmative commitment to proceed without the note. When the State explicitly represents that it can and will proceed without the prejudicial evidence, that representation must be given substantial weight in the manifest necessity analysis. The trial court could not simply ignore this offer and declare a mistrial without demonstrating why this less severe alternative was inadequate.

Furthermore, the prosecution's characterization of the surprise testimony as "fundamentally alter[ing] the trial landscape" overstates the record. The testimony involved a scrap of paper that had been in the buyer's wife's possession for three years before being disclosed two weeks before trial. This was not a situation involving newly discovered evidence requiring immediate termination; it was a disclosure timing issue that could have been addressed through exclusion. The prosecutor's own acknowledgment that exclusion was feasible demonstrates that the trial could have continued fairly.

## II. DICKINSON DID NOT "OPEN THE DOOR" TO THE SURPRISE EVIDENCE IN A MANNER THAT WOULD INVOKE THE "GOADING" EXCEPTION

The prosecution's argument that Dickinson "opened the door" to the surprise evidence and therefore cannot claim double jeopardy protection misapplies the "goading" exception established in *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667 (1982), and *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600 (1976).

The "goading" exception applies only when a defendant intentionally engages in misconduct designed to provoke a mistrial. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. at 676. The prosecution bears the burden of demonstrating that the defendant's conduct was "designed to provoke the mistrial," not merely that the defendant's litigation strategy created some strategic disadvantage. *Id.*

Dickinson's opening statement—that the mountain bike he sold on Craigslist was not the stolen bike because the serial numbers differed—was a legitimate defense strategy. Pointing out weaknesses in the prosecution's evidence, including evidential discrepancies, is precisely what defense counsel is supposed to do. This is not misconduct; it is the essence of adversarial representation.

Critically, Dickinson did not seek a mistrial. To the contrary, defense counsel expressly stated: "I prefer instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper." When the trial court broached the subject of a mistrial, counsel indicated he would seek dismissal with prejudice if the court deemed a mistrial necessary—demonstrating that Dickinson was actively fighting against termination of the trial.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Dinitz* is inapposite. In *Dinitz*, the defendant deliberately engaged in disruptive behavior, including insulting the trial judge and refusing to obey court orders, specifically to force a mistrial. 424 U.S. at 603-04. No such conduct occurred here. Dickinson's opening statement was a routine defense presentation, not an act of sabotage. The prosecution cannot convert a legitimate defense strategy into "goading" simply because it proved effective in exposing weaknesses in the State's case.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S OFFER TO EXCLUDE THE EVIDENCE WAS CLEAR AND UNAMBIGUOUS

The prosecution argues that its offer to exclude the evidence was "equivocal" because the prosecutor stated the exclusion was "not the State’s first priority." This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecutor's full statement was: "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence. It's not the State's first priority, because all facts should go to the jury. But, however, it's an option for the Court." This is a clear, affirmative representation that the State could and would proceed without the note. The phrase "not the State's first priority" is a rhetorical acknowledgment of preference, not a reservation of rights or a conditional offer. The prosecutor explicitly identified exclusion as an "option for the Court" and represented that the State could proceed in that manner.

Second, *Arizona v. Washington* requires trial courts to consider "the prosecution's position" in determining whether manifest necessity exists. 434 U.S. at 509-10. When the prosecution explicitly states it can proceed without the evidence causing prejudice, that position must be given substantial weight. The trial court could not simply disregard this representation and declare a mistrial without explaining why the prosecution's own proposed solution was inadequate.

Third, the prosecution's argument that the offer was "ambivalent" conflates preference with reservation. A prosecutor may prefer to present all evidence while simultaneously acknowledging that exclusion is appropriate under the circumstances. Here, the prosecutor made both statements in the same breath, and the clear commitment to proceed without the evidence if so ordered is what matters for manifest necessity analysis.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S HARMLESS ERROR ARGUMENT FAILS BECAUSE DOUBLE JEOPARDY VIOLATIONS ARE STRUCTURAL ERRORS

The prosecution's final argument—that any error in declaring the mistrial was harmless because Dickinson received a fair second trial—misapprehends the fundamental nature of double jeopardy protection.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense, not merely against unfair trials. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 82, 98 (1978), when a mistrial is declared without the defendant's consent and without manifest necessity, retrial is constitutionally prohibited regardless of the fairness of the subsequent proceedings. The violation lies in the improper termination of the first prosecution, not in the conduct of any subsequent trial.

The prosecution's reliance on *Ohio v. Johnson*, 467 U.S. 493 (1984), is misplaced. In *Johnson*, the Court held that double jeopardy does not bar successive prosecutions where the defendant has not yet been placed in jeopardy for all charges. 467 U.S. at 500-01. Here, Dickinson was placed in jeopardy when the first trial began before the jury was sworn. The mistrial terminated that jeopardy without Dickinson's consent and without manifest necessity, thereby barring reprosecution.

Double jeopardy violations are structural errors that infect the entire proceedings and cannot be cured by demonstrating that subsequent proceedings were fair. The very purpose of the Double Jeopardy Clause is to prevent the government from subjecting a defendant to the "embarrassment, expense, and ordeal" of repeated prosecutions. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957). Allowing retrial whenever the government can demonstrate that the second trial was fair would eviscerate this protection.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and as set forth in this Court's preliminary ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its ruling granting Dickinson's motion for relief based on double jeopardy, reverse the convictions, and remand with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that manifest necessity existed when the prosecution itself offered to exclude the evidence causing any prejudice. Dickinson did not consent to the mistrial, did not engage in misconduct designed to provoke it, and actively fought against termination of the trial. The prosecution's offer to proceed without the note was clear and unambiguous, and the trial court's failure to accept this offered remedy was an abuse of discretion. Under these circumstances, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial.
