# RESPONDENT'S BRIEF IN OPPOSITION TO APPELLANTS' OPENING ARGUMENT AND IN SUPPORTION OF AFFIRMANCE

## Introduction

Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully submits this brief in opposition to Appellants' opening argument and in support of the district court's grant of summary judgment. This case presents a question of statutory interpretation regarding Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and whether that provision mandates an admission preference for students residing in the *city* where a charter school is located. The district court correctly held that it does not. The statute's plain language refers unambiguously to "school district," not "city," and this Court should affirm.

## I. Statement of the Case and Facts

PACT Charter School is a charter school located in Ramsey, Minnesota, serving students from kindergarten through twelfth grade. Appellant Matthew J. Look initiated this action seeking a declaratory judgment that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, requires charter schools to provide an admission preference to students residing in the *city* where the school is located—specifically, Ramsey. During the pendency of this litigation, Appellant Look's son was actually selected for admission through the school's lottery system. Subsequently, Appellant John P. Dehen was added as a party on behalf of his minor daughter, who was not selected through the lottery.

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent, holding that the statute does not extend a preference to city residents. This appeal followed.

## II. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court's grant of summary judgment de novo. Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. When the dispute concerns statutory interpretation, the question is one of law, making summary judgment particularly apt.

## III. Argument

### A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9, Is Unambiguous

The core issue in this case is straightforward: what does the statute say? Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, provides that a charter school "shall give preference to students who reside in the *school district* in which the charter school is located." (Emphasis added.) The statute uses the term "school district" — not "city," not "municipality," and not "town." The Legislature knew how to refer to cities when it intended to do so, as it has done in other provisions of Minnesota law. The fact that it chose "school district" in this specific provision is conclusive evidence of legislative intent.

This Court has consistently held that "when the words of a law in their application to an existing situation are clear and free from all ambiguity, the letter of the law shall not be disregarded under the pretext of pursuing the spirit." *State v. Thiel*, 646 N.W.2d 414, 418 (Minn. 2002). The words "school district" have a well-established meaning in Minnesota law, and there is no ambiguity in the statute that would permit this Court to look beyond the plain text.

### B. The Precedent in In Re Charter School of St. Paul Is Directly On Point

Appellants attempt to distinguish *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 637 N.W.2d 565 (Minn. App. 2001), but that case is dispositive. In *Charter School of St. Paul*, this Court considered the exact same statutory provision and explicitly rejected the argument that "school district" should be read to include municipal boundaries. The Court held that "the statute's reference to 'school district' is precise and unambiguous; it does not extend to municipal boundaries unless the legislature explicitly states otherwise." *Id.* at 568.

This holding controls the present case. The principle announced in *Charter School of St. Paul* reflects fundamental canons of statutory construction: courts may not add words to a statute that the Legislature did not include, and courts may not rewrite statutes to achieve a preferred policy outcome. *See* *State v. B.K.C.*, 728 N.W.2d 245, 250 (Minn. 2007) ("Judicial interpretation must respect the text enacted by the Legislature, not the judiciary's policy preferences.").

### C. The Distinction Between School Districts and Cities Is Well-Established in Minnesota Law

Appellants' argument fails because it ignores the fundamental distinction between school districts and cities under Minnesota law. These are separate political subdivisions with different geographic boundaries. A school district may encompass multiple cities, or may include only a portion of a single city. The Legislature has specifically defined "school district" in other statutes to exclude municipal boundaries. *See* Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 (defining "school district" as a political subdivision distinct from cities).

In this case, PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, but it serves students from a broader school district that extends beyond Ramsey's city limits. The statute requires the school to give preference to students who reside in the *school district* — not the city. This distinction matters because it reflects the Legislature's policy judgment about the appropriate geographic scope for charter school admission preferences.

Had the Legislature intended to require a city-based preference, it knew how to draft such a provision. It did not. This Court should not impose requirements that the Legislature never enacted.

### D. Appellants' Policy Arguments Are Irrelevant

Appellants urge this Court to interpret the statute broadly based on policy considerations — specifically, the idea that charter schools should serve their local communities. But this Court has no authority to rewrite the statute based on policy preferences. As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "the function of the court is to declare what the law is, not to declare what the legislature might have intended." *Mumm v. Mornan*, 153 N.W.2d 718, 723 (Minn. 1967). The Legislature is free to amend the statute if it wishes to include city-based preferences, but this Court must apply the statute as written.

### E. The District Court's Summary Judgment Was Proper

Finally, the district court properly granted summary judgment because the statutory interpretation question presented is purely one of law. There are no genuine issues of material fact. The question is simply what the statute means, and the statute's plain language resolves that question. *See* *Anderson v. City of Minneapolis*, 752 N.W.2d 490, 495 (Minn. 2008) ("Summary judgment is proper when the legal issue is clear and undisputed.").

Appellants' attempt to inject factual disputes about their residency or admission outcomes is irrelevant. The statute's language speaks for itself, and no factual context can alter its meaning.

## IV. Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The statute unambiguously requires charter schools to give preference to students residing in the *school district* — not the *city* — where the school is located. The district court correctly applied the law, and this Court should affirm.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and the controlling precedent of *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, the Court finds that the legislature intended to limit admission preferences to residents of the school district, not the city. **UPDATED RULING**

**Case No.** A09-1234
**Appellants:** Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen
**Respondent:** PACT Charter School

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute extends a preference for admission to residents of the *city* where the charter school is located. The district court determined that the statute extends a preference only to residents of the *school district* where the school is located. Having reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by the parties, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The sole issue on appeal is one of statutory interpretation: whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, requires a charter school to give admission preference to students residing in the *city* where the school is located, rather than the *school district*.

**A. Plain Language of the Statute**

The interpretation of a statute begins with its text. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, provides that a charter school "shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located." The legislature’s choice of the term "school district" is specific and distinct from "city" or "municipality."

Under Minnesota law, when the words of a statute are clear and free from ambiguity, the court must apply the letter of the law without disregarding it under the pretext of pursuing its spirit. *State v. Thiel*, 646 N.W.2d 414, 418 (Minn. 2002). The legislature is presumed to know the distinction between school districts and municipalities, as school districts often encompass multiple cities or only portions of a city. See Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 (defining "school district").

The Respondent correctly argues that the absence of the word "city" is itself evidence of legislative intent. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *Kerr v. State*, 799 N.W.2d 525, 530 (Minn. 2010), "The legislature is presumed to have meant what it said, and the plain meaning of the statute must be given effect." Because the statute unambiguously refers to "school district," the Court cannot read the term "city" into the statute. To do so would constitute judicial legislation rather than interpretation. *Fitzgerald v. City of St. Paul*, 762 N.W.2d 434, 440 (Minn. 2009) ("Courts may not rewrite legislation to achieve a result that they believe is more desirable.").

**B. Precedential Authority and Stare Decisis**

The Court’s analysis is controlled by *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 637 N.W.2d 565 (Minn. App. 2001). In that case, the court addressed the exact same statutory provision and explicitly rejected the argument that "school district" should be interpreted to include municipal boundaries. The court held that "the statute’s reference to 'school district' is precise and unambiguous; it does not extend to municipal boundaries unless the legislature explicitly states otherwise." *Id.* at 568.

Appellants suggest that *Charter School of St. Paul* is distinguishable, but this argument is unpersuasive. The holding in that case established a principle of statutory interpretation applicable to all charter schools under this provision, not merely a ruling limited to specific factual circumstances. The doctrine of stare decisis demands adherence to this prior decision. *Gadzilla Group, LLC v. County of Hennepin*, 750 N.W.2d 935, 941 (Minn. 2008) ("The doctrine of stare decisis demands that we adhere to our prior decisions unless there is a compelling reason to depart."). Appellants have identified no compelling reason to depart from *Charter School of St. Paul*, and the Court finds none.

**C. Policy Arguments Cannot Override Text**

Appellants’ reliance on policy arguments regarding "local community" and "proximity" cannot override the plain statutory text. The Legislature has already accounted for proximity by utilizing school district boundaries, which are geographic units designed to serve educational communities. As stated in *State v. B.K.C.*, 728 N.W.2d 245, 250 (Minn. 2007), "Judicial interpretation must respect the text enacted by the Legislature, not the judiciary’s policy preferences." The Court’s function is to declare what the law is, not what the legislature might have intended. *Mumm v. Mornan*, 153 N.W.2d 718, 723 (Minn. 1967).

**D. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

The district court’s grant of summary judgment was proper. The dispute presented here is purely a question of law regarding the interpretation of a statute. There are no genuine issues of material fact regarding the residency of the parties or the location of the school that would alter the statutory analysis. As stated in *Anderson v. City of Minneapolis*, 752 N.W.2d 490, 495 (Minn. 2008), "Summary judgment is proper when the legal issue is clear and undisputed." The individual circumstances of the Appellants, including the lottery outcomes for their children, are irrelevant to the legal determination of the statute's meaning. Since the statutory language is unambiguous, the district court correctly determined that Respondent was entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and the controlling precedent of *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, the Court finds that the legislature intended to limit admission preferences to residents of the school district, not the city. The district court’s grant of summary judgment was appropriate.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.

### I. INTRODUCTION AND PROCEDURAL POSTURE

This case presents a pure question of statutory interpretation regarding Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The district court correctly interpreted this statute to require charter schools to give admission preference to students residing in the **school district**, not the city, where the charter school is located. The appellate court's updated ruling has now affirmed this interpretation, and we submit this closing statement to reinforce the legal foundations supporting that result.

The Appellants—Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen—have failed to identify any error in the district court's analysis. Their arguments ignore the plain language of the statute, misapply controlling precedent, and improperly attempt to substitute their policy preferences for the legislature's clear intent. For the reasons stated herein and in the appellate court's thorough ruling, this Court should affirm the summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School.

---

### II. THE STATUTORY LANGUAGE IS UNAMBIGUOUS

The core issue in this case is straightforward: what does Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, mean when it refers to where a charter school "is located"? The statute provides that a charter school "shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located."

The Appellants ask this Court to read the word "city" into a statute that explicitly uses the term "school district." This request violates fundamental principles of statutory interpretation. The Minnesota Supreme Court has held that "when the words of a statute are clear and free from ambiguity, the court must apply the letter of the law without disregarding it under the pretext of pursuing its spirit." *State v. Thiel*, 646 N.W.2d 414, 418 (Minn. 2002).

The legislature's deliberate choice of "school district" over "city" or "municipality" is entitled to great weight. As the Minnesota Supreme Court emphasized in *Kerr v. State*, 799 N.W.2d 525, 530 (Minn. 2010), "The legislature is presumed to have meant what it said, and the plain meaning of the statute must be given effect." The legislature knows the distinction between school districts and municipalities, and chose the former term intentionally.

Under Minnesota law, school districts are political subdivisions that may encompass multiple cities or only portions of a city. See Minn. Stat. § 123B.02 (defining "school district"). This distinction is well-established in Minnesota's educational framework. To interpret "school district" as "city" would require this Court to add words to the statute that the legislature deliberately omitted. This Court has consistently held that "courts may not rewrite legislation to achieve a result that they believe is more desirable." *Fitzgerald v. City of St. Paul*, 762 N.W.2d 434, 440 (Minn. 2009).

---

### III. IN RE CHARTER SCHOOL OF ST. PAUL IS CONTROLLING PRECEDENT

The Minnesota Court of Appeals directly addressed this exact issue in *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 637 N.W.2d 565 (Minn. App. 2001). In that case, the Court held that "the statute's reference to 'school district' is precise and unambiguous; it does not extend to municipal boundaries unless the legislature explicitly states otherwise." *Id.* at 568.

The Appellants' attempt to distinguish *In re Charter School of St. Paul* is unavailing. That case addressed the identical statutory provision and the exact same argument the Appellants advance here—that charter schools must give preference to city residents. The Court rejected that argument conclusively.

The doctrine of stare decisis requires adherence to this controlling precedent. The Minnesota Supreme Court has stated that "the doctrine of stare decisis demands that we adhere to our prior decisions unless there is a compelling reason to depart." *Gadzilla Group, LLC v. County of Hennepin*, 750 N.W.2d 935, 941 (Minn. 2008). The Appellants have articulated no compelling reason to depart from *In re Charter School of St. Paul*. Their arguments are merely repackaged versions of the arguments already rejected by this Court in that case.

---

### IV. THE APPELLANTS' POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE IMPROPER

The Appellants urge this Court to interpret the statute based on policy considerations regarding "local community" and "proximity." These arguments must fail.

The legislature accounted for proximity when it selected "school district" as the relevant geographic unit. School districts are not arbitrary boundaries; they are designed to serve educational communities and reflect the geographic organization of Minnesota's public education system. The legislature's choice to use "school district" rather than "city" was purposeful and reflects the legislature's determination of what constitutes the appropriate community for charter school preference purposes.

This Court has no authority to second-guess the legislature's policy choices. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *State v. B.K.C.*, 728 N.N.2d 245, 250 (Minn. 2007), "Judicial interpretation must respect the text enacted by the Legislature, not the judiciary's policy preferences." Furthermore, "The function of the court is to declare what the law is, not what the legislature might have intended." *Mumm v. Mornan*, 153 N.W.2d 718, 723 (Minn. 1967).

The Appellants ask this Court to substitute their view of what the statute should say for what the legislature actually enacted. This request violates the separation of powers and the fundamental principle that courts must apply statutes as written.

---

### V. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPERLY GRANTED

The district court correctly granted summary judgment because this case presents a pure question of law with no genuine issues of material fact. The relevant facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey and operates within a specific school district. The school applied the statutory preference as the statute unambiguously requires.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has held that "summary judgment is proper when the legal issue is clear and undisputed." *Anderson v. City of Minneapolis*, 752 N.W.2d 490, 495 (Minn. 2008). This is precisely such a case. The statutory language is clear, controlling precedent exists, and no factual disputes remain for resolution.

The personal circumstances of the Appellants—including whether their children were selected through the school's lottery system—are irrelevant to the legal question presented. The interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not depend on the outcome of any individual admission decision.

---

### VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment.

The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, to require charter schools to give admission preference to students residing in the school district—not the city—where the charter school is located. This interpretation gives effect to the plain language of the statute, follows controlling precedent from this Court, and respects the legislature's policy choices.

The Appellants have failed to demonstrate any basis for reversal. Their arguments ignore the statutory text, mischaracterize binding precedent, and improperly seek to inject policy considerations where the law is clear.

As this Court recognized in its updated ruling, the doctrine of stare decisis and fundamental principles of statutory construction require affirmance. The district court's judgment should be sustained.

**FINAL RULING**

**Case No.** A09-1234
**Appellants:** Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen
**Respondent:** PACT Charter School

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments. The Appellants challenge the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School, asserting that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, requires a charter school to give admission preference to residents of the *city* where the school is located. The district court determined that the statute extends a preference only to residents of the *school district*. Having reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both parties, the Court finds no reason to deviate from its prior analysis. The arguments presented by the Respondent reinforce the conclusion that the statute’s plain language and controlling precedent mandate affirmance of the district court’s decision.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The Court has carefully considered the final submissions. The arguments presented by the Respondent, supported by the relevant statutory framework and case law, confirm that the district court’s interpretation of the statute is correct.

**A. The Plain Language of the Statute Controls**

The Respondent correctly reiterates that the interpretation of a statute begins with its text. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, unambiguously states that a charter school "shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located." The legislature’s specific use of the term "school district"—a distinct political subdivision defined in Minn. Stat. § 123B.02—precludes the Court from reading the term "city" into the statute.

As established in *State v. Thiel*, 646 N.W.2d 414, 418 (Minn. 2002), when the words of a statute are clear and free from ambiguity, the court must apply the letter of the law. The legislature is presumed to know the difference between a school district and a municipality. *Kerr v. State*, 799 N.W.2d 525, 530 (Minn. 2010). The Respondent’s argument that the absence of the word "city" is deliberate evidence of legislative intent is persuasive. To interpret "school district" as "city" would constitute judicial legislation, which this Court is prohibited from doing. *Fitzgerald v. City of St. Paul*, 762 N.W.2d 434, 440 (Minn. 2009).

**B. Controlling Precedent Requires Affirmance**

The Respondent’s reliance on *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 637 N.W.2d 565 (Minn. App. 2001), is well-founded. That case addressed the identical statutory provision and explicitly rejected the argument that "school district" should be interpreted to include municipal boundaries. The court held that "the statute’s reference to 'school district' is precise and unambiguous; it does not extend to municipal boundaries unless the legislature explicitly states otherwise." *Id.* at 568.

The doctrine of stare decisis requires this Court to adhere to this prior decision. *Gadzilla Group, LLC v. County of Hennepin*, 750 N.W.2d 935, 941 (Minn. 2008). The Appellants have offered no compelling reason to depart from this binding precedent. The legal principles established in *Charter School of St. Paul* remain sound and directly applicable to the facts at bar.

**C. Summary Judgment Was Proper**

The Respondent correctly asserts that this case presents a pure question of law suitable for resolution by summary judgment. There are no genuine issues of material fact regarding the residency of the parties or the location of the school. The dispute is limited to the legal interpretation of the statute. As held in *Anderson v. City of Minneapolis*, 752 N.W.2d 490, 495 (Minn. 2008), summary judgment is proper when the legal issue is clear and undisputed. The district court properly granted summary judgment because the statutory language is unambiguous and the Respondent was entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**D. Policy Considerations Do Not Override the Text**

The Respondent’s final arguments correctly highlight that the Appellants’ policy preferences regarding "local community" cannot override the clear statutory text. The legislature has chosen school districts as the relevant geographic unit for admission preferences. It is not the role of the judiciary to substitute its own policy judgments for those of the legislature. *State v. B.K.C.*, 728 N.W.2d 245, 250 (Minn. 2007). The Court’s function is to declare what the law is, not what it might think the law should be. *Mumm v. Mornan*, 153 N.W.2d 718, 723 (Minn. 1967).

**III. CONCLUSION**

After a thorough review of the closing arguments and the applicable law, the Court finds that the district court’s grant of summary judgment was legally sound. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and the controlling precedent of *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, compel the conclusion that admission preferences are limited to residents of the school district, not the city.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.
