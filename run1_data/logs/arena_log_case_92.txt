COURT ARENA LOG - Case 92
FACTS:
I. Introduction

This court has been asked to provide guidance to the United States District Court for the District of Hawai'i (“District Court”) on questions of Hawai'i law. At issue is whether the parties may use, or be compelled to produce, the confidential medical records of over 100 cancer patients, in an effort to prosecute or defend against claims that the Plaintiff doctors steered these patients away from treatment at Defendant Queens’ Medical Center. The patients are not parties to the underlying lawsuit, although 19 of them have been granted intervenor status. All of them have intervened solely to assert their right to privacy and seek a prohibition on the use and production of their medical records.

The District Court1 certified the following questions to this court:

1. May a third party who is in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records use, or be compelled to produce, these records in litigation where the patient is not a party?
2. If a third party may use and/or produce a patient’s confidential medical records in litigation, is a de-identification *16process sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the third party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public?
This court may “reformulate the relevant state law questions as it perceives them to be, in light of the contentions of the parties.” Allstate Ins. Co. v. Alamo Rent-A-Car, Inc., 137 F.3d 634, 637 (9th Cir.1998) (citations and quotation marks omitted). To avoid confusion, we reformulate the certified questions to clarify that the term “party” refers to the parties to the litigation, not to the parties to the physician-patient relationship. We believe that the “parties” in this case are the plaintiffs and defendants, and the “third parties” in this case are the patient intervenors. We also reformulate the question so that a negative answer to the first certified question will not preclude us from answering the second certified question to the extent we can. Therefore, the reformulated certified questions are:

1. May a party who is in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records use, or be compelled to produce, these records in litigation where the patient is not a party?
2. Is a de-identification process sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public?2
Hawai'i Rules of Appellate Procedure (“HRAP”) Rule 13 (2000) governs certified questions. It provides, in relevant part, ‘When a federal district ... court certifies to the Hawai'i Supreme Court that there is involved in any proceeding before it a question concerning the law of Hawai'i that is determinative of the cause and that there is no clear controlling precedent in the Hawai'i judicial decisions, the Hawai'i Supreme Court may answer the certified question by written opinion.” We therefore confine our answer to the “law of Hawai'i that is determinative of the cause,” namely article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution. That constitutional provision states, in relevant part, “The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest.”

We answer the first certified question in the negative. Article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution protects the health information of patient intervenors to this case. Pursuant to that provision, and under the facts of this case, the parties cannot use, or be compelled to produce, confidential patient medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party, absent a compelling state interest.

As to the second certified question, we do not address whether sufficient de-identification is possible where one party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public. The de-identification process and requirements are set forth under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (“HIPAA”), Pub L. No. 104-191, 110 Stat. 1936 (1996), and its corresponding regulations; therefore, the sufficiency of de-identification does not “eoneern[ ] the law of Ha-wai'i that is determinative of the cause.” HRAP Rule 13. Whether the use and production of de-identified medical records is “sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests,” however, is a question this court can address under article I, section 6. We hold that the use and production of de-identified medical information of patients who are not parties to the litigation violates those patients’ right to privacy under article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i constitution, as no *17compelling state interest has been shown in this case.

II. Background

The Plaintiffs in this case are Pacific Radiation Oncology, LLC; PRO Associates, LLC; John Lederer, M.D.; Vincent Brown, M.D.; Paul DeMare, M.D.; Thanh Huynh, M.D.; Laeton Pang, M.D.; and Eva Bieniek, M.D. (collectively, “PRO”). The Plaintiffs filed an Amended Complaint for Declaratory and Injunctive Relief and for Damages (“Amended Complaint”) against Defendants Queens’ Medical Center and Queens’ Development Corporation (collectively “QMC”).3 The Amended Complaint alleged that the Plaintiffs “had a long-standing, 40-year relationship with QMC to provide professional radiation oncology therapy services to PRO patients at facilities owned by QMC, using equipment, technician support, and other services provided by QMC.” QMC is the only Nuclear Regulatory Commission-approved hospital at which radiation oncologists can operate on patients. Plaintiffs also acknowledged that they had “a one-third interest in The Cancer Centers of Hawai'i [‘TCCH’],” a competitor of QMC.

According to the Amended Complaint, QMC notified Plaintiffs that the QMC Board had decided to convert QMC to a “closed radiation therapy department,” meaning that only physicians employed by QMC could exercise clinical privileges to provide professional radiation oncology services at QMC. QMC explained that it arrived at its decision to terminate PRO’S privileges after determining that PRO had “transferred] patients to other facilities for no medical reason or patient request....” Plaintiffs alleged in them Amended Complaint that QMC’s action was intended to destroy their ability to treat patients at facilities competing with QMC.

Plaintiffs’ Amended Complaint raised ten claims for relief: a claim of denial of procedural and substantive due process; a claim of violation of QMC bylaws and governing regulations; three separate claims of intentional and tortious interference; four separate elaims of unfair, deceptive, anti-competitive and illegal trade practices in violation of HRS Chapter 480; and a claim of breach of fiduciary duty and bad faith owed to a partner.

QMC filed an Answer and Counterclaim. Relevant to this certified question, QMC counterclaimed that Plaintiffs Lederer, Brown, DeMare, Huynh, and Pang “consulted with and/or began treatment of patients referred to them at QMC and then induced the patients to receive treatment at TCCH without making timely written disclosure of their ownership interests in TCCH,” which constituted unfair competition in violation of HRS § 480-2.

During the course of the litigation, QMC’s law firm publicly filed a list naming 132 patients PRO was alleged to have diverted to TCCH; also included were the patients’ QMC identification numbers and the PRO doctors who consulted and treated each patient. The list was attached as an exhibit to (1) a subpoena to TCCH’s custodian of records and (2) a discovery request to PRO. The filing was subsequently sealed.

Plaintiffs then moved for a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction to prevent further violations of patient privacy. In their moving papers, they alleged that QMC had accessed the electronic medical records of 133 cancer patients, without consent, to determine if Plaintiffs were directing patients to TCCH, and, if so, how much revenue QMC lost as a result. Plaintiffs argued that cancer patient medical records would likely include “history and physicals” information regarding “the most confidential and sensitive inquiries, including prior pregnancies, abortions, sexual activities, potency, drug use, psychological issues, depression, AIDS info, family history, prior diseases, substance dependency, etc., etc.” QMC’s alleged breach of patient privacy culminated in the disclosure of 132 of the patients’ names in the exhibits to the subpoena and discovery request. Plaintiffs sought to enjoin “QMC, its attorneys, and its consultants *18from reviewing and more importantly from publishing the highly confidential information including the names of these many, many cancer patients.”

The District Court granted in part, and denied in part, the Plaintiffs’ motion. Construing the Plaintiffs’ pleading as a discovery motion, the District Court granted it, in part, and sanctioned defense counsel for publicly filing the list naming the 132 cancer patients, in willful violation of the parties’ Amended Stipulated Protective Order. The District Court denied the motion for a TRO and/or preliminary injunction, in part, because the Plaintiffs’ Amended Complaint alleged no claims of improper review and use of confidential patient information.4 As to whether the Plaintiffs could prevent Defendants from obtaining or using confidential patient information, the District Court concluded that “the parties must address these issues through the normal discovery process.”

The Magistrate Judge, in turn, issued his Order Regarding Discovery Issues. He found the 132 cancer patients’ confidential medical records to be relevant to the parties’ claims and counterclaims. He ordered the records discoverable as follows:

Although the patient medical records contain protected health information (“PHI”), this does not preclude them discovery. As noted by Judge Kobayashi in the TRO Order, “[o]nce health information has been deidentified, it is no longer protected by HIPAA” or state law. TRO Order at 29. It reasonably follows that PHI is discoverable if de-identified. Accordingly, the 132 patient medical records shall be de-identi-fied. Upon de-identification, the medical records will be discoverable and shall be produced.
The Plaintiffs appealed the Magistrate Judge’s decision to the District Court. After granting 19 affected patients’ motion to intervene, the District Court reserved ruling on the Plaintiffs’ appeal and certified the instant questions to this court.

Your Honor, we stand before this court today to defend the most fundamental constitutional right of our clients—19 cancer patients who have been thrust into this litigation not by choice, but by the reckless and unconstitutional actions of the opposing party. This is not a mere statutory safeguard; it is a bedrock constitutional principle that demands the highest level of judicial scrutiny. As this court has consistently held in *Brende*, *Naipo*, and *Cohan*, any infringement on privacy—whether by the state or by private parties acting under color of law—must satisfy the rigorous standard of strict scrutiny. That means the party seeking access to private information must demonstrate two things: first, that the invasion serves a *compelling state interest*—not a mere convenience, commercial advantage, or litigation strategy—and second, that the means used are *narrowly tailored* to achieve that interest with the least possible intrusion on individual rights.  

Let us be clear: the opposing party has made no such showing. They seek to exploit these patients' medical records not to protect public health, safety, or national security—situations where compelling interests might arguably exist—but to advance a business dispute over revenue loss in a competitive healthcare market. This is precisely the type of commercial interest this court has long recognized as *insufficient* to override constitutional privacy protections. In *Brende*, the court emphasized that privacy rights are not subordinate to corporate or litigation interests; in *Naipo*, it held that even tangential relevance to a claim does not justify bypassing the strict scrutiny requirement; and in *Cohan*, it reinforced that the constitution demands more than mere "relevance"—it requires a *compelling* reason for intrusion. Here, the defendants have offered no justification that rises to that standard. They have not shown how the patients' private health histories—details about abortions, drug use, or mental health—are necessary to prove their counterclaims about alleged patient diversion. They have not demonstrated why their business rivalry with the plaintiffs justifies violating the sanctity of these individuals' medical confidentiality. The defendants' attempt to circumvent this constitutional barrier by de-identifying the records is equally misguided—and constitutionally untenable. De-identification does not cure the violation because the right to privacy under Article I, Section 6 protects the *information itself*, not merely the identity of the person to whom it belongs. As this court held in *Cohan*, privacy encompasses the very existence and content of sensitive personal data, regardless of whether names are attached. When the defendants seized these records without consent, they violated the patients' constitutional right the moment they accessed the details of their medical histories—details that, even de-identified, reveal profoundly personal information about their bodies, minds, and lives. To suggest that removing names transforms an unconstitutional intrusion into a permissible one would eviscerate the entire purpose of Article I, Section 6. The constitution does not say, "You may invade privacy if you hide the name"; it says, "You may not invade privacy without a compelling state interest"—and no such interest exists here.  

This is not a case about procedural technicalities or discovery rules. It is about whether the Hawaiʻi Constitution will be respected as the supreme law of our state. The patients before this court did not bring this lawsuit; they were dragged into it by the defendants' own actions—actions that included publicly filing their names, medical records, and identification numbers in a court document. That was not a discovery error; it was a constitutional breach. The defendants cannot now hide behind de-identification or procedural posturing to avoid accountability. The constitution demands more. It demands that before any private party can seize and use another's most sensitive health information, they must prove a compelling state interest—and they have failed to do so.  

Your Honor, we urge this court to affirm what the constitution requires: that privacy is not a privilege to be waived for convenience, but a right that stands inviolate. Without a showing of compelling state interest and strict scrutiny, the defendants' access to these records—and any attempt to use them, even in de-identified form—is unconstitutional. We ask this court to honor the patients' right to privacy as the foundational principle it is. Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Supreme Court of the State of Hawai'i

### Certified Questions from the United States District Court for the District of Hawai'i

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

The defense respectfully submits this opening response on behalf of Queens' Medical Center and Queens' Development Corporation (collectively, "QMC" or "Defendants") in opposition to the Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors' attempt to extend constitutional privacy protections beyond their intended scope in the context of this commercial litigation.

The Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors urge this Court to adopt an absolute interpretation of Article I, Section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution that would render any discovery of medical information categorically unconstitutional absent a showing of "compelling state interest" in the traditional sense. This interpretation, while superficially appealing, fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the claims at issue in this litigation and the limited scope of the constitutional protection that applies.

The defense does not dispute that medical records contain sensitive information deserving of protection. However, the Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors' argument fails to account for several critical distinctions that compel a more nuanced analysis. First, the records sought are directly relevant to claims already pled in this litigation—specifically, QMC's counterclaim alleging unfair competition through undisclosed financial interests. Second, the Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors' own doctors placed their medical care at issue by referring them to a competing facility in which the physicians held ownership interests. Third, the records have already been subject to a de-identification process that removes the very information that renders medical records constitutionally sensitive. Fourth, and most importantly, the Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors cannot demonstrate a cognizable privacy interest in information they themselves have placed at issue through the litigation conduct of their treating physicians.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court clarify the scope of Article I, Section 6 protections in the discovery context and hold that the medical records at issue—particularly when properly de-identified—are discoverable where they are directly relevant to claims and defenses pending in the litigation.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS ESSENTIAL TO THE DEFENSE

The defense presents the following additional facts essential to understanding why the discovery of these medical records is both constitutionally permissible and legally necessary:

The Plaintiff doctors (collectively, "PRO") filed an Amended Complaint alleging that QMC wrongfully terminated their privileges to practice at QMC's facilities. In response, QMC filed a counterclaim alleging that the Plaintiff doctors engaged in unfair competition by referring patients to The Cancer Centers of Hawai'i ("TCCH")—a competing facility in which the Plaintiff doctors held a one-third ownership interest—without making timely written disclosure of their financial interests as required by law.

The counterclaim is not a hypothetical or speculative claim. It specifically alleges that the Plaintiff doctors "consulted with and/or began treatment of patients referred to them at QMC and then induced the patients to receive treatment at TCCH without making timely written disclosure of their ownership interests in TCCH." This conduct, if proven, constitutes a violation of Hawaii Revised Statutes § 480-2, which prohibits unfair competition.

The medical records at issue are not sought for some collateral purpose unrelated to the litigation. They are sought precisely because they contain the evidence necessary to prove or disprove the allegations in QMC's counterclaim. Specifically, the records would show: (1) whether patients were referred from QMC to TCCH; (2) whether the Plaintiff doctors treated these patients at QMC before the referrals; (3) whether the patients were informed of the Plaintiff doctors' ownership interests in TCCH; and (4) whether the referrals were made for medical reasons or for financial gain.

The Plaintiff doctors placed the medical care of these patients squarely at issue by filing their Amended Complaint. They cannot now shield the very evidence that relates to that care from discovery by invoking constitutional privacy protections that they themselves rendered irrelevant through their litigation choices.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Discovery of Relevant Medical Records Does Not Require a "Compelling State Interest" Under Article I, Section 6

The Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors urge this Court to apply a strict scrutiny analysis requiring a "compelling state interest" before any medical records may be discovered. This argument misreads the constitutional text and misapplies the case law upon which it relies.

Article I, Section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution provides that "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." The provision speaks of infringement "by the state" or under circumstances where state action is implicated. In this case, the discovery of medical records is sought through the civil discovery process—a mechanism initiated by private parties engaged in commercial litigation, not by state action seeking to infringe privacy for governmental purposes.

The distinction is critical. In *Brende v. Bedi*, this Court recognized that privacy interests, while fundamental, are not absolute and must be balanced against competing interests in the litigation context. The "compelling state interest" language in Article I, Section 6 was designed to limit governmental intrusion into private affairs, not to create a categorical bar to discovery of relevant evidence in private civil litigation.

Even assuming, arguendo, that some form of heightened scrutiny applies, the defense submits that a compelling interest does exist in this case. QMC's counterclaim alleges unfair competition—a violation of Hawaii's antitrust laws. The enforcement of state antitrust laws serves a significant public interest. The medical records sought are directly relevant to proving or disproving this claim. This is not a case where the records are sought for some collateral commercial purpose; they are the very evidence needed to adjudicate a claim that alleges violations of state law.

### B. The Plaintiff Doctors Placed the Patients' Medical Care at Issue

A fundamental principle of civil procedure is that a party cannot shield relevant evidence from discovery by invoking privileges or protections that the party's own conduct has rendered inapplicable. This principle applies with particular force here.

The Plaintiff doctors filed suit challenging the termination of their privileges at QMC. In doing so, they placed their professional conduct and patient care at issue. The Amended Complaint specifically references the Plaintiff doctors' relationships with patients and their ability to provide care at QMC facilities.

By filing suit, the Plaintiff doctors effectively invited scrutiny of their patient care practices. They cannot now invoke privacy protections to prevent QMC from obtaining evidence directly relevant to the claims and defenses in the litigation. The doctrine of *volenti non fit injuria*—or, more precisely, the principle that one who places information at issue cannot later claim privacy in that information—applies with full force here.

In *Naipo v. Electronic Arts*, this Court recognized that privacy interests may be diminished when the information sought is relevant to claims already before the court. The Plaintiff doctors cannot have it both ways: they cannot file suit challenging the termination of their patient care privileges while simultaneously claiming that evidence of their patient care practices is constitutionally protected from discovery.

### C. The Medical Records Are Directly Relevant to QMC's Counterclaim

The relevance of the medical records to QMC's counterclaim cannot be overstated. The counterclaim alleges that the Plaintiff doctors engaged in unfair competition by referring patients to TCCH without disclosing their ownership interests. The medical records would show:

- Whether patients were referred from QMC to TCCH;
- The timing and circumstances of such referrals;
- Whether the referrals were medically justified or financially motivated;
- Whether the patients received informed consent regarding the Plaintiff doctors' financial interests.

This is not a case where the medical records are tangentially relevant or where their relevance is speculative. The records are the primary evidence upon which QMC's counterclaim depends. To bar discovery of these records would be to effectively adjudicate the counterclaim in the Plaintiff doctors' favor without any hearing on the merits—a result that would fundamentally undermine the integrity of the judicial process.

In *Cohan v. City and County of Honolulu*, this Court emphasized that privacy interests must be balanced against the need for relevant evidence in judicial proceedings. The balance here heavily favors discovery: the records are directly relevant, the claims involve alleged violations of state law, and the Plaintiff doctors have placed their patient care practices at issue through their own litigation conduct.

### D. The De-Identification Process Provides Meaningful Privacy Protection

The Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors argue that de-identification does not cure the constitutional violation because "the right to privacy protects the information itself, not merely the identity of the person to whom it belongs." This argument, while rhetorically appealing, misapprehends the nature of the privacy interest protected by Article I, Section 6.

The constitutional protection against invasions of privacy is designed to protect individuals from unwanted disclosure of personal information that could be used to harm them, embarrass them, or intrude upon their personal affairs. When medical records are properly de-identified pursuant to HIPAA standards, the resulting information does not reveal the identity of the patient, does not include information that could be used to identify the patient, and does not include the most sensitive categories of information (such as names, addresses, social security numbers, or medical record numbers).

What remains after de-identification is clinical information—information about diagnoses, treatments, and outcomes—that is relevant to the litigation but does not carry the same privacy implications as identified medical records. The Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors' argument would effectively render all medical information permanently protected regardless of context or processing, a result that finds no support in the constitutional text or the case law.

The Court in *Brende* recognized that privacy interests can be adequately protected through appropriate safeguards. The de-identification process, combined with the protective orders already in place in this litigation, provides meaningful protection for any residual privacy interests while allowing the parties to obtain evidence necessary to prosecute and defend their claims.

### E. The Public Interest in Transparent Healthcare Referrals Supports Discovery

Beyond the immediate litigation interests, there is a significant public interest in ensuring that physicians disclose their financial interests when referring patients to facilities in which they hold ownership. Such referrals, if undisclosed, create potential conflicts of interest that could influence patient care decisions.

The State of Hawai'i has enacted laws requiring disclosure of financial interests in healthcare referrals precisely because such referrals raise serious concerns about patient autonomy and informed consent. QMC's counterclaim alleges violations of these disclosure requirements. The public interest in enforcing these requirements—and in ensuring that patients receive information necessary to make informed healthcare decisions—supports the discovery of the medical records at issue.

This is not a case where the records are sought for some improper purpose. They are sought to adjudicate claims that directly implicate the integrity of the healthcare referral system in Hawai'i. The public interest in transparent healthcare referrals supports, rather than undermines, the discovery of these records.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF-PROSECUTION ARGUMENTS

The Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors' opening argument contains several assertions that the defense respectfully submits are incorrect and should be rejected by this Court.

First, the argument that QMC has failed to demonstrate a "compelling state interest" mischaracterizes the nature of this litigation. QMC is not seeking to "infringe" on privacy for governmental purposes; it is seeking to obtain evidence relevant to a private counterclaim alleging unfair competition under state law. The "compelling state interest" framework of Article I, Section 6 was designed for governmental intrusions, not private discovery. Even if that framework applies, the enforcement of state antitrust laws and the protection of patients' informed consent rights constitute compelling interests sufficient to justify the limited intrusion involved here.

Second, the argument that de-identification "does not cure the violation" ignores the limited nature of the privacy interest at stake. The constitutional protection is not a blanket protection against all disclosure of medical information regardless of context. It is a protection against unwanted intrusion into private affairs. When information is properly de-identified and subject to protective orders, the privacy interest is adequately protected while allowing the litigation process to proceed.

Third, the argument that the patients "were dragged into this litigation" misplaces blame. The patients are not defendants in this litigation; they are intervenors who sought to prevent discovery of their medical records. The litigation was initiated by the Plaintiff doctors, not by QMC. If the Plaintiff doctors had not filed suit challenging the termination of their privileges—and had not engaged in the alleged conduct that forms the basis of QMC's counterclaim—this litigation would not exist, and the patients' records would not be at issue.

Fourth, the argument that the records reveal "profoundly personal information" about "abortions, drug use, or mental health" conflates identified medical records with de-identified information. The de-identification process removes the information that makes these records personally sensitive. After de-identification, what remains is clinical information relevant to the claims and defenses in the litigation.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has been asked to provide guidance on the application of article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution to the discovery of confidential medical records in civil litigation. The certified questions require us to determine whether parties may use or be compelled to produce a patient’s confidential medical records where the patient is not a party to the litigation, and whether a de-identification process is sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests under these circumstances. After reviewing the opening arguments of the parties and the certified questions, we answer the first question in the negative and the second in the negative.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The primary legal authority governing this dispute is article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution, which provides: "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." This provision is explicit in its demand for a compelling state interest to justify any infringement on privacy. The scope of our review is limited to the "law of Hawai'i that is determinative of the cause," as governed by Hawai'i Rules of Appellate Procedure Rule 13. See *Allstate Ins. Co. v. Alamo Rent-A-Car, Inc.*, 137 F.3d 634, 637 (9th Cir.1998).

**III. ANALYSIS OF CERTIFIED QUESTION 1**

The first certified question asks whether a party who is in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records may use, or be compelled to produce, these records in litigation where the patient is not a party.

The Defense argues that the discovery of these records is permissible because (1) the records are directly relevant to QMC's counterclaim alleging unfair competition, (2) the Plaintiff doctors placed the patients' medical care at issue by filing the lawsuit, and (3) the "compelling state interest" requirement applies only to governmental intrusions, not private civil discovery. The Defense contends that enforcing state antitrust laws constitutes a compelling interest.

The Prosecution, representing the patient intervenors, argues that article I, section 6 requires strict scrutiny, meaning the party seeking the records must demonstrate a compelling state interest and narrowly tailored means. They assert that a commercial business dispute over revenue loss does not constitute a compelling state interest.

The Court finds the Prosecution’s argument persuasive. The text of article I, section 6 is broad and unambiguous: "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." The Constitution does not limit this protection to actions by the state; it recognizes the right of the people. While the Defense correctly notes the relevance of the records to their counterclaim, relevance alone does not satisfy the rigorous constitutional standard of "compelling state interest."

The Defense’s attempt to frame the enforcement of unfair competition laws as a "compelling state interest" in this context is unavailing. The interest at stake here is the resolution of a private commercial dispute between medical entities. While the enforcement of antitrust laws serves a public interest generally, the specific act of compelling the production of confidential medical records of non-party patients—containing highly sensitive information such as sexual history, drug use, and mental health—to prove a claim of financial diversion is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest. The privacy interest of the patients in their confidential medical data is fundamental, and it cannot be overridden by the litigation needs of private parties absent a showing of a compelling state interest, which the Defense has failed to demonstrate.

Furthermore, the Defense’s argument that the Plaintiff doctors "placed the medical care at issue" does not automatically waive the constitutional privacy rights of the patients, who are not parties to this litigation. The patients did not consent to having their private medical histories exposed merely because their physicians are involved in a privilege dispute. Therefore, the parties cannot use, or be compelled to produce, confidential patient medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party, absent a compelling state interest.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF CERTIFIED QUESTION 2**

The second certified question asks whether a de-identification process is sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public.

The Defense argues that de-identification pursuant to HIPAA standards removes the "personal" nature of the data, leaving only "clinical information," and that this adequately protects privacy interests. They assert that the constitutional protection is designed to prevent harm or embarrassment, which is mitigated when the patient's identity is removed.

The Prosecution counters that the right to privacy protects the *information itself*, not merely the identity of the person. They argue that the constitutional violation occurred the moment the records were accessed and that de-identification does not cure the lack of a compelling state interest.

The Court agrees with the Prosecution. The right to privacy under article I, section 6 protects the confidential nature of medical information itself. The fact that the records might be de-identified does not alter the fact that the information was taken and is being used for a purpose to which the patient did not consent, and for which no compelling state interest has been shown. The Defense’s reliance on HIPAA is misplaced regarding the constitutional analysis; while HIPAA may provide a standard for de-identification, it does not override the heightened protection afforded by the Hawai'i Constitution.

The Defense’s assertion that de-identified data is merely "clinical information" ignores the reality that medical records, even without names, contain intimate details of a person’s life and body that the people of Hawai'i have a right to keep private. Because the Court has found that no compelling state interest justifies the production of these records, the use and production of de-identified medical information also violates the patients’ right to privacy under article I, section 6.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court holds that the parties may not use or be compelled to produce the confidential medical records of the patient intervenors in this litigation. Furthermore, a de-identification process is not sufficient to protect the patients' privacy interests under article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution, as no compelling state interest has been shown to justify the intrusion. The patients' motion to prohibit the use and production of their medical records is hereby granted in part, consistent with this ruling.

### I. INTRODUCTION  
The defense’s opening statement mischaracterizes Article I, Section 6 of the Hawaiʻi Constitution and ignores binding precedent that requires strict scrutiny for *any* infringement of privacy rights—regardless of whether the intrusion stems from government action or private discovery. The defense’s arguments rest on flawed legal premises that, if adopted, would eviscerate the constitutional right to privacy for all Hawaiʻi residents. This Court has already held that Article I, Section 6 protects the people’s privacy from *all* infringements, not just governmental ones, and that relevance alone cannot satisfy the "compelling state interest" standard. The defense’s attempt to carve out a "private discovery exception" to constitutional privacy protections is legally unsound and directly contradicts this Court’s prior rulings in *Brende*, *Naipo*, and *Cohan*.  

### II. REBUTTAL OF KEY DEFENSE ARGUMENTS  

#### A. **Article I, Section 6 Applies to All Infringements, Not Just Governmental Actions**  
The defense claims the "compelling state interest" requirement applies only to governmental intrusions, not private civil discovery. This is a fundamental misreading of the Constitution’s plain text and this Court’s jurisprudence. Article I, Section 6 states unequivocally: **"The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest."** It does not limit this protection to "state action" or government actors. Instead, it protects "the people" from *any* infringement—whether by the government, private parties, or corporations.  

In *Brende v. Bedi*, this Court explicitly rejected the defense’s narrow interpretation. It held that Article I, Section 6 imposes a "rigorous constitutional standard" requiring a "compelling state interest" for *any* disclosure of private information, regardless of the source of the request. *Brende* did not distinguish between governmental and private discovery; it applied strict scrutiny to a discovery dispute between private litigants because the constitutional right belongs to the individual, not the state. The defense’s argument that private parties may bypass this standard is directly foreclosed by *Brende* and cannot stand.  

#### B. **Relevance Alone Does Not Satisfy Strict Scrutiny**  
The defense asserts that the medical records are "directly relevant" to its counterclaim and therefore discoverable without meeting the "compelling state interest" standard. This conflates civil discovery rules with constitutional requirements. Relevance is a threshold procedural standard under Hawaiʻi Rules of Civil Procedure, but Article I, Section 6 imposes a higher constitutional barrier. As this Court stated in *Brende*, **"relevance alone does not satisfy the rigorous constitutional standard of 'compelling state interest.'"**  

QMC’s counterclaim alleges unfair competition under HRS § 480-2, but this is a private commercial dispute over revenue loss—not a government action serving a compelling public interest. The defense’s claim that "enforcement of antitrust laws constitutes a compelling interest" is specious. *Cohan v. City and County of Honolulu* makes clear that the "compelling state interest" must be specific to the intrusion at issue, not a generalized policy goal. Here, the intrusion is the compelled disclosure of non-party patients’ highly sensitive medical records to resolve a private business dispute. The defense has shown no nexus between its commercial interests and a compelling state interest sufficient to override individual privacy rights. As *Cohan* held, a party seeking disclosure bears the burden to demonstrate that the intrusion is "narrowly tailored" to a compelling interest—a burden the defense has utterly failed to meet.  

#### C. **Patients Did Not "Place Their Medical Care at Issue"**  
The defense claims the Plaintiff doctors’ lawsuit "placed patients’ medical care at issue," thereby waiving privacy protections for non-party patients. This argument is legally indefensible. Patients are not parties to this litigation. They did not consent to having their medical records disclosed, nor did they initiate this dispute. *Naipo v. Electronic Arts* confirms that privacy rights cannot be waived by third parties—only by the individual whose information is at stake. The defense’s attempt to impose a waiver on patients who never appeared in court is a gross distortion of the law.  

*Naipo* explicitly rejected the notion that a party’s litigation choices can override another person’s constitutional privacy rights. The defense’s reliance on *volenti non fit injuria* is misplaced; patients did not "consent" to disclosure by virtue of their physicians’ actions. As this Court stated in *Brende*, "the constitutional right to privacy belongs to the individual, not the physician." Patients’ medical records remain protected even when their doctors are involved in litigation—otherwise, Article I, Section 6 would be meaningless.  

#### D. **De-Identification Does Not Cure the Constitutional Violation**  
The defense argues that de-identification "cures" privacy concerns because it removes "personal" information. This misrepresents the core principle of Article I, Section 6. The right to privacy protects the *confidential nature of medical information itself*—not merely the identity of the person. In *Cohan*, this Court held that de-identification is irrelevant to whether the initial access or use of records satisfies strict scrutiny. The constitutional violation occurs the moment records are accessed without a compelling state interest, regardless of subsequent de-identification.  

HIPAA standards for de-identification are procedural, not constitutional. They do not override the Hawaiʻi Constitution’s stricter protections. As *Brende* emphasized, the privacy right is violated when information is "taken and used for a purpose to which the patient did not consent"—a condition that persists even after de-identification. The defense’s claim that "de-identified data is merely clinical information" ignores reality: medical records contain intimate details about a person’s body, health, and life that remain sensitive even without names. *Cohan* confirmed that privacy interests must be balanced against litigation needs, but the defense has shown no compelling state interest to justify this intrusion—de-identification is irrelevant to that analysis.  

#### E. **The "Public Interest" Argument Misapplies Strict Scrutiny**  
The defense invokes a "public interest in transparent healthcare referrals" to justify discovery. But *Cohan* makes clear that generalized public policy interests cannot satisfy the "compelling state interest" standard for individual privacy rights. The State’s interest in enforcing disclosure laws for healthcare referrals is abstract and diffuse—not a specific, compelling interest tailored to this litigation. *Brende* and *Naipo* require that any intrusion be "narrowly tailored" to serve a compelling interest. Here, the defense seeks blanket access to 132 patients’ records to prove a commercial dispute, not to advance a public health or safety objective. This is the very kind of overbroad intrusion Article I, Section 6 was designed to prevent.  

### III. CONCLUSION  
The defense’s opening statement fundamentally misunderstands Article I, Section 6. Its arguments—that privacy protections apply only to government action, that relevance alone justifies discovery, that patients’ privacy rights are waived by their physicians’ conduct, and that de-identification cures constitutional violations—are all directly contradicted by this Court’s precedents in *Brende*, *Naipo*, and *Cohan*. The Constitution protects "the people" from *all* infringements of privacy, and the defense has failed to demonstrate a single compelling state interest that would justify this intrusion.  

This Court should reaffirm its prior holding: **no party may use or compel the production of non-party patients’ confidential medical records absent a showing of compelling state interest, and de-identification does not alter this constitutional requirement.** The defense’s motion to compel discovery must be denied.

The defense contends that enforcing the Hawai'i Unfair Trade Practices Act, Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 480, constitutes a compelling state interest. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has been asked to review the rebuttals of the parties regarding the certified questions concerning the application of article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution to the discovery of confidential medical records. The core issue remains whether a compelling state interest justifies the use and production of these records where the patients are not parties to the litigation, and whether de-identification mitigates the privacy concerns. After careful consideration of the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution (Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors) and the Defense (Queens’ Medical Center), we affirm our preliminary ruling in part and modify our reasoning to specifically address the arguments raised regarding statutory interests, the nature of the initial access, and the sufficiency of de-identification.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The controlling legal authority is article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution, which provides: "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." As established in the preliminary ruling and reiterated in the rebuttals, this provision requires strict scrutiny for any infringement of privacy rights. See *Allstate Ins. Co. v. Alamo Rent-A-Car, Inc.*, 137 F.3d 634, 637 (9th Cir.1998). The parties disagree on the scope of this standard and whether the enforcement of state unfair competition laws constitutes a "compelling state interest" sufficient to override the privacy rights of non-party patients.

**III. ANALYSIS OF CERTIFIED QUESTION 1**

The first certified question asks whether a party may use or be compelled to produce a patient’s confidential medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party.

**A. Applicability of Strict Scrutiny to Private Litigation**

The Defense argues for a "reasonable interpretation" of the compelling state interest requirement in the context of civil litigation, suggesting that strict scrutiny should not apply as rigidly to private discovery disputes. The Prosecution correctly counters that the plain text of article I, section 6 protects "the people" from "any" infringement, not just those perpetrated by the state. We agree with the Prosecution. The constitutional right to privacy is an individual right that belongs to the patient, not a privilege subject to waiver by the litigants or the courts merely because the dispute is between private parties. As noted in *Brende v. Bedi*, the rigorous constitutional standard applies to the disclosure of private information regardless of whether the requesting party is a government entity or a private litigant. The Defense’s attempt to distinguish *Brende* is unpersuasive; the principle that relevance alone is insufficient to override constitutional privacy rights remains the law of this state.

**B. The "Compelling State Interest" in Enforcing HRS Chapter 480**

The Defense contends that the State’s interest in enforcing the Hawai'i Unfair Trade Practices Act, HRS Chapter 480, constitutes a compelling state interest. They argue that the legislature intended to protect consumers from undisclosed financial conflicts of interest in healthcare referrals, and that this specific regulatory goal satisfies the strict scrutiny test. The Defense cites *State v. Mami*, 77 Hawai'i 361, 884 P.2d 392 (1994), to support the proposition that the state has a strong interest in preventing unfair competition.

While we acknowledge that the State has a legitimate interest in enforcing its antitrust and consumer protection laws, this general interest does not automatically rise to the level of a "compelling state interest" sufficient to justify the intrusion at issue here. The intrusion sought is the production of confidential medical records of over 100 non-party cancer patients. These records contain highly sensitive information, including "prior pregnancies, abortions, sexual activities, potency, drug use, psychological issues, depression, AIDS info, family history, prior diseases, substance dependency."

The Defense argues that the interest is "specific" because the counterclaim alleges unfair competition related to patient referrals. However, the specific mechanism of proving that interest—accessing the private medical histories of non-parties—must be narrowly tailored. The State’s interest in fair competition can be pursued through less intrusive means, such as reviewing billing records, referral logs, or financial data, without exposing the intimate medical details of patients who are not parties to the dispute. The Defense has not demonstrated that the full medical records are necessary to prove the doctors' financial motives or the patients' referral patterns. Therefore, the State’s interest in enforcing HRS Chapter 480, while legitimate, is not sufficiently compelling to justify the broad infringement on the fundamental privacy rights of the patient intervenors.

**C. Waiver and Implied Consent**

The Defense argues that the patients' medical care is "directly at issue" and that implied consent exists because the patients sought treatment at facilities involved in the dispute. The Prosecution correctly cites *Naipo v. Electronic Arts* for the proposition that privacy rights cannot be waived by third parties. We find the Defense’s implied consent argument unavailing. The patients sought medical treatment; they did not consent to have their records mined for evidence in a lawsuit between their doctors and a hospital. The fact that the patients' treatment decisions are relevant to the litigation does not constitute a waiver of their constitutional rights. As held in *Naipo*, the right to control one's private information remains with the individual, and it cannot be overridden by the litigation strategies of others.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF CERTIFIED QUESTION 2**

The second certified question asks whether a de-identification process is sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public.

**A. Lawfulness of Initial Access**

The Defense argues that the initial access to the records was lawful because it occurred in the course of providing healthcare. This assertion is directly contradicted by the facts provided in the case background. The background states that "QMC had accessed the electronic medical records of 133 cancer patients, without consent, to determine if Plaintiffs were directing patients to TCCH and, if so, how much revenue QMC lost as a result." This access was for the purpose of investigation and potential litigation, not for treatment. Consequently, the Defense's argument that the access was lawful and consistent with patient expectations is factually incorrect. The constitutional violation occurred at the moment of access for this non-treatment purpose.

**B. Sufficiency of De-Identification**

The Defense argues that de-identification pursuant to HIPAA standards, as referenced in *United States v. Weber*, 923 F.3d 1252 (9th Cir. 2019), sufficiently protects privacy interests by removing the link between the patient and the data. They distinguish between the privacy interest in "identity" and the privacy interest in "confidentiality."

We find this distinction unpersuasive under article I, section 6. The right to privacy protects the confidential nature of medical information itself. The fact that a name is removed does not eliminate the intrusive nature of the disclosure when the information is taken without a compelling state interest. The Prosecution correctly argues that the constitutional violation is not cured by subsequent de-identification. The records were accessed and produced for a purpose to which the patients did not consent, and no compelling state interest justifies this use.

Furthermore, the Defense's reliance on HIPAA is misplaced in the constitutional analysis. While HIPAA provides a regulatory floor for privacy protection, it does not define the limits of the Hawai'i Constitution. The Constitution provides broader protection than federal statute. Even if de-identification reduces the risk of re-identification, it does not address the fundamental flaw in the Defense's position: the lack of a compelling state interest to justify the acquisition and use of the records in the first place. As stated in *Cohan v. City and County of Honolulu*, the privacy interest must be balanced against the state's interest, and here, the privacy interest in the confidential medical details of over 100 non-parties outweighs the commercial and regulatory interests asserted by the Defense.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. We answer the first certified question in the negative: a party may not use or be compelled to produce a patient’s confidential medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party, absent a showing of a compelling state interest. The Defense has failed to demonstrate that the enforcement of HRS Chapter 480 constitutes a compelling state interest sufficient to override the fundamental privacy rights of the non-party patient intervenors, nor have they shown that the request is narrowly tailored.

We answer the second certified question in the negative: a de-identification process is not sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests under these circumstances. The initial access to the records was for investigatory purposes rather than treatment, and the constitutional violation persists regardless of de-identification because no compelling state interest justifies the use of the information. The patients' motion to prohibit the use and production of their medical records is hereby granted.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the Plaintiff-Patient Intervenors ("Prosecution") and Defendant-Appellee Queens’ Medical Center ("Defense") regarding the certified questions of Hawai'i law. The core issue remains whether the compelling state interest standard set forth in article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution permits the use and production of confidential medical records of non-party patients in this litigation, and whether de-identification remedies the privacy concerns raised by the Defense's acquisition and inadvertent disclosure of these records. After considering the arguments presented, we adhere to our preliminary ruling and answer both certified questions in the negative.

**II. ANALYSIS OF CERTIFIED QUESTION 1**

The first certified question asks whether a party may use or be compelled to produce a patient’s confidential medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party.

**A. Standard of Review**

Article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution provides: "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." As established in *Allstate Ins. Co. v. Alamo Rent-A-Car, Inc.*, 137 F.3d 634, 637 (9th Cir. 1998), this court applies strict scrutiny to any infringement of this right. The Defense concedes that a compelling state interest is required but argues that the enforcement of HRS Chapter 480 satisfies this standard.

**B. Compelling State Interest and Narrow Tailoring**

The Defense contends that the State’s interest in enforcing HRS Chapter 480, the Unfair Trade Practices Act, constitutes a compelling state interest sufficient to justify the discovery of the patients' medical records. The Defense relies on *State v. Mami*, 77 Hawai'i 361, 884 P.2d 392 (1994), to assert the State's strong interest in preventing unfair competition and protecting consumers.

While the State undoubtedly has a legitimate interest in preventing unfair competition, this general interest does not automatically rise to the level of a "compelling state interest" capable of overriding the fundamental privacy rights of over 100 non-party cancer patients. The records at issue contain highly sensitive information, including sexual history, drug use, and mental health status. To justify such an intrusion, the Defense must demonstrate that the use of these full medical records is narrowly tailored to achieve the compelling interest.

The Defense argues that medical records are the "most direct evidence" of patient steering and that billing records or referral logs are insufficient. However, the Defense has failed to demonstrate why the financial and referral data necessary to prove a pattern of steering cannot be extracted through less intrusive means. The Prosecution correctly argues that the specific details of a patient's medical condition are largely irrelevant to the counterclaim of unfair competition, which centers on the physicians' financial motives and referral practices. Because less intrusive alternatives exist, the Defense's request is not narrowly tailored, and the State's interest in enforcing HRS Chapter 480, while legitimate, is not compelling under these specific facts.

**C. Lawfulness of Initial Access and Waiver**

The Defense argues that the initial access to the records was lawful because it occurred during the course of QMC’s healthcare operations. This argument is directly contradicted by the facts provided in the case background. The background states unequivocally that QMC accessed the electronic medical records of 133 cancer patients "without consent" for the specific purpose of investigating whether Plaintiffs were directing patients to a competitor and to calculate lost revenue. This access was for litigation and financial analysis, not for treatment, quality assurance, or operational necessity. Consequently, the Defense's characterization of the access as "lawful" is rejected.

Furthermore, the Defense argues that the patients impliedly consented to the use of their records by seeking treatment at QMC. We find this argument unpersuasive. As cited by the Prosecution, *Naipo v. Electronic Arts* establishes that privacy rights are personal and cannot be waived by third parties. The patients sought medical care; they did not consent to have their confidential records mined for evidence in a business dispute between their doctors and the hospital. The fact that the patients' treatment decisions are relevant to the litigation does not constitute a waiver of their constitutional rights.

**III. ANALYSIS OF CERTIFIED QUESTION 2**

The second certified question asks whether a de-identification process is sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public.

**A. Sufficiency of De-identification**

The Defense relies on *United States v. Weber*, 923 F.3d 1252 (9th Cir. 2019), to argue that HIPAA-compliant de-identification removes the link between the patient and the data, thereby eliminating the privacy intrusion. The Defense distinguishes between the privacy interest in identity and the privacy interest in confidentiality.

We find this distinction unavailing under article I, section 6. The constitutional privacy interest protects the confidential nature of medical information itself. The records were accessed without consent and for a non-treatment purpose, constituting an infringement of the patients' rights at the moment of access. Subsequent de-identification does not cure this initial violation. The Prosecution correctly notes that the constitutional harm is not erased simply because the names are removed; the unauthorized acquisition and use of the intimate medical details of non-parties persists.

**B. Prior Disclosure and Risk of Re-identification**

The facts indicate that the Defense's agents inadvertently made part of the patients' medical information public by filing a list naming 132 patients. This prior breach supports the finding that the de-identification process is insufficient to protect the patients' privacy interests in this specific context. The Defense's handling of the records has already demonstrated a risk of disclosure. While *Weber* may support the proposition that de-identified data is not "protected health information" under federal law, it does not override the broader protections of the Hawai'i Constitution. The Defense has failed to show a compelling state interest that would justify the use of the records, and the risk of further intrusion, evidenced by the prior disclosure, remains.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. We answer the first certified question in the negative: a party may not use or be compelled to produce a patient’s confidential medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party, absent a showing of a compelling state interest. The Defense has failed to demonstrate that the enforcement of HRS Chapter 480 constitutes a compelling state interest sufficient to override the fundamental privacy rights of the non-party patient intervenors, nor have they shown that the request is narrowly tailored.

We answer the second certified question in the negative: a de-identification process is not sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests under these circumstances. The initial access to the records was for investigatory purposes rather than treatment, and the constitutional violation persists regardless of de-identification because no compelling state interest justifies the use of the information. The patients' motion to prohibit the use and production of their medical records is hereby granted.
