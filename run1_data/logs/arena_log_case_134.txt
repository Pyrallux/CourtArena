COURT ARENA LOG - Case 134
FACTS:
DECISION AND JOURNAL ENTRY
HENSAL, Presiding Judge.
{¶ 1} Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, appeals her conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs from the Summit County Court of Common Pleas. This Court affirms.
I.
{¶ 2} This case involves the discovery of a methamphetamine lab and Ms. Kittle's involvement with same. Officer Lemonier from the Akron Police Department testified that he and another officer conducted surveillance on an apartment where they believed illegal activity was occurring. The officers observed Ms. Kittle and another woman, J.S., leave the apartment and get into Ms. Kittle's car, with J.S. taking the driver's seat. Shortly thereafter, the officers performed a traffic stop on the vehicle. During the stop, the officers discovered that J.S. did not have a driver's license and that she had an outstanding felony warrant. As a result, they placed her under arrest and searched her, finding three bags of methamphetamine in her purse. The officers did not arrest or search Ms. Kittle, but did request that she empty her pockets, which revealed no illegal substances.
{¶ 3} The officers then returned to the apartment and knocked on the door. After about twenty minutes of waiting outside, one of the occupants allowed the officers inside. Upon entering, Officer Lemonier immediately smelled the distinct odor associated with the production of methamphetamine. The occupant then consented to a search of the apartment, which revealed a number of items associated with the production of methamphetamine, as well as several small bags containing the finished product.
{¶ 4} Through his investigation of the matter, including an interview with J.S., Officer Lemonier discovered information that implicated Ms. Kittle. Another officer located Ms. Kittle and brought her to the Akron Police Department for questioning. Officer Lemonier conducted the interview, wherein Ms. Kittle admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.
*1243{¶ 5} A Grand Jury indicted Ms. Kittle on the following three counts: (1) illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs in violation of Revised Code Section 2925.041(A) ; (2) illegal manufacture of drugs in violation of Section 2925.04(A); and (3) aggravated possession of drugs in violation of 2925.11(A),(C)(1). The State dismissed the latter two counts prior to trial.
{¶ 6} The case proceeded to a bench trial. The State presented testimony from one witness: Officer Lemonier. The State also played portions of Officer Lemonier's interview with Ms. Kittle, and introduced records from The National Precursor Log Exchange ("NPLEX"), which is a system that electronically tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine. Ms. Kittle did not present any evidence.
{¶ 7} The trial court found Ms. Kittle guilty, and subsequently sentenced her to five years of imprisonment. She now appeals, raising three assignments of error for our review.
II.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ADMITTED THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE AND ALLOWED A POLICE OFFICER WHO WAS NOT A QUALIFIED WITNESS TO LAY THE FOUNDATION FOR INTRODUCING THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE, IN VIOLATION OF MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS PURSUANT TO ARTICLE I, SECTION 10, OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE OF THE SIXTH AMENDMENT OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 8} In her first assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records because he was not the custodian of those records, nor was he otherwise qualified to do so. Ms. Kittle asserts that a de novo standard of review applies because the trial court's error in this regard implicated her rights under the Confrontation Clause.
{¶ 9} We will begin by addressing the standard of review. While Ms. Kittle's assignment of error suggests that she believes NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause, she does not develop an argument in that regard. See App.R. 16(A)(7). Rather, she cites Ohio Supreme Court precedent for the proposition that business records are nontestimonial, and states: "[a]ssuming that the NPLEX records of the pharmacy admitted into evidence in [this] case were truly nontestimonial in nature, the foundation for their proper authentication as 'business records' under the meaning of Evid.R. 803(6) could not have been established by [Officer Lemonier]." Ms. Kittle does, however, develop such an argument in her reply brief, arguing that NPLEX records are testimonial because the primary purpose of that system is to "assist in the interdiction of methamphetamine creation and use[.]"
{¶ 10} As an initial matter, we note that "[a]ppellate courts generally will not consider a new issue presented for the first time in a reply brief." State v. Quarterman , 140 Ohio St.3d 464, 2014-Ohio-4034, 19 N.E.3d 900, ¶ 18. Notwithstanding, we are unpersuaded by Ms. Kittle's argument that NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause. This is because "[t]estimonial statements are those created or *1244given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." State v. Garcia , 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, quoting Ohio v. Clark , --- U.S. ----, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015). The purpose of the NPLEX system, however, is to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. See R.C. 3715.05 ; State v. Coleman , 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 ("The purpose of the NPLEx system is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets."). We cannot say that NPLEX records are "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." Garcia at id. ; United States v. Collins , 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir.2015) (holding that a similar "MethCheck" record was "not clearly testimonial in nature," and stating that "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial."). We, therefore, reject Ms. Kittle's contentions that her rights under the Confrontation Clause were implicated, and that a de novo standard of review applies.
{¶ 11} We now turn to Ms. Kittle's argument that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records. This Court reviews a trial court's decision to admit a business record into evidence for an abuse of discretion. State v. Baker , 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 9 ; Coleman at ¶ 35-42 (acknowledging that a NPLEX record is a business record). "A trial court is deemed to abuse its discretion where it admits a business record when the party lays an inadequate foundation to establish its admissibility in accordance with Evid.R. 803(6)." Baker at ¶ 9.
{¶ 12} Evidence Rule 803(6) provides that a business record is excepted from the hearsay rule if it is "kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity, and if it was the regular practice of that business activity to make the * * * record * * *, all as shown by the testimony of the custodian or other qualified witness or as provided by Rule 901(B)(10)[.]" "A 'qualified witness' for this purpose would be someone with 'enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business.' " (Citation omitted.) State v. Hood , 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, quoting 5 McLaughlin, Weinstein's Federal Evidence , Section 803.08[8][a] (2d Ed.2009). Notably, "[t]he witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." Baker at ¶ 11, citing Evid.R. 803(6). The witness, however, must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." Id. , quoting Keeva J. Kekst Architects, Inc. v. George Dev. Group , 8th Dist. Cuyahoga No. 70835, 1997 WL 253171, *5 (May 15, 1997).
{¶ 13} Here, Officer Lemonier testified that, through his training as a law enforcement officer, he has personal knowledge as to how pharmacies use the NPLEX system to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. He testified that he applied for-and was granted-a license to access the system, which requires a username and password. He indicated that he accesses the NPLEX system every day, and that he *1245receives real-time alerts for pseudoephedrine purchases.
{¶ 14} Regarding the process for electronically tracking purchases, Officer Lemonier explained that a person attempting to buy pseudoephedrine must provide an ID, which is then swiped. As soon as the ID is swiped, the information contained thereon is immediately transmitted to the NPLEX database. Within a few minutes, more specific information is transmitted, including the brand name, pill count, grams of pseudoephedrine contained in the package, and whether the purchase was accepted or denied. He stated that a purchase will be denied if the individual attempts to purchase more than nine grams of pseudoephedrine during a 30-day period.
{¶ 15} Officer Lemonier testified that he personally obtained the NPLEX record for Ms. Kittle, which was introduced as an exhibit, and that the record is kept in the ordinary course of business. To obtain the record, he entered Ms. Kittle's name, date of birth, and driver's license number into the NPLEX system, which then generated a list of pseudoephedrine purchases associated with that information. Officer Lemonier then testified as to the purchases of pseudoephedrine reflected in the NPLEX record.
{¶ 16} While not every police officer may have familiarity with the NPLEX system or the ability to explain how those records came into existence in the ordinary course of business, the record reflects that Officer Lemonier did, in fact, have this knowledge. He, therefore, was a "qualified witness" for purposes of Evidence Rule 803(6). Thus, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion when it admitted the NPLEX records into evidence. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ABDICATED ITS DUTIES AS A NEUTRAL AND IMPARTIAL FINDER OF FACT BY DIRECTING THE PROSECUTION TO SUPPLEMENT ITS EVIDENCE AGAINST MS. KITTLE MID-TRIAL, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO A FAIR TRIAL AND DUE PROCESS UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS TO THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 17} In her second assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its duties as a neutral and impartial finder of fact by directing the State to supplement its evidence against her at trial. Similar to her first assignment of error, she contends that a de novo standard of review applies, arguing that the trial court's actions deprived her of a fair trial. We disagree that a de novo standard of review applies, and otherwise find no merit in her argument.
{¶ 18} Evidence Rule 614(B) provides that "[t]he court may interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." As this Court has stated, " '[t]he evident purpose of Evid. R. 614(B) is to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness.' " State v. Grad , 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, quoting State v. Armstrong , 2d Dist. Montgomery No. 13498, 1993 WL 294834, *5 (Aug. 6, 1993). When, as is the case here, the matter is proceeding as a bench trial, the trial judge is afforded greater flexibility in questioning a witness because there is no risk of prejudicially *1246influencing a jury. Id. " 'In absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony, it will be presumed that the trial court acted with impartiality [in propounding to the witness questions from the bench] in attempting to ascertain a material fact or to develop the truth.' " (Alterations sic.) State v. Baston , 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), quoting Jenkins v. Clark , 7 Ohio App.3d 93, 98, 454 N.E.2d 541 (2d Dist.1982). "In reviewing any alleged prejudicial errors resulting from the court's own interrogation of a witness, a reviewing court will examine the questions in light of the entire record and, based upon the totality of the circumstances, determine whether there has been a manifest abuse of discretion." Grad at ¶ 43, quoting State v. Hoover , 9th Dist. Medina No. 1549, 1987 WL 12247, *2 (June 3, 1987).
{¶ 19} Here, when cross-examining Officer Lemonier, Ms. Kittle's counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record did not contain certain information, such as the form of identification used to purchase the pseudoephedrine. Officer Lemonier indicated that if he logged into the NPLEX system and clicked on a certain field, that information would populate onto the record, which he could then print. He further indicated that it was not his typical practice to print that information, and that it had never been an issue in prior trials that he has been involved in.
{¶ 20} The trial court noted that it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth[,]" and did not fault the State for failing to provide an expanded version of the NPLEX record. The trial court then allowed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version, which included the missing information. Despite the trial court's willingness to allow Ms. Kittle's counsel a few days to review the record and prepare accordingly, her counsel elected to proceed as scheduled and chose not to cross-examine Officer Lemonier on the expanded record.
{¶ 21} Based upon the totality of the circumstances present in this case, we cannot say that the trial court's actions resulted in a manifest abuse of discretion. Id. at ¶ 43. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III
THE TRIAL COURT COMMITTED REVERSIBLE ERROR WHEN IT AMENDED THE INDICTMENT ON THE MORNING OF TRIAL TO EXPAND THE SINGLE DATE DETERMINED BY THE GRAND JURY TO INCLUDE THE ENTIRE PREVIOUS WEEK, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO DUE PROCESS AND INDICTMENT BY A GRAND JURY UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH, AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 22} In her third assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing the State to amend the timeframe of the indictment to include the previous week. We disagree.
{¶ 23} "We review a trial court's decision to allow the amendment of an indictment for an abuse of discretion." State v. Sauto , 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. An abuse of discretion implies that the court's attitude is unreasonable, arbitrary or unconscionable. Blakemore v. Blakemore , 5 Ohio St.3d 217, 219, 450 N.E.2d 1140 (1983). To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show that: (1) the trial court abused its discretion; and (2) the amendment prejudiced her defense.
*1247State v. Dudukovich , 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.
{¶ 24} Here, the indictment listed the date of the illegal-assembly-or-possession-of-chemicals-for-the-manufacture-of-drugs offense as on or about December 2, 2014 (the date the officers discovered the methamphetamine lab). On the morning of trial, the trial court allowed the State to extend the timeframe of the indictment to include the preceding seven days. Despite allowing the State to amend the indictment, the trial court ultimately determined that the evidence indicated that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, that she gave those pills to J.S. so that J.S. could make methamphetamine, and that methamphetamine was made on December 2, 2014. The trial court's journal entry indicates that it found Ms. Kittle guilty based upon the December 2, 2014, date only. Thus, even assuming without deciding that the trial court abused its discretion, Ms. Kittle cannot establish that she suffered prejudice and, consequently, cannot establish reversible error. Id. at ¶ 16. Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error is overruled.
III.
{¶ 25} Ms. Kittle's assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
Judgment affirmed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## DEFENSE OPENING BRIEF

---

### I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, respectfully submits this defense opening response to rebut the prosecution's arguments and to demonstrate that the judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas should be reversed. The prosecution's opening argument fails to address the fundamental deficiencies in the evidence presented at trial and the procedural errors that plagued the proceedings below. This Court should reverse the conviction because: (1) the NPLEX records were improperly admitted through a witness lacking the requisite foundation; (2) the trial court improperly intervened on behalf of the prosecution; and (3) the evidence was insufficient to establish that Ms. Kittle committed the offense of illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs.

---

### II. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING NPLEX RECORDS ADMISSIBILITY

#### A. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial and Implicate the Confrontation Clause

The prosecution contends that the NPLEX records are nontestimonial in nature and therefore do not implicate the Confrontation Clause. This Court should reject this argument. The primary purpose of the NPLEX system, while nominally a tracking mechanism for pseudoephedrine purchases, has evolved into a critical law enforcement tool specifically designed to combat methamphetamine manufacturing. See State v. Coleman, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 ("The purpose of the NPLEx system is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets."). The fact that the system serves a dual purpose—regulatory compliance and law enforcement—does not render it nontestimonial.

In Ohio v. Clark, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015), the United States Supreme Court held that testimonial statements are those "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." The prosecution argues that because pharmacy employees creating NPLEX records do not anticipate their use at trial, the records are nontestimonial. However, this reasoning is flawed. The Clark Court explicitly recognized that the primary purpose analysis must consider the context in which the statements are made. Here, the NPLEX system was created pursuant to Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05, which specifically mandates electronic tracking "for the purpose of monitoring" pseudoephedrine purchases "to assist in the interdiction of methamphetamine creation and use." The very statutory purpose of NPLEX is law enforcement-oriented—to assist in drug interdiction. This distinguishes NPLEX records from traditional business records like invoices or receipts, which serve commercial rather than investigative purposes.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Collins, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), is misplaced. While that case held similar "MethCheck" records were "not clearly testimonial," the Collins court expressly acknowledged that the question was not settled and that the records served a law enforcement purpose. Moreover, as the defense noted in its appellate brief, the Collins decision is non-binding authority from the Sixth Circuit. This Court should follow the reasoning of Ohio appellate courts that have more carefully examined the nature of NPLEX records and should apply a stricter interpretation of the primary purpose test.

#### B. Officer Lemonier Was Not a Qualified Witness

Even assuming arguendo that NPLEX records are nontestimonial business records, the prosecution's argument fails because Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" capable of laying the proper foundation under Evidence Rule 803(6).

Evidence Rule 803(6) requires that business records be admitted through "the testimony of the custodian or other qualified witness." A qualified witness must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." State v. Hood, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40. The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier's daily access to NPLEX and his understanding of how the system works qualifies him as a witness. However, this conflates knowledge of how to access a system with knowledge of how the system creates and maintains records.

The critical distinction is this: Officer Lemonier knows how to retrieve records from NPLEX because he uses the system as a law enforcement tool. He does not know—nor did he testify to knowing—how pharmacy employees input data into the system, how NPLEX maintains data integrity, or whether the specific records at issue were made in the regular course of business. His testimony established only that he entered Ms. Kittle's information into the system and retrieved a report. This is insufficient.

In State v. Baker, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, this Court held that a witness must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." Officer Lemonier's testimony did not establish his familiarity with the preparation or maintenance of NPLEX records—he testified only to his use of the system as an end-user. A police officer who uses a database is not equivalent to a custodian of that database or a qualified witness familiar with the record-keeping system.

The prosecution's argument that Officer Lemonier was qualified because he "explained the real-time process" is unavailing. Explaining how a system functions technologically is not the same as testifying that records are kept in the regular course of business. The witness must establish the business records exception requirements: that the records were made at or near the time of the transaction, by or from information transmitted by a person with knowledge, and that the records were kept in the ordinary course of business. Officer Lemonier never testified that he had personal knowledge of any of these elements from the business side of the operation.

### III. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING TRIAL COURT'S QUESTIONING

The prosecution fails to address the defense's second assignment of error regarding the trial court's improper intervention. The facts establish that during cross-examination, defense counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record did not contain certain information. Rather than permitting the defense to explore this deficiency, the trial court interjected and directed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version of the record. The trial court stated it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth" and allowed the prosecution to supplement its evidence.

This Court should reject the prosecution's implicit endorsement of such behavior. While Evidence Rule 614(B) permits the court to "interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner," the rule's evident purpose is to prevent the trial judge from conveying impressions as to witness credibility. See State v. Grad, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. In a bench trial, the trial judge serves as both the arbiter of credibility and the determiner of guilt. When the trial court actively assists the prosecution in filling evidentiary gaps, it abandons its role as a neutral arbiter.

The prosecution argues that no prejudice resulted because defense counsel was given time to review the expanded record and prepare. However, this ignores the structural unfairness of the proceeding. The trial court, by directing the prosecution to supplement evidence, effectively signaled its belief that the evidence was necessary for conviction and that the prosecution's case was incomplete. This inherently biased the proceedings against Ms. Kittle. The prosecution cannot cure structural error by offering the defense an opportunity to respond to evidence that should never have been admitted in the first place.

---

### IV. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT CONVICTION

#### A. The Prosecution Failed to Establish That Ms. Kittle Possessed or Assembled Chemicals

The prosecution's case rested entirely on the following evidence: (1) Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine; (2) she admitted to purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S.; and (3) a methamphetamine lab was discovered in an apartment where Ms. Kittle was present only briefly on one occasion. This evidence is insufficient to establish a violation of Ohio Revised Code Section 2925.041(A), which prohibits "illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs."

The prosecution failed to establish that Ms. Kittle possessed any chemicals. The officers never found chemicals in Ms. Kittle's possession. She was not present at the apartment when the methamphetamine lab was discovered. The officers did not arrest or search Ms. Kittle during the traffic stop. The only evidence connecting her to the manufacturing process is her own statement that she bought pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.

Under Ohio law, mere presence at the scene of a crime or mere association with a person who commits a crime is insufficient to establish participation. The prosecution was required to prove that Ms. Kittle knowingly assembled or possessed chemicals for drug manufacturing. Buying pseudoephedrine—which is a legal, over-the-counter medication—is not equivalent to possessing chemicals for methamphetamine manufacturing. Pseudoephedrine becomes a "chemical" for purposes of the statute only when it is possessed with the intent to use it in drug manufacturing. The prosecution presented no evidence that Ms. Kittle intended the pseudoephedrine to be used in manufacturing beyond her own statement, and her statement establishes only that she provided the pills to J.S.—not that she participated in or was present during the manufacturing process.

#### B. The Amendment of the Indictment Prejudiced Ms. Kittle

The prosecution argues that any error in amending the indictment was harmless because the trial court relied only on the December 2, 2014 date. This argument ignores the prejudice caused by the amendment. The indictment originally charged the offense as occurring "on or about December 2, 2014." On the morning of trial, the prosecution moved to amend the indictment to include "the preceding seven days." This amendment expanded the timeframe of the alleged offense from a single day to an entire week.

The defense was prepared to defend against an offense occurring on a specific date—December 2, 2014. By expanding the timeframe, the prosecution was permitted to introduce evidence of conduct occurring at any time during the preceding week. This prevented Ms. Kittle from mounting a targeted defense. While the trial court ultimately found Ms. Kittle guilty based only on the December 2 date, the amendment allowed the prosecution to present a broader scope of evidence than originally charged, which inherently prejudiced the defense.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I**

The Defendant argues that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records through Officer Lemonier, asserting the records are testimonial and Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive.

First, regarding the Confrontation Clause, the NPLEX records are admissible as business records because they are not testimonial in nature. A statement is testimonial if it is "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015). The NPLEX system is designed to track pseudoephedrine purchases for regulatory compliance and law enforcement monitoring, not to accuse a specific individual of a crime at the time of the transaction. As the Sixth Circuit noted regarding similar "MethCheck" records, it is improbable that a pharmacy employee conducting a standard transaction would anticipate the records being used against a specific defendant at trial. *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015). Therefore, the records are nontestimonial business records, and the Confrontation Clause is not implicated.

Second, Officer Lemonier was a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6). The rule allows a business record to be authenticated by "the testimony of the custodian or other qualified witness." A qualified witness is defined as someone with "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40. Officer Lemonier testified that he accesses the NPLEX system daily, holds a license to do so, and understands the real-time process by which data is transmitted from the pharmacy to the database. While he is not a pharmacy employee, his testimony demonstrated sufficient familiarity with the system's operation and retrieval to authenticate the records. The witness need not be the custodian of records or have personal knowledge of the specific transaction's preparation, provided they can verify the record's origin and maintenance. *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.

**Assignment of Error I is overruled.**

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II**

The Defendant contends the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the State to supplement its evidence mid-trial. Specifically, the trial court allowed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version of the NPLEX record after cross-examination revealed missing information.

Evidence Rule 614(B) permits the court to "interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." The purpose of this rule is to clarify facts, and in a bench trial, the risk of influencing a jury is absent. *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. The trial court's stated intent was to "find the truth," and it offered the defense time to review the expanded record. Defense counsel elected to proceed without delay. There is no indication of bias, prejudice, or that the court "prodded" the witness to elicit partisan testimony. *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999). The trial court's actions do not constitute a manifest abuse of discretion.

**Assignment of Error II is overruled.**

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III**

The Defendant argues the trial court committed reversible error by amending the indictment to include the preceding week. The standard for reviewing such amendments is whether the trial court abused its discretion and whether the amendment prejudiced the defendant. *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10.

Here, the trial court allowed the amendment to expand the timeframe, but the journal entry explicitly states that the finding of guilt was based solely on the original date, December 2, 2014. Because the conviction rested on the conduct originally alleged, the Defendant cannot demonstrate that the amendment prejudiced her defense or impaired her ability to prepare for trial. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16. Furthermore, viewing the evidence in a light most favorable to the prosecution, the record supports the conviction. The Defendant admitted to purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine, and J.S. was found in possession of methamphetamine and items associated with its manufacture. This evidence is sufficient to sustain a conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs under R.C. 2925.041(A).

**Assignment of Error III is overruled.**

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Defendant's assignments of error are overruled. The trial court's admission of the NPLEX records was proper, the court did not act with partiality in supplementing the record, and the amendment of the indictment did not prejudice the Defendant. The evidence was sufficient to support the conviction. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal fails to overcome the fundamental constitutional and evidentiary deficiencies in the trial court proceedings. While the prosecution correctly notes that this Court reviews the trial court's rulings for abuse of discretion, it overlooks the substantial record evidence demonstrating that the trial court erred in multiple respects. The defense maintains that the NPLEX records were improperly admitted, the trial court exceeded its role in supplementing the State's evidence, the indictment amendment violated fundamental due process rights, and the evidence was insufficient to sustain the conviction. This Court should sustain the defense's assignments of error and reverse the judgment of the trial court.

## I. THE NPLEX RECORDS WERE IMPROPERLY ADMITTED

### A. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial Under the Primary Purpose Test

The prosecution misapplies the primary purpose test established in Ohio v. Clark, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), by focusing on the moment of the record's creation rather than examining the system's design and operational context. The defense acknowledges that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check may not anticipate trial use, but this case presents entirely different circumstances.

The NPLEX system was established pursuant to Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05 specifically for the purpose of monitoring pseudoephedrine purchases to assist law enforcement in combating methamphetamine manufacturing. Unlike ordinary business records created for internal operational purposes, the NPLEX system exists precisely because state and federal legislatures determined that tracking these purchases would aid criminal investigations. The system is designed to generate real-time alerts to law enforcement when purchases exceed thresholds, demonstrating that the records are created with law enforcement intervention as a primary purpose. See State v. Coleman, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 (acknowledging the purpose of the NPLEx system is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets).

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Collins, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), is misplaced because that case involved different records with different primary purposes. Here, the NPLEX system is an integrated law enforcement tool, not an incidental business record. The defense maintains that when a system is designed specifically to generate evidence for criminal prosecutions, the records it produces are testimonial regardless of who physically enters the data.

### B. Officer Lemonier Was Not a Qualified Witness

The prosecution's argument that Officer Lemonier satisfied the qualified witness requirement misreads Evidence Rule 803(6) and the governing precedent. The rule requires testimony from "the custodian or other qualified witness" who can explain "how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." State v. Hood, 135 Ohio St. 3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40.

Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he has law enforcement access to the NPLEX system and understands how to retrieve records. This falls far short of demonstrating familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business that creates the records—namely, the pharmacies that input pseudoephedrine purchase data. The prosecution conflates knowledge of how to access law enforcement databases with knowledge of how businesses maintain records in the ordinary course of business. The defense notes that the pharmacies themselves maintain no custody or control over the NPLEX system; the data is transmitted to a centralized database operated by the National Association of Drug Diversion Investigators.

As this Court acknowledged in State v. Baker, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, the witness must demonstrate sufficient familiarity with "the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record." Officer Lemonier offered no testimony about how pharmacies prepare pseudoephedrine sales records, their internal maintenance procedures, or their regular practices regarding data entry. His testimony established only his law enforcement use of the system, not the business practices of the pharmacies that generate the underlying data. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting the records through this inadequate foundation.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT IMPROPERLY ABDICATED ITS ROLE AS IMPARTIAL FINDER OF FACT

The prosecution's defense of the trial court's intervention misconstrues the nature of judicial impartiality and the protections afforded to defendants in criminal proceedings. While Evidence Rule 614(B) permits courts to interrogate witnesses, this authority is not unlimited and must be exercised in a manner that does not prejudice the defendant's rights.

The prosecution argues that the trial court's actions were proper because it sought to "find the truth" and because this was a bench trial. However, the rule's purpose—"to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness"—applies with equal force to bench trials. See State v. Grad, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. The danger is not merely that a judge might influence a jury; it is that the judge might develop or convey a bias that affects the outcome of the proceeding.

Here, the trial court did not merely ask clarifying questions. Rather than awaiting the State's case-in-chief or requiring the State to present evidence properly, the trial court interjected during cross-examination to identify a deficiency in the State's evidence and then directed the State to remedy that deficiency. This intervention fundamentally altered the adversarial dynamic of the trial. The trial court effectively became an advocate for the prosecution by identifying evidentiary gaps and directing their correction.

The prosecution notes that defense counsel was offered time to review the expanded record and elected to proceed. This argument ignores that the damage was already done. The trial court's intervention signaled to the prosecution that it should strengthen its case and provided a roadmap for doing so. The defense's decision to proceed cannot cure the prejudice caused by the court's improper intervention. The prosecution's reliance on State v. Baston, 85 Ohio St. 3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), is unavailing because that case involved isolated questions aimed at clarifying testimony, not directed supplementation of the prosecution's case.

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUSTAIN THE CONVICTION

The prosecution fundamentally misreads the elements of Ohio Revised Code Section 2925.041(A), which prohibits "illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs." The statute requires proof that the defendant either possessed or assembled chemicals for the purpose of manufacturing drugs.

The prosecution relies entirely on Ms. Kittle's admission that she purchased pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine. This admission, standing alone, does not establish that Ms. Kittle possessed or assembled chemicals for methamphetamine manufacturing. At most, it establishes that she provided a precursor chemical to another person who then manufactured methamphetamine.

The prosecution conflates the act of purchasing a legal cold medication with the illegal act of assembling or possessing chemicals for drug manufacturing. Pseudoephedrine is a legal substance that can be purchased lawfully by any adult. The statute targets the assembly or possession of chemicals specifically for manufacturing illegal drugs—not the mere acquisition of precursor materials. Ms. Kittle's act of purchasing pseudoephedrine and giving it to J.S. is legally indistinguishable from countless lawful transactions in which individuals purchase products that may be misused by others.

The prosecution's argument that the statute does not require physical presence during manufacturing ignores the statutory language requiring "possession or assembly." To "possess" chemicals means to exercise dominion and control over them. To "assemble" chemicals means to collect or combine them for the manufacturing process. Ms. Kittle exercised no dominion over the methamphetamine lab, did not combine any chemicals, and was not present during the manufacturing process. Her conviction rests on mere association with J.S. and the act of purchasing a legal product—neither of which satisfies the statutory elements.

The prosecution's reliance on the discovery of methamphetamine lab materials in the apartment where Ms. Kittle had been present proves too much. If mere presence in a location where manufacturing occurs were sufficient, the statute would criminalize innocent individuals who merely visit premises where drugs are manufactured. The statute requires the defendant to have possessed or assembled the chemicals herself, not merely to have been present where others did so.

## IV. THE INDICTMENT AMENDMENT VIOLATED DUE PROCESS

The prosecution's argument that the indictment amendment caused no prejudice ignores the fundamental constitutional concerns raised by expanding the timeframe of the charge. A qualified witness is defined as someone with "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining Officer Lemonier was qualified to authenticate the records.

When the trial court allowed the State to amend the indictment to expand the timeframe from a single date to an entire week, it effectively permitted the prosecution to convict Ms. Kittle for conduct that was never presented to the grand jury.

The prosecution's argument that there was no prejudice because the conviction rested on the original date fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the constitutional violation. The harm lies not in the resulting conviction but in the *process*—Ms. Kittle was deprived of her right to have the specific conduct alleged against her reviewed by a grand jury. As the Ohio Supreme Court has recognized, the right to grand jury indictment is a fundamental constitutional protection that cannot be waived by the trial court's subsequent narrowing of the charges.

### B. The Amendment Impaired Ms. Kittle's Defense

Even under the prosecution's own framework requiring a showing of prejudice, the amendment caused concrete harm. Ms. Kittle was charged with illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. The amendment expanded the relevant timeframe from December 2, 2014, to include the entire preceding week—November 25 through December 2, 2014. This expansion required Ms. Kittle to defend against conduct that was not specified in the original indictment, potentially including multiple purchases of pseudoephedrine rather than the single purchase she prepared to address.

The prosecution argues that Ms. Kittle was aware of the November 30 purchase through discovery. However, awareness of evidence is not the same as awareness of the *charge*. Ms. Kittle had a right to prepare her defense specifically addressing the conduct alleged in the indictment. The amendment deprived her of this right.

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court sustain Assignment of Error III and find that the trial court abused its discretion in allowing the amendment.

**JUDGE'S FINAL RULING**

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I**

The Defendant, Dana Kittle, argues that the trial court erred by admitting the NPLEX records through the testimony of Officer Lemonier. She contends the records are testimonial, violating her Sixth Amendment rights, and that Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6). Having reviewed the rebuttal arguments, the Court finds the Defendant's assertions unpersuasive.

**A. Testimonial Nature of NPLEX Records**

The Defendant argues that because the NPLEX system was statutorily designed for law enforcement interdiction, the records generated are testimonial. She relies on *State v. Coleman*, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, to assert the primary purpose is criminal prosecution. However, the "primary purpose" test established in *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015), focuses on whether the statement was created "with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony."

The record reflects that NPLEX entries are created when a pharmacy employee swipes a customer's identification during a routine sales transaction. As the Sixth Circuit noted in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), regarding a similar system, "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial." The fact that the legislature intended the system to aid law enforcement does not alter the primary purpose of the record's creation at the pharmacy level, which is to facilitate a regulated commercial transaction. Therefore, the records are nontestimonial business records, and the Confrontation Clause is not implicated.

**B. Qualified Witness**

The Defendant contends Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness because he is a user of the NPLEX system rather than a representative of the pharmacies that create the records. Evidence Rule 803(6) requires testimony from "the custodian or other qualified witness" who can explain "how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40.

The Defendant argues that the "business" in question is the individual pharmacy, and since Officer Lemonier lacks knowledge of internal pharmacy procedures, he cannot authenticate the record. While this distinction is noted, the Court finds it unpersuasive in the context of a centralized electronic database. The NPLEX record is a product of the electronic tracking system itself. Officer Lemonier testified regarding the mechanics of this system: the swiping of the ID, the real-time transmission of data, and the specific information fields populated. This testimony demonstrated sufficient familiarity with the operation of the NPLEX system to explain how the record came into existence.

Furthermore, as held in *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, "the witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." Officer Lemonier was not required to testify regarding the specific quality control measures of each pharmacy; he only needed to demonstrate that the record was generated in the ordinary course of the electronic business activity, which he did. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.

**Assignment of Error I is overruled.**

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II**

The Defendant argues the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence mid-trial, specifically by allowing Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version of the NPLEX record. She claims this constituted "prodding" the witness to elicit partisan testimony.

Evidence Rule 614(B) permits the court to "interrogateate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." The purpose of this rule is to prevent the judge from conveying an impression of witness credibility to a jury. *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44. In a bench trial, the risk of influencing a jury is absent, and the court is afforded greater latitude to ascertain the truth. *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999).

The record indicates that during cross-examination, defense counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record lacked specific information. Officer Lemonier stated that the information was available in the system. The trial court, stating it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth," allowed the officer to access the system to print the expanded record. The Defendant characterizes this as the court acting as an advocate for the prosecution. However, the trial court's actions were aimed at clarifying the evidence before the court, not advocating for a particular outcome. Crucially, the trial court offered the defense a continuance to review the expanded record. Defense counsel elected to proceed without delay.

"In absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony, it will be presumed that the trial court acted with impartiality." *Baston* at 426, quoting Jenkins v. Clark, 7 Ohio App.3d 93, 98, 454 N.E.2d 541 (2d Dist.1982). The offer of a continuance mitigated any potential prejudice, and the Defendant has not demonstrated that the court's questioning was partial or designed to elicit partisan testimony. The trial court did not commit a manifest abuse of discretion.

**Assignment of Error II is overruled.**

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III**

The Defendant argues the trial court committed reversible error by amending the indictment on the morning of trial to expand the timeframe from a single date to the preceding week. She asserts this violated her constitutional rights to indictment by a grand jury and due process.

The standard for reviewing such amendments is whether the trial court abused its discretion and whether the amendment prejudiced the defendant. *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. The Defendant argues that the amendment itself was a structural error violating the grand jury process, regardless of the outcome. However, to constitute reversible error, the defendant must show prejudice. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.

Here, the trial court allowed the amendment to include the preceding week, but the journal entry of conviction explicitly states that the finding of guilt was based solely on the original date, December 2, 2014. The Defendant was aware of the November 30, 2014 purchase through discovery prior to trial. Because the conviction rested on the conduct originally alleged in the indictment, and the Defendant had notice of the evidence regarding the additional date, she cannot demonstrate that the amendment prejudiced her defense or impaired her ability to prepare for trial. The Defendant's constitutional arguments regarding the grand jury process are not supported by a showing of prejudice in this instance.

**Assignment of Error III is overruled.**

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Defendant's assignments of error are overruled. The trial court properly admitted the NPLEX records as nontestimonial business records authenticated by a qualified witness. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in supplementing the record during the bench trial, and the amendment of the indictment did not prejudice the Defendant. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
