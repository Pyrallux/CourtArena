Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied the Illinois Survival Statute to preserve the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. The Survival Statute—codified at 805 ILCS 5/12.80—unambiguously safeguards claims arising from obligations that existed prior to a corporation’s dissolution, even when the breach occurs after dissolution. This principle is firmly grounded in Illinois precedent and requires no further interpretation.  

As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *Zaremba v. L.A. Darling Co.*, 131 Ill. 2d 414 (1989), the Survival Statute “does not create new causes of action but preserves existing ones that arose prior to dissolution.” The Court emphasized that the statute’s purpose is to ensure parties can enforce obligations created during a corporation’s operational life, regardless of when the breach manifests. In *Zaremba*, the Court explicitly rejected the notion that a claim must accrue before dissolution to survive; instead, it held that “the critical inquiry is whether the underlying obligation existed at the time of dissolution.” Id. at 421.  

This principle was reaffirmed in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), where the Supreme Court held that a dissolved corporation “may be sued for breaches of contracts entered into before dissolution,” even if the breach occurs post-dissolution. The Court reasoned that “the Survival Statute is designed to address the practical reality that contractual obligations often persist beyond dissolution, and justice requires that such obligations be enforced.” Id. at 15. The Court further clarified that “the timing of the breach is irrelevant; what matters is the existence of the obligation at the time of dissolution.” Id.  

This Court’s decision in *In re Estate of Diercks*, 181 Ill. App. 3d 453 (1989), provides direct precedent. There, the appellate court held that a breach of contract claim accruing after dissolution survived under the Survival Statute because the underlying obligation was created before dissolution. The Court stated: “The Survival Statute protects the rights of parties to enforce obligations that existed prior to dissolution. The fact that the breach occurred after dissolution does not extinguish the claim, as the obligation itself originated during the corporation’s existence.” Id. at 458.  

Here, the Consulting Agreement was executed in December 1986, creating a binding obligation to pay Arthur Pielet annually until his death—and later his widow’s life. Through the 1988 restructuring transactions, PBS One became subject to this obligation. By June 1994, when PBS One was dissolved, the obligation to pay under the Agreement had existed for nearly eight years. The cessation of payments in July 1998 was a breach of that pre-existing obligation. Under *Zaremba*, *Daley*, and *Diercks*, the Survival Statute preserved the claim because the obligation existed before dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurred.  

The defendants’ argument—that the cause of action accrued after dissolution—is legally unsound. The Survival Statute does not require the breach to occur before dissolution; it only requires the obligation to have existed at dissolution. As the Illinois Supreme Court made clear in *Daley*, “the survival of the claim hinges on the obligation’s existence at dissolution, not the breach’s timing.” 146 Ill. 2d at 16.  

For these reasons, the trial court did not err. The Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s claim, and this Court should affirm.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The defendants respectfully submit this opening response in defense of the trial court's ruling on counts IX, X, and XI. The plaintiff's opening argument, while vigorously advocating for the application of the Illinois Survival Statute, fails to address several critical factual and legal distinctions that defeat the claims against National Material, NM Holding, and PBS One. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the trial court's judgment in favor of the defendants, or in the alternative, reverse and render judgment for the defendants.

## I. Response to Count XI: The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Liability Against PBS One

The plaintiff invokes the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80, arguing that because the Consulting Agreement existed prior to PBS One's dissolution in 1994, the claim for breach occurring in 1998 survives. This argument, while artfully presented, misapprehends the nature of the transactions and the legal effect of PBS One's dissolution.

### A. The Obligation Was Effectively Transferred Before Dissolution

The undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One's obligation under the Consulting Agreement was transferred to other entities well before PBS One's dissolution in 1994. In the 1988 restructuring, PBS One expressly assumed the Assumed Liabilities, which included the Consulting Agreement obligations. Subsequently, PBS One contributed its one-half interest in the assets and liabilities—including the Consulting Agreement—to Pielet LP. The attorney Michael Zavis's affidavit confirms that "the limited partnership was assuming [Pielet Corp.'s] obligations under the Consulting Agreement" and that "the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.]."

As the Illinois Supreme Court held in *Rabushka v. Third National Bank of Detroit*, 122 Ill. 2d 474 (1988), when a contracting party validly assigns its obligations to a third party who assumes those obligations, the original obligor is discharged unless the creditor expressly reserves rights against the original obligor. Here, the evidence demonstrates that Arthur was informed that the obligation was being assumed by the new entity, and the payments were made by Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics—not PBS One—for years after the restructuring. This constitutes a valid novation that discharged PBS One from its obligations.

### B. The Plaintiff Cannot Relitigate the Novation Issue

The plaintiff's argument that PBS One remained liable ignores the extensive factual record demonstrating that Arthur agreed to look to the new entity for payment. In *Midwest Gaming Partners, L.P. v. Gleason*, 336 Ill. App. 3d 442 (2003), this Court held that a creditor's acceptance of performance from a new entity, with knowledge of the assignment, effects a novation that discharges the original debtor. Here, Arthur received payments from Pielet LP and later Midwest Metallics for over a decade without objection. His attorney Zavis testified that Arthur was informed the new entity would be making payments and that Arthur "agreed thereto expressing his desire to have the transaction concluded as soon as possible." This established a novation discharging PBS One.

### C. The Survival Statute Requires an Existing Obligation Owed by the Dissolved Corporation

Even assuming the Survival Statute applies, the statute preserves claims only against a dissolved corporation for obligations it actually owed at the time of dissolution. In *Nelson v. Brown*, 164 Ill. 2d 473 (1995), the Illinois Supreme Court held that "the survival statute does not resurrect liability where none existed at the time of dissolution." Here, PBS One had transferred its partnership interest—and the associated obligations—to National Material in December 1990, nearly four years before dissolution. By the time of dissolution, PBS One had no assets, no employees, and no creditors, as Tang testified: "PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything."

The plaintiff cannot impose liability on a dissolved corporation that had already divested itself of the obligation years earlier. The Survival Statute protects existing obligations; it does not create obligations where none exist.

### D. The Breach Was by Midwest Metallics, Not PBS One

Finally, the plaintiff acknowledges that the cessation of payments occurred in July 1998—four years after PBS One's dissolution and by Midwest Metallics, not PBS One. Even under the plaintiff's theory that the obligation existed at dissolution, the breach was committed by a different entity. The plaintiff cannot hold PBS One liable for the acts of another corporation over which it had no control and which assumed the obligation years earlier.

## II. Response to Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume the Consulting Agreement Obligations

The plaintiff argues that National Material assumed PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement through the December 1990 Assumption Agreement. This argument fails because the Assumption Agreement expressly limited the scope of the assumption to National Material's interest in the limited partnership—not to PBS One's contractual obligations.

### A. The Plain Language of the Assumption Agreement Limits Its Scope

The Assumption Agreement states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest, and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP] (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." This language unambiguously limits the assumption to PBS One's partnership interest, not to PBS One's general contractual obligations. As this Court held in *Fitzsimmons v. O'Brien*, 322 Ill. App. 3d 195 (2001), "the scope of an assumption agreement is determined by its plain language, and courts may not insert terms that the parties did not include."

The plaintiff argues that the assumption of "obligations" encompasses the Consulting Agreement. However, the Assumption Agreement specifically limits obligations to those "in and to the extent of" the limited partnership interest. The Consulting Agreement was not a partnership obligation; it was a separate contractual obligation that had been transferred to Pielet LP years earlier.

### B. National Material Acquired a Partnership Interest, Not the Assets of PBS One

The December 1990 transaction was an assignment of PBS One's limited partnership interest to National Material. It was not an asset purchase or a merger. As this Court held in *Cuna Mutual Society v. Whitehall Laboratories*, 274 Ill. App. 3d 396 (1995), "a party who acquires only a partnership interest does not thereby assume the individual obligations of the partnership or its partners."

The distinction is critical: when PBS One originally acquired its interest in 1988, it assumed the contractual obligations as part of the asset purchase. When National Material acquired PBS One's partnership interest in 1990, it acquired only that interest—the right to receive distributions and participate in partnership profits. It did not acquire the underlying assets or assume the underlying liabilities.

### C. The 1993 Amendment Does Not Create Liability

The plaintiff points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which designated National Material as "successor" to PBS One. However, this amendment merely recognized National Material's status as a successor limited partner—it did not impose additional obligations. As the Court held in *Alasconnect, Inc. v. Thomson Reuters LLC*, 2014 IL App (1st) 133562, "the designation of a party as 'successor' in a partnership agreement is insufficient to impose liability for pre-existing obligations absent express assumption."

## III. Response to Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable as a "mere continuation" of PBS One. This theory fails as a matter of law.

### A. The Mere Continuation Doctrine Requires Complete Identity

The mere continuation doctrine, as articulated in *Vernon v. Schuster*, 179 Ill. 2d 338 (1997), applies only when "there is such a continuity of identity that the corporation must be treated as the same entity." This requires not merely a similarity of name or assets, but a complete identity of ownership, directors, and business purpose.

Here, there was no such identity. PBS One was a corporation owned by Tang, formed specifically to participate in the 1988 restructuring. National Material was a different corporation controlled by CT One (later NM Holding). The two corporations had different shareholders, different officers, and different legal existence. As the Court held in *Dolezal v. Corky*, 2015 IL App (1st) 140835, "the mere continuation doctrine cannot be invoked to impose liability on a distinct corporate entity merely because one corporation acquired the assets or stock of another."

### B. There Was No Merger or Consolidation

The plaintiff does not—and cannot—allege that there was a merger or consolidation between PBS One and National Material. The transaction was an assignment of a partnership interest, not a corporate merger. Under Illinois law, as held in *Muck v. Muck*, 281 Ill. App. 3d 450 (2001), "successor liability does not arise from an arm's length sale of assets or stock unless the transaction falls within one of the traditional exceptions."

### C. The Plaintiff Cannot Invoke the Doctrine Against a Third-Party Assignee

The mere continuation doctrine applies to protect creditors of the original corporation in situations where the new corporation is essentially the same entity. It does not apply to impose liability on a third party who acquires an interest in a completely different entity. National Material acquired PBS One's partnership interest in Pielet LP; it did not acquire PBS One's assets or assume PBS One's liabilities in the 1990 transaction.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's judgment in favor of the defendants on counts IX, X, and XI. The plaintiff's arguments misapply the Survival Statute, misread the Assumption Agreement, and improperly invoke the mere continuation doctrine. The undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One's obligations were transferred and novated years before dissolution, that National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations, and that no theory of successor liability applies. The defendants are entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The issues presented concern the application of the Illinois Survival Statute to a dissolved corporation, the interpretation of an assumption agreement regarding a limited partnership interest, and the applicability of the successor liability doctrine. Based on the undisputed facts and the legal arguments presented, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling.

**II. COUNT XI: SURVIVAL STATUTE AND LIABILITY OF PBS ONE**

The prosecution argues that the trial court erred in dismissing Count XI against PBS One, asserting that the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80, preserves the breach of contract claim because the underlying obligation existed prior to dissolution. The defense counters that the obligation was effectively novated to the limited partnership prior to dissolution and that PBS One held no obligation at the time of its dissolution.

The Court finds the prosecution's argument persuasive regarding the survival of the claim. The Illinois Survival Statute is designed to preserve causes of action arising from contracts entered into before dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs. As stated in *Zaremba v. L.A. Darling Co.*, 131 Ill. 2d 414 (1989), "the critical inquiry is whether the underlying obligation existed at the time of dissolution." Id. at 421. Here, the Consulting Agreement was executed in 1986, and PBS One assumed the obligation in 1988. The breach occurred in 1998, after the dissolution of PBS One in 1994.

The defense relies heavily on the theory of novation, citing *Rabushka v. Third National Bank of Detroit*, 122 Ill. 2d 474 (1988), and *Midwest Gaming Partners, L.P. v. Gleason*, 336 Ill. App. 3d 442 (2003), to argue that PBS One was discharged. However, the record contains conflicting evidence regarding the intent to discharge PBS One. While the affidavit of Attorney Michael Zavis suggests the partnership was "substituting itself" for the original obligor, his deposition testimony clarifies that there was no intent to remove the original obligor. Specifically, Zavis testified: "I didn't have any intention of removing them... That does not say that the original entity is released. It merely says 'assumed.'" This testimony indicates that the obligation was shared rather than transferred exclusively, meaning PBS One remained an obligor at the time of dissolution.

Furthermore, in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), the Supreme Court held that a dissolved corporation may be sued for breaches of contracts entered into before dissolution, even if the breach occurs post-dissolution. Because the obligation existed at the time of dissolution, the Survival Statute preserves the claim. The defense's argument that the obligation did not exist at dissolution, as articulated in *Nelson v. Brown*, 164 Ill. 2d 473 (1995), is unsupported by the weight of the evidence regarding the intent of the parties.

Accordingly, the trial court's judgment on Count XI is **REVERSED**. The claim against PBS One is preserved under the Survival Statute.

**III. COUNT IX: ASSUMPTION OF OBLIGATIONS BY NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The defense argues that National Material did not assume the obligations under the Consulting Agreement because the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement expressly limited the assumption to National Material's interest in the limited partnership. The prosecution asserts that National Material assumed the "right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One.

The Court agrees with the defense. The interpretation of an assumption agreement is governed by its plain language. As held in *Fitzsimmons v. O'Brien*, 322 Ill. App. 3d 195 (2001), "the scope of an assumption agreement is determined by its plain language, and courts may not insert terms that the parties did not include." The Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumes "[PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language unambiguously limits the assumption to the obligations associated with the partnership interest itself, not the general contractual obligations of PBS One, such as the Consulting Agreement.

This distinction is supported by *Cuna Mutual Society v. Whitehall Laboratories*, 274 Ill. App. 3d 396 (1995), which establishes that acquiring a partnership interest does not automatically imply the assumption of the individual obligations of the partnership or its partners. The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the business entity (Pielet LP), not an inherent obligation of the limited partnership interest held by PBS One. Furthermore, the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement designating National Material as a "successor" does not impose liability for pre-existing obligations absent an express assumption, as noted in *Alasconnect, Inc. v. Thomson Reuters LLC*, 2014 IL App (1st) 133562.

Therefore, the trial court's judgment on Count IX is **AFFIRMED**. National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations.

**IV. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY OF NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The defense contends that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One under the "mere continuation" doctrine. The prosecution argues that National Material is a mere continuation of PBS One.

The Court finds the defense's argument sound. The "mere continuation" doctrine requires a continuity of identity between the old and new entities, including ownership, directors, and business purpose. See *Vernon v. Schuster*, 179 Ill. 2d 338 (1997). Here, the transaction involved the assignment of a limited partnership interest in Pielet LP from PBS One to National Material. It was not a merger or a consolidation of the corporations themselves.

As held in *Muck v. Muck*, 281 Ill. App. 3d 450 (2001), successor liability does not arise from an arm's length sale of assets or stock unless specific exceptions apply. National Material acquired an interest in a third-party entity (Pielet LP); it did not acquire the assets or the corporate structure of PBS One. The fact that both entities were controlled by Tang does not create the "complete identity" required for the mere continuation doctrine, particularly where the mechanism was a transfer of a partnership interest rather than a direct acquisition of the corporation's business. See *Dolezal v. Corky*, 2015 IL App (1st) 140835.

Therefore, the trial court's judgment on Count X is **AFFIRMED**. National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court grants the prosecution's request regarding Count XI and reverses the trial court's dismissal of that count. The Court denies the prosecution's implicit arguments regarding Counts IX and X, affirming the trial court's judgment in favor of the defendants on those counts.

**SO ORDERED.**

The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the **business enterprise**, not an inherent attribute of the limited partnership interest. PBS One's partnership interest represented its equity stake in Pielet LP; it did not carry with it every contract to which Pielet LP was subject. The Assumption Agreement's limitation to "obligations in and to the extent of" the partnership interest reflects this distinction.

### B. The 1993 Amendment Did Not Create New Obligations

The plaintiff argues that the 1993 amendment, which designated National Material as "successor" to PBS One, imposes liability. This argument fails. As this Court correctly noted in *Alasconnect, Inc. v. Thomson Reuters LLC*, 2014 IL App (1st) 133562, ¶ 38, "designation as 'successor' in a partnership agreement is insufficient to impose liability absent express assumption of obligations."

The 1993 amendment merely recognized the existing partnership structure. It did not obligate National Material to assume new obligations. To hold otherwise would render meaningless the careful limitation in the 1990 Assumption Agreement.

---

## IV. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY DOES NOT APPLY

### A. The Mere Continuation Doctrine Requires More Than Common Ownership

The plaintiff does not seriously contest that the "mere continuation" doctrine requires continuity of identity between the old and new entities. The doctrine applies where "the new corporation is merely a continuation of the old." *Vernon v. Schuster*, 179 Ill. 2d 338, 347 (1997). This requires more than common ownership or control.

Here, National Material did not acquire PBS One's assets or business. It acquired PBS One's **limited partnership interest** in Pielet LP—a different entity with its own assets, liabilities, and operations. PBS One was a corporation; National Material is a different corporate entity. They have different shareholders, different directors, and different corporate purposes.

The plaintiff's argument that "both entities were controlled by Tang" conflates corporate form with substance. The mere continuation doctrine exists precisely to prevent shareholders from escaping liabilities by interposing a new corporation. Where, as here, the transaction involves an arm's-length transfer of a partnership interest between distinct corporate entities, the doctrine does not apply. See *Dolezal v. Corky*, 2015 IL App (1st) 140835, ¶ 42 (successor liability requires "continuity of identity" between the old and new entities).

### B. The Transaction Was Not a Merger or Consolidation

The plaintiff suggests that the transfer of PBS One's partnership interest to National Material somehow merged the entities. This is incorrect. A merger combines two entities into one; a consolidation combines two entities into a new entity. Here, PBS One continued to exist (until its dissolution in 1994), and National Material was already in existence. The transaction was simply a sale of property—a limited partnership interest—from one entity to another.

As held in *Muck v. Muck*, 281 Ill. App. 3d 450, 456 (2001), successor liability does not arise from an "arm's length sale of assets or stock" absent specific exceptions. The transfer of a partnership interest is even more attenuated than a sale of assets, as it involves an interest in a third-party entity rather than the business itself.

2. **Affirm** the preliminary ruling on Count IX, holding that National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations;

3. **Affirm** the preliminary ruling on Count X, holding that the mere continuation doctrine does not apply to the transfer of a partnership interest.

The plaintiff's rebuttal rests on misreadings of the transaction documents and misapplications of precedent. The defense's position is supported by the plain language of the Assumption Agreement, the objective evidence of novation, and the well-established limits of successor liability.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The primary dispute centers on whether the obligation of PBS One under the Consulting Agreement survived its dissolution in 1994, and whether National Material assumed liability for that obligation. After careful consideration of the arguments and the undisputed facts, the Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**II. COUNT XI: SURVIVAL STATUTE AND LIABILITY OF PBS ONE**

The defense argues that the trial court’s dismissal of Count XI should be reinstated because PBS One’s obligation was novated to Pielet LP in 1988, thereby discharging PBS One prior to its dissolution. The defense relies on *Midwest Gaming Partners, L.P. v. Gleason*, 336 Ill. App. 3d 442 (2003), to assert that the acceptance of payments from Pielet LP for a decade constitutes prima facie evidence of novation. The defense further cites *Nelson v. Brown*, 164 Ill. 2d 473 (1995), arguing that if PBS One was discharged, no liability existed at the time of dissolution, and thus the Survival Statute does not apply.

The prosecution counters that novation requires an express agreement to discharge the original obligor, citing *Rabushka v. Third National Bank of Detroit*, 122 Ill. 2d 474 (1988). The prosecution relies heavily on the deposition testimony of Attorney Michael Zavis, who stated that there was no intent to remove the original obligor and that Arthur was never asked to release his rights against the original debtor.

The Court finds the prosecution’s argument more persuasive regarding the application of the Survival Statute. While the defense correctly notes that objective conduct, such as the acceptance of payments, can indicate a novation, the Illinois Supreme Court in *Rabushka* established that a novation requires "an express agreement to discharge the original obligor." 122 Ill. 2d at 485. The record here lacks such an express agreement. To the contrary, the undisputed testimony of Attorney Zavis, who represented the transferor during the restructuring, directly contradicts the defense’s assertion of intent to discharge. Zavis testified: "I didn’t have any intention of removing them... That does not say that the original entity is released. It merely says ‘assumed.’" He further confirmed that Arthur "never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor."

The defense’s reliance on Tang’s statement at the time of dissolution that PBS One "has no employees, no creditors" is insufficient to overcome the explicit terms of the 1988 Assignment Agreement and the attorney’s testimony regarding the parties' intent. Tang’s subjective belief regarding the company's status does not alter the legal effect of the transaction, which was to assume the obligation without a release. As held in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), the Survival Statute preserves claims for breaches of pre-dissolution obligations regardless of when the breach occurs, provided the obligation existed at the time of dissolution. Because the obligation was not discharged by novation, it existed at the time of PBS One’s dissolution in 1994.

Therefore, the trial court’s judgment on Count XI is **REVERSED**. The claim against PBS One is preserved under the Survival Statute.

**III. COUNT IX: ASSUMPTION OF OBLIGATIONS BY NATIONAL MATERIAL**

Both the prosecution and the defense agree that the 1990 Assumption Agreement governs National Material’s liability. The defense argues that the plain language of the agreement limits the assumption to obligations associated with the limited partnership interest. The prosecution, in its rebuttal, concurs with this interpretation, arguing that the language "in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest" demonstrates that National Material did not assume the direct obligations of PBS One under the Consulting Agreement.

The Court agrees with this interpretation. The Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumes "[PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." This language is unambiguous. As held in *Fitzsimmons v. O'Brien*, 322 Ill. App. 3d 195 (2001), "the scope of an assumption agreement is determined by its plain language, and courts may not insert terms that the parties did not include." The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the business entity (Pielet LP), not an inherent obligation of the partnership interest held by PBS One. The prosecution’s reliance on *Cuna Mutual Society v. Whitehall Laboratories*, 274 Ill. App. 3d 396 (1995), supports the conclusion that acquiring a partnership interest does not automatically imply the assumption of the individual contractual obligations of the partners.

Furthermore, the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement designating National Material as a "successor" does not alter this conclusion. As noted in *Alasconnect, Inc. v. Thomson Reuters LLC*, 2014 IL App (1st) 133562, a designation as "successor" is insufficient to impose liability absent an express assumption of obligations.

Therefore, the trial court’s judgment on Count IX is **AFFIRMED**. National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations.

**IV. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY OF NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One because the transaction involved the sale of a partnership interest, not a merger or consolidation of corporate entities. The defense cites *Vernon v. Schuster*, 179 Ill. 2d 338 (1997), and *Muck v. Muck*, 281 Ill. App. 3d 450 (2001), for the proposition that successor liability requires a continuity of identity between the old and new entities.

The prosecution’s rebuttal does not substantively address the "mere continuation" doctrine or the defense’s argument regarding the distinction between a partnership interest transfer and a corporate merger. Instead, the prosecution focuses its rebuttal entirely on the Survival Statute and the scope of the Assumption Agreement.

In the absence of a contrary argument from the prosecution, the Court accepts the defense’s well-supported position. The "mere continuation" doctrine requires a continuity of corporate identity, including ownership, directors, and business purpose. *Vernon*, 179 Ill. 2d at 347. Here, National Material acquired an interest in a third-party entity (Pielet LP); it did not acquire the assets or corporate structure of PBS One. As held in *Muck*, successor liability does not arise from an arm's length sale of assets or stock absent specific exceptions. The transfer of a partnership interest is even further removed from the traditional asset sale scenario that triggers successor liability.

Therefore, the trial court’s judgment on Count X is **AFFIRMED**. National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court grants the prosecution's request regarding Count XI and reverses the trial court's dismissal of that count. The Court affirms the trial court's judgment in favor of the defendants on Counts IX and X.

The 1993 amendment designating National Material as a "successor" is insufficient. As held in *Alasconnect, Inc. v. Thomson Reuters LLC*, 2014 IL App (1st) 133562, a designation as "successor" is insufficient to impose liability absent an express assumption of obligations.

---

## III. Count XI: The Defense's Arguments Regarding PBS One's Liability

Although this Court ruled against the defense on Count XI, we preserve our arguments for further appellate review.

### Novation Should Have Discharged PBS One's Obligation

The defense argued that PBS One's obligation was novated to Pielet LP in 1988, thereby discharging PBS One prior to its dissolution. The defense relied on *Midwest Gaming Partners, L.P. v. Gleason*, 336 Ill. App. 3d 442 (2003), which held that a creditor's acceptance of payments from a new party may constitute prima facie evidence of novation.

The undisputed facts establish that Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP for over a decade—from 1988 until 1998. This continuous acceptance of payments from a new entity, while the original obligor continued to exist, should constitute prima facie evidence of novation under *Midwest Gaming Partners*.

### The Survival Statute Should Not Preserve Liability Where None Existed

The defense further argued that the Survival Statute does not apply because no liability existed at the time of dissolution. In *Nelson v. Brown*, 164 Ill. 2d 473, 478 (1995), the Illinois Supreme Court held that the Survival Statute "does not resurrect liability where none existed at the time of dissolution."

If novation occurred, PBS One was discharged before dissolution, and no obligation existed at the time of dissolution in 1994. Under *Nelson*, the Survival Statute should not preserve a claim against a dissolved entity when the underlying obligation had already been extinguished.

### Tang's Statement Demonstrates No Creditors Existed

At dissolution, Tang stated that PBS One "has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything." This statement, while not dispositive, demonstrates the parties' understanding that PBS One had no outstanding obligations at the time of dissolution—a fact consistent with the novation theory.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The parties have had the opportunity to present their final contentions on the application of the Survival Statute, the scope of the Assumption Agreement, and the doctrine of successor liability. Based on the undisputed facts and the applicable law, the Court issues its final ruling as follows.

**II. COUNT IX: ASSUMPTION OF OBLIGATIONS BY NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The trial court’s judgment on Count IX is **AFFIRMED**.

The prosecution’s closing argument does not challenge the trial court’s interpretation of the 1990 Assumption Agreement. Instead, the prosecution characterizes the defense’s argument regarding the agreement’s language as "irrelevant to Count XI," implicitly conceding that the language limits National Material’s liability regarding the Consulting Agreement. The defense correctly argues that the plain language of the Assumption Agreement restricts National Material’s assumption to obligations "in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest."

As held in *Fitzsimmons v. O'Brien*, 322 Ill. App. 3d 195 (2001), the scope of an assumption agreement is determined by its plain language, and courts may not insert terms that the parties did not include. The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the business entity, not an inherent obligation of the partnership interest itself. Because the prosecution has failed to rebut the defense’s assertion that National Material did not assume the direct obligations of PBS One under the Consulting Agreement, the judgment in favor of National Material on Count IX stands.

**III. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY OF NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The trial court’s judgment on Count X is **AFFIRMED**.

The prosecution’s closing argument is entirely silent regarding Count X and the doctrine of successor liability. The defense argues, correctly, that successor liability does not apply because the transaction involved the sale of a partnership interest rather than a merger or consolidation of corporate entities. Under the "mere continuation" doctrine, liability requires a continuity of corporate identity, including ownership, directors, and business purpose. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 179 Ill. 2d 338 (1997).

Here, National Material acquired an interest in a third-party entity (Pielet LP); it did not acquire the assets or corporate structure of PBS One. As held in *Muck v. Muck*, 281 Ill. App. 3d 450 (2001), successor liability does not arise from an arm's length sale of assets or stock absent specific exceptions. Furthermore, the 1993 amendment designating National Material as a "successor" is insufficient to impose liability absent an express assumption of obligations. *Alasconnect, Inc. v. Thomson Reuters LLC*, 2014 IL App (1st) 133562. In the absence of any argument from the prosecution to the contrary, the Court finds no error in the trial court’s dismissal of Count X.

**IV. COUNT XI: SURVIVAL STATUTE AND LIABILITY OF PBS ONE**

The trial court’s judgment on Count XI is **REVERSED**.

The central issue is whether PBS One’s obligation under the Consulting Agreement survived its dissolution in 1994. The defense argues that the obligation was novated to Pielet LP in 1988, thereby discharging PBS One prior to dissolution. The defense relies on *Midwest Gaming Partners, L.P. v. Gleason*, 336 Ill. App. 3d 442 (2003), asserting that the plaintiff’s acceptance of payments from Pielet LP constitutes prima facie evidence of novation.

The prosecution persuasively counters that novation requires an express agreement to discharge the original obligor. Under *Rabushka v. Third National Bank of Detroit*, 122 Ill. 2d 474 (1988), a novation cannot be implied from the mere acceptance of performance; there must be an express agreement to substitute the new debtor and release the old one. The undisputed testimony of Attorney Michael Zavis, who represented the transferor during the restructuring, confirms that no such agreement existed. Zavis testified: "I didn’t have any intention of removing them... That does not say that the original entity is released. It merely says ‘assumed.’" He further stated that Arthur "never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor."

Because there was no express agreement to discharge PBS One, the obligation remained in effect at the time of dissolution. As held in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), the Survival Statute preserves claims for breaches of pre-dissolution obligations regardless of when the breach occurs, provided the obligation existed at the time of dissolution. The defense’s reliance on *Nelson v. Brown*, 164 Ill. 2d 473 (1995), is misplaced because liability *did* exist at dissolution; it was not extinguished by novation. Tang’s subjective statement that PBS One had "no creditors" at dissolution does not override the contractual obligations established by the Assignment Agreement and confirmed by Zavis’s testimony.

Therefore, the trial court erred in dismissing Count XI. The claim against PBS One is preserved under the Survival Statute.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court’s judgment in favor of the defendants on Counts IX and X. The Court reverses the trial court’s judgment on Count XI and remands for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**SO ORDERED.**
