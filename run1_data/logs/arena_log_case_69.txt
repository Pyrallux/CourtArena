**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, today we stand before this Court to defend the district court’s well-reasoned and legally sound grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School. The district court correctly interpreted Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, and no error occurred in its decision. This Court should affirm that ruling because the statute’s plain language is unambiguous, it contains no provision for a preference based on municipal residency, and the appellants’ interpretation would improperly rewrite the law to insert language that simply does not exist.  

The core issue here is straightforward: Does Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, require charter schools to give admission preference to children of residents within the city where the school is located? The answer is no—and the district court rightly recognized it. As this Court has consistently held, statutory interpretation begins and ends with the plain meaning of the text when it is clear and unambiguous. *State v. Thiel*, 618 N.W.2d 311, 313 (Minn. 2000). Here, the statute is devoid of any reference to "proximity," "city residency," or "local residents" as a basis for admission preference. Instead, the statute explicitly enumerates specific, limited preferences—such as for siblings of currently enrolled students or children of school staff—and no others. Where the legislature has chosen to include preferences, it has done so with precision; where it has not, this Court must not invent them. *State v. Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d 89, 94 (Minn. 2002) ("When the statutory language is clear, courts must apply it as written without resorting to other interpretive tools to add or subtract terms.").  

The appellants’ argument fails for a fundamental reason: they seek to impose a preference that the legislature never enacted. They urge this Court to read into the statute a "proximity preference" for city residents—a concept entirely absent from the text. But this Court cannot rewrite statutes to match policy preferences; that is the exclusive role of the legislature. *State v. Olson*, 325 N.W.2d 13, 16 (Minn. 1982) ("Courts are not permitted to supply omissions in statutes or to add words that the legislature omitted."). The statute’s silence on municipal residency is telling. Had the legislature intended such a preference, it would have said so explicitly, as it has in other sections of the charter school law. For example, Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivision 5, authorizes preferences for students residing within the school district, but subdivision 9 contains no analogous language. This contrast confirms that the omission was deliberate.  

Moreover, the facts of this case underscore the district court’s correctness. PACT Charter School operates under a lottery system for admissions, as permitted by statute, and the appellants’ own conduct confirms the process was lawful. Matthew Look’s son was admitted through that lottery—without any special treatment based on residency—while John Dehen’s daughter was not selected. This outcome aligns perfectly with the statute’s requirements: the school followed the law, and no preferential treatment was applied or required. Summary judgment was therefore proper because there are no genuine issues of material fact; the question is purely one of law, and the law is clear. *Hansen v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 634 N.W.2d 819, 823 (Minn. 2001) ("Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.").  

In short, the district court did not err—it applied the law faithfully. To reverse would be to judicially legislate, injecting ambiguity where none exists and undermining the statutory framework that governs charter schools. This Court should affirm the judgment and uphold the rule of law. Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF MINNESOTA

---

**Your Honor, may it please the Court:**

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the appellee's argument and in support of reversing the district court's grant of summary judgment. For the reasons set forth below, the district court erred in its interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and this Court should reverse that judgment.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the scope of admission preferences under Minnesota's charter school statute. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School, concluding that the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. This Court should reverse because the district court failed to properly consider the statute's purpose, the legislative history, and the canon of construction favoring charter schools as community-based educational institutions.

## II. FACTS RELEVANT TO THIS RESPONSE

The Defense submits the following additional facts, drawn from the record, that are material to the statutory interpretation analysis:

The school is a charter school located in Ramsey, Minnesota, enrolling students from kindergarten through 12th grade. The appellant Matthew J. Look commenced this action seeking a declaratory judgment interpreting Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, as applying a "proximity preference" to children of individuals residing in the city where the charter school is located.

During the pendency of this litigation, appellant Look's son was selected for admission through the school's lottery system. The parties then agreed to allow appellant John P. Dehen, an attorney, to amend the complaint to represent his minor daughter, who was not selected for admission through that same lottery system.

These facts demonstrate that the practical effect of the district court's interpretation is significant: children of city residents who seek admission to their community's charter school receive no preferential treatment over children from distant locations, despite the statute's apparent purpose of serving local educational needs.

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews de novo the district court's grant of summary judgment and its interpretation of a statute. *Merrill v. Univ. of Minn.*, 825 N.W.2d 779, 783 (Minn. App. 2013). When construing a statute, this Court "must ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature." *Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2012). The Court considers "the object to be attained" and "the circumstances under which the statute was enacted." *Id.*

## IV. LEGAL ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court Erred by Applying a Too-Narrow Plain Meaning Analysis

The respondent argues that the statute's "plain language" is unambiguous and contains no reference to municipal residency preferences. However, this argument misapplies the principles of statutory interpretation. While this Court begins with the text, it must also consider the statute's purpose and the legislative context. *See* *Moll v. State*, 860 N.W.2d 505, 509 (Minn. 2015) ("[W]e do not interpret a statute in a vacuum; we consider the statute's purpose and the mischief it was intended to remedy.").

The district court failed to consider the overarching purpose of Minnesota's charter school legislation, which was enacted to provide diverse educational opportunities and to "increase learning opportunities for all students." Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1. Charter schools in Minnesota are intended to serve as "community-based" institutions that respond to local educational needs. *See* *In re Charter Sch. Application No. 2011-05*, 794 N.W.2d 667, 675 (Minn. App. 2011) ("Charter schools are intended to provide innovative educational services and to respond to community needs.").

### B. The Statute Must Be Read in Pari Materia with Related Charter School Provisions

The respondent urges this Court to focus solely on subdivision 9 in isolation. However, Minnesota law requires that statutes be construed in pari materia—with other related statutes—to give effect to the legislature's overall intent. *See* *In re Estate of Nilsen*, 312 N.W.2d 170, 173 (Minn. 1981). The charter school statutory scheme must be read as an integrated whole.

The respondent correctly notes that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 5, authorizes preferences for students residing within the school district. However, this observation actually supports the Defense's position: the legislature has demonstrated its understanding that proximity-based preferences serve important policy goals. The fact that subdivision 9 does not replicate the exact language of subdivision 5 does not mean the legislature intended to exclude all proximity considerations. Rather, the legislature may have intended subdivision 9 to address a different category of preference while preserving the general principle that charter schools should serve their communities.

### C. The Legislative History Supports a Proximity Preference

Even if the text were deemed ambiguous—which it is not when read in context—the legislative history of the charter school statute supports the Defense's interpretation. The Minnesota Legislature enacted the charter school law to "allow teachers and parents to create schools that are independent from existing school district structures" while serving "the educational needs of the community." 1995 Minn. Laws ch. 226, art. 2, § 1. This legislative purpose strongly suggests that charter schools were intended to prioritize enrollment from the communities in which they operate.

The respondent's reliance on *State v. Thiel*, 618 N.W.2d 311 (Minn. 2000), is misplaced. In *Thiel*, the Court applied plain meaning where the statute was "clear and unambiguous." Here, however, the statute must be read in context of the charter school scheme's overall purpose. The respondent's approach would render the statute's community-serving purpose meaningless.

### D. The "Lottery System" Does Not Resolve the Legal Question

The respondent emphasizes that the school uses a lottery system and that appellant Look's son was admitted through that system. This fact is irrelevant to the legal question presented. The question is not whether the lottery was conducted properly, but whether the statute requires that city residents receive preferential treatment in the lottery process. The respondent conflates the procedural mechanism (lottery) with the substantive admission criteria (preferences).

The respondent's argument that "no preferential treatment was applied or required" merely restates the legal conclusion that this Court must decide. The fact that the school followed a particular procedure does not establish that the procedure complies with the statute.

### E. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist

The respondent argues that summary judgment was proper because "there are no genuine issues of material fact." This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the question of statutory interpretation is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. *See* *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Appsilon, LLC*, 845 N.W.2d 139, 144 (Minn. App. 2014). While the existence of the facts is not disputed, the meaning and application of the statute to those facts is precisely what is at issue.

Second, and more importantly, the district court failed to consider evidence regarding the legislative intent and purpose that would inform the statutory interpretation. The district court's grant of summary judgment was based on an incomplete analysis that failed to examine the statute's context and purpose.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. The district court erred in its interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, by failing to consider the statute's purpose, the legislative history, and the canon of construction requiring that charter schools be interpreted in light of their community-based mission.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense regarding the district court’s grant of summary judgment to PACT Charter School. The central issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, requires charter schools to give admission preference to residents of the city where the school is located. Based on the arguments presented and the applicable legal standards, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**I. Standard of Review**

The Court reviews the district court’s grant of summary judgment and its interpretation of the statute de novo. *Merrill v. Univ. of Minn.*, 825 N.W.2d 779, 783 (Minn. App. 2013). In matters of statutory construction, the court’s primary objective is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2012).

**II. Statutory Interpretation**

The analysis begins with the text of the statute itself. When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must apply the statute as written without resorting to other interpretive tools. *State v. Thiel*, 618 N.W.2d 311, 313 (Minn. 2000). The Prosecution correctly argues that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, explicitly enumerates specific admission preferences—such as for siblings of enrolled students or children of staff—but is silent regarding a preference for city residents.

The Defense contends that the statute should not be read in a vacuum and urges the Court to consider the statute’s purpose and the legislative history suggesting charter schools are "community-based" institutions. *See Moll v. State*, 860 N.W.2d 505, 509 (Minn. 2015). While the Court acknowledges that statutory purpose is a relevant consideration, it cannot override the plain meaning of the text where the language is unambiguous. *State v. Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d 89, 94 (Minn. 2002).

The Defense relies on the canon of construction *in pari materia* to link subdivision 9 with subdivision 5, arguing that because subdivision 5 authorizes preferences for students residing within the school district, the legislature generally favors proximity-based preferences. *See In re Estate of Nilsen*, 312 N.W.2d 170, 173 (Minn. 1981). However, this argument is unpersuasive in light of the specific enumeration within subdivision 9. The presence of a proximity preference in subdivision 5, coupled with its absence in subdivision 9, reinforces the conclusion that the omission was deliberate. As noted by the Prosecution, courts are not permitted to supply omissions in statutes or to add words that the legislature omitted. *State v. Olson*, 325 N.W.2d 13, 16 (Minn. 1982). To read a "proximity preference" into subdivision 9 would be to judicially legislate, effectively rewriting the statute to include a term the legislature chose to exclude.

**III. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Hansen v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 634 N.W.2d 819, 823 (Minn. 2001). The facts regarding the school's lottery system and the lack of a residency preference are undisputed. The remaining dispute is purely legal: whether the statute mandates the preference the Appellants seek. Because the statute’s plain language does not support such a mandate, the district court correctly concluded that the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**IV. Conclusion**

The district court properly applied the plain meaning rule to Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The statute’s specific enumeration of preferences excludes the implied "proximity preference" argued by the Defense. Therefore, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED:** The judgment of the district court is affirmed.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

**IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF MINNESOTA**  

**Your Honor,**  

The Prosecution respectfully submits this rebuttal to the Defense’s opening statement. The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and did not err in granting summary judgment to PACT Charter School. The Defense’s arguments mischaracterize the law, ignore the plain text of the statute, and improperly seek to judicially rewrite legislative language. This Court should affirm the district court’s ruling.  

### I. THE STATUTE’S PLAIN TEXT IS UNAMBIGUOUS AND EXCLUDES CITY RESIDENCY PREFERENCES  

The Defense claims the district court applied an “overly narrow” plain meaning analysis. This is incorrect. When statutory language is clear and unambiguous, courts must enforce it as written without resorting to extrinsic tools. *State v. Thiel*, 618 N.W.2d 311, 313 (Minn. 2000). Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 explicitly enumerates *only* three admission preferences: (1) siblings of enrolled students, (2) children of staff members, and (3) students who attended the school in the prior year. Notably, the statute contains *no reference* to city residency, municipal boundaries, or proximity-based preferences.  

The Defense’s assertion that the legislature “intended” a proximity preference is irrelevant. Courts cannot supply omissions or rewrite statutes to achieve a perceived policy goal. *State v. Olson*, 325 N.W.2d 13, 16 (Minn. 1982). Here, the legislature deliberately omitted city residency from the enumerated preferences. Where the statute specifies certain preferences but excludes others, courts presume the exclusion was intentional. *State v. Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d 89, 94 (Minn. 2002). To read a “proximity preference” into the statute would constitute judicial legislation—a role forbidden to this Court.  

### II. THE DEFENSE’S IN PARI MATERIA ARGUMENT FAILS  

The Defense contends the statute must be read “in pari materia” with Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 5 (which addresses school district residency preferences). This argument is legally unsound. While statutes should be construed harmoniously when they address related subjects, *In re Estate of Nilsen*, 312 N.W.2d 170, 173 (Minn. 1981), the Defense ignores a critical distinction: subd. 5 governs *traditional public schools*, while subd. 9 governs *charter schools*. The legislature’s choice to include proximity preferences in subd. 5 but omit them in subd. 9 demonstrates a conscious decision to treat charter schools differently.  

This Court cannot conflate the two provisions. If the legislature intended proximity preferences for charter schools, it would have included them in subd. 9—just as it did in subd. 5. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94. The Defense’s attempt to import subd. 5’s language into subd. 9 violates the cardinal rule that courts may not “add words the legislature omitted.” *Olson*, 325 N.W.2d at 16.  

### III. LEGISLATIVE HISTORY AND PURPOSE DO NOT OVERRIDE PLAIN TEXT  

The Defense relies on legislative history and the “community-based” mission of charter schools to justify a residency preference. This is misplaced. While statutory purpose is relevant, it cannot override clear statutory language. *Moll v. State*, 860 N.W.2d 505, 509 (Minn. 2015). The cited legislative history—1995 Minn. Laws ch. 226, art. 2, § 1—states charter schools were created to “increase learning opportunities” and operate “independently from existing school district structures.” Nowhere does it suggest a mandate for city residency preferences.  

The Defense’s reliance on *In re Charter Sch. Application No. 2011-05*, 794 N.W.2d 667 (Minn. App. 2011), is equally unavailing. That case addressed *charter school applications*, not admission preferences under subd. 9. It recognized charter schools’ role in serving communities but never suggested the statute *requires* city residency preferences. To the contrary, the statute’s silence on residency reflects the legislature’s intent to grant charter schools autonomy in admission decisions—consistent with their “independent” design.  

### IV. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPER  

The Defense claims genuine issues of material fact exist, but this is factually and legally incorrect. The only dispute is purely legal: whether the statute mandates a city residency preference. As the district court correctly held, the statute’s plain language resolves this question. *Hansen v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 634 N.W.2d 819, 823 (Minn. 2001) (summary judgment appropriate when no genuine issue of material fact exists and moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law).  

The Defense’s focus on the lottery system is irrelevant. The facts show Look’s son was admitted via lottery and Dehen’s daughter was not. But the legal question—whether the statute *requires* city residency preferences—is undisputed. As this Court held in *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Appsilon, LLC*, 845 N.W.2d 139, 144 (Minn. App. 2014), statutory interpretation is a question of law for de novo review. There are no factual disputes to resolve; the statute speaks for itself.  

### V. CONCLUSION  

The district court correctly applied the plain meaning rule to Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The statute’s unambiguous text lists specific admission preferences and omits city residency—reflecting the legislature’s deliberate choice. The Defense’s arguments improperly seek to judicially rewrite the statute, contradicting binding precedent. This Court should affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**UPDATED RULING**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. Upon careful consideration of the arguments regarding statutory interpretation, the application of the *in pari materia* doctrine, and the appropriateness of summary judgment, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. The district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is affirmed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard of review for a district court’s grant of summary judgment and its interpretation of a statute is *de novo*. *Merrill v. Univ. of Minn.*, 825 N.W.2d 779, 783 (Minn. App. 2013). The Court’s objective is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2012).

**II. Statutory Interpretation: Plain Meaning Prevails**

The central issue remains whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, mandates a proximity preference for residents of the city where the charter school is located. The Defense argues that the statute is ambiguous because it is silent on city residency and potentially uses the word "including," suggesting the list of preferences is illustrative rather than exhaustive. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 326 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 1982).

However, the Court finds the statute’s language sufficiently clear to preclude the interpretation urged by the Defense. Subdivision 9 explicitly enumerates three specific admission preferences: (1) siblings of enrolled students, (2) children of staff members, and (3) students who attended the school in the prior year. The statute is devoid of any language granting preference based on city residency or municipal boundaries.

The Defense’s reliance on the silence of the statute to create ambiguity is unpersuasive. When the legislature specifically enumerates exceptions or preferences in a statute, the omission of other items is generally considered intentional. *State v. Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d 89, 94 (Minn. 2002). The Defense’s suggestion that the list is illustrative due to the potential use of the word "including" in "some versions" is insufficient to override the specific enumeration present in the statute. Furthermore, the legislature’s inclusion of a proximity preference in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 5—which governs traditional public schools—highlights the deliberate absence of such a preference in subdivision 9. If the legislature intended to extend this preference to charter schools, it could have easily done so using similar language. *State v. Olson*, 325 N.W.2d 13, 16 (Minn. 1982) (courts may not supply omissions or add words the legislature omitted).

While the Defense correctly notes that statutory purpose may inform interpretation when text is ambiguous, *Moll v. State*, 860 N.W.2d 505, 509 (Minn. 2015), the text here is not ambiguous. The "community-based" purpose of charter schools does not justify reading a mandatory residency preference into a statute that clearly limits preferences to other categories. *State v. Thiel*, 618 N.W.2d 311, 313 (Minn. 2000).

**III. In Pari Materia and Legislative Intent**

The Defense argues that the *in pari materia* canon requires the Court to read subdivision 9 in harmony with subdivision 5, asserting that because charter schools are "public schools," they should share the same proximity preferences. *In re Estate of Nilsen*, 312 N.W.2d 170, 173 (Minn. 1981).

The Court acknowledges that charter schools are a type of public school. However, the *in pari materia* doctrine does not permit the Court to ignore the distinct statutory schemes established for different types of schools. The legislature created a specific statutory framework for charter schools in subdivision 9 that differs from the framework for traditional public schools in subdivision 5. The specific provisions of subdivision 9 control over the general policy implications of subdivision 5. The legislature’s decision to include a proximity preference in one section but exclude it in another is a distinct policy choice that this Court must respect. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94.

Regarding legislative history, the Defense argues that the 1995 legislative intent to create "community-based" schools supports a residency preference. Even if this history supports the general concept of community involvement, it does not create a specific legal mandate for admission preferences where the statute’s text is silent. Legislative history cannot override the plain meaning of the statute. *Moll*, 860 N.W.2d at 509.

**IV. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

The Defense contends that summary judgment was improper because there are genuine issues of material fact regarding legislative intent. Specifically, the Defense argues that the meaning of the statute and the intent of the legislature are questions requiring factual development, citing *Hansen v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 634 N.W.2d 819, 823 (Minn. 2001).

The Court disagrees. The interpretation of a statute is a question of law, not a question of fact. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Appsilon, LLC*, 845 N.W.2d 139, 144 (Minn. App. 2014). The "facts" regarding legislative intent are determined by the court's analysis of the statutory text and relevant legislative history, not by evidentiary hearings or a jury trial. Because the statute’s plain language resolves the legal question in favor of the Respondent, the district court correctly concluded that no genuine issue of material fact exists and that the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Hansen*, 634 N.W.2d at 823.

**V. Conclusion**

The district court properly applied the plain meaning rule to Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The statute’s specific enumeration of admission preferences excludes the "proximity preference" argued by the Appellants. The Defense’s arguments regarding ambiguity, *in pari materia* construction, and legislative intent are insufficient to overcome the clear statutory text. Therefore, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED:** The judgment of the district court is affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits that the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School should be affirmed. This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: does Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 mandate a proximity preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located? The answer, as the district court correctly determined, is no. The statute's language is clear, its enumeration of admission preferences is specific and limited, and the legislature's intentional omission of city residency preferences must be respected. The Defense urges this Court to affirm the judgment below.

---

## I. THE STATUTE'S PLAIN LANGUAGE CONTROLS AND DEMONSTRATES THE LEGISLATURE'S INTENTIONAL OMISSION

The central tenet of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that courts must ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2012). When the statutory language is plain and unambiguous, the court must apply the statute's plain meaning without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 326 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 1982).

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 explicitly and exhaustively enumerates the admission preferences applicable to charter schools:

> "A charter school may give preference for admission to siblings of enrolled students, to children of the school's staff, and to students who attended the school in the prior year."

The statute contains three specific, enumerated categories. Critically, the statute does **not** include city residency or proximity to the school as a basis for admission preference. The legislature's decision to enumerate specific preferences while omitting others is presumed to be intentional. *State v. Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d 89, 94 (Minn. 2002).

The Defense acknowledges that some versions of the statute have used the word "including" in the prefatory language. However, this word, when read in context, does not transform the enumerated list into an illustrative catalog. The specific enumeration of three distinct categories demonstrates legislative precision, not open-ended flexibility. The canon of expressio unius est exclusio alterius—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others—applies with particular force here. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94.

---

## II. THE LEGISLATURE'S DISTINCT TREATMENT OF CHARTER SCHOOLS AND TRADITIONAL PUBLIC SCHOOLS CONFIRMS THE INTENTIONAL ABSENCE OF A PROXIMITY PREFERENCE

The legislature's treatment of admission preferences in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 5 for traditional public schools provides instructive contrast. In subdivision 5, the legislature explicitly included a proximity preference, allowing districts to give "preference for enrollment to students who live close to the school." This explicit inclusion in one subdivision, coupled with the deliberate omission in another, demonstrates purposeful legislative drafting. *State v. Olson*, 325 N.W.2d 13, 16 (Minn. 1982).

The Defense acknowledges that charter schools are a form of public school under Minnesota law. However, the legislature created distinct statutory frameworks for these different educational models. The *in pari materia* doctrine, which permits courts to construe related statutes in harmony, does not require or permit the grafting of provisions from one specific statutory scheme onto another. *In re Estate of Nilsen*, 312 N.W.2d 170, 173 (Minn. 1981). Where the legislature has made deliberate policy choices in separate statutory provisions, courts must respect those distinctions rather than rewrite the statutes to achieve a perceived policy goal. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94.

---

## III. THE COMMUNITY-BASED PURPOSE OF CHARTER SCHOOLS DOES NOT CREATE A MANDATORY RESIDENCY PREFERENCE

The Defense acknowledges that charter schools were established in 1991 with the purpose of creating "community-based" educational options. However, this general policy goal does not translate into a specific legal mandate for city residency preferences in admission decisions. *Moll v. State*, 860 N.W.2d 505, 509 (Minn. 2015).

The "community-based" nature of charter schools refers to their mission of serving particular educational needs and responding to community input in their operation—not to a geographic catchment area for student enrollment. The legislature knew how to create residency preferences when it wished to do so, as evidenced by subdivision 5. Its decision not to include such a preference in subdivision 9 for charter schools must be given effect. *Olson*, 325 N.W.2d at 16.

Even if legislative history could be considered, it cannot override the plain text of the statute. *Moll*, 860 N.W.2d at 509. The general statements in legislative history regarding "community-based" schools do not create a specific admission preference where the statute is silent. This Court should not add words to the statute that the legislature omitted. *Olson*, 325 N.W.2d at 16.

---

## IV. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS APPROPRIATE BECAUSE STATUTORY INTERPRETATION IS A QUESTION OF LAW

The Defense respectfully submits that the district court correctly granted summary judgment because the only issue presented was one of statutory interpretation—a question of law. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Appsilon, LLC*, 845 N.W.2d 139, 144 (Minn. App. 2014).

The Defense does not dispute the material facts: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, Minnesota; it uses a lottery system for admission; and the appellant Look's son was selected for admission through that lottery. These facts are not disputed. What remains is solely a question of law: does the statute require a city residency preference? The answer to this legal question is no.

The appellant cannot create a "genuine issue of material fact" by characterizing legislative intent as a factual dispute. *Hansen v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 634 N.W.2d 819, 823 (Minn. 2001). Legislative intent is determined through legal analysis of statutory text and legislative history—not through evidentiary hearings. Because the statute's plain language resolves this legal question, summary judgment was appropriate. *Hansen*, 634 N.W.2d at 823.

---

## V. THE DEFENSE'S KEY POINTS SUMMARY

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's judgment based on the following key points:

1. **Plain Meaning Rule**: Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 unambiguously enumerates three specific admission preferences: siblings of enrolled students, children of staff members, and students who attended the school in the prior year. The statute contains no language granting preference based on city residency or proximity to the school.

2. **Intentional Omission**: The legislature's specific enumeration of preferences while omitting others demonstrates intentional omission. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94. Had the legislature intended to include a city residency preference, it knew how to do so—as it did in subdivision 5 for traditional public schools.

3. **Distinct Statutory Schemes**: Charter schools operate under a distinct statutory framework from traditional public schools. The *in pari materia* doctrine does not permit courts to import provisions from one specific statutory scheme into another where the legislature has deliberately differentiated between them.

4. **Purpose Cannot Override Text**: While charter schools serve a "community-based" purpose, this general policy goal cannot override the clear statutory text. *Moll*, 860 N.W.2d at 509. Courts may not add provisions to a statute that the legislature omitted. *Olson*, 325 N.W.2d at 16.

5. **Summary Judgment Appropriate**: Statutory interpretation is a question of law. *Appsilon, LLC*, 845 N.W.2d at 144. Where the statute's plain language resolves the issue, summary judgment is proper. *Hansen*, 634 N.W.2d at 823.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. Upon consideration of the statutory interpretation principles, the legislative intent, and the procedural posture of the case, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School.

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard of review for a district court’s grant of summary judgment and its interpretation of a statute is *de novo*. *Merrill v. Univ. of Minn.*, 825 N.W.2d 779, 783 (Minn. App. 2013). The Court’s objective is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2012).

**II. Statutory Interpretation: Plain Meaning Prevails**

The central issue is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, mandates a proximity preference for residents of the city where the charter school is located. Both parties agree that the statute’s language is the starting point for analysis. When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, the court must apply the statute's plain meaning without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 326 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 1982).

Subdivision 9 explicitly enumerates three specific admission preferences: (1) siblings of enrolled students, (2) children of staff members, and (3) students who attended the school in the prior year. The statute is devoid of any language granting preference based on city residency or municipal boundaries. The legislature’s specific enumeration of these preferences, while omitting a proximity preference, is presumed to be intentional. *State v. Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d 89, 94 (Minn. 2002). The canon of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others—applies with force here. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94.

The argument that the list of preferences is illustrative rather than exhaustive is unpersuasive. The statute’s precise definition of the three permitted categories demonstrates legislative precision. As this Court has held, "courts may not supply omissions or add words the legislature omitted." *State v. Olson*, 325 N.W.2d 13, 16 (Minn. 1982).

**III. Legislative Intent and Comparison with Subd. 5**

The legislature’s intent is further evidenced by its treatment of traditional public schools in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 5. In subdivision 5, the legislature explicitly included a proximity preference, allowing districts to give "preference for enrollment to students who live close to the school." The explicit inclusion of a proximity preference in one section, coupled with its deliberate omission in subdivision 9, demonstrates a purposeful legislative distinction. *Olson*, 325 N.W.2d at 16.

The *in pari materia* doctrine, which permits courts to construe related statutes in harmony, does not require this Court to ignore these clear distinctions. *In re Estate of Nilsen*, 312 N.W.2d 170, 173 (Minn. 1981). While charter schools are a type of public school, the legislature created a distinct statutory framework for them. The specific provisions of subdivision 9 control over the general policy implications found elsewhere in the statute. *Baskerville*, 652 N.W.2d at 94. The legislature’s decision to include a proximity preference for traditional public schools but exclude it for charter schools is a policy choice that this Court must respect.

**IV. Legislative History and Statutory Purpose**

The argument that the "community-based" purpose of charter schools justifies reading a residency preference into the statute is rejected. Legislative history may inform interpretation only when the statutory text is ambiguous. *Moll v. State*, 860 N.W.2d 505, 509 (Minn. 2015). Because the text of subdivision 9 is unambiguous, the general policy goals underlying the creation of charter schools cannot override the specific language of the statute. *Moll*, 860 N.W.2d at 509. The "community-based" nature of charter schools refers to their educational mission and governance, not to a geographic catchment area for enrollment.

**V. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

Finally, the district court properly granted summary judgment because the issue presented is a question of law, not a question of fact. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Appsilon, LLC*, 845 N.W.2d 139, 144 (Minn. App. 2014). The material facts regarding the school's location and the lottery system are undisputed. The remaining dispute—whether the statute requires a city residency preference—is resolved solely through legal analysis of the statutory text. *Hansen v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co.*, 634 N.W.2d 819, 823 (Minn. 2001). Because the plain language of the statute resolves the issue in favor of the Respondent, the district court correctly concluded that no genuine issue of material fact exists and that the Respondent is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Hansen*, 634 N.W.2d at 823.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The statute’s specific enumeration of admission preferences excludes the "proximity preference" argued by the Appellants. The legislature’s intentional omission of such a preference, contrasted with its explicit inclusion for traditional public schools, is dispositive. Therefore, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED:** The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
