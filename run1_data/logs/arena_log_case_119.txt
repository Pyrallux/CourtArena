FACTS:
II. Factual and Procedural Background

On October 1, 2012, Joki filed the initial complaint against the State Defendants, 114 public school districts, and one charter school. Joki sought to proceed as a representative of a class consisting of all students currently enrolled in the defendant school districts, together with their parents or guardians. In the initial complaint, Joki requested reimbursement for the damages that he and other class members suffered, and continued to suffer, as a result of the unconstitutional fees charged by the defendant school districts. On October 9, 2012, Joki filed an amended class action complaint, adding a second cause of action against the State Defendants. Specifically, Joki requested a declaratory judgment that Idaho’s current system of funding education is unconstitutional. Joki cited article IX, section 1 of the Idaho Constitution as the basis for his claims, which provides, in pertinent part: “[I]t shall be the *7duty of the legislature of Idaho, to establish and maintain a general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools.” Idaho Const, art. IX, § 1.

On October 30, 2012, the State Defendants filed a motion to dismiss, which the district court granted on March 19, 2013. On the same day, 53 of the 114 school districts were voluntarily dismissed by Joki. On March 27, 2013, Joki submitted a motion to alter or amend the order dismissing the State Defendants. On June 27, 2013, Joki, Meridian Joint District #2, and the State Defendants stipulated that Joki may file a second amended complaint, with the provision that Meridian Joint District #2 and the State Defendants reserved the right to seek dismissal, summary judgment, and resist class certification. The district court granted Joki’s motion for leave to file a second amended complaint. On July 16, 2013, Joki filed the second amended complaint, which sought: (1) reimbursement from Meridian Joint District #2 or the legislature of certain fees imposed by the school districts; and (2) a declaratory judgment against the State Defendants that the current system of funding education in Idaho is unconstitutional.

On July 17, 2013, the State Defendants filed a motion to dismiss the second amended complaint. In an accompanying memorandum, the State Defendants argued that the claims against them fell squarely within the terns of the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act, Idaho Code sections 6-2201-2216 (hereinafter referred to as the “CBECA”), and should be dismissed according to the provisions therein. In response, Joki argued that the CBECA did not require the dismissal of the State Defendants. Specifically, Joki asserted that according to this Court’s holding in Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (hereinafter referred to as “ISEEO IV”), the CBECA was unconstitutional as applied to his second cause of action.2

On November 27, 2013, the district court issued a memorandum decision and order regarding the State Defendants’ motion to dismiss. 

Next, the district court analyzed Joki’s request for a declaratory judgment that the current system of funding education in Idaho is unconstitutional. The district court acknowledged Joki’s argument that in ISEEO V this Court found the system of funding school facilities in Idaho was unconstitutional. However, the district court held that Joki’s complaint failed to state a claim to enforce ISEEO V. The district court emphasized that ISEEO V addressed the narrow issue of funding for school facilities, not the education system as a whole. Because Joki’s complaint did not allege inadequate school facilities, the district court concluded that Joki failed to state a claim to enforce ISEEO V. Further, the district court reasoned that even if Joki were to allege facts within the purview of this *8Court’s holding in ISEEO V, the CBECA would nevertheless apply and require Joki to first exhaust the remedies under the CBECA before pursuing action against the State Defendants. In sum, the district court held that the CBECA governed all of Joki’s claims, as brought against the State Defendants, and required dismissal.

On June 27, 2014, the district court denied Joki’s motion for class certification due to “concerns over the suitability of [Joki] as representative[ ] of the proposed class.” Ultimately, on December 7, 2016, the district court entered judgment in favor of Joki against Meridian Joint District #2 in the amount of $85. Joki appealed the dismissal of the State Defendants.

## INTRODUCTION

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE CBECA GOVERNS JOKI'S CLAIMS AGAINST THE STATE DEFENDANTS

The district court held that the CBECA applies to all claims against the State Defendants and requires dismissal. This holding was erroneous because this Court in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) ("ISEEO IV"), explicitly held that the CBECA is unconstitutional as applied to claims seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution.

In *ISEEO IV*, this Court addressed the precise question of whether the CBECA could validly require plaintiffs to exhaust administrative remedies before bringing constitutional claims against the state. This Court held that the CBECA's exhaustion requirement was unconstitutional as applied because it effectively barred citizens from enforcing their constitutional right to a "general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools" under Article IX, Section 1. The Court reasoned that the constitutional nature of the claim warranted judicial relief independent of the administrative process contemplated by the CBECA.

The district court in this case acknowledged *ISEEO IV* but attempted to distinguish it by noting that Joki's second cause of action sought a declaratory judgment rather than the specific relief addressed in *ISEEO IV*. This distinction is without merit. The principle established in *ISEEO IV* applies with equal force to any claim seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1 against state officials. The constitutional nature of the claim does not transform based on the form of relief requested.

Accordingly, the district court erred in applying the CBECA to dismiss Joki's claims. This Court should reverse this holding.

---

## II. JOKI'S COMPLAINT STATES A VIABLE CLAIM FOR DECLARATORY RELIEF UNDER ISEEO V

The district court further erred in holding that Joki failed to state a claim under *ISEEO V*. In *ISEEO V*, this Court held that Idaho's system of funding school facilities was unconstitutional because it failed to provide adequate funding for school facilities, thereby violating Article IX, Section 1. The Court emphasized that the state's failure to fund school facilities adequately resulted in an unconstitutional system.

Joki's complaint, as alleged in the second amended complaint, seeks a declaratory judgment that Idaho's "current system of funding education is unconstitutional." The complaint specifically cites Article IX, Section 1 as the constitutional basis for this claim. While the district court characterized this as a broad attack on the entire education funding system, the complaint must be read in the context of the factual allegations.

Joki alleged that defendant school districts imposed unconstitutional fees on students. These fees, if proven, would demonstrate that the state's funding system has failed to provide a "thorough" system of public schools as required by Article IX, Section 1. A system that forces local school districts to impose fees on students to meet operational costs cannot be considered "free" as required by the Idaho Constitution.

The district court reasoned that *ISEEO V* addressed only "the narrow issue of funding for school facilities" and that Joki's complaint did not allege inadequate school facilities. This reasoning misapprehends the scope of *ISEEO V*. While the specific holding in *ISEEO V* addressed school facilities funding, the underlying constitutional principle—that the state must provide adequate funding to enable school districts to fulfill their constitutional obligations—applies with equal force to all aspects of the educational system.

Furthermore, the district court's reasoning that Joki failed to "allege facts within the purview of this Court's holding in ISEEO V" reflects an unduly narrow reading of the complaint. Joki's allegations regarding unconstitutional fees imposed on students are directly related to the state's funding obligations. If the state's funding system is so inadequate that school districts must impose fees on students, that system fails to provide the "free common schools" mandated by Article IX, Section 1.

---

## III. THE STATE DEFENDANTS HAVE A CONSTITUTIONAL DUTY THAT EXTENDS TO THE ALLEGED UNCONSTITUTIONAL FEES

The prosecution argues that the State Defendants cannot be held liable for fees imposed by local school districts because school districts operate as separate legal entities with autonomy over local decisions. This argument, while addressing the relationship between state and local authority, misses the central point of Joki's claim.

Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution provides that "it shall be the duty of the legislature of Idaho, to establish and maintain a general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools." This duty rests with the state legislature and, by extension, state officials responsible for implementing legislative policies. The legislature cannot abdicate its constitutional duty by delegating authority to local school districts and then claiming no responsibility when those districts impose unconstitutional fees.

In *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 138 Idaho 806, 69 P.3d 184 (2003) ("ISEEO I"), this Court recognized that the legislature bears the ultimate responsibility for ensuring that the state's education funding system complies with constitutional requirements. The Court stated that "the legislature has the duty to establish and maintain a general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools" and that this duty cannot be avoided.

If local school districts are imposing fees that render the public school system non-free, this is a failure of the state's funding system—a system that the legislature is constitutionally mandated to establish and maintain. The State Defendants, as officials responsible for implementing the state's education funding system, bear responsibility for this alleged failure.

The prosecution's reliance on the proposition that school districts operate as separate legal entities is not disputed. However, this separate status does not absolve the state of its constitutional duty. The state created the system of school districts and continues to fund that system. When the funding system results in unconstitutional consequences—such as fees that render public schools no longer "free"—the state cannot hide behind the separate legal status of local school districts.

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING JOKI'S CLAIM FOR FEE REIMBURSEMENT AGAINST THE STATE DEFENDANTS

Joki's complaint seeks reimbursement for fees allegedly unconstitutionally charged by defendant school districts. The prosecution argues that these claims are "misdirected" because the fees were imposed by local school districts, not state officials.

The fact that local school districts acted as the instrumentality does not sever the causal connection to state policy.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument proves too much. If accepted, it would immunize state officials from any constitutional claim relating to education funding simply by delegating decisions to local entities. This result contradicts the fundamental principle that the state bears ultimate responsibility for the constitutional adequacy of its education system.

The defense submits that Joki's complaint, taken as true as it must be on a motion to dismiss, states a viable claim for reimbursement against the State Defendants. The district court erred in dismissing this claim.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants. The Appellant, Joki, challenges the dismissal of his claims for declaratory judgment and fee reimbursement, arguing that the district court erred in its application of the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act (CBECA) and in determining that his complaint failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. After reviewing the parties' opening arguments and the factual background provided, the Court finds that the district court’s dismissal was premature and based on an overly restrictive interpretation of the applicable precedent and constitutional mandates.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The District Court Erred in Applying the CBECA to Bar Joki’s Claims**

The district court dismissed Joki’s claims against the State Defendants on the grounds that the CBECA governed the action and required dismissal. The State Defendants argue that this was correct because the claims fall within the statute's purview. However, the Defense correctly identifies that this Court’s holding in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (*ISEEO IV*), directly addresses this issue.

In *ISEEO IV*, this Court held that the CBECA was unconstitutional as applied to claims seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution. The district court acknowledged this precedent but attempted to distinguish it based on the form of relief sought by Joki (declaratory judgment versus the specific relief in *ISEEO IV*). The Defense persuasively argues that this distinction is without merit. The constitutional right to a "general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools" is the substantive right at issue, and the procedural mechanism of the CBECA cannot be used to bar access to the courts to enforce that right, regardless of the specific form of relief requested. Therefore, the district court erred in relying on the CBECA to dismiss the State Defendants.

**B. Joki’s Complaint States a Viable Claim Under Article IX, Section 1**

The district court dismissed Joki’s request for a declaratory judgment, reasoning that *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 143 Idaho 802, 153 P.3d 1086 (2007) (*ISEEO V*), addressed only the narrow issue of funding for school facilities, and because Joki did not allege inadequate facilities, he failed to state a claim. The Prosecution echoes this, arguing that *ISEEO V* is the sole controlling precedent and limits constitutional challenges to the specific context of facilities.

While the Prosecution is correct that *ISEEO V* focused on facilities, the Defense correctly argues that the underlying constitutional principle—that the state must provide adequate funding to ensure a "free" system—is not so limited. Joki’s complaint alleges that the current system of funding education is unconstitutional because it forces school districts to impose fees on students. If proven, this would directly violate the mandate of Article IX, Section 1 that the legislature maintain a system of "free common schools."

The district court’s conclusion that Joki failed to state a claim relies on an unduly narrow reading of the complaint. The Defense argues, and the Court agrees, that allegations regarding unconstitutional fees are intrinsically related to the adequacy of state funding. As noted in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 138 Idaho 806, 69 P.3d 184 (2003) (*ISEEO I*), the legislature bears the ultimate responsibility for ensuring the education system complies with constitutional requirements. The State Defendants cannot evade this responsibility by pointing to the autonomy of local school districts. If the state’s funding scheme is insufficient to cover operational costs, thereby necessitating fees that render schools not "free," this states a plausible claim for relief under Article IX, Section 1.

**C. The Claims Against the State Defendants Are Not Misdirected**

The Prosecution contends that the fee reimbursement claim is misdirected because local districts, not state officials, impose fees. However, viewed in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, Joki’s allegation is that the fees are a symptom of the state’s constitutional failure to fund education adequately. The Defense’s argument that the State Defendants have a constitutional duty that extends to the consequences of an inadequate funding system is consistent with the principle that the state cannot delegate away its constitutional obligations. Therefore, the causal connection between the state funding system and the alleged harm (fees) is sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing the State Defendants. The application of the CBECA was improper under *ISEEO IV*, and Joki’s complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a claim that the current funding system violates the "free schools" mandate of the Idaho Constitution. The Prosecution’s attempt to limit the scope of constitutional education claims solely to the facilities context of *ISEEO V* is rejected.

**ORDER**

IT IS ORDERED that the district court’s judgment dismissing the State Defendants is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

### I. ISEEO V IS STRICTLY LIMITED TO SCHOOL FACILITIES FUNDING, AND JOKI’S COMPLAINT FAILS TO ALLEGE SUCH CLAIMS  
The Idaho Supreme Court in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 143 Idaho 802, 153 P.3d 1086 (2007) (ISEEO V), explicitly confined its holding to "the narrow issue of funding for school facilities." The Court did not address the broader education funding system or operational costs unrelated to facilities. As the district court correctly noted, Joki’s complaint contains no allegations regarding inadequate school facilities funding. Instead, it focuses solely on "unconstitutional fees" imposed by local school districts for general operational expenses. This distinction is dispositive: ISEEO V does not support a claim for fee-based relief because it was never intended to govern non-facilities-related funding disputes. A claim that fails to meet the precise factual predicate of a precedent cannot survive dismissal.  

### II. STATE DEFENDANTS ARE IMPROPER PARTIES FOR LOCAL SCHOOL DISTRICT FEES UNDER IDAHO LAW  
Joki’s claim for fee reimbursement against the State Defendants is legally unsound because school districts operate as separate legal entities with independent authority over fee imposition. While the legislature bears the ultimate duty to "establish and maintain a general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools" under Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution, this duty does not transform state officials into personal defendants for local school district actions. As this Court held in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 138 Idaho 806, 69 P.3d 184 (2003) (ISEEO I), the legislature’s constitutional obligation is systemic in nature and does not create direct liability for state officials regarding discrete local decisions like fee imposition. The defense’s argument that state officials must "bear responsibility" for fees imposed by school districts ignores the fundamental separation of responsibilities between state and local education authorities under Idaho law.  

### III. ISEEO IV DOES NOT APPLY TO JOKI’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT WAS LIMITED TO FACILITIES FUNDING DISPUTES  
The defense’s reliance on *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (ISEEO IV), is misplaced. The Court did not hold that CBECA is unconstitutional as applied to all Article IX claims universally. Joki’s complaint concerns general school fees, which fall outside the facilities funding framework central to ISEEO IV. Because the factual and legal context of Joki’s claim differs fundamentally from the facilities funding disputes resolved in ISEEO IV, that precedent does not bar application of CBECA here. The district court properly applied CBECA to dismiss claims that do not align with the specific constitutional challenges addressed in ISEEO IV.  

### CONCLUSION  
The district court did not err in dismissing the State Defendants. Joki’s complaint fails to state a claim under ISEEO V because it lacks allegations about school facilities funding, the State Defendants are not proper parties for local fee impositions under ISEEO I, and ISEEO IV does not invalidate CBECA’s application to non-facilities-related claims. The defense’s arguments misread controlling precedent and ignore the legal boundaries between state and local education responsibilities. This Court should affirm the dismissal.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Constitutional Mandate for "Free" Schools Extends Beyond Facilities**

The Prosecution argues that *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 143 Idaho 802, 153 P.3d 1086 (2007) (*ISEEO V*), is strictly limited to school facilities funding and therefore does not support Joki’s claims regarding operational fees. While it is true that *ISEEO V* addressed the specific factual predicate of facilities deficiencies, the Prosecution conflates the specific factual context of that case with the broader scope of the constitutional provision at issue.

Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution mandates that the legislature “establish and maintain a general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools.” The term “free” is not qualified by a reference solely to facilities. As the Defense argues, the underlying principle of *ISEEO V* is that the state must provide adequate funding to ensure the constitutional mandate is met. If the state’s funding scheme is insufficient to cover operational costs, thereby necessitating fees that render schools not “free,” this constitutes a violation of Article IX, Section 1 just as much as a failure to fund buildings. The district court erred by concluding that Joki failed to state a claim simply because his allegations did not mirror the specific factual record of *ISEEO V*. The constitutional right to a “free” education encompasses the entirety of the educational experience, not merely the physical structure in which it occurs.

**B. The State Defendants Are Proper Parties Under a Non-Delegable Duty Theory**

The Prosecution contends that the State Defendants are improper parties because local school districts, as separate legal entities, impose the fees. This argument misunderstands the nature of the duty imposed by Article IX, Section 1. As held in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. This duty is non-delegable. **C. ISEEO IV Bars the Application of the CBECA to Joki’s Claims**

The Prosecution argues that *ISEEO IV*, which held the CBECA unconstitutional as applied to Article IX claims, is limited to the facilities funding context. This argument is legally flawed for two reasons. First, *ISEEO IV* was decided in 2004, three years prior to *ISEEO V* in 2007. Whether the claim involves facilities or operational fees, the CBECA cannot be used to bar access to the courts to enforce the “free common schools” guarantee. The district court erred in holding that the CBECA governed Joki’s claims and required their dismissal.

The Prosecution’s rebuttal fails to demonstrate that the district court’s dismissal was proper. The constitutional mandate for “free” schools is not restricted to the funding of facilities, and the state’s duty to maintain such a system is non-delegable. The Defense demonstrates that the district court erred in applying the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act (CBECA) to bar Joki's claims and in concluding that Joki failed to state a valid constitutional claim. The preliminary ruling correctly recognized that: (1) the constitutional mandate for "free" schools extends beyond mere facilities funding; (2) the State Defendants are proper parties under the non-delegable duty doctrine; and (3) *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) (ISEEO IV), bars the application of CBECA to Joki's claims. The Defense addresses each of the Prosecution's arguments below and reaffirms why this Court should uphold the preliminary ruling.

---

## II. THE CONSTITUTIONAL MANDATE FOR "FREE" SCHOOLS EXTENDS BEYOND FACILITIES

### A. The Plain Text of the Idaho Constitution

The Prosecution's central argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the scope of Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution. That provision mandates that "it shall be the duty of the legislature of Idaho, to establish and maintain a general, uniform and thorough system of public, free common schools." The term "free" is not modified by any reference to facilities, buildings, or physical infrastructure. As this Court recognized in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 143 Idaho 802, 153 P.3d 1086 (2007) (ISEEO V), the constitutional mandate encompasses the entirety of the educational experience.

### B. The Underlying Principle of ISEEO V

The Prosecution attempts to confine ISEEO V to its specific factual context of facility deficiencies, arguing that Joki cannot invoke that precedent because his claims concern operational fees rather than inadequate buildings. This argument conflates the specific factual predicate of a case with the broader legal principle it establishes. The underlying principle of ISEEO V is that the State must provide adequate funding to satisfy the constitutional mandate. If the State's funding scheme is insufficient to cover operational costs, thereby necessitating fees that render schools not "free," this constitutes a violation of Article IX, Section 1 just as much as a failure to fund buildings or facilities.

The Defense acknowledges, as the Prosecution correctly notes, that ISEEO V addressed "the specific factual predicate of facilities deficiencies." However, the legal principle derived from that case—that the State cannot fund its education system in a manner that violates the constitutional guarantee—applies with equal force to operational funding. The constitutional right to a "free" education encompasses all aspects of the educational experience, not merely the physical structure in which it occurs.

### C. The Prosecution's Narrow Reading Is Unsupported

The Prosecution argues that "the constitutional mandate for 'free common schools' under Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution does not transform every local fee into a state constitutional violation." This argument, while superficially appealing, ignores the systemic nature of Joki's challenge. Joki does not challenge isolated local fee decisions; rather, he challenges the systemic inadequacy of State funding that forces local districts to impose fees. The causal connection between the State's funding mechanism and the alleged harm is precisely what distinguishes Joki's claim from a typical local fee dispute.

The district court erred in requiring Joki to allege facts "within the purview of this Court's holding in ISEEO V" before his claim could proceed. The constitutional guarantee of "free common schools" does not permit such arbitrary confinement. The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reject the Prosecution's attempt to read a limiting clause into the Idaho Constitution that simply does not exist.

---

## III. THE STATE DEFENDANTS ARE PROPER PARTIES UNDER A NON-DELEGABLE DUTY THEORY

### A. The Legislature's Ultimate Responsibility

The Prosecution contends that the State Defendants are improper parties because local school districts, as separate legal entities, impose the fees. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the duty imposed by Article IX, Section 1. As this Court held in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 138 Idaho 806, 69 P.3d 184 (2003) (ISEEO I), "the legislature bears the 'ultimate responsibility' for ensuring the education system complies with constitutional requirements." 138 Idaho at 818, 69 P.3d at 196.

### B. The Non-Delegable Nature of the Duty

The duty established by Article IX, Section 1 is non-delegable. The State cannot evades its constitutional responsibility by delegating the administration of schools to local districts and then claiming immunity when the State's own funding failures force those districts to impose unconstitutional fees. Joki's challenge is to the systemic inadequacy of State funding, which he alleges is the root cause of the fees. The causal connection between the State's funding mechanism and the alleged harm is sufficient to establish the State Defendants as proper parties.

The Prosecution argues that "CBECA—Idaho Code sections 6-2201–2216—expressly governs claims against state defendants for constitutional education challenges." However, this argument conflates the existence of a statutory scheme with its constitutionality. As discussed in Section IV below, CBECA cannot be applied to bar claims seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1.

### C. The Prosecution's Separation of Powers Argument Fails

The Prosecution's reliance on "the separation of state and local authority" ignores the State's ultimate constitutional obligation to ensure the system remains "free." The legislature's duty to "establish and maintain" a system does not evaporate upon delegation to local entities. The State remains constitutionally accountable for the system it creates and maintains through its funding mechanisms.

---

## IV. ISEEO IV BARS THE APPLICATION OF CBECA TO JOKI'S CLAIMS

### A. The Prosecution's Temporal Argument Is Logically Inconsistent

The Prosecution argues that ISEEO IV, which held the CBECA unconstitutional as applied to Article IX claims, is limited to the facilities funding context because it was decided in 2004, three years prior to ISEEO V in 2007. The Prosecution contends that "a later case on the merits of facilities funding can retroactively narrow the procedural holding of an earlier case regarding access to the courts."

This argument is legally flawed. It is logically inconsistent to argue that a later case on the merits can retroactively narrow an earlier procedural holding. ISEEO IV addressed the fundamental question of whether the legislature can use procedural hurdles (the CBECA) to block citizens from enforcing their constitutional rights under Article IX, Section 1. This question was answered in the negative. The specific subject matter of the underlying claim—whether facilities or operational fees—is irrelevant to the procedural question of access to the courts.

### B. The Right to Enforce Article IX, Section 1 Cannot Be Subject-Matter Dependent

The Prosecution's attempt to limit ISEEO IV to facilities claims undermines the supremacy of the Constitution. The right to enforce the mandate of Article IX, Section 1 cannot be dependent on the specific subject matter of the challenge. Whether the claim involves facilities or operational fees, the CBECA cannot be used to bar access to the courts to enforce the "free common schools" guarantee.

The Prosecution argues that "ISEEO IV did not invalidate CBECA broadly; it addressed only the specific procedural posture of that case." However, the Idaho Supreme Court's holding in ISEEO IV was clear: the CBECA's exhaustion requirements could not bar claims seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1. This holding was not limited to facilities claims, nor could it be, as such a limitation would render the constitutional guarantee unenforceable for certain categories of claims—a result that would be antithetical to constitutional supremacy.

### C. The District Court Erred in Applying CBECA

The district court held that "the CBECA governed all of Joki's claims, as brought against the State Defendants, and required dismissal." This holding directly contradicts the holding of ISEEO IV. The preliminary ruling correctly recognized that "the district court erred in holding that the CBECA governed Joki's claims and required their dismissal."

---

## V. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ADDITIONAL ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Defense's Position

The Prosecution accuses the Defense of "mischaracterizing controlling precedent." To the contrary, it is the Prosecution that mischaracterizes precedent by attempting to read limiting clauses into constitutional provisions and judicial holdings that do not exist.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on CBECA Is Misplaced

The Prosecution argues that "CBECA's statutory framework properly bars state defendant liability for local fee decisions." This argument assumes the constitutionality of CBECA as applied to Joki's claims—a proposition that ISEEO IV directly contradicts. The CBECA cannot be used to bar claims seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1, regardless of whether those claims concern facilities or operational fees.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court **AFFIRM** the preliminary ruling reversing the district court's dismissal of the State Defendants and **REMAND** the case for further proceedings consistent with the preliminary ruling.

The constitutional mandate for "free" schools is not restricted to the funding of facilities, and the State's duty to maintain such a system is non-delegable. Furthermore, ISEEO IV unequivocally holds that the CBECA cannot be applied to bar claims seeking to enforce Article IX, Section 1. The Defense's arguments are legally sound and supported by the text of the Idaho Constitution and controlling precedent.

The Prosecution's rebuttal fails to demonstrate that the district court's dismissal was proper. The preliminary ruling correctly identified the errors in the district court's analysis and properly reversed the dismissal. This Court should uphold the preliminary ruling and ensure that Idaho's citizens have access to the courts to enforce their constitutional right to a "free" education.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Prosecution argues that *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 143 Idaho 802, 153 P.3d 1086 (2007) (*ISEEO V*), is strictly limited to school facilities funding and therefore cannot support Joki’s claims regarding operational fees. While it is accurate that *ISEEO V* addressed the specific factual predicate of facilities deficiencies, the Prosecution conflates the specific factual context of that case with the broader scope of the constitutional provision at issue.

As the Defense argues, the underlying principle of *ISEEO V* is that the state must provide adequate funding to ensure the constitutional mandate is met. If the state’s funding scheme is insufficient to cover operational costs, thereby necessitating fees that render schools not “free,” this constitutes a violation of Article IX, Section 1 just as much as a failure to fund buildings. The district court erred by concluding that Joki failed to state a claim simply because his allegations did not mirror the specific factual record of *ISEEO V*. The constitutional right to a “free” education encompasses the entirety of the educational experience, not merely the physical structure in which it occurs.

**B. The State Defendants Are Proper Parties Under a Non-Delegable Duty Theory**
