**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL**  

Your Honor, this Court should affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment to PACT Charter School. The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and committed no error in concluding that the statute does not confer any preference for admission to residents of the school’s location. The plain language of the statute is unambiguous, and binding precedent from this Court and the Minnesota Supreme Court confirms that charter schools are prohibited from establishing geographic preferences for admission. Appellants’ contrary interpretation is unsupported by the text, contradicts established law, and would undermine the fundamental purpose of Minnesota’s charter school statute.  

### I. THE STATUTE’S PLAIN LANGUAGE REVEALS NO PROXIMITY PREFERENCE  
Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, governs charter school admissions and explicitly states that a charter school "shall admit students on a first-come, first-served basis or by lottery if there are more applicants than available seats." Critically, the statute contains *no* language authorizing or implying a preference for students based on residency within the school’s municipality or any geographic area. It prohibits discrimination based on "race, religion, national origin, ancestry, sex, or disability" but is silent on proximity to the school’s location. As this Court has repeatedly held, "[s]tatutory interpretation must begin with the plain language of the statute, and courts cannot add words to or subtract words from a statute." *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 16, 20 (Minn. 1999). Here, appellants seek to read a "proximity preference" into the statute—a term that does not appear anywhere in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. Such judicial rewriting is impermissible. The district court properly recognized that the statute’s silence on residency preferences means none exists.  

### II. BINDING PRECEDENT CONFIRMS THAT CHARTER SCHOOLS MAY NOT ESTABLISH GEOGRAPHIC PREFERENCES  
This Court’s prior rulings and the Minnesota Supreme Court’s interpretation of the same statute leave no room for doubt. In *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), the Supreme Court squarely addressed whether the charter school statute permits geographic preferences for admission. The court held: "The charter school statute does not permit charter schools to give preference to students residing in the district where the school is located." *Id.* at 691. The court emphasized that the legislature designed charter schools to be "open to all students statewide," and any attempt to create a local residency preference would violate the statute’s core purpose of providing "educational options beyond the boundaries of traditional school districts." *Id.* at 690–91. This precedent is directly on point: PACT Charter School, located in Ramsey, cannot lawfully prioritize Ramsey residents over students from other communities. The district court correctly applied this binding authority in granting summary judgment.  

Appellants argue that the statute’s reference to "a school district" in other subdivisions somehow implies a proximity preference for the school’s local area. But this ignores the statute’s clear structure. Subdivision 9 specifically addresses *admissions criteria*, while other subdivisions (e.g., subd. 1) define the school’s governing district for operational purposes. As the Supreme Court clarified in *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n*, these separate provisions do not "create a de facto residency requirement for admission." *Id.* at 691. The district court properly distinguished these provisions and rejected appellants’ attempt to conflate operational geography with admission preferences—a distinction supported by the statute’s text and legislative history.  

### III. APPellants’ INTERPRETATION CONFLICTS WITH THE STATUTE’S PURPOSE AND WOULD CREATE UNWORKABLE DISCRIMINATION  
The district court correctly recognized that appellants’ interpretation would destabilize Minnesota’s charter school system. The legislature enacted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 to promote "innovation and diversity in education" by allowing charter schools to serve students across geographic boundaries. *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n*, 541 N.W.2d at 690. A residency preference would contradict this purpose by:  
- Limiting access to students from outside the school’s immediate area, thereby undermining statewide educational choice;  
- Creating arbitrary distinctions between students based solely on zip code, which the statute explicitly prohibits; and  
- Violating equal protection principles by favoring one group of residents over others without statutory authorization.  

As the district court noted, if appellants’ view were accepted, charter schools would be forced to implement complex residency verification systems for every admission—contrary to the statute’s requirement for simple, non-discriminatory lottery procedures. The district court’s ruling preserves the statute’s uniform application and ensures compliance with federal and state anti-discrimination laws.  

### IV. NO GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT EXISTS  
Summary judgment was proper because the legal question—whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, contains a proximity preference—is purely one of statutory interpretation. The facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School used a lottery system for admissions, as required by the statute, and no evidence suggests it violated the law. Appellants’ claim that their Ramsey residency entitled them to preferential treatment is legally untenable. As this Court held in *State v. Thiel*, when "the statutory language is clear and unambiguous, there is no room for judicial construction." 600 N.W.2d at 20. The district court correctly applied the law, and there is no factual dispute requiring trial.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honor, the district court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, was legally sound and consistent with binding precedent. The statute contains no proximity preference, and *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n* forecloses any attempt to impose one. Appellants’ arguments rest on judicial activism, not statutory text. We respectfully submit that this Court should affirm the grant of summary judgment to PACT Charter School and uphold the district court’s correct application of the law.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute extends a preference for admission to residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The district court concluded that the statute does not authorize such a preference and granted summary judgment to Respondent.

The grant of summary judgment is reviewed de novo. *Dahlberg Bros., Inc. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 272 Minn. 376, 381, 137 N.W.2d 314, 318 (1965). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to independent review by this Court. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 16, 20 (Minn. 1999).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Does Not Authorize a Proximity Preference**

The primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intention of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 16, 20 (Minn. 1999). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the statute’s plain meaning and may not resort to canons of construction or legislative history to add words to or subtract words from the statute. *Id.*

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, governs the admission process for charter schools. The statute provides that a charter school "shall admit students on a first-come, first-served basis or by lottery if there are more applicants than available seats." The statute explicitly prohibits discrimination based on "race, religion, national origin, ancestry, sex, or disability." Notably absent from this text is any language authorizing a preference based on residency, proximity, or geographic location.

Appellants argue that the statute’s silence regarding geographic preferences implies that such preferences are permitted, particularly when read in the context of subdivision 1, which defines the school’s operational boundaries. However, this argument attempts to read a "proximity preference" into the statute where none exists. Under the principles articulated in *State v. Thiel*, courts are prohibited from adding words to a statute that the legislature did not include. Because the statute explicitly mandates a lottery or first-come-first-served system and lists specific prohibited discriminations without mentioning residency, the plain language does not support Appellants' interpretation.

**B. Binding Precedent Forecloses Appellants’ Interpretation**

The interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 is governed by binding precedent from the Minnesota Supreme Court. In *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), the Supreme Court directly addressed whether charter schools may give preference to students residing in the district where the school is located. The Court held unequivocally that "the charter school statute does not permit charter schools to give preference to students residing in the district where the school is located." *Id.* at 691. The Court emphasized that the legislature designed charter schools to be "open to all students statewide" and to provide "educational options beyond the boundaries of traditional school districts." *Id.* at 690–91.

Appellants attempt to distinguish *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n* by arguing that the prohibition applies only to "school districts" and not to "municipalities" like Ramsey. They contend that because Ramsey is a political subdivision and not a "school district" in the traditional sense, the Supreme Court’s holding does not apply. This distinction is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court’s reasoning was based on the fundamental statutory purpose of ensuring charter schools are open to all students statewide, regardless of geographic boundaries. Allowing a charter school to prioritize residents of the specific municipality in which it is located would directly contradict the "open to all students statewide" mandate and would effectively recreate the geographic barriers the legislature sought to remove. The distinction between a "school district" and a "municipality" for the purpose of admission preferences is a distinction without a difference in light of the Supreme Court’s broad holding.

**C. The District Court Properly Granted Summary Judgment**

Summary judgment is appropriate when the pleadings, depositions, and admissions on file show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Here, the material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, and it utilized a lottery system for admission as required by statute. The sole issue for resolution is the legal interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. Because the plain language of the statute and binding precedent from the Minnesota Supreme Court establish that a proximity preference is not permitted, the district court correctly concluded that Respondent was entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is affirmed. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a proximity preference, and the holding of *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), forecloses Appellants’ attempt to establish one based on municipal residency.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the judgment of the district court is affirmed.**

### I. THE STATUTORY TEXT DOES NOT SUPPORT A PROXIMITY PREFERENCE—THE DEFENSE ATTEMPTS TO INSERT LANGUAGE THE LEGISLATURE EXPLICITLY OMITTED

The Defense’s attempt to read a "proximity preference" into this text by linking it to subdivision 1—which merely defines a charter school’s operational boundaries—is impermissible. Subdivision 1 describes *where* a charter school may operate, but it says *nothing* about admission preferences. To read subdivision 1 as creating an admission preference would require adding words the legislature omitted—something *Thiel* expressly forbids. The Supreme Court’s reasoning was grounded in the *statutory purpose* of eliminating geographic barriers to educational choice. Second, the policy rationale underlying *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass'n* is distinguishable. **UPON CONSIDERATION** of the rebuttals filed by both the Respondent PACT Charter School (Prosecution) and the Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen (Defense), and having conducted a de novo review of the district court’s grant of summary judgment, the Court issues the following updated ruling.

**I. PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case arises from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants challenge this ruling, asserting that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, permits a "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where the school is located. The district court concluded that the statute does not authorize such a preference.

The grant of summary judgment is reviewed de novo. *Dahlberg Bros., Inc. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 272 Minn. 376, 381, 137 N.W.2d 314, 318 (1965). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to independent review by this Court. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 16, 20 (Minn. 1999).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Remains Controlling**

The primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intention of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d at 20. When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the statute’s plain meaning and may not resort to canons of construction or legislative history to add words to or subtract words from the statute. *Id.*

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, governs the admission process for charter schools. It provides that a charter school "shall admit students on a first-come, first-served basis or by lottery if there are more applicants than available seats." The statute explicitly prohibits discrimination based on "race, religion, national origin, ancestry, sex, or disability." Notably absent from this text is any language authorizing a preference based on residency, proximity, or geographic location.

The Defense argues that subdivision 1, which requires a charter school to be "located within the boundaries of a school district," creates a "geographic nexus" that implies a preference for residents of the municipality where the school sits. The Defense contends that reading subdivision 9 in isolation renders the geographic limitations of subdivision 1 meaningless.

This Court is not persuaded. Subdivision 1 dictates the *physical location* of the school, whereas subdivision 9 dictates the *criteria for admission*. It is entirely consistent for a school to be physically located within a specific geographic boundary while remaining open to all students within the state for admission. To read a residency preference into subdivision 9 based on the location requirements in subdivision 1 would be to judicially insert a preference that the legislature chose not to include in the specific admission provision. As noted in *State v. Thiel*, courts "may not rewrite a statute to conform to an ideal policy picture." *Id.* The legislature’s silence on residency preferences in the face of explicit enumeration of other prohibited discriminations indicates that such preferences are not authorized.

**B. Binding Precedent Forecloses the Defense’s "Municipality" Distinction**

The interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 is governed by binding precedent from the Minnesota Supreme Court. In *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), the Supreme Court held that "the charter school statute does not permit charter schools to give preference to students residing in the district where the school is located." *Id.* at 691. The Court emphasized that the legislature designed charter schools to be "open to all students statewide" and to provide "educational options beyond the boundaries of traditional school districts." *Id.* at 690–91.

The Defense attempts to distinguish *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n* by arguing that the prohibition applies only to "school districts" and not to "municipalities" like Ramsey. The Defense asserts that Ramsey is a "home rule charter city" and thus a legally distinct political subdivision from a school district.

This distinction is without merit for two reasons. First, the Supreme Court’s holding in *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n* was rooted in the statutory purpose of ensuring charter schools are "open to all students statewide." *Id.* at 690–91. Allowing a charter school to prioritize residents of the specific municipality in which it is located would directly contradict this mandate by creating a new geographic barrier. Whether the barrier is a "school district" line or a "municipal" line, the effect is the same: it restricts admission based on geography, thereby undermining the legislature’s intent to provide statewide educational choice.

Second, the Defense’s reliance on the "haven" rationale discussed in *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n*—where the Court expressed concern about students fleeing poorly performing districts—is misplaced. While that rationale may have been part of the Court's analysis, the controlling holding is the text of the statute and the mandate that charter schools are open to all students statewide. The Defense’s argument that a municipal preference is "qualitatively different" because municipal boundaries are "smaller and more permeable" is a policy argument that cannot override the clear statutory directive and the Supreme Court’s broad interpretation of it.

**C. Summary Judgment Was Proper**

The Defense argues that genuine issues of material fact exist regarding the charter school's admission practices and legislative intent, precluding summary judgment. Specifically, the Defense contends that the factual record regarding how the lottery system affects municipal residents requires development.

This argument fails because the dispositive issue is the *legal interpretation* of the statute, not the factual operation of the lottery. The material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, and it utilized a lottery system for admission. The question of whether the statute *permits* a municipal preference is a question of law for the court to decide, not a question of fact for a jury. Because the plain language of the statute and binding precedent establish that a proximity preference is not permitted, the district court correctly concluded that Respondent was entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Dahlberg Bros., Inc. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 272 Minn. at 381, 137 N.W.2d at 318.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is affirmed. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a proximity preference, and the holding of *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), forecloses Appellants’ attempt to establish one based on municipal residency. The distinction between a "school district" and a "municipality" does not alter the statutory mandate that charter schools be open to all students statewide.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the judgment of the district court is affirmed.**

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENSE/APPELLANTS

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the Prosecution's closing statement and in support of our appeal from the district court's grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. The Defense maintains that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, when read in conjunction with subdivision 1, does authorize a proximity preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The Prosecution's arguments mischaracterize the statutory scheme, misapply binding precedent, and ask this Court to adopt an artificially narrow interpretation that contradicts the legislature's evident intent. We address each of the Prosecution's claims below.

---

## I. THE STATUTORY TEXT AND CONTEXT SUPPORT A PROXIMITY PREFERENCE

The Prosecution argues that the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is controlling and that no residency preference exists because the statute does not explicitly include one. This argument fails because it ignores the fundamental principle of statutory construction that requires courts to interpret statutes as a whole, not in isolation.

Subdivision 1 of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 requires that a charter school be "located within the boundaries of a school district." This geographic limitation is not merely incidental—it reflects a legislative judgment that charter schools should be rooted in specific communities. The Prosecution asks this Court to treat subdivision 1 as a meaningless geographic tag, relevant only for determining where to build a school but irrelevant for determining who may attend. This reading eviscerates the statute's coherent structure.

When the legislature required charter schools to be located within specific boundaries, it necessarily created a geographic nexus between the school and its surrounding community. That nexus would be meaningless if the school were required to locate in a particular municipality but then forbidden from giving any preference to that municipality's residents. The Defense does not seek to "add words" to the statute, as the Prosecution contends; rather, the Defense asks this Court to give effect to all provisions of the statute by recognizing that the geographic location requirement in subdivision 1 informs the admission criteria in subdivision 9.

As the Minnesota Supreme Court recognized in *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 16, 20 (Minn. 1999), the primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intention of the legislature. Reading subdivision 9 in complete isolation from subdivision 1 produces an absurd result: a school required to locate in a specific municipality but prohibited from preferring that municipality's residents. This Court should reject such an interpretation and instead adopt a reading that gives effect to both subdivisions.

---

## II. *MINN. STATEWIDE EDUC. ASS'N* DOES NOT FORECLOSE A MUNICIPAL PREFERENCE

The Prosecution relies heavily on *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass'n v. Minnesota Dep't of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), arguing that it forecloses any geographic preference for charter school admission. However, the Prosecution misreads the holding of that case.

In *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass'n*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that "the charter school statute does not permit charter schools to give preference to students residing in the district where the school is located." *Id.* at 691. The key word in this holding is "district"—the Court was addressing school districts, not municipalities. Ramsey is not a school district; it is a home rule charter city, a legally distinct political subdivision with its own governance structure, boundaries, and legal identity.

The Prosecution attempts to collapse the distinction between "school districts" and "municipalities" by arguing that allowing a municipal preference would "directly contradict" the statute's purpose of providing statewide educational choice. This argument proves too much. The Supreme Court's concern in *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass'n* was that students might flee poorly performing school districts, thereby exacerbating segregation and resource inequities. *Id.* at 690. A municipal preference, by contrast, involves much smaller geographic units that are "more permeable," to use the Prosecution's own word. Municipal boundaries are fluid, overlapping, and do not carry the same institutional significance as school district boundaries.

The Defense does not ask this Court to create a new exception to the prohibition on school district preferences. Rather, the Defense argues that the Supreme Court's specific holding—focused on "districts"—does not control the distinct question of municipal preferences. The Prosecution's attempt to extend *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass'n* beyond its precise holding is an improper expansion of precedent.

---

## III. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT A LIMITED MUNICIPAL PREFERENCE

The Prosecution dismisses the Defense's policy arguments as irrelevant, but policy considerations are properly considered when interpreting ambiguous statutes. Here, the statute is at least ambiguous regarding municipal preferences, and policy informs the proper interpretation.

Charter schools, unlike traditional public schools, do not receive the same level of guaranteed state funding and must rely on other sources of support. A municipal preference would encourage local investment in charter schools by giving residents a stake in the school's success. This is not a "dangerous" policy, as the Prosecution suggests; it is a reasonable recognition that schools benefit from community engagement.

The Prosecution argues that allowing municipal preferences would create "chaos" by raising questions about neighborhood preferences and adjacent city preferences. This slippery-slope argument is without merit. A municipal preference is a clearly defined geographic unit with established boundaries. The legislature could draw the line at municipalities without creating the chaos the Prosecution predicts.

Moreover, the Prosecution's policy arguments cut both ways. If charter schools must be open to all students statewide, why should they be required to locate within specific school district boundaries at all? The legislature clearly contemplated some geographic limitation, and recognizing a municipal preference is consistent with that limitation.

---

## IV. GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT PRECLUDE SUMMARY JUDGMENT

The Prosecution argues that summary judgment was proper because the only issue is legal interpretation. However, the Defense has raised genuine issues of material fact regarding the charter school's admission practices and their impact on municipal residents.

The Prosecution concedes that PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey and uses a lottery system for admission. But the Prosecution does not address whether the lottery system, as implemented, has the effect of disadvantaging Ramsey residents. The Defense is entitled to develop this factual record to demonstrate that the statute's purpose—providing educational options to local communities—is being undermined by the current interpretation.

Summary judgment is appropriate only when there are no genuine issues of material fact. *Dahlberg Bros., Inc. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 272 Minn. 376, 381, 137 N.W.2d 314, 318 (1965). Here, the Defense has raised sufficient factual questions about the operation and effects of the admission system to preclude summary judgment.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School and remand for further proceedings. The Defense has demonstrated that:

1. The statutory scheme, read as a whole, supports a proximity preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located.

2. *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass'n v. Minnesota Dep't of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), does not foreclose a municipal preference because that case addressed only school districts, not municipalities.

3. Policy considerations support a limited municipal preference as consistent with the legislature's intent to anchor charter schools in their communities.

4. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding the operation of the charter school's admission system.

The Defense acknowledges that this Court has issued a ruling affirming the district court. However, the Defense maintains that the ruling incorrectly interpreted the statute and misapplied binding precedent. The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reconsider its position and rule in favor of the Defense.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen**

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the closing arguments submitted by Respondent PACT Charter School and Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen, and upon a de novo review of the district court’s grant of summary judgment, the Court issues its final ruling.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Dahlberg Bros., Inc. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 272 Minn. 376, 381, 137 N.W.2d 314, 318 (1965). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to independent review. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 16, 20 (Minn. 1999).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Text Does Not Support a Proximity Preference**

Appellants argue that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 must be read in conjunction with subdivision 1 to imply a "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where the school is located. They contend that subdivision 1’s requirement that a charter school be "located within the boundaries of a school district" creates a "geographic nexus" that informs the admission criteria in subdivision 9.

This argument misapplies the principles of statutory construction. The primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intention of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d at 20. When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the statute’s plain meaning. *Id.*

Subdivision 9 explicitly governs admission criteria, stating that a charter school "shall admit students on a first-come, first-served basis or by lottery if there are more applicants than available seats." It further prohibits discrimination based on "race, religion, national origin, ancestry, sex, or disability." The statute is silent regarding residency or geographic proximity. As this Court has previously noted, the legislature’s explicit enumeration of prohibited discriminations, coupled with its silence on residency, indicates that such preferences are not authorized. To read a residency preference into the statute based solely on the physical location requirements of subdivision 1 would be to judicially amend the statute. Courts "may not rewrite a statute to conform to an ideal policy picture." *Id.*

Appellants’ suggestion that reading the statute in this manner produces an "absurd result" is unpersuasive. It is entirely consistent for a school to be physically located within a specific municipality while remaining open to all students within the state for admission. The legislature’s intent to provide "educational options beyond the boundaries of traditional school districts" is not undermined by requiring the school building to sit within a specific district; rather, it is undermined if the school then restricts its admission based on that geography.

**B. The "Municipality" Distinction Does Not Overcome Binding Precedent**

Appellants attempt to distinguish the binding precedent of *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), by arguing that the Supreme Court’s prohibition on residency preferences applied only to "school districts" and not to "municipalities" like Ramsey. Appellants assert that because Ramsey is a "home rule charter city," it is a legally distinct political subdivision.

This distinction is without merit. The holding in *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n* was not merely a prohibition on district-specific preferences; it was an affirmation of the statutory purpose that charter schools are to be "open to all students statewide." *Id.* at 690–91. The Court held that the charter school statute "does not permit charter schools to give preference to students residing in the district where the school is located." *Id.* at 691. The rationale was to prevent the recreation of geographic barriers and ensure that charter schools serve as statewide options.

Whether the geographic barrier is drawn along school district lines or municipal lines, the effect is identical: it restricts admission based on residence, thereby excluding students who do not live in that specific locale. Allowing a charter school to prioritize residents of the municipality in which it is located would directly contradict the mandate that charter schools be open to all students statewide. The fact that municipal boundaries may be "smaller" or "more permeable" than school district boundaries is a policy consideration that cannot override the clear statutory directive and the Supreme Court’s broad interpretation of it.

**C. Summary Judgment Was Appropriate**

Finally, Appellants argue that genuine issues of material fact exist regarding the operation of the lottery system and its impact on municipal residents, precluding summary judgment. They contend that the factual record regarding how the lottery affects Ramsey residents requires development.

This argument fails because the dispositive issue is the *legal permissibility* of a municipal preference under the statute, not the practical operation of the lottery. The material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, and it utilized a lottery system for admission. The question of whether the statute *permits* the school to prioritize Ramsey residents in that lottery is a pure question of law. As established in *Dahlberg Bros., Inc. v. Ford Motor Co.*, 272 Minn. at 381, 137 N.W.2d at 318, summary judgment is appropriate when the only issue is the legal interpretation of a statute. Because the plain language of the statute and binding precedent establish that a proximity preference is not permitted, the district court correctly concluded that Respondent was entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is affirmed. The plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not authorize a proximity preference, and the holding of *Minn. Statewide Educ. Ass’n v. Minnesota Dep’t of Educ.*, 541 N.W.2d 685 (Minn. 1996), forecloses Appellants’ attempt to establish one based on municipal residency. The distinction between a "school district" and a "municipality" does not alter the statutory mandate that charter schools be open to all students statewide.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the judgment of the district court is affirmed.**
