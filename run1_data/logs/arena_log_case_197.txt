COURT ARENA LOG - Case 197
FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic (""Argentina"") and YPF S.A. (""YPF"") (together, ""defendants"") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering (""IPO"") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the ""shares"").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts (""ADRs"") listed on the New York Stock Exchange (""NYSE""). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission (""SEC"") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer ""in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed."" App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that ""[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends."" App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, ""[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote."" App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that ""[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws."" App. 23. The prospectus further stated that ""any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired."" Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. (""Repsol"") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, ""Petersen"") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the ""governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights."" Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A ""classic example"" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a ""taking"" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) (""It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights.""). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) (""States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ....""); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., ""Indirect Expropriation"" and the ""Right to Regulate"" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the ""General Expropriation Law."" App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress ""shall particularly refer to specific property"" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that ""[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved."" App. 185-86. The law further declares that ""[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...."" App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive ""the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation,"" such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that ""[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator."" App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an ""Intervenor"" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as ""fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law."" App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as ""unfair"" and a ""bear trap."" Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the ""YPF Expropriation Law""). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving ""self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply,"" App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating ""public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons,"" App. 166.
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to ""exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation"" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. **OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, this case is not about sovereign immunity or political acts. It is about Argentina and YPF S.A. breaking binding commercial promises made to investors in a transparent, market-driven transaction. The defendants’ motions to dismiss fail because Petersen plausibly alleged that Argentina breached its contractual obligations under YPF’s bylaws—obligations that arose from a purely commercial context—and that YPF, as a private corporation, failed in its duty to enforce those same obligations. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) does not shield Argentina here, and the act of state doctrine is inapplicable. The district court correctly denied the motions to dismiss, and we urge this Court to affirm.  

### I. Argentina’s Breach of the Tender Offer Obligation Was Commercial in Nature, Triggering the FSIA’s Commercial Activity Exception  
The FSIA’s commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies when a foreign sovereign’s conduct is “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 619 (1992). Here, Argentina’s breach of the tender offer requirement was not a sovereign act—it was a commercial one.  

When Argentina privatized YPF in 1993, it did so through a public offering of shares listed on the New York Stock Exchange. To attract global investors, Argentina and YPF filed a prospectus with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission explicitly stating that “under YPF’s By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of YPF’s capital stock, the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws.” This was not a political promise; it was a commercial representation to investors in U.S. capital markets. The bylaws themselves—amended as part of the IPO—were contractual terms governing shareholder rights in a publicly traded corporation. They were designed to protect investors in a commercial transaction, not to assert sovereign authority.  

When Argentina expropriated Repsol’s 51% stake in 2012 under Law 26,741, it did so without making the tender offer required by the bylaws. Section 28(A) of the bylaws explicitly mandated a tender offer for any acquisition by Argentina exceeding 49% of shares. Yet Argentina’s own officials publicly dismissed the requirement as a “bear trap” and a “fool’s” obligation. Crucially, the expropriation law did not override the bylaws—it said nothing about tender offers or subsequent market transactions. Instead, Argentina unilaterally seized control of YPF’s shares and immediately exercised voting rights in contravention of Section 7(h), which flatly prohibits voting rights for shares acquired in breach of the tender offer rules.  

This conduct is squarely commercial. As the Supreme Court held in *Weltover*, when a foreign state “acts as a private investor” in a commercial enterprise, it is not entitled to sovereign immunity. 504 U.S. at 619. Argentina did not expropriate YPF to build a highway or nationalize a public utility; it acquired shares in a publicly traded corporation through a process structured as a commercial transaction. Its obligation to make a tender offer arose from a contract it voluntarily entered into with investors in U.S. markets. The FSIA exception applies precisely because Argentina’s actions were “commercial” in nature—“not because of the purpose behind the act, but because of the nature of the act itself.” *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993).  

### II. The Breach Caused Direct Effects in the United States, Satisfying the FSIA’s Jurisdictional Prong  
The FSIA exception also requires that the claim be “based upon” an act “causing a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Petersen’s allegations satisfy this requirement.  

Argentina’s breach directly harmed U.S. investors. YPF’s shares were listed on the NYSE as American Depository Receipts (“ADRs”), held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. When Argentina refused to make the tender offer, it canceled scheduled dividend payments to U.S. shareholders—including Petersen, which owned 25% of YPF’s ADRs. Petersen relied on those dividends to meet loan obligations secured by its NYSE-listed ADRs. When dividends ceased, Petersen defaulted, and its lenders foreclosed on the collateral—ADRs held in New York. The district court correctly found that this chain of events had a “direct effect in the United States” because it “enabled Argentina to cancel planned dividend payments, some of which would have been made to investors based in the United States,” and “precipitated Petersen’s default on its loan obligations and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen’s ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City.”  

This is not a case where foreign sovereign action in a foreign country had only indirect effects on U.S. markets. As the Second Circuit has held, when a foreign state’s breach of contract causes harm to U.S.-based investors through U.S. financial instruments, the “direct effect” requirement is met. *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 597 (2d Cir. 2006). Here, the expropriation triggered a cascade of consequences in New York: canceled dividends to U.S. investors, defaulted loans secured by NYSE-listed ADRs, and the seizure of collateral by a U.S. bank. These effects are the very reason Petersen’s claims fall squarely within the FSIA exception.  

### III. YPF’s Role as a Private Corporation Does Not Shield It from Liability  
YPF is not entitled to sovereign immunity because it acted as a commercial entity—not a sovereign—when it failed to enforce the bylaws. YPF is a publicly traded company whose shares are listed on the NYSE. Its bylaws were adopted as part of a commercial transaction to attract investors. When YPF permitted Argentina to exercise voting rights over shares acquired in breach of the tender offer requirement, it violated its own contractual obligations. Section 7(h) explicitly states that shares acquired without a tender offer “shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends.” Yet YPF allowed Argentina to vote those shares at a shareholder meeting and canceled dividend payments—actions that directly harmed Petersen.  

As the Supreme Court has made clear, a state-owned enterprise is not immune when it engages in commercial activity. *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 626 (1983). YPF’s failure to enforce its bylaws was a commercial act—part of its role as a private corporation managing shareholder relations. Its obligations under the bylaws were not sovereign duties but contractual promises made to investors in U.S. markets.  

### IV. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply  
The act of state doctrine bars courts from questioning the validity of a foreign sovereign’s public acts within its own territory. But this doctrine does not apply when the claim involves a commercial contract, not a sovereign act. *First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 621–22. Petersen does not challenge the validity of Argentina’s expropriation under Argentine law; it alleges that Argentina and YPF breached private contractual obligations. The bylaws were not part of Argentina’s sovereign authority—they were commercial terms adopted to secure investor confidence in a U.S.-listed IPO.  

As the Second Circuit held in *Garb*, the act of state doctrine “does not bar claims for breach of contract when the sovereign acts in a commercial capacity.” 440 F.3d at 597. Here, Argentina and YPF were not asserting sovereign power; they were acting as private parties to a commercial agreement. The district court properly recognized that this case is about enforcing contractual terms—not second-guessing Argentina’s sovereign decisions.  

### Conclusion  
Your Honor, this case is straightforward. Argentina and YPF made clear, commercial promises to investors in U.S. markets: if Argentina acquired control of YPF, it would make a tender offer, and shares acquired without such an offer would carry no voting rights. They broke those promises. The harm to U.S. investors—canceled dividends, defaulted loans, and seized collateral—occurred squarely in New York. The FSIA exception applies because the breach was commercial, and the act of state doctrine is irrelevant because this is not a challenge to sovereign power but to private contractual obligations. The district court rightly denied the motions to dismiss. We ask this Court to affirm.  

Thank you.

Argentina's decision to expropriate YPF shares was an act of governmental authority—implementing a public policy declared by the Argentine Congress—not a private commercial transaction.

Furthermore, the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims. The doctrine provides that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall be deemed valid" by United States courts. *Underwood v. Burger*, 176 F.3d 74, 78 (2d Cir. 1999). Petersen asks this Court to declare that Argentina's expropriation was improper because it did not comply with private corporate bylaws—a breathtaking request that would have this Court sit in judgment of Argentina's sovereign determination of what constitutes "public use" under its own Constitution.

We respectfully ask this Court to reverse the district court's denial of Argentina's motion to dismiss under the FSIA and to dismiss the claims against Argentina.

## I. ARGENTINA IS ENTITLED TO SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY UNDER THE FSIA

### A. The Standard for Foreign Sovereign Immunity

The FSIA provides that "a foreign state shall be immune from the jurisdiction of the courts of the United States" unless one of the statutory exceptions applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The exceptions are narrowly construed, and the burden falls on the plaintiff to establish that an exception applies. *See Virtual Countries, Inc. v. Republic of South Africa*, 302 F.3d 149, 154 (2d Cir. 2002).

The commercial activity exception, invoked by Petersen, provides that a foreign state is not entitled to immunity in any case "in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). This exception contains three separate clauses, and the plaintiff must satisfy at least one. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 611. The first clause requires that the commercial activity have a "direct effect in the United States." *Id.* at 618.

### B. Argentina's Expropriation Was a Sovereign Act, Not a Commercial Activity

The critical question under the FSIA is whether the foreign state acted "as a private player in the marketplace" or "as a sovereign." *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). This distinction does not depend on the subjective intent of the foreign state but on the "nature of the act." *Id.*

Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares was a quintessential sovereign act. Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution grants the state the power of expropriation for public use upon payment of just compensation. This power is among the most fundamental attributes of sovereignty. The Argentine Congress declared, through Law 26,741, that the expropriation served the public interest in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply." This was a legislative determination of public use—exactly the sort of sovereign judgment that the FSIA was designed to shield from foreign judicial scrutiny.

The fact that the property Argentina expropriated happened to be shares in a publicly traded company does not transform the act into a commercial one. The Supreme Court has recognized that "the distinction between sovereign and commercial activity turns not on the merely incidental involvement of a foreign state in an activity that might otherwise be conducted by private parties, but rather on the fundamental character of the activity." *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360. Argentina did not purchase YPF shares in the open market as a private investor might. It exercised its eminent domain power to take property for a public purpose—a sovereign function if ever there was one.

The district court erroneously focused on the bylaws and the tender offer provisions as establishing a "commercial" context for Argentina's actions. This analysis gets things backwards. The bylaws were subordinate to Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation. No private contract can bind a sovereign state from exercising its constitutional powers of eminent domain. *See West v. Multibanco Comermex, S.A.*, 807 F.2d 820, 825 (9th Cir. 1987) ("A sovereign state is not bound by contracts it has not entered into, and a sovereign's constitutional or statutory powers cannot be contracted away.").

### C. The "Direct Effect" Requirement Is Not Satisfied

Even assuming arguendo that Argentina's expropriation could be characterized as "commercial," Petersen's claims are not "based upon" commercial activity having a direct effect in the United States. The plaintiff conflates the effects of Argentina's sovereign expropriation with the separate question of whether those effects satisfy the FSIA's nexus requirement.

Petersen's theory rests on the following chain of causation: Argentina expropriated YPF shares → Argentina cancelled dividends → Petersen defaulted on loans → lenders foreclosed on ADRs in New York. But this chain begins with a sovereign act—expropriation—and the subsequent effects, however unfortunate for Petersen, do not retroactively transform the expropriation into a commercial activity.

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the FSIA's "direct effect" requirement demands a close causal connection between the commercial activity and the claim. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. In *Weltover*, the Court found direct effects where Argentina's failure to make timely payments on bonds "directly affected" United States bondholders who had purchased the bonds in New York. *Id.* at 619. But Argentina's bonds in *Weltover* were "commercial paper" issued in the United States marketplace—a fundamentally different instrument from sovereign expropriation of property.

Here, the expropriation itself occurred in Argentina. The shares that Argentina took were owned by a Spanish company (Repsol). The declaration of public use and the expropriation law were enacted by the Argentine Congress. These are sovereign acts taking place entirely within Argentine territory. The subsequent effects on U.S. investors—cancelled dividends, defaulted loans—are too attenuated to satisfy the "direct effect" requirement. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 594 (2d Cir. 2006) (finding no jurisdiction where the "center of gravity" of the alleged conduct was in Poland, not the United States).

The district court erred in finding that the "center of gravity" of this case was in the United States because YPF's shares were listed on the NYSE and ADRs were held in New York. This analysis conflates the location of the property with the nature of the act. Argentina's expropriation was an act of sovereign authority directed from Argentina, not a commercial transaction conducted in U.S. markets. The FSIA does not permit plaintiffs to convert sovereign acts into commercial ones merely by pointing to the location of the affected property.

### D. YPF Is Entitled to Immunity for Its Sovereign Actions

YPF's actions in this case were taken at the direction of the Argentine government and in furtherance of the sovereign expropriation. YPF did not act as a private corporation when it cancelled dividend payments or permitted Argentina to exercise voting rights. Rather, YPF acted as an instrument of the Argentine state, implementing the Intervenor's directives and the Expropriation Law.

Under the FSIA, a state-owned enterprise is entitled to immunity when it acts as an instrumentality of the sovereign. *See Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 768 (2019). YPF's actions in connection with the expropriation were governmental, not commercial, and are shielded by the FSIA.

## II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS PETERSEN'S CLAIMS

### A. The Doctrine's Application

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall be deemed valid" by United States courts. *Underwood v. Burger*, 176 F.3d at 78. The doctrine reflects "the strong sense of the judicial branch that its engagement in the task of passing on the validity of foreign sovereign acts would hinder the conduct of foreign affairs." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964).

Petersen's claims require this Court to pass judgment on Argentina's sovereign determination that the expropriation of YPF served the public interest. Specifically, Petersen argues that Argentina's failure to conduct a tender offer violated the bylaws. But the bylaws cannot override the Argentine Congress's determination that the expropriation served "public use" under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution. To adjudicate Petersen's claims, this Court would necessarily have to evaluate the validity of Argentina's expropriation—exactly what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

### B. The Commercial Activity Exception to the Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply

Petersen invokes the exception to the act of state doctrine for "commercial activity" as recognized in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino* and its progeny. But this exception is narrow and does not apply here.

The commercial activity exception applies when the foreign state acts "as a private party would in a commercial transaction." *See Mannington Mills, Inc. v. Congoleum Corp.*, 595 F.2d 1287, 1294 (3d Cir. 1979). Argentina did not act as a private party when it enacted the YPF Expropriation Law. The law was a legislative act declaring public use and authorizing expropriation—textbook exercises of sovereign power. The fact that the law resulted in Argentina acquiring ownership of a commercial enterprise does not make the act itself commercial.

In *Sabbatino*, the Supreme Court refused to adjudicate the validity of Cuba's expropriation of property from American citizens, despite the commercial nature of the property taken. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428-29. The same result is required here. Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares was a sovereign act, and the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's attempt to challenge it through the backdoor of contract claims.

### C. The Bylaws Cannot Override Argentina's Sovereign Powers

The district court erred in suggesting that the bylaws could somehow constrain Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation. This suggestion is contrary to fundamental principles of sovereign immunity and international law.

Argentina adopted the bylaws in 1993 as part of the privatization of YPF. But no privatization agreement can bind a sovereign state from exercising its constitutional powers of eminent domain. The Argentine Constitution grants the state the power of expropriation; this power cannot be contracted away by private bylaws. *See Viscount v. Government of Guam*, 761 F.2d 458, 462 (9th Cir. 1985) ("A sovereign is not bound by contracts it has not entered into.").

Petersen's theory would allow private corporate bylaws to dictate the terms on which a sovereign state may exercise its inherent powers—a result that would profoundly interfere with the sovereignty of foreign states and the conduct of foreign affairs. The FSIA and the act of state doctrine were designed precisely to prevent such interference.

---

## III. RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prospectus Representations Were Not Binding on Argentina

Petersen argues that Argentina's inclusion of the tender offer requirement in the prospectus filed with the SEC creates a binding contractual obligation. This argument fails.

The prospectus was a disclosure document required by U.S. securities law. It summarized the bylaws but did not create independent contractual obligations. The bylaws themselves are the operative document, and they cannot be interpreted to constrain Argentina's sovereign powers.

Moreover, the prospectus expressly stated that the tender offer requirement applied to acquisitions "in accordance with" the bylaws. Argentina's acquisition was not "in accordance with" the bylaws—it was a sovereign expropriation, which the bylaws did not and could not address. The prospectus cannot be read to bind Argentina to conduct a tender offer in connection with a sovereign act of expropriation.

### B. The Expropriation Law Did Not Require a Tender Offer

Petersen's argument that the Expropriation Law "did not override" the bylaws misunderstands the relationship between sovereign acts and private contracts. The Expropriation Law did not need to "override" the bylaws because the bylaws, as a matter of law, could not apply to sovereign expropriation.

Argentine law authorized the expropriation of YPF shares "for public use." The tender offer provisions in the bylaws were designed to address voluntary acquisitions by private parties—not expropriations by the sovereign. To interpret the bylaws to require a tender offer whenever the government acquires shares would be to read them in a manner that contradicts their fundamental purpose and exceeds their legal authority.

### C. The Effects on U.S. Investors Do Not Create Jurisdiction

Petersen's emphasis on the effects on U.S. investors—cancelled dividends, defaulted loans, foreclosed ADRs—cannot salvage jurisdiction over a sovereign act. The FSIA requires that the claim be "based upon" commercial activity having a direct effect. Here, the claim is based upon Argentina's sovereign expropriation, which is not a commercial activity.

The chain of causation advanced by Petersen—expropriation leads to cancelled dividends leads to default—proves too much. Under this logic, any sovereign act that affects property in which U.S. investors have an interest would be subject to U.S. jurisdiction. This would eviscerate the FSIA's protection for sovereign states.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's order denying Argentina's motion to dismiss under the FSIA and dismiss the claims against Argentina. The act of state doctrine independently bars Petersen's claims, and this Court should affirm the dismissal on that ground as well.

Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of expropriation is precisely the type of governmental action that the FSIA and the act of state doctrine were designed to shield from adjudication in United States courts. Petersen may disagree with the terms on which Argentina chose to exercise its constitutional powers, but that disagreement must be resolved in Argentine courts, not in the courts of the United States.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon consideration of the defendants-appellants' appeal from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss, and based solely on the facts and arguments presented in the opening statements, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

The defendants argue that the district court erred in denying their motion to dismiss under the FSIA, 28 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq., contending that their actions constitute sovereign acts rather than commercial activity. The Court finds the prosecution's argument to be the more persuasive interpretation of the statute and precedent at this stage.

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

The FSIA provides that a foreign state is not immune from jurisdiction in any case "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state... that causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The threshold inquiry is whether the defendants' conduct constitutes "commercial activity." Under *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the "commercial activity" exception turns on the "nature" of the conduct rather than its "purpose." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). When a foreign sovereign acts in the manner of a private player in the market, its actions are commercial.

Here, the prosecution correctly identifies that the conduct upon which the claim is based—the breach of the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws and the exercise of voting rights in contravention of those bylaws—is fundamentally commercial. The bylaws in question were contractual obligations voluntarily undertaken by Argentina and YPF as part of the privatization of YPF and its listing on the New York Stock Exchange. These provisions governed the rights of shareholders in a publicly traded corporation, a quintessential private commercial arrangement. While the defendants characterize the acquisition of the shares as an exercise of eminent domain, the specific acts challenged—refusing to make a tender offer, cancelling dividends, and voting shares in violation of the corporate bylaws—are actions taken by a shareholder in a commercial enterprise. As the Supreme Court noted in *Weltover*, a foreign state's participation in the market, including the issuance and management of securities, constitutes commercial activity. 504 U.S. at 614.

The defense's reliance on the sovereign nature of expropriation is insufficient to shield the subsequent commercial conduct. The fact that the shares were acquired via sovereign power does not retroactively transform the management of those shares and the breach of shareholder agreements into sovereign acts. The "nature" of the act of breaching a corporate bylaw regarding tender offers is commercial.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

The prosecution has also plausibly alleged that the defendants' commercial activity caused a "direct effect" in the United States. Under *Weltover*, a direct effect exists if the effect follows as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity. 504 U.S. at 618. The prosecution established a direct causal chain: Argentina's refusal to comply with the bylaws led to the cancellation of dividend payments to U.S. investors, which precipitated Petersen's default on its loans, resulting in the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This financial injury to U.S. investors and the seizure of collateral located in New York constitutes a direct effect within the meaning of the statute. See *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 597 (2d Cir. 2006) (recognizing that harm to U.S. investors through U.S. financial instruments satisfies the direct effect requirement).

**C. YPF's Status**

Regarding YPF, the defense argues it acted as an instrumentality of the state. However, under *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 628-29 (1983), the determination of whether an entity is an instrumentality requires examining the degree of autonomy and the nature of the activity involved. YPF is a separately incorporated entity whose shares were publicly traded on the NYSE. Its actions in enforcing its own bylaws against shareholders are commercial in nature. Therefore, the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss YPF under the FSIA.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

The defendants argue that the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims because adjudicating the breach of contract would require the Court to sit in judgment of Argentina's sovereign expropriation. The Court is not persuaded that the doctrine applies to the claims as presented.

The act of state doctrine dictates that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall be deemed valid" by U.S. courts. *Underwood v. Burger*, 176 F.3d 74, 78 (2d Cir. 1999). However, the doctrine is prudential, not jurisdictional, and yields in cases where the foreign sovereign acts as a commercial participant. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428-29 (1964).

The prosecution argues, and the Court agrees, that Petersen's claims are based on breach of contract, not on the validity of the expropriation itself. Petersen does not seek to invalidate Law 26,741 or challenge Argentina's right to expropriate property for public use. Rather, Petersen seeks to enforce the specific contractual terms—the bylaws—that governed the relationship between the shareholders and the company. These terms were included in the bylaws and the prospectus to induce investment in the United States.

As held in *First National City Bank*, when a sovereign enters the commercial marketplace, it must adhere to the rules of that marketplace. 462 U.S. at 621-22. To allow a foreign sovereign to unilaterally disregard the commercial contracts it entered into when it privatized a company, simply by invoking the act of state doctrine, would undermine the purpose of the FSIA and the stability of international commercial transactions. The defense's argument that the bylaws cannot bind the sovereign's constitutional power is a matter of Argentine law to be decided by the Argentine courts if necessary, but it does not automatically bar a U.S. court from adjudicating a claim for breach of a commercial contract governed by U.S. jurisdictional statutes. See *Garb*, 440 F.3d at 597 (holding that the act of state doctrine does not bar claims for breach of contract when the sovereign acts in a commercial capacity).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the prosecution has plausibly alleged that the defendants' actions fall within the commercial activity exception of the FSIA and caused a direct effect in the United States. Furthermore, the act of state doctrine does not bar the breach of contract claims at this stage. The district court's order denying the motions to dismiss is affirmed.

The defense argues that because the acquisition of shares was effected through Law 26,741—a sovereign exercise of eminent domain—the subsequent management of those shares is also sovereign. The Court finds this reasoning unpersuasive as a matter of jurisdictional law. The specific conduct challenged in this complaint is the refusal to make a tender offer, the cancellation of dividends, and the voting of shares in contravention of YPF's bylaws. These are actions that a private shareholder might take. The "nature" of these acts is the management of corporate securities, which the Supreme Court has explicitly identified as commercial activity. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

The defense's argument that the bylaws are "subordinate to Argentina's sovereign power" raises a substantive question of Argentine law regarding the validity of the contract. However, for purposes of subject matter jurisdiction, the Court looks to the nature of the act upon which the claim is based. The act of voting shares and cancelling dividends is commercial in nature. Here, the injury—the foreclosure of collateral located in New York—was an immediate consequence of the commercial activity (the cancellation of dividends). Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 597 (2d Cir. 2006), confirms that financial harm to U.S. investors from a foreign state’s commercial acts suffices. In *Garb*, this Court found direct effect where Polish government actions caused immediate financial injury to U.S. investors holding securities tied to the state’s conduct. Here, the foreclosure of collateral in New York is an immediate consequence of defendants’ refusal to comply with contractual obligations. The defense’s attempt to isolate the expropriation as the “proximate cause” ignores that the *specific breach*—cancelling dividends and voting shares in violation of section 7(h)—is what triggered Petersen’s default. Without that breach, the foreclosure would not have occurred.  

### III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY BECAUSE WE SEEK TO ENFORCE CONTRACTUAL OBLIGATIONS, NOT JUDGE SOVEREIGN POWER  
The defense claims adjudicating this claim would require this Court to “sit in judgment of Argentina’s sovereign determination to expropriate property for public use.” *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). This is a misstatement of the law. The act of state doctrine bars courts from questioning the validity of a foreign sovereign’s official acts—*not* from enforcing contractual obligations arising from commercial participation. *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428–29.  

Petersen does not challenge the validity of Law 26,741 or Argentina’s right to expropriate. It alleges only that defendants breached specific contractual terms they voluntarily adopted and publicly touted in their SEC prospectus. As *First National City Bank* holds, when a sovereign “enters the commercial marketplace,” it “must adhere to the rules of that marketplace.” 462 U.S. at 621–22. The defense’s argument that “the bylaws cannot bind Argentina’s constitutional authority” is irrelevant. The question is not whether Argentina *could* expropriate—it is whether, having promised investors a compensated exit via tender offers, Argentina breached that promise. That is a question of contract interpretation, not sovereign power.  

*Garb* is controlling: “The act of state doctrine does not bar claims for breach of contract when the sovereign acts in a commercial capacity.” 440 F.3d at 597. Here, defendants acted as commercial participants: they issued ADRs on the NYSE, published prospectuses in New York newspapers, and promised investors specific protections. The defense’s attempt to conflate contract enforcement with sovereign judgment is precisely what *Sabbatino* prohibits.  

### IV. YPF IS NOT ENTITLED TO IMMUNITY BECAUSE ITS CONDUCT WAS COMMERCIAL  
The defense argues YPF is “an instrumentality of the Argentine government” entitled to immunity. But even if YPF is an instrumentality, the FSIA does not protect it when it engages in commercial activity. *First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 628–29. YPF’s actions—cancelling dividends, refusing to enforce section 7(h)’s penalties, and voting shares without tendering—were commercial acts. A privately held corporation would be sued for the same conduct. The fact that YPF’s shares were traded on the NYSE and its prospectus was filed with the SEC confirms it operated as a commercial entity.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honor, this case is not about challenging Argentina’s sovereign power. It is about holding defendants accountable for breaching binding contractual terms they adopted to attract U.S. investors. Argentina and YPF chose to participate in the New York financial markets. They promised investors a compensated exit if they acquired control. They then broke that promise, causing direct financial harm in New York. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies, and the act of state doctrine has no place here. The district court correctly denied the motions to dismiss, and this Court should affirm.  

We urge you to uphold the rule of law: sovereigns cannot hide behind immunity when they breach contracts in the commercial marketplace.  

Respectfully submitted,

## I. THE "WHOLE TRANSACTION" DOCTRINE PREVAILS: THE NATURE OF THE CONDUCT IS SOVEREIGN, NOT COMMERCIAL

The prosecution's central argument—that the specific acts of refusing to make a tender offer and cancelling dividends constitute commercial activity—fails because it improperly severs these acts from the overarching sovereign transaction of expropriation. The Supreme Court in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992), instructs that courts must consider the "entire context" of the transaction when determining whether commercial activity exists. The prosecution's interpretation would allow plaintiffs to circumvent sovereign immunity simply by recasting sovereign acts as commercial breaches, eviscerating the protections afforded by the FSIA.

The prosecution cites *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 621–22 (1983), for the proposition that "when a foreign state enters the commercial marketplace, it must adhere to the rules of that marketplace." However, this principle applies when a sovereign voluntarily enters commercial competition *as a commercial actor*—not when it exercises its inherent sovereign powers. Argentina did not enter the commercial marketplace to compete with private actors; it exercised its constitutional authority to expropriate property for public use. The act of state doctrine applies not only to the act of expropriation itself, but to all acts taken in furtherance thereof.

The prosecution argues that YPF is not entitled to immunity because it engaged in commercial activity. The appointment of an Intervenor to take control of YPF's operations demonstrates that YPF acted as an instrumentality of the Argentine state.

Under *First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 628-29, the determination of whether an entity is an instrumentality involves examining the degree of autonomy and the nature of the activity. Here, YPF's total relationship with the Argentine government demonstrates that it was an instrumentality of the state. The prosecution's argument that YPF's commercial activity strips it of immunity ignores that an instrumentality does not lose its immunity for all acts merely because it engages in some commercial activity. The key is whether the act in question is sovereign or commercial in nature.

The FSIA was enacted to balance the need for access to international markets with the principle of sovereign immunity. **FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying the defendants' motions to dismiss. The defendants have failed to demonstrate that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) or that the act of state doctrine bars the plaintiff's claims.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

The defendants argue that the "whole transaction" of expropriation is sovereign in nature and that the specific acts of refusing to make a tender offer, cancelling dividends, and voting shares cannot be severed from this sovereign context. The Court is not persuaded.

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is immune from suit unless one of the exceptions applies, including the "commercial activity" exception. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has instructed that in determining whether an activity is commercial, we must look to the "nature" of the conduct rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The "nature" test asks whether the particular conduct that forms the basis for the plaintiff's claim is of a type that a private party might engage in. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993).

Here, the conduct upon which the plaintiff's claim is based includes the refusal to honor the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws, the cancellation of dividend payments, and the voting of shares in contravention of corporate governance rules. These are quintessential acts of a private shareholder or corporate participant. While the defendants correctly note that these actions were taken in furtherance of a sovereign expropriation, the Supreme Court in *Weltover* rejected the argument that the commercial nature of specific acts is transformed into sovereign activity merely because they are part of a broader sovereign program. In *Weltover*, the Court held that Argentina's rescheduling of bonds was commercial activity despite the government's assertion that it was done to stabilize the national economy. 504 U.S. at 618-19. Similarly, here, the management of corporate securities and the refusal to comply with shareholder agreements are commercial acts, regardless of the sovereign purpose behind the acquisition of the shares.

The defendants' contention that the bylaws are "subordinate to Argentina's sovereign power" and that enforcing them would require the Court to limit Argentina's constitutional authority is a merits defense, not a jurisdictional bar. As the Supreme Court held in *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 621–22 (1983), "when a foreign state enters the commercial marketplace, it must adhere to the rules of that marketplace." Whether the sovereign is *excused* from adhering to those rules due to its constitutional authority is a question for the merits phase, not a basis to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction at this stage. The district court correctly identified that the nature of the defendants' conduct is commercial.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

The defendants argue that the plaintiff's injuries flow from the expropriation itself, which targeted Repsol, and that the causal chain is too attenuated to constitute a "direct effect" in the United States. We disagree.

The FSIA requires that the commercial activity "cause[] a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has defined "direct effect" to mean that the effect must follow as an "immediate consequence" of the defendant's activity. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

The plaintiff has alleged a direct causal chain: (1) Argentina and YPF refused to comply with the bylaws and cancelled dividend payments; (2) the cancellation of dividends caused the plaintiff to default on its loan obligations; and (3) the default triggered the foreclosure of the plaintiff's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This chain of events is sufficiently direct. The injury—the foreclosure of collateral located in New York—was an immediate consequence of the commercial activity (the cancellation of dividends). The fact that the expropriation legislation targeted Repsol does not break the causal link between the breach of the bylaws and the financial injury to the plaintiff in the United States. This reasoning is consistent with the Second Circuit's holding in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 597 (2d Cir. 2006), where the court found a direct effect where financial harm to U.S. investors flowed from the state's actions.

**C. YPF's Status**

The defendants argue that YPF is an instrumentality of the Argentine state entitled to immunity. Even assuming YPF is an instrumentality, the commercial activity exception applies to instrumentalities when they engage in commercial acts. *First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 628-29. As discussed above, YPF's acts of cancelling dividends and voting shares in violation of the bylaws are commercial in nature. Therefore, YPF is not immune from suit based on these specific activities.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

The defendants contend that adjudicating the plaintiff's claim would require the Court to sit in judgment of Argentina's sovereign determination to expropriate property, thereby invoking the act of state doctrine. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). Specifically, they argue that enforcing the bylaws requires the Court to determine whether private contracts can limit Argentina's constitutional power of expropriation.

The act of state doctrine is a prudential doctrine that "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 401. However, the doctrine yields when the sovereign acts as a commercial participant. As noted in *Garb*, "the act of state doctrine does not bar claims for breach of contract when the sovereign acts in a commercial capacity." 440 F.3d at 597.

The plaintiff does not seek to invalidate Law 26,741 or challenge Argentina's right to expropriate property under Article 17 of the Argentine Constitution. Rather, the plaintiff seeks to enforce the contractual terms of the bylaws that Argentina voluntarily adopted and represented to investors. The question of whether the bylaws bind the sovereign in this specific instance is a matter of contract interpretation and the application of Argentine law to the parties' agreement. It does not require this Court to declare the expropriation itself invalid or to sit in judgment of Argentina's public use determination. The defendants' argument that the bylaws are subordinate to sovereign power is a substantive argument for why the contract should not be enforced, but it does not invoke the act of state doctrine to bar the Court from hearing the case entirely.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments fail to demonstrate that the district court erred in denying the motions to dismiss. The plaintiff has plausibly alleged that the defendants' conduct constitutes commercial activity causing a direct effect in the United States under the FSIA. Furthermore, the act of state doctrine does not bar adjudication of the breach of contract claims. The district court's order is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
