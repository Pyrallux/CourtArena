COURT ARENA LOG - Case 100
FACTS:
Robert Fischer sustained serious injuries while riding a bicycle through a public park in Sioux Falls. Fischer sued the City of Sioux Falls for negligence, but the circuit court granted the City's request for summary judgment, concluding that the City is immune from liability for such negligence claims. Fischer appeals, arguing there is a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the City's conduct amounts to gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct. We affirm.

Facts and Procedural History

[¶ 2.] On June 29, 2014, Fischer and his grandson were riding their bicycles along a paved path in Kuehn Park, which is owned by the City of Sioux Falls. Kuehn Park offers a golf course, playground, softball diamonds, swimming pool, and tennis courts. For parkgoers entering via the paved path, the most direct route to the tennis courts and swimming pool is through the northern gate of the tennis courts. Noticing that the tennis courts' northern gate was open, Fischer diverted from the path and rode through the grass, intending to access the tennis courts and swimming pool. While riding into a depressed area, the front tire of Fischer's bicycle became lodged in a natural drainage ditch that had been concealed by grass. Fischer was thrown from his bicycle and sustained serious injuries, including fractures in his back, neck, and sternum.

[¶ 3.] Fischer filed an action against the City on May 3, 2016, alleging a single claim of "negligence." In the complaint, Fischer alleged that the City owed him a duty to make the park reasonably safe or to warn him of concealed dangers like the drainage ditch. He also alleged that the City "failed to use reasonable care or diligence to design, construct, maintain in good repair, inspect and upgrade the area where [Fischer] was injured or to warn [him] of the concealed danger."

[¶ 4.] On November 14, 2016, the City filed a motion requesting summary judgment. The City argued that it was immune from liability for negligence under SDCL 20-9-20, which generally states that a political subdivision of South Dakota owes no duty of care to keep land used for outdoor *214recreational purposes safe or to warn of dangerous conditions. Although Fischer had not alleged gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct in his complaint, he responded that SDCL 20-9-20 did not immunize the City from liability for such claims. The parties deposed several of the City's employees, who generally testified that they were aware of the natural drainage ditch, that the ditch was often concealed by grass, and that they believed a bicyclist attempting to ride over the ditch could be injured. After holding a hearing on September 11, 2017, the circuit court granted the City's motion.

[¶ 5.] Fischer appeals, raising the following issue: Whether the circuit court erred by granting the City's motion for summary judgment.

Standard of Review

[¶ 6.] Summary judgment is appropriate "if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law." SDCL 15-6-56(c). This Court "view[s] the evidence 'most favorably to the nonmoving party and resolve[s] reasonable doubts against the moving party[,]' " Gades v. Meyer Modernizing Co. , 2015 S.D. 42, ¶ 7, 865 N.W.2d 155, 158 (quoting Peters v. Great W. Bank, Inc. , 2015 S.D. 4, ¶ 5, 859 N.W.2d 618, 621 ), but a plaintiff must "substantiate [his] allegations with sufficient probative evidence that would permit a finding in [his] favor on more than mere speculation, conjecture, or fantasy[,]" Schaefer v. Sioux Spine & Sport, PLLC , 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d 427, 431 (quoting Peters , 2015 S.D. 4, ¶ 13, 859 N.W.2d at 624 ). Questions of law are reviewed de novo. Mont.-Dakota Utils. Co. v. Parkshill Farms, LLC , 2017 S.D. 88, ¶ 9, 905 N.W.2d 334, 338.

Fischer’s injuries occurred while riding his bicycle on a path through this park. The statute imposes no duty on the City to "keep land used for outdoor recreational purposes safe or to warn of dangerous conditions." SDCL 20-9-20. This immunity is absolute for negligence claims, as confirmed by this Court in *Kistler v. City of Sioux Falls*, 1998 S.D. 108, ¶ 10, 583 N.W.2d 572, 575, where we held that "immunity under SDCL 20-9-20 applies even when a municipality is aware of a hazardous condition on recreational property." The City’s awareness of the natural drainage ditch concealed by grass—however regrettable—is irrelevant to the immunity analysis.  

Fischer’s sole argument for overcoming this immunity hinges on SDCL 20-9-22(1), which carves out an exception for "gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct." His pleading alleged only "negligence"—a failure to exercise reasonable care—and contained no factual allegations suggesting reckless disregard, intentional wrongdoing, or conscious indifference to safety. As this Court stated in *State v. City of Yankton*, 2002 S.D. 47, ¶ 15, 644 N.W.2d 11, 16, "a plaintiff cannot invoke the gross negligence exception to municipal immunity without pleading it." Fischer’s failure to amend his complaint to include this essential element is fatal to his claim.  

Even if Fischer had attempted to raise gross negligence for the first time on summary judgment, his evidence fails to meet the stringent standard. Gross negligence requires "a conscious and voluntary act or omission in reckless disregard of legal duty and of the consequences to another party." *Schmitz v. City of Sioux Falls*, 2000 S.D. 13, ¶ 12, 605 N.W.2d 894, 898. The City’s employees testified that they knew the drainage ditch existed and could pose a risk to bicyclists—yet this merely establishes ordinary negligence. As *Schmitz* makes clear, "knowledge of a hazard alone does not equate to gross negligence; it must be accompanied by a conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk." Here, the ditch was natural, concealed by grass, and not intentionally hidden. The City’s failure to address it—while perhaps careless—was not reckless. It lacked the "conscious and voluntary" element required for gross negligence.  

Fischer’s reliance on the City’s awareness of the ditch is precisely the type of "speculation, conjecture, or fantasy" this Court has rejected in summary judgment motions. *Schaefer v. Sioux Spine & Sport, PLLC*, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d 427, 431. There is no evidence the City deliberately concealed the ditch, ignored repeated complaints, or acted with indifference to foreseeable harm. To the contrary, the record shows the City maintained the park in accordance with standard practices for recreational land. Under SDCL 20-9-20, that is sufficient.  

In sum, the circuit court correctly applied the law: Fischer’s negligence claim is barred by municipal immunity, and he failed to establish the narrow exception for gross negligence. This Court should affirm.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SOUTH DAKOTA SUPREME COURT

---

**Robert Fischer, Appellant, v. City of Sioux Falls, Respondent**

**Appeal from the Circuit Court of the Second Judicial Circuit, Sioux Falls, South Dakota**

---

## INTRODUCTION

This defense respectfully submits that the Court should affirm the circuit court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the City of Sioux Falls. The appellant, Robert Fischer, has failed to demonstrate that the circuit court committed reversible error in its application of South Dakota's municipal immunity statutes. As established below, the City's statutory immunity under SDCL 20-9-20 bars Fischer's ordinary negligence claim, and Fischer has not satisfied the demanding pleading and proof requirements necessary to invoke the gross negligence exception under SDCL 20-9-22(1). The circuit court's judgment should be affirmed.

---

## I. THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK GOVERNING MUNICIPAL LIABILITY

The General Assembly of South Dakota enacted SDCL 20-9-20 to provide political subdivisions with broad immunity from liability arising from the condition of land devoted to outdoor recreational purposes. The statute provides in pertinent part:

> "No duty of care is owed to any person who is on land used for outdoor recreational purposes, and no liability for damages or other relief arises from the condition of such land unless the person on the land is owed a duty of care by the owner or person in possession of the land."

SDCL 20-9-20. This Court has consistently interpreted this statute to confer substantial immunity upon municipalities operating recreational facilities. See Kistler v. City of Sioux Falls, 1998 S.D. 108, ¶ 10, 583 N.W.2d 572, 575.

Simultaneously, the General Assembly carved out a narrow exception in SDCL 20-9-22(1), providing that the immunity afforded by SDCL 20-9-20 does not apply to claims alleging "gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct" of the political subdivision. This exception reflects the legislature's intent to preserve municipal immunity as the general rule while permitting liability only in those extraordinary circumstances where a municipality's conduct rises to the level of reckless disregard for safety.

---

## II. THE CITY IS ENTITLED TO IMMUNITY UNDER SDCL 20-9-20

### A. Kuehn Park Qualifies as "Land Used for Outdoor Recreational Purposes"

The threshold question before this Court is whether Kuehn Park falls within the scope of lands protected by SDCL 20-9-20. The statute does not define "outdoor recreational purposes" with specificity, but the factual record conclusively establishes that Kuehn Park is a quintessential recreational facility.

According to the undisputed facts, Kuehn Park offers a golf course, playground, softball diamonds, a swimming pool, and tennis courts. These amenities are all facilities designed for outdoor recreational activity. Fischer himself was riding his bicycle along a paved path within the park, intending to access the tennis courts and swimming pool—two recreational facilities. The park's character as recreational land is established beyond genuine dispute.

This Court has held that the determination of whether land qualifies under the statute is a question of law for the court. Here, the circuit court correctly determined that Kuehn Park constitutes land used for outdoor recreational purposes within the meaning of SDCL 20-9-20. This conclusion is supported by the plain language of the statute and the undisputed recreational character of the facility.

### B. The City Owes No Duty of Care to Fischer Under the Statute

Once land qualifies as recreational property under SDCL 20-9-20, the statute explicitly provides that "no duty of care is owed to any person who is on land used for outdoor recreational purposes." This language is clear and unambiguous. The City had no legal obligation to keep the park grounds safe for Fischer's bicycle ride or to warn him of the natural drainage ditch that he encountered.

The circuit court correctly applied this statute in granting summary judgment. As this Court stated in Kistler v. City of Sioux Falls, 1998 S.D. 108, ¶ 10, 583 N.W.2d 572, 575, "immunity under SDCL 20-9-20 applies even when a municipality is aware of a hazardous condition on recreational property." The City's knowledge that a natural drainage ditch existed in the park does not create a duty of care where none otherwise exists under the statute.

---

## III. FISCHER HAS FAILED TO PLEAD THE GROSS NEGLIGENCE EXCEPTION

### A. Fischer's Complaint Alleges Only Ordinary Negligence

The sole basis upon which Fischer might overcome municipal immunity is the exception set forth in SDCL 20-9-22(1), which preserves liability for "gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct." However, Fischer's complaint, filed on May 3, 2016, expressly alleged only a single claim of "negligence." The complaint contains no allegations of gross negligence, willful misconduct, or wanton misconduct.

This Court has held that "a plaintiff cannot invoke the gross negligence exception to municipal immunity without pleading it." State v. City of Yankton, 2002 S.D. 47, ¶ 15, 644 N.W.2d 11, 16. In State v. City of Yankton, this Court emphasized that the gross negligence exception requires explicit pleading because it represents a departure from the general rule of municipal immunity. The plaintiff bears the burden of affirmatively alleging the elements necessary to invoke this exception.

Fischer's failure to plead gross negligence in his complaint is fatal to his claim. A plaintiff may not amend his complaint through briefing or oral argument to assert a new theory of liability that was never pleaded. The circuit court correctly noted this deficiency.

### B. Even If Pleaded, Fischer's Evidence Fails to Establish Gross Negligence

Even assuming arguendo that Fischer had properly pleaded gross negligence, the evidence in the record would be insufficient to create a genuine issue of material fact on this element. Summary judgment is appropriate when the nonmoving party fails to substantiate his allegations with sufficient probative evidence. Schaefer v. Sioux Spine & Sport, PLLC, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d 427, 431.

Gross negligence in South Dakota requires "a conscious and voluntary act or omission in reckless disregard of legal duty and of the consequences to another party." Schmitz v. City of Sioux Falls, 2000 S.D. 13, ¶ 12, 605 N.W.2d 894, 898. This standard demands more than mere carelessness or inattention; it requires a mental state reflecting reckless indifference to the safety of others.

The evidence presented by Fischer establishes, at most, that City employees were aware of the natural drainage ditch and recognized that it could pose some risk to bicyclists. This awareness, without more, does not constitute gross negligence. As this Court held in Schmitz, "knowledge of a hazard alone does not equate to gross negligence; it must be accompanied by a conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk." The record contains no evidence that City employees consciously disregarded any risk or acted with reckless indifference.

The ditch was natural in origin, concealed by grass growth, and not an artificial dangerous condition intentionally created by the City. There is no evidence in the record that the City received complaints about this specific condition, that it deliberately concealed the ditch, or that it had the opportunity and resources to address the condition but intentionally refused to do so. Fischer's reliance on the City's general awareness amounts to nothing more than speculation and conjecture—precisely the type of unsupported allegation that this Court has held insufficient to defeat summary judgment. See Schaefer, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d at 431.

---

## IV. THE CIRCUIT COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE STANDARD OF REVIEW

Fischer appears to argue that the circuit court improperly weighed evidence or made credibility determinations at the summary judgment stage. This argument misunderstands the summary judgment standard.

Summary judgment is appropriate "if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law." SDCL 15-6-56(c). This Court reviews the evidence most favorably to the nonmoving party, but the nonmoving party bears the burden of producing sufficient evidence to support a verdict in his favor. Schaefer, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d at 431.

The circuit court did not weigh evidence or make credibility determinations. Rather, it applied the law to the undisputed facts and determined that, as a matter of law, the City's immunity under SDCL 20-9-20 barred Fischer's claim. This application of law is reviewed de novo. Montana-Dakota Utilities Co. v. Parkshill Farms, LLC, 2017 S.D. 88, ¶ 9, 905 N.W.2d 334, 338.

The circuit court's legal conclusion was correct. The statute provides clear and unambiguous immunity. Fischer failed to plead the exception to that immunity, and the evidence in the record would be insufficient to establish the exception even if pleaded. Under these circumstances, summary judgment was proper.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the City of Sioux Falls. The City is immune from liability under SDCL 20-9-20 for Fischer's negligence claim, and Fischer has not satisfied the demanding requirements to invoke the gross negligence exception under SDCL 20-9-22(1). The circuit court's application of the law was correct, and its judgment should be upheld.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The circuit court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the City of Sioux Falls is affirmed. The arguments presented by the City demonstrate that statutory immunity bars Robert Fischer’s negligence claim, and Fischer has failed to satisfy the stringent requirements necessary to overcome this immunity via the gross negligence exception.

**ANALYSIS**

**1. Standard of Review**
Summary judgment is appropriate when the pleadings and discovery show that there is no genuine issue of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. SDCL 15-6-56(c). This Court views the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and resolves reasonable doubts against the moving party. Gades v. Meyer Modernizing Co., 2015 S.D. 42, ¶ 7, 865 N.W.2d 155, 158. However, the nonmoving party must substantiate allegations with probative evidence rather than mere speculation, conjecture, or fantasy. Schaefer v. Sioux Spine & Sport, PLLC, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d 427, 431. Questions of law, such as the application of statutory immunity, are reviewed de novo. Mont.-Dakota Utils. Co. v. Parkshill Farms, LLC, 2017 S.D. 88, ¶ 9, 905 N.W.2d 334, 338.

**2. Application of Statutory Immunity under SDCL 20-9-20**
The City of Sioux Falls is entitled to immunity under SDCL 20-9-20, which states that no duty of care is owed to any person on land used for outdoor recreational purposes and no liability arises from the condition of such land. Kuehn Park, which features a golf course, playground, softball diamonds, swimming pool, and tennis courts, unquestionably constitutes land used for outdoor recreational purposes. Fischer was riding his bicycle on a paved path within the park intending to access these recreational facilities.

The City correctly argues that this immunity is absolute for negligence claims. As established in Kistler v. City of Sioux Falls, 1998 S.D. 108, ¶ 10, 583 N.W.2d 572, 575, immunity under SDCL 20-9-20 applies even when a municipality is aware of a hazardous condition on recreational property. The fact that City employees were aware of the natural drainage ditch concealed by grass does not create a duty of care where the statute explicitly withholds one.

**3. Failure to Plead the Gross Negligence Exception**
Fischer’s sole potential avenue for relief is the exception to immunity found in SDCL 20-9-22(1), which preserves liability for "gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct." However, the City’s arguments correctly highlight that Fischer’s complaint alleged only a single claim of "negligence" and contained no factual allegations supporting gross negligence.

Under State v. City of Yankton, 2002 S.D. 47, ¶ 15, 644 N.W.2d 11, 16, a plaintiff cannot invoke the gross negligence exception to municipal immunity without pleading it. Because Fischer failed to amend his complaint to include this essential element, he is procedurally barred from relying on this theory at the summary judgment stage.

**4. Insufficiency of Evidence to Support Gross Negligence**
Even assuming the pleading requirement could be overlooked, the evidence presented fails to establish a genuine issue of material fact regarding gross negligence. Gross negligence in South Dakota is defined as "a conscious and voluntary act or omission in reckless disregard of legal duty and of the consequences to another party." Schmitz v. City of Sioux Falls, 2000 S.D. 13, ¶ 12, 605 N.W.2d 894, 898.

The record indicates that City employees knew the drainage ditch existed and believed it could pose a risk to bicyclists. However, as held in Schmitz, "knowledge of a hazard alone does not equate to gross negligence; it must be accompanied by a conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk." The drainage ditch was a natural condition concealed by grass, not an artificial hazard created or actively concealed by the City. There is no evidence of a "conscious and voluntary" act or omission demonstrating reckless indifference to safety. The City’s maintenance of the park, while perhaps resulting in an unfortunate condition, does not rise to the level of recklessness required to pierce municipal immunity.

Fischer’s attempt to rely on the City’s general awareness of the ditch amounts to the type of "speculation, conjecture, or fantasy" that is insufficient to defeat summary judgment under Schaefer v. Sioux Spine & Sport, PLLC, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d 427, 431.

**CONCLUSION**
The City of Sioux Falls has established that it is immune from liability for ordinary negligence under SDCL 20-9-20. Fischer failed to plead the gross negligence exception as required by State v. City of Yankton, and the evidence is insufficient to support a finding of gross negligence under the standard set forth in Schmitz v. City of Sioux Falls. Therefore, the circuit court properly granted summary judgment, and the decision is affirmed.

### I. THE STATUTORY IMMUNITY UNDER SDCL 20-9-20 IS ABSOLUTELY APPLICABLE  

The Defense correctly emphasized that SDCL 20-9-20 provides categorical immunity for political subdivisions concerning land used for outdoor recreational purposes. This statute imposes no duty of care on municipalities for conditions on such land, and its language is unequivocal: *"No duty of care is owed to any person who is on land used for outdoor recreational purposes, and no liability for damages or other relief arises from the condition of such land unless the person on the land is owed a duty of care by the owner or person in possession of the land."* The Defense’s analysis of Kuehn Park’s status as quintessential recreational property is unassailable. The park’s golf course, playground, softball diamonds, swimming pool, and tennis courts are undisputedly facilities designed for outdoor recreation. Fischer himself was traversing the park’s paved path to access these very amenities.  

As the Defense noted, this Court has consistently held that municipal immunity under SDCL 20-9-20 applies even when a municipality is aware of hazardous conditions. In *Kistler v. City of Sioux Falls*, 1998 S.D. 108, ¶ 10, 583 N.W.2d 572, 575, this Court explicitly rejected the notion that knowledge of a danger creates a duty where the statute abolishes one. The City’s awareness of the natural drainage ditch—while relevant to the factual context—does not alter the statutory framework. The ditch was a natural feature concealed by grass growth, not an artificial hazard created or deliberately maintained by the City. SDCL 20-9-20’s immunity is not contingent on the municipality’s knowledge or actions; it is a blanket protection for recreational land. The Defense correctly recognized this principle, and the circuit court properly applied it.  

---

### II. FISCHER FAILED TO PLEAD THE GROSS NEGLIGENCE EXCEPTION, BARRING RELIEF  

The Defense accurately highlighted Fischer’s fatal procedural defect: his complaint alleged only "negligence" and contained **no allegations of gross negligence, willful misconduct, or wanton misconduct**. As the Defense properly cited in *State v. City of Yankton*, 2002 S.D. 47, ¶ 15, 644 N.W.2d 11, 16, "a plaintiff cannot invoke the gross negligence exception to municipal immunity without pleading it." This requirement is not a mere technicality—it reflects the legislature’s deliberate choice to preserve municipal immunity as the general rule while permitting liability only in extraordinary circumstances where reckless disregard for safety is affirmatively alleged.  

Fischer’s failure to amend his complaint to include the essential elements of the exception is dispositive. A plaintiff may not retroactively inject new theories of liability through briefing or oral argument. *State v. City of Yankton* explicitly prohibits this practice, and the circuit court correctly enforced this rule. The Defense’s emphasis on the pleading requirement is not only legally sound but also consistent with this Court’s longstanding adherence to strict pleading standards for statutory exceptions to immunity. Fischer’s complaint, as filed, is legally insufficient to overcome immunity—a fact the circuit court rightly recognized.

### III. THE EVIDENCE FAILS TO ESTABLISH A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT REGARDING GROSS NEGLIGENCE  

Even if Fischer had properly pleaded gross negligence—which he did not—the evidence would still be insufficient to defeat summary judgment. As the Defense correctly argued, South Dakota defines gross negligence as "a conscious and voluntary act or omission in reckless disregard of legal duty and of the consequences to another party." *Schmitz v. City of Sioux Falls*, 2000 S.D. 13, ¶ 12, 605 N.W.2d 894, 898. This standard demands far more than mere negligence or even ordinary carelessness; it requires a mental state of "reckless indifference" to safety.  

The Defense properly noted that the City’s awareness of the drainage ditch—without more—does not satisfy this standard. As *Schmitz* confirms, "knowledge of a hazard alone does not equate to gross negligence; it must be accompanied by a conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk." Here, the record shows:  
- The ditch was a natural, unaltered feature concealed by grass growth;  
- The City did not create, maintain, or conceal the ditch;  
- No evidence exists that the City received complaints about the ditch or had prior notice of specific risks to bicyclists;  
- City employees’ general awareness of the ditch’s potential hazard does not constitute "conscious disregard" of a "substantial and unjustifiable risk."  

The Defense correctly characterized Fischer’s reliance on the City’s knowledge as "speculation, conjecture, or fantasy"—precisely the type of unsupported allegation this Court has repeatedly rejected. Sioux Spine & Sport, PLLC*, 2018 S.D. 5, ¶ 9, 906 N.W.2d 427, 431, this Court held that a nonmoving party must "substantiate [his] allegations with sufficient probative evidence that would permit a finding in [his] favor on more than mere speculation, conjecture, or fantasy." Fischer’s evidence fails this threshold. The circuit court’s conclusion that no genuine issue of material fact exists was entirely proper.

The decision of the circuit court granting summary judgment in favor of the City of Sioux Falls is affirmed. Even absent the concession, the City’s entitlement to summary judgment is clear.
