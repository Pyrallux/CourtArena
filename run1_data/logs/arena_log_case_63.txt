**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR THE PLAINTIFF/PROSECUTION**  

May it please the Court:  

Today, we stand before you to correct a fundamental legal error that denied Mr. Van Ness his rightful workers’ compensation benefits. The Director of Labor and Industrial Relations abused her discretion by misapplying *Flor v. Holguin* to this case—a clear misstep that ignores the settled law governing aggravation of pre-existing conditions. The Director erroneously concluded that Mr. Van Ness’s asthma aggravation was not compensable because vog was present island-wide on Maui. But this reasoning collapses under the weight of binding precedent. *Flor v. Holguin* does not apply here. This is not a case about whether an occupational disease "results from the nature of the employment." It is a case about the aggravation of a pre-existing medical condition—asthma—proximately caused by workplace conditions. For such claims, Hawaii law imposes the unitary test: the employment need only be a contributing factor to the aggravation. The Director’s decision, built on this legal misstep, must be reversed.  

Let us be unequivocal: *Flor v. Holguin* is inapplicable. In *Flor v. Holguin*, 105 Haw. 486, 100 P.3d 352 (2004), this Court explicitly limited its holding to diseases that are "inherent in or peculiar to the employment." There, the Court held that compensability for occupational diseases turns on whether the disease is a "natural incident" of the job. But this case is not about a new disease arising from the nature of employment. Mr. Van Ness had a pre-existing, well-documented history of mild persistent asthma—controlled for years with medication and immunotherapy. His claim is solely about the *aggravation* of that condition due to workplace exposure. As this Court confirmed in *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 192 P.3d 583 (2008), "the test for whether an injury arises out of employment is whether the employment was a contributing factor to the injury" when dealing with pre-existing conditions. *Flor* has no role here; it was never designed to govern aggravation claims.  

The Director’s error was fatal. She stated that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment," implying that compensability requires vog to be unique to the workplace. But under the unitary test, uniqueness is irrelevant. The law demands only that employment be a contributing factor to the aggravation. Here, the evidence is overwhelming that Mr. Van Ness’s work environment intensified his vog exposure in ways that his home and other non-work settings did not. At Lahainaluna High School, Mr. Van Ness worked in non-air-conditioned buildings with open louvered windows—conditions that allowed vog and volcanic ash to permeate freely. He spent less than five percent of his workday in his air-conditioned office, instead moving throughout campus where buildings lacked functional cooling systems. This environment also generated excessive dust from computer maintenance: Mr. Van Ness frequently cleaned clogged filters and dealt with overheated machines due to poor air circulation, directly exposing him to concentrated particulates. Dr. Mathison’s expert report confirms this nexus. He documented that Mr. Van Ness’s symptoms "seriously flared" during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure" at work, and noted that "exposure to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" is a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." Crucially, Dr. Mathison also observed that Mr. Van Ness’s symptoms "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu," where vog exposure was minimal.  

Vice Principal Dennis’s testimony further corroborates the unique workplace conditions: she verified that "unusually severe vog-polluted days" occurred at Lahainaluna during the relevant period, with visibility so poor that "we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School." This was not mere island-wide vog; it was a workplace-specific exacerbation. The Director ignored this evidence and misapplied *Flor* to impose an impossible standard—requiring vog to be exclusive to the school—which no Hawaii court has ever endorsed for aggravation claims. *Kekona* is clear: even if vog existed elsewhere, the employment need only contribute to the harm. Here, the school’s ventilation practices and dust-generating tasks made Mr. Van Ness’s job a significant contributing factor to his asthma’s deterioration.  

In short, the Director’s decision rests on a legal misunderstanding. *Flor v. Holguin* does not govern aggravation cases; the unitary test does. Under that test, the evidence proves that Mr. Van Ness’s employment was a contributing factor to his injury. The Director’s denial of benefits was arbitrary, capricious, and legally unsound. We ask this Court to reverse that decision and remand with instructions to award benefits.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Intermediate Court of Appeals of the State of Hawai'i

---

**DEFENSE RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF-APPELLANT'S OPENING ARGUMENT**

---

May it please the Court:

The Director of Labor and Industrial Relations appeals to this Court from a decision of the Labor and Industrial Relations Appeals Board (LIRAB), which reversed the Director's denial of workers' compensation benefits. The Defendant-Appellant Department of Education (DOE) respectfully submits that the Director's decision was supported by substantial evidence in the record and should be reinstated. While the Plaintiff-Appellant Van Ness has presented a compelling narrative regarding workplace conditions, the record evidence does not support his characterization that his employment was a contributing factor to his asthma aggravation. The Director correctly applied the framework established in *Flor v. Holguin* and her decision should be affirmed.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

Van Ness filed a workers' compensation claim alleging that his exposure to vog at Lahainaluna High School exacerbated his pre-existing asthma condition, resulting in chronic bronchitis and other respiratory ailments. After a thorough hearing, the Director denied the claim, finding that while Van Ness's exposure to vog did aggravate his asthma, the vog was present island-wide on Maui and was not unique to his workplace. The Director concluded there was insufficient evidence to establish a nexus between Van Ness's employment and his injury.

This Court reviews the Director's factual findings under the substantial evidence standard. As this Court has held, "the director's findings of fact are conclusive, and this court may not pass upon the weight of the evidence." *Matsuda v. State*, 71 Haw. 479, 483, 795 P.2d 63, 66 (1990). The Director's decision must be upheld if supported by substantial evidence in the record.

---

## II. THE DIRECTOR'S DECISION WAS SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

### A. The Record Establishes That Vog Was Present Island-Wide

The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment." This finding is amply supported by the record.

Van Ness himself testified that there was a "significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006." He further testified that on days when the vog was severe, he was unable to see the administrative building "halfway down the campus" from the library. Most significantly, Van Ness testified that he lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted that "the vog was even worse" than at the school. Van Ness also testified that "a film of volcanic ash would accumulate on his car and windshield" during his commute.

The record contains no evidence distinguishing the vog exposure at Lahainaluna from the vog exposure elsewhere on Maui. Vice Principal Dennis testified that "we experienced some unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding that winter break," but she also confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived. This testimony establishes that vog exposure was a general condition affecting the entire island, not a workplace-specific hazard.

### B. Van Ness's Home Environment Also Involved Vog Exposure

The record demonstrates that Van Ness was exposed to vog not only at work but also in his daily life on Maui. He testified that he spent "little time outside, other than when he was at work," but this was by personal preference—"I was not much of an outdoors person." However, the vog was present in the air regardless of whether Van Ness was indoors or outdoors. The evidence shows that volcanic ash accumulated on his car and windshield during his commute, demonstrating that vog particles penetrated his vehicle and, by extension, his home.

While Van Ness contends his home was protected by air conditioning and HEPA filters, the record does not establish that these measures completely eliminated vog exposure. Dr. Mathison's own report noted that Van Ness's symptoms improved after moving to Oahu, but this improvement is equally consistent with reduced island-wide vog exposure as it is with reduced workplace exposure.

### C. The Evidence Does Not Establish That Employment Was a Contributing Factor

Under Hawaii's workers' compensation framework, the claimant bears the burden of proving that the injury arose out of and in the course of employment. *See* HRS § 386-3. For aggravation of pre-existing conditions, this Court has held that "the test for whether an injury arises out of employment is whether the employment was a contributing factor to the injury." *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 465, 192 P.3d 583, 589 (2008).

The Director found that Van Ness failed to establish that his employment was a contributing factor to his asthma aggravation. This finding is supported by substantial evidence.

The record establishes that Van Ness had a long-standing history of asthma that was exacerbated by vog exposure generally. Dr. Mathison's report stated that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis has largely been controlled with avoidances" and that he has had "significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash including that carried by meteorological conditions holding the pollutant in moist air over Maui." This language describes a general susceptibility to vog exposure, not a workplace-specific condition.

Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's symptoms "seriously flared" during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog" from November 2005 into spring 2006. However, this description refers to meteorological conditions affecting Maui generally, not conditions unique to Lahainaluna High School. The "rainy weather with vog" was an island-wide phenomenon.

The Plaintiff argues that Van Ness's workplace conditions—non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows—intensified his vog exposure. However, the record does not establish that these conditions caused exposure significantly greater than what Van Ness experienced elsewhere. Van Ness testified that he lived in an air-conditioned home with HEPA filters and had HEPA filters in his car. While he spent less than five percent of his workday in his air-conditioned office, he also testified that he spent little time outside his home due to personal preference. The evidence does not establish that his workplace exposure was materially greater than his home exposure.

---

## III. THE DIRECTOR DID NOT MISAPPLY *FLOR V. HOLGUIN*

### A. *Flor v. Holguin* Remains Good Law

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 105 Haw. 486, 100 P.3d 352 (2004), this Court addressed the standard for compensability of occupational diseases. The Court held that for a disease to be compensable, it must be "inherent in or peculiar to the employment" rather than "a disease of general occurrence to which the working public is exposed." *Id.* at 497, 100 P.3d at 363.

The Plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Flor* by arguing that it applies only to occupational disease cases, not to aggravation cases. This argument misreads the holding of *Flor*.

In *Flor*, this Court explicitly addressed the relationship between occupational diseases and the "arising out of" requirement. The Court held that "the phrase 'arising out of' employment is not limited to injuries caused by a risk peculiar to the employment, but also includes injuries caused by a risk to which the employment contributes as a contributing factor." *Id.* at 493, 100 P.3d at 359. The Court thus adopted the "unitary test" for occupational disease cases, under which the employment need only be a contributing factor to the disease.

However, the *Flor* Court also held that even under the unitary test, the disease must still be "inherent in or peculiar to the employment" to be compensable as an occupational disease. *Id.* at 497, 100 P.3d at 363. A disease that is merely "a disease of general occurrence to which the working public is exposed" is not compensable even if employment is a contributing factor, because the employment is not the proximate cause of the disease.

### B. The Director Correctly Applied the Framework

The Director found that Van Ness's exposure to vog aggravated his asthma, but that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment." She further found that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition."

This analysis is consistent with *Flor*. In *Flor*, the Court emphasized that a disease must be "peculiar to the employment" to be compensable. 105 Haw. at 497, 100 P.3d at 363. Vog is a general environmental condition affecting all residents of Maui, not a hazard peculiar to employment at Lahainaluna High School. As the Director correctly found, there is insufficient evidence to establish that Van Ness's exposure to vog at the school was "so overwhelming" that his exposure occurred at his place of employment only.

The Plaintiff argues that the Director imposed a "uniqueness" requirement that is not supported by *Flor*. However, this argument mischaracterizes the Director's reasoning. The Director did not require that vog be unique to the workplace. Rather, she found that the evidence failed to establish that Van Ness's employment was a contributing factor to his injury, given that vog exposure was an island-wide phenomenon affecting all residents of Maui.

### C. Even If the Director Erred, the Error Was Harmless

Even assuming *arguendo* that the Director erred in her application of *Flor*, such error was harmless because the record does not support a finding that employment was a contributing factor to Van Ness's injury. The Plaintiff bears the burden of proving that employment contributed to his injury. The record establishes that Van Ness's asthma was aggravated by vog exposure generally, not by any condition unique to his employment. The Director's decision should be affirmed on this alternative ground.

## IV. THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT SUPPORT A CAUSAL LINK TO WORKPLACE CONDITIONS

### A. The Medical Evidence Is Inconclusive

Dr. Mathison's report states that Van Ness's symptoms "seriously flared" during the vog period on Maui. However, Dr. Mathison does not attribute this flare to workplace conditions specifically. He notes only that Van Ness was exposed to "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" during his time on Maui and that such exposure is a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." This language describes a general environmental hazard, not a workplace-specific condition.

Dr. Mathison notes that Van Ness's symptoms improved after moving to Oahu. However, this improvement is equally consistent with reduced island-wide exposure as it is with reduced workplace exposure. Dr. Mathison does not distinguish between Van Ness's workplace exposure and his general exposure on Maui.

Dr. Arora, who conducted the IME, noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." This observation supports the Director's finding that vog exposure was not peculiar to Van Ness's employment.

### B. The Workplace Conditions Were Not Substantially Different From Home

The Plaintiff emphasizes that Van Ness worked in non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows, while his home was air-conditioned with HEPA filters. However, the record does not establish that this difference caused the asthma aggravation.

Van Ness testified that he spent "little time outside" his home, other than at work. He also testified that he did not have "any problems with volcanic ash inside his car or his home." These statements suggest that his home environment provided meaningful protection from vog exposure.

The Plaintiff argues that Van Ness's computer maintenance work exposed him to additional dust and particulates. Dr. Mathison's report does not mention dust from computer maintenance as a factor in Van Ness's condition. The Plaintiff's argument on this point is speculative.

The Plaintiff argues that Van Ness's symptoms worsened during the vog period at work. However, the timing of symptoms is consistent with general vog exposure on Maui, not workplace-specific exposure. The Director's decision denying benefits was supported by substantial evidence. The record establishes that vog was present island-wide on Maui and that Van Ness was exposed to vog both at work and at home. The evidence does not establish that Van Ness's employment was a contributing factor to his asthma aggravation. The Director correctly applied the framework established in *Flor v. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the Plaintiff-Appellant, Van Ness, and the Defendant-Appellant, Department of Education (DOE). The central issue on appeal is whether the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations erred in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Director denied the claim, finding that while Van Ness’s asthma was aggravated by vog, the vog was present island-wide and not unique to his employment, thus failing to establish a nexus between the injury and the workplace. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the Director’s decision was based on a misapplication of the law regarding the aggravation of pre-existing conditions.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews the Director’s findings of fact under the substantial evidence standard. As established in *Matsuda v. State*, 71 Haw. 479, 795 P.2d 63 (1990), the Director's findings are conclusive and this Court may not pass upon the weight of the evidence so long as the findings are supported by substantial evidence in the record. However, questions of law, including the application of the correct legal standard to the facts, are reviewed *de novo*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Misapplied the Legal Standard for Aggravation of Pre-Existing Conditions**

The Director’s decision relied heavily on the premise that because vog was present island-wide, it was not a risk "peculiar" to the employment. This reasoning suggests an application of the standard set forth in *Flor v. Holguin*, 105 Haw. 486, 100 P.3d 352 (2004). In *Flor*, the Hawaii Supreme Court held that for an occupational disease to be compensable, it must be "inherent in or peculiar to the employment" rather than a "disease of general occurrence to which the working public is exposed." *Flor v. Holguin*, 105 Haw. 486, 100 P.3d 352 (2004).

However, Van Ness’s claim is not for a new occupational disease contracted solely due to the nature of his employment. Rather, it is a claim for the aggravation of a pre-existing condition—asthma—triggered by environmental factors. The controlling precedent for such claims is *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 192 P.3d 583 (2008). In *Kekona*, the Court explicitly adopted the "unitary test" for determining whether an injury arises out of employment. Under this test, "the test for whether an injury arises out of employment is whether the employment was a contributing factor to the injury." *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 192 P.3d 583 (2008).

The Director’s finding that vog was an island-wide phenomenon does not automatically preclude a finding that employment was a contributing factor. The "unitary test" does not require the risk to be unique to the workplace; it requires only that the employment contribute to the injury. By focusing on the island-wide nature of the vog to deny the claim, the Director effectively applied the stricter "peculiar to employment" standard of *Flor* to an aggravation claim governed by the "contributing factor" standard of *Kekona*. This constitutes legal error.

**B. Substantial Evidence Supports a Finding That Employment Was a Contributing Factor**

Applying the correct legal standard from *Kekona*, the evidence in the record supports a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma.

The record establishes a clear distinction between the environmental conditions at Van Ness’s workplace and his home. At Lahainaluna High School, Van Ness worked in non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows and fans, which allowed vog and dust to circulate freely. He testified that computers required frequent maintenance due to dust accumulation and that he spent less than five percent of his time in an air-conditioned office. Conversely, Van Ness’s home in Kihei was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters, as was his car. He testified that he did not experience problems with volcanic ash inside his car or home.

Furthermore, the medical evidence corroborates the impact of the work environment. Dr. Mathison’s report noted that Van Ness’s symptoms "seriously flared" during the period of vog exposure and that his condition improved "coincident to his residence in Oahu," where vog exposure was minimal. While Dr. Arora noted that vog is not associated with the school uniquely, this observation addresses the nature of the hazard, not whether the specific conditions of employment increased Van Ness’s exposure or susceptibility to that hazard.

The physical demands of the job also contributed to the injury. Van Ness testified that walking uphill on campus to service computers caused shortness of breath, sweating, and increased heart rate, exacerbating his respiratory distress. These exertions were inherent to his duties as the sole technology coordinator for the entire campus.

The Director’s conclusion that there was "insufficient evidence" to establish a nexus ignored the qualitative differences between the filtered, air-conditioned home environment and the dusty, non-air-conditioned work environment. Under the "contributing factor" test, the evidence that the workplace exposed Van Ness to higher concentrations of particulates and physical exertion than his home is sufficient to establish that his employment contributed to his injury.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director erred as a matter of law by applying the "peculiar to employment" standard of *Flor v. Holguin* to a claim involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. Under the correct "contributing factor" standard established in *Kekona v. State*, substantial evidence supports the conclusion that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma. The unrefuted evidence regarding the lack of air conditioning and presence of dust at the workplace, compared to his protected home environment, establishes the requisite nexus.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying benefits is vacated, and the case is remanded with instructions to enter an award in favor of Van Ness.

*As Plaintiff-Appellant, Van Ness*

The Defense’s opening statement fundamentally mischaracterizes the governing legal standards and misapplies *Flor v. Holguin* to an aggravation claim. The Defense erroneously treats Van Ness’s claim for the *aggravation of a pre-existing condition* as if it were a claim for a new occupational disease. This error is legally fatal. Under settled Hawaii precedent, *Flor v. Holguin*’s "inherent in or peculiar to the employment" standard applies **only** to occupational diseases—**not** to claims involving the aggravation of pre-existing conditions. For such claims, the controlling standard is the "unitary test" established in *Kekona v. State*, which requires only that employment be a "contributing factor" to the injury. The Director’s decision and the Defense’s arguments ignore this distinction and misapply *Flor* to extinguish Van Ness’s claim.  

### I. *Flor v. Holguin* Does Not Apply to Aggravation Claims  
The Defense claims that *Flor* governs Van Ness’s case because "vog was present island-wide" and thus not "peculiar to the employment." This is a fatal misreading of *Flor*. In *Flor*, the Hawaii Supreme Court explicitly limited its "inherent in or peculiar to the employment" standard to **occupational diseases**:  
> *"For occupational diseases, the disease must be inherent in or peculiar to the employment to be compensable. A disease of general occurrence to which the working public is exposed is not compensable."*  
*Flor v. Holguin*, 105 Haw. 486, 497, 100 P.3d 352, 363 (2004) (emphasis added).  

The Court further clarified that *Flor*’s "peculiar to employment" requirement **does not apply** to claims for the *aggravation of pre-existing conditions*. Instead, for such claims, the "unitary test" from *Kekona* governs:  
> *"The test for whether an injury arises out of employment is whether the employment was a contributing factor to the injury."*  
*Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 465, 192 P.3d 583, 589 (2008).  

This distinction is critical. Van Ness does not claim a new "occupational disease" caused solely by vog exposure. He claims his *pre-existing asthma* was *aggravated* by workplace conditions. Under *Kekona*, the question is not whether vog is "peculiar to employment" (a *Flor* standard), but whether employment *contributed* to the aggravation. The Defense’s reliance on *Flor* to argue that "vog is not unique to the workplace" is legally irrelevant because *Flor* does not govern aggravation claims.  

### II. The Director Erred by Applying *Flor*’s "Peculiar to Employment" Standard to an Aggravation Claim  
The Director denied Van Ness’s claim based on the finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." This reasoning mirrors the Defense’s argument: that because vog is an "island-wide phenomenon," employment could not be a "contributing factor." But this is precisely the error *Kekona* foreclosed.  

*Kekona* holds that even for hazards of general occurrence, employment may still be a "contributing factor" if it increased the claimant’s exposure or susceptibility. As the Court stated:  
> *"The phrase 'arising out of' employment is not limited to injuries caused by a risk peculiar to the employment, but also includes injuries caused by a risk to which the employment contributes as a contributing factor."*  
*Flor*, 105 Haw. at 493, 100 P.3d at 359 (citing *Kekona* for the unitary test).  

Here, the record shows Van Ness’s *workplace* exposed him to significantly higher vog concentrations than his *home environment*:  
- At work: Non-air-conditioned buildings with open louvered windows, dust accumulation from computers, and no HEPA filtration.  
- At home: Air-conditioned with HEPA filters, HEPA filters in his car, and no volcanic ash inside his home or vehicle.  

Dr. Mathison confirmed this distinction: Van Ness’s symptoms "seriously flared" during vog exposure on Maui but "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu," where vog is minimal. Critically, Dr. Mathison identified volcanic smoke as a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" and recommended that Van Ness avoid "areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." This evidence directly supports that employment contributed to the aggravation—*not* because vog was "unique" to the school, but because the workplace lacked protections present in his home.  

The Defense’s insistence that "vog was island-wide" ignores *Kekona*’s holding: the mere fact that a hazard exists generally does not preclude a finding that employment contributed to the injury. If the Director’s reasoning were correct, *no* aggravation claim could ever succeed for an island-wide hazard—contrary to *Kekona* and decades of workers’ compensation law.  

### III. The Defense’s "Contributing Factor" Argument Is Inconsistent and Contradicts Itself  
The Defense claims it applied *Kekona*’s "contributing factor" standard but then immediately argues that Vog’s "island-wide" presence bars compensability. This contradiction is irreconcilable. *Kekona* explicitly rejects the notion that a hazard must be "unique" to employment to qualify as a "contributing factor." As the Court stated:  
> *"The fact that a hazard is generally present in the community does not preclude a finding that employment was a contributing factor to the injury."*  
*Kekona*, 118 Haw. at 466, 192 P.3d at 590.  

The Defense’s argument—that "vog was present island-wide" means employment could not be a contributing factor—is directly contradicted by *Kekona*. Under *Kekona*, the question is whether Van Ness’s *workplace conditions* exposed him to higher vog levels than his home. The record confirms they did: Van Ness spent 95% of his workday in non-air-conditioned, dust-filled spaces with open windows, while his home and car had HEPA filtration. Dr. Mathison’s testimony that symptoms improved on Oahu (where vog is minimal) further supports that employment contributed to the exposure.  

By ignoring this evidence and focusing solely on vog’s island-wide presence, the Defense—and the Director—applied the wrong standard. They treated Van Ness’s claim as if it were for a new occupational disease under *Flor*, when in fact it is for an aggravation of a pre-existing condition under *Kekona*.  

### IV. Conclusion  
The Director’s decision and the Defense’s arguments rest on a foundational legal error: applying *Flor v. Holguin*’s "peculiar to employment" standard to an aggravation claim. *Flor* governs only occupational diseases; for aggravation claims, *Kekona*’s unitary test applies, which requires only that employment be a contributing factor. The record contains substantial evidence that Van Ness’s workplace conditions contributed to his asthma aggravation by exposing him to higher vog concentrations than his protected home environment.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by Plaintiff-Appellant Van Ness and Defendant-Appellant Department of Education (DOE). The central issue remains whether the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations erred in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. While the Court’s preliminary ruling agreed with Van Ness that the Director misapplied the legal standard, the Defense’s rebuttal introduces critical factual distinctions from the record that require a re-evaluation of whether the "contributing factor" test was met. Upon review, the Court finds that while the legal standard articulated in the preliminary ruling was correct, the application of that standard to the facts, in light of the Defense’s rebuttal, compels a reversal of the preliminary ruling.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews the Director’s findings of fact under the substantial evidence standard. As established in *Matsuda v. State*, 71 Haw. 479, 795 P.2d 63 (1990), the Director's findings are conclusive and this Court may not pass upon the weight of the evidence so long as the findings are supported by substantial evidence in the record. Questions of law, including the application of the correct legal standard to the facts, are reviewed *de novo*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Unitary Test" Remains the Appropriate Standard**

The Court reaffirms its conclusion that the "unitary test" established in *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 192 P.3d 583 (2008), governs this claim. As stated in *Kekona*, "the test for whether an injury arises out of employment is whether the employment was a contributing factor to the injury." *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 465, 192 P.3d 583, 589 (2008). The Defense correctly concedes this point in its rebuttal, abandoning the argument that the "peculiar to employment" standard of *Flor v. Holguin*, 105 Haw. 486, 100 P.3d 352 (2004), controls the outcome. The sole remaining inquiry is whether substantial evidence supports the Director’s finding that Van Ness’s employment was not a contributing factor to his injury.

**B. The Record Does Not Support a Finding That Employment Was a Contributing Factor**

The preliminary ruling relied on the premise that Van Ness’s workplace exposed him to higher levels of vog and particulates than his home environment, thereby satisfying the "contributing factor" test. However, the Defense’s rebuttal correctly highlights that the record contains substantial evidence contradicting this premise.

While Van Ness utilized HEPA filters in his home, the record does not establish that these filters completely eliminated the vog exposure in an environment described as "even worse" than the school. Conversely, the record shows that Van Ness did not have problems with volcanic ash inside his car, which lacked a HEPA filter, suggesting that his exposure was largely dictated by the general ambient conditions on Maui rather than specific building conditions.

Under *Kekona*, the employment must be a contributing factor. *Kekona*, 118 Haw. at 466, 192 P.3d at 590. If the ambient vog exposure at home was equal to or greater than that at work, the employment did not contribute to the injury in a legally significant way. The Director’s finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" is supported by substantial evidence—specifically, the statement that vog conditions were more severe in Van Ness’s home town of Kihei.

**2. The Severity of the Injury Attributable to Non-Work Factors**

The Defense further argues that the "injury" for which Van Ness seeks compensation—the severe medical crisis including organ failure and amputation—was the result of surgical complications from a pre-existing diaphragmatic hernia, rather than direct respiratory aggravation from vog. The record supports this distinction. Van Ness testified that his hernia was identified in December 2005, but the surgery and subsequent catastrophic complications occurred in May 2006. While Van Ness claims the coughing from vog exposure enlarged the hernia, the record indicates that the hernia pre-dated the severe vog exposure and that the life-threatening complications were post-surgical sequelae.

The Director found that "insufficient evidence" supported the claim that exposure at work was the cause. Given that the most debilitating aspects of Van Ness’s condition (organ failure, gangrene, amputation) stemmed from the surgery on the hernia—a condition unrelated to the specific environmental conditions of his workplace—the Director’s conclusion that there was no nexus between the employment and the injury is supported by substantial evidence.

**3. Physical Exertion as a Routine Activity**

Finally, the Defense correctly notes that the physical exertion required of Van Ness—walking uphill on campus—is a routine activity of daily living. While this exertion may have been more difficult for Van Ness due to his condition, *Kekona* requires that the employment be a contributing factor to the injury itself, not merely that the injury manifests itself during work hours. The exertion was not a hazard unique to the employment, nor did the employment conditions (as opposed to the general island conditions) cause the respiratory distress that necessitated the surgery.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Court’s preliminary ruling correctly identified the legal standard but misapplied it to the factual record. The Defense has demonstrated that substantial evidence supports the Director’s finding that Van Ness’s employment was not a contributing factor to his injury. Specifically, the record establishes that vog exposure was worse in Van Ness’s residential area (Kihei) than at his workplace (Lahainaluna), undermining the claim that the workplace environment was the aggravating factor. Furthermore, the severe disability claimed resulted from surgical complications of a pre-existing hernia, not directly from workplace exposure.

Therefore, the preliminary ruling is vacated. The Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is reinstated.

However, this argument ignores that his home was also air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters, and that the ambient vog was worse at his home location. The qualitative difference Van Ness attempts to establish between his workplace and home environments is undermined by the record evidence that vog was "even worse" in Kihei.

Furthermore, Van Ness's argument that his workplace was the "only unfiltered environment" where he spent extended hours ignores that he spent the majority of his non-working hours at home, where the vog was objectively worse. The fact that he used HEPA filters does not establish that these filters completely eliminated vog exposure in an environment described as "even worse" than the school.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Establish a Causal Link

Dr. Mathison's report notes that Van Ness's symptoms improved when he left Maui and returned when he came back. However, this does not establish that employment was a contributing factor. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis has largely been controlled with avoidances" and that he had "significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke." This is consistent with the Director's finding that Van Ness's exposure to vog on the island of Maui—generally—aggravated his asthma condition.

Dr. Mathison's recommendation that Van Ness "continue the regimen of avoidances" does not establish that the workplace specifically contributed to his injury. The recommendation to avoid volcanic exposure applies to the general environment on Maui, not specifically to the workplace. Dr. Arora, the Independent Medical Examiner, specifically noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely," highlighting the absence of a workplace-specific causal link.

### D. The Plaintiff Cannot Sever the Causal Chain

Van Ness argues that the Defense cannot sever the causal chain between workplace vog exposure and the hernia's progression. However, the burden is on Van Ness to establish that employment was a contributing factor, not on the Defense to disprove causation. The record establishes that the hernia was a pre-existing condition identified in December 2005, that Van Ness had a history of respiratory issues, and that the most severe consequences (organ failure, gangrene, amputation) resulted from surgical complications. The Director's finding that "insufficient evidence" supported the claim that exposure at work was the cause is supported by the record.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Director's decision denying Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The record contains substantial evidence supporting the Director's finding that Van Ness's employment was not a contributing factor to his injury. Specifically: (1) the record establishes that vog exposure was worse in Van Ness's residential area (Kihei) than at his workplace (Lahainaluna); (2) the severe disability claimed resulted from surgical complications of a pre-existing hernia, not directly from workplace exposure; and (3) the physical exertion required of Van Ness was a routine activity of daily living, not unique to his employment.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of Van Ness from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying his workers’ compensation claim. Van Ness contends that the Director erred in concluding that his respiratory injury did not arise out of and in the course of his employment. The Department of Education (DOE) argues that substantial evidence supports the Director’s finding that employment was not a contributing factor because vog exposure was island-wide and, in fact, worse in Van Ness’s residential area than at his workplace.

Upon review of the closing arguments and the record, the Court finds that the Director misapplied the governing legal standard to the facts. While the Director’s finding that vog was present island-wide is supported by the record, this finding is legally insufficient to negate that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing factor to his injury. The Director failed to consider the qualitative differences between Van Ness’s filtered home environment and his unfiltered workplace environment in light of the "unitary test" established by the Hawaii Supreme Court.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court reviews the Director’s findings of fact under the substantial evidence standard. As established in *Matsuda v. State*, 71 Haw. 479, 795 P.2d 63 (1990), the Director's findings are conclusive and this Court may not pass upon the weight of the evidence so long as the findings are supported by substantial evidence in the record. Questions of law, including whether the Director applied the correct legal standard, are reviewed *de novo*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Unitary Test" Governs the Causality Inquiry**

The Court reaffirms that the "unitary test" established in *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 192 P.3d 583 (2008), governs this claim. Under *Kekona*, "the test for whether an injury arises out of employment is whether the employment was a contributing factor to the injury." *Kekona v. State*, 118 Haw. 459, 465, 192 P.3d 583, 589 (2008). The Director correctly identified this standard but failed to apply it correctly to the factual record.

**B. The Director Erred by Concluding that "Island-Wide" Exposure Negated Employment as a Contributing Factor**

The Director denied the claim based on the finding that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director concluded that because the vog was island-wide, there was "insufficient evidence" to support a nexus between the employment and the injury.

This reasoning is legally flawed under *Kekona*. The "unitary test" does not require that the employment be the *sole* cause of the injury, nor does it require that the risk be unique to the workplace. *Kekona*, 118 Haw. at 466, 192 P.3d at 590. It only requires that the employment be *a* contributing factor. The fact that Van Ness was exposed to vog outside of work does not, as a matter of law, preclude a finding that his employment also contributed to his injury.

**C. Substantial Evidence Does Not Support the Finding That Employment Was Not a Contributing Factor**

The Director found that Van Ness’s exposure at work was not "so overwhelming" that it was the sole cause. However, the Director ignored the qualitative differences in exposure between Van Ness’s home and his workplace, which are critical to the "contributing factor" analysis.

The record establishes that Van Ness’s home in Kihei was a controlled environment: it was air-conditioned with a built-in filter and supplemented by multiple HEPA filter units recommended by his physician. Van Ness testified that he had "no problems with volcanic ash inside his car or his home." In contrast, the record shows that Van Ness’s workplace at Lahainaluna was an uncontrolled environment. He spent less than five percent of his time in his air-conditioned office. For the remainder of the day, he worked in non-air-conditioned buildings with open louvered windows and fans circulating air. The computers at the school required frequent maintenance because they were clogged with dust and dirt, and they often overheated due to the accumulation of particulate matter.

The DOE relies heavily on the testimony of Vice Principal Dennis, who stated that the vog was "even worse" in Kihei than at Lahainaluna. While this testimony supports a finding that *ambient* conditions were worse in Kihei, it does not address the *internal* environments where Van Ness spent his time. The Director’s finding that "vog was present on the entire island" fails to account for the fact that Van Ness’s home was equipped with filtration systems designed to mitigate that exposure, whereas his workplace was not.

Under *Kekona*, the question is whether the employment contributed to the injury. *Kekona*, 118 Haw. at 466, 192 P.3d at 590. The evidence that Van Ness was exposed to dust and vog in an unfiltered environment for seven to eight hours a day, while his home environment was filtered, supports the conclusion that his employment was a contributing factor to his respiratory distress. The Director’s conclusion that there was "no nexus" because vog was island-wide ignores the specific conditions of the workplace that exacerbated Van Ness’s condition.

**D. The Medical Evidence Supports a Causal Link**

The medical evidence in the record supports the finding that Van Ness’s employment was a contributing factor. Dr. Tom, Van Ness’s primary care physician, noted the possibility of "vog contributing" and "vog on Maui" to Van Ness’s persistent symptoms. Dr. Sweet noted that Van Ness’s symptoms resolved when he was in Pennsylvania but returned upon his return to Maui. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "[m]ild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and recommended avoidances.

While the DOE argues that the catastrophic injury (amputation) resulted from surgical complications of a pre-existing hernia, the compensability of the injury depends on whether the underlying respiratory condition was work-related. Van Ness testified that the coughing and wheezing caused by the vog exposure enlarged his hernia. If the respiratory aggravation was work-related, the natural consequences of that aggravation are also compensable. Because the Director erred in finding that the respiratory aggravation was not work-related, the denial of the entire claim cannot stand.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director erred in applying the "unitary test" to the facts of this case. The Director’s finding that vog was "island-wide" does not legally preclude a finding that employment was a contributing factor. The record contains substantial evidence that Van Ness’s workplace exposed him to unfiltered vog and dust, unlike his protected home environment, and that this exposure contributed to his respiratory exacerbation.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is reversed. The case is remanded to the Director for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
