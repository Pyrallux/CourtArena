## I. THE IMPROPER TESTIMONY BY OFFICER SHUGRUE CONSTITUTES REVERSIBLE ERROR

### A. The Prosecution Concedes the Error

The prosecution graciously concedes that Officer Shugrue's testimony "contained an improper comment prejudicial to the Defendant." This Court likewise acknowledged that "we cannot in good conscience condone such conduct from officers who know or should know better." The Defense acknowledges this concession and proceeds to address the harmless error analysis.

### B. The Prosecution's Harmless Error Argument Fails

The prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. at 24. The Florida Supreme Court in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d at 1132, emphasized that the State must demonstrate "beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict." This Court must examine the error "viewed against the totality of the evidence." *Id.*

The prosecution's argument rests upon the assertion that the evidence of guilt was "overwhelming." However, this characterization merits close scrutiny. The evidence upon which the prosecution relies consists of two components: (1) a breathalyzer reading of .12, and (2) the Defendant's refusal to perform field sobriety tests.

### C. The Breathalyzer Reading Alone Is Insufficient to Establish Harmlessness

The Defense does not dispute that a blood alcohol reading of .12 exceeds the .10 statutory threshold under Florida Statute § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). However, the mere existence of a numerical reading does not automatically render every associated error harmless. The breathalyzer reading itself was obtained under circumstances that form the basis of the second appellate issue—the State's alleged failure to produce monthly inspection reports.

The prosecution cannot simultaneously rely upon the breathalyzer reading as conclusive evidence of guilt while simultaneously arguing that the Defendant was not entitled to inspect the very records that would challenge the reliability of that reading. This circular reasoning undermines the prosecution's harmless error argument.

### D. The Prosecution Cannot Rely Upon the Conduct That Forms the Basis of the Discovery Violation

The prosecution places significant weight upon the Defendant's refusal to perform field sobriety tests. However, the prosecution's reliance upon this evidence is problematic given the context in which Officer Shugrue made his improper comment.

Officer Shugrue volunteered information about the Defendant's refusal to answer questions in response to defense counsel's inquiry about the Defendant's sleep the night preceding the arrest. This improper comment—regarding the Defendant's exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent—directly relates to the same conduct (the Defendant's exercise of his right to refuse certain tests) that the prosecution now cites as evidence of guilt.

The prosecution cannot bootstrap the Defendant's exercise of constitutional rights into proof of guilt while simultaneously arguing that comments about the exercise of those rights were harmless. The improper comment explicitly drew the jury's attention to the Defendant's silence, and the prosecution now asks this Court to rely upon that same silence as proof of guilt.

### E. The Lower Appellate Court Applied the Wrong Standard

This Court previously concluded that "we doubt that the comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury to return a verdict of guilty." This formulation improperly shifts the burden to the Defendant. Under *Chapman* and *DiGuilio*, the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did *not* contribute to the verdict—not merely that the Court doubts that it did.

The lower appellate court's doubt-based standard falls far short of the prosecution's constitutional burden. This Court should find that the prosecution has failed to carry its burden of proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt.

---

## II. THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION REQUIRES REVERSAL

### A. The State Violated Discovery Rules

The Appellant contends that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer's monthly inspection reports. The lower appellate court found no violation, reasoning that "these rules do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facie case."

This reasoning is fundamentally flawed. The rules governing discovery exist precisely to ensure fairness in criminal proceedings. While the lower appellate court correctly notes that the rules "help serve as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment," this observation does not address the timing of such challenges.

### B. The Defendant Was Entitled to Inspect the Reports Before Trial

The Defense's ability to effectively challenge the breathalyzer's reliability depends upon access to the inspection reports. The State's failure to disclose these reports prior to trial deprived the Defendant of the opportunity to prepare a meaningful challenge to the equipment's condition.

The lower appellate court's reasoning—that the State need only produce documentation "upon such challenge"—creates an impossible Catch-22. A defendant cannot meaningfully challenge the condition of equipment without first knowing what inspections were performed and what their results revealed. The State's obligation to disclose exists independent of any challenge by the defense.

### C. The Error Was Not Harmless

Even assuming *arguendo* that this Court applies a harmless error analysis to the discovery violation, the prosecution cannot meet its burden. The inspection reports could have revealed calibration errors, maintenance failures, or other deficiencies that would have undermined the reliability of the .12 breathalyzer reading. Without access to these reports, the Defense was unable to develop this line of questioning or present it to the jury.

The prosecution cannot prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the undisclosed inspection reports would have confirmed the breathalyzer's accuracy. The violation therefore cannot be deemed harmless.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**ON APPEAL**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND BACKGROUND**

This case is before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) the trial court erred in admitting certain testimony by Officer Shugrue regarding the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions; (2) the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports; and (3) the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third point. Accordingly, the cause is remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing court costs and for appropriate proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE IMPROPER TESTIMONY**

The first issue on appeal concerns Officer Shugrue’s testimony, where he commented on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions regarding sleep. The State concedes this comment was improper and prejudicial. The central question is whether this error requires reversal.

The standard for reviewing constitutional error is established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967), which holds that a federal constitutional error is harmless if the State proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. This standard was adopted by the Florida Supreme Court in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986). In *State v. DiGuilio*, the court emphasized that the State bears the burden of demonstrating harmlessness, and the analysis must be conducted "viewed against the totality of the evidence." 491 So.2d at 1135.

The Defense argues that the lower appellate court improperly applied a "doubt-based" standard, stating "we doubt that the comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury," rather than requiring the State to prove harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. While the Defense correctly identifies the rigorous burden placed on the State by *State v. DiGuilio*, the Court must look to the totality of the evidence to determine if the State has met that burden.

The record establishes that the Defendant’s blood alcohol content was measured at 0.12. Under Florida Statute § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding 0.10 establishes *prima facie* evidence that the Defendant was under the influence of alcohol. Furthermore, the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests. The combination of a breathalyzer reading significantly above the legal limit and the refusal to perform field sobriety tests constitutes substantial evidence of guilt.

The Defense argues that the State cannot rely on the breathalyzer reading because its reliability is compromised by the discovery violation, nor can it rely on the refusal to perform tests because the improper comment drew attention to the Defendant's silence. However, the improper comment specifically concerned the refusal to answer questions about sleep, whereas the evidence of refusal to perform *roadside tests* is distinct physical conduct. Regarding the breathalyzer, the admissibility and weight of the reading are separate from the discovery issue addressed below.

Viewing the error against the totality of the evidence, the Court finds that the State has met its burden under *Chapman* and *State v. DiGuilio*. The evidence of guilt was overwhelming, consisting of a statutory *prima facie* violation via the breathalyzer and corroborating behavior. No reasonable jury would have acquitted the Defendant absent the improper comment. Therefore, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The second issue involves the State’s failure to provide the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Appellant argues this violated discovery rules, depriving the Defense of the opportunity to challenge the machine's reliability.

The background information indicates that the State’s duty under § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules is to administer tests in accordance with those rules. The provided facts state that these rules "do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facia case." Rather, the documentation serves as a predicate for admissibility "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment."

The Defense characterizes this as a "Catch-22," arguing that a defendant cannot meaningfully challenge the equipment without first seeing the inspection reports. While this argument highlights a practical difficulty for the defense, the Court is bound by the interpretation of the rules provided in the case background. The background explicitly states that the duty to show documentation arises "upon such challenge." There is no indication in the provided facts that the Defense made a specific challenge to the condition of the equipment which the State failed to meet.

Because the rules do not require the State to prove the machinery's fitness for a *prima facie* case, the failure to produce the reports absent a specific challenge does not constitute a discovery violation requiring reversal. The Defense’s argument that the reports were necessary to challenge the reading is noted, but under the procedural posture described, the State fulfilled its duty for the *prima facie* case.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the improper testimony by Officer Shugrue was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt in light of the overwhelming evidence of guilt. Furthermore, the State did not commit a discovery violation requiring reversal under the administrative rules as applied to the *prima facie* case. The State’s concession on the issue of court costs is accepted.

**ORDERED**

1.  The judgment of the Trial Court is **AFFIRMED** as to the conviction.
2.  The cause is **REMANDED** to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing court costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**ON APPEAL**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND BACKGROUND**

This case is before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) the trial court erred in admitting certain testimony by Officer Shugrue regarding the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions; (2) the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports; and (3) the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third point. Accordingly, the cause is remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing court costs and for appropriate proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The Appellant argues that the State committed a discovery violation and denied the Defendant due process by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Defense characterizes the State's reliance on the administrative rules as creating a "Catch-22," wherein the Defendant cannot challenge the equipment's condition without first seeing the reports, yet the State is not required to produce the reports until a challenge is made. The Defense relies on *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 83 S. Ct. 1194, 10 L. Ed. 2d 215 (1963), to argue that the State withheld material evidence favorable to the defense.

The Court is bound by the specific administrative rules and facts provided in the record. The record indicates that the State’s duty is governed by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules. The provided facts explicitly state: "These rules do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facia case. Rather, these rules help serve as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment. Upon such challenge, there is a duty placed upon the State to show the documentation required by the rules."

The Defense argues that this procedural mechanism prevents the Defendant from making a specific challenge. However, the plain language of the rules places the trigger for the duty to produce documentation "upon such challenge." The rules distinguish between the requirements for a *prima facie* case and the predicate for admissibility upon challenge. Because the rules do not require the State to prove the machinery's fitness for a *prima facie* case, the inspection reports are not material to the State's burden of proof for that initial case. Under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 83 S. Ct. 1194, 10 L. Ed. 2d 215 (1963), the State is only required to disclose evidence that is material to guilt or punishment. Since the reports are not required to establish the *prima facie* case, they were not material to the State's burden at that stage. The failure to produce the reports absent a specific challenge to the equipment's condition does not constitute a discovery violation requiring reversal. The State complied with the procedural requirements as outlined in the administrative rules.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE IMPROPER TESTIMONY**

The Appellant contends that the lower appellate court erred in its harmless error analysis regarding Officer Shugrue’s improper testimony. Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions regarding sleep. The Defense argues that the lower court’s statement—"we doubt that the comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury to return a verdict of guilty"—constitutes a "doubt-based" standard rather than the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard required by *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986).

The Court agrees with the Defense that the lower court's language was imprecise. Under *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the State bears the burden of proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt. The use of the phrase "we doubt" suggests a speculative assessment rather than the rigorous finding of fact required by the *DiGuilio* standard. The State must prove that the error did not contribute to the verdict, not merely express doubt that it did.

However, the Court must determine whether this error in phrasing requires reversal. The standard for reviewing constitutional error is established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967). To determine if the error was harmless, the Court looks to the totality of the evidence.

The breathalyzer reading of .12 is admissible and valid evidence. Under Florida Statute § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding 0.10 establishes *prima facie* evidence that the Defendant was under the influence of alcohol. Furthermore, the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests.

The Defense argues that the improper comment regarding the refusal to answer questions about sleep "contaminated the jury's view" of the Defendant's refusal to perform physical roadside tests. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive. The refusal to answer a verbal query about sleep is distinct from the physical refusal to perform roadside sobriety exercises. The improper comment pertained to the former, while the evidence of guilt relies heavily on the latter and the statutory *prima facie* evidence of the breathalyzer result.

Viewing the totality of the evidence, the Court finds that the State has met its burden under *Chapman* and *State v. DiGuilio*, despite the lower court's imprecise language. The evidence of guilt was overwhelming, consisting of a breathalyzer reading significantly above the legal limit and the refusal to perform field sobriety tests. No reasonable jury would have acquitted the Defendant absent the improper comment regarding sleep. Therefore, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the State did not commit a discovery violation under the administrative rules provided. The "Catch-22" argument raised by the Defense, while highlighting a tension in the procedural mechanics, does not override the explicit text of the rules which govern the State's duty. Furthermore, while the lower court used imprecise language regarding the harmless error standard, the improper testimony by Officer Shugrue was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt in light of the overwhelming evidence of guilt. The State’s concession on the issue of court costs is accepted.

**ORDERED**

1.  The judgment of the Trial Court is **AFFIRMED** as to the conviction.
2.  The cause is **REMANDED** to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing court costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**SO ORDERED.**
