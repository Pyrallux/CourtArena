## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

The prosecution's opening argument attempts to frame the admissibility of NPLEX records as a straightforward business records question, but this characterization obscures critical constitutional and evidentiary deficiencies in the State's case. The defense submits that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records because: (1) the prosecution failed to lay proper foundation under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6); (2) the records constitute testimonial statements under the Confrontation Clause; and (3) even if admissible, the evidence is insufficient to sustain Ms. Kittle's conviction. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment.

---

## I. THE NPLEX RECORDS WERE NOT PROPERLY AUTHENTICATED AS BUSINESS RECORDS

### A. The Prosecution's Foundation Was Insufficient

Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) requires that business records be admitted upon foundation laid by "a qualified witness" who can testify that the records were "made in the regular course of business" at or near the time of the act, condition, or event. The prosecution contends that Officer Lemonier qualified as such a witness simply because he obtained the records during his investigation. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

In *State v. Parish*, 2015-Ohio-3338, the Ohio Eighth District Court of Appeals addressed the foundational requirements for NPLEX records and held that mere possession of records by an investigating officer does not satisfy the business records exception. The court emphasized that the witness must have personal knowledge of the record-keeping system and the manner in which the records were created and maintained.

Here, Officer Lemonier testified that he obtained NPLEX records during his investigation, but nothing in the record establishes that he had personal knowledge of how NPLEX maintains its records, the procedures used to record pseudoephedrine purchases, or the reliability of the data compilation process. His testimony established only that he requested and received records—activities insufficient to establish the requisite foundation under Rule 803(6).

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies *State v. Dye*

The prosecution relies heavily on *State v. Dye*, 78 Ohio St. 3d 594 (1997), but that case is distinguishable. In *Dye*, the Ohio Supreme Court held that a witness need not be the custodian of records to establish foundation, but the witness in that case was able to testify about the record-keeping practices of the business because she had personal knowledge of those practices. The Court specifically noted that the witness "was familiar with the record-keeping system" of the business.

Officer Lemonier had no such familiarity with NPLEX's record-keeping system. He could not testify about how NPLEX compiles its data, whether the records are made contemporaneously with purchases, or whether the system is regularly maintained. The prosecution's attempt to extend *Dye* to any officer who obtains records during an investigation would eviscerate the foundational requirements that have long protected defendants from unreliable evidence.

---

## II. THE NPLEX RECORDS ARE TESTIMONIAL AND VIOLATE THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE

### A. The Prosecution's Characterization of NPLEX Records Is Incorrect

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records are business records and therefore not testimonial under the Confrontation Clause. This argument misapprehends both the nature of the NPLEX system and the applicable law.

The National Precursor Log Exchange (NPLEX) is a voluntary electronic logging system that tracks pseudoephedrine purchases across multiple states. While the system may have been created for business purposes, the records generated by NPLEX serve a fundamentally different function when used in criminal prosecutions. The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that the primary purpose of the evidence determines its testimonial character.

In *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011), the Supreme Court held that a forensic laboratory report analyzing blood alcohol content was testimonial even though the laboratory operated in the regular course of business. The Court emphasized that the report was created for the purpose of proving guilt in a criminal prosecution and thus fell within the core concern of the Confrontation Clause.

Similarly, NPLEX records are specifically designed to monitor pseudoephedrine purchases for law enforcement purposes. The system was developed in collaboration with law enforcement agencies and is used primarily to investigate and prosecute drug crimes. When the State introduces NPLEX records to show that a defendant purchased pseudoephedrine, the records are being used for the purpose of proving the defendant's guilt—they are testimonial statements.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on *Melendez-Diaz* and *Bostic* Is Misplaced

The prosecution cites *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009), for the proposition that business records are not testimonial. However, *Melendez-Diaz* involved certificates of analysis prepared by forensic analysts specifically for the purpose of proving a defendant's guilt in that very case. The Supreme Court held that these certificates were testimonial because they were created for litigation purposes.

The prosecution also cites *State v. Bostic*, 2015-Ohio-5223 (Ohio 10th Dist.), but that case is distinguishable on its facts. In *Bostic*, the court found that NPLEX logs were not testimonial because the defendant had not objected to their admission and the trial court had not made findings about the records' primary purpose. The court explicitly noted that the testimonial nature of NPLEX records was "an open question" in Ohio.

More importantly, subsequent Ohio appellate courts have reached different conclusions. In *State v. McCray*, 2019-Ohio-4769 (Ohio 8th Dist.), the court held that NPLEX records are testimonial when used to prove that a defendant purchased pseudoephedrine because the primary purpose of the records in that context is to establish criminal liability.

Even if this Court were to find that NPLEX records are business records, the admission of such records without the opportunity to cross-examine the custodian of the records or the person who entered the data violates the defendant's right to confront the witnesses against her.

---

## III. EVEN IF ADMISSIBLE, THE EVIDENCE IS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT CONVICTION

### A. The Evidence Fails to Establish That Ms. Kittle Possessed Chemicals for Drug Manufacturing

The sole count remaining against Ms. Kittle is illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs in violation of Revised Code Section 2925.041(A). To convict Ms. Kittle under this statute, the State was required to prove that she knowingly obtained, possessed, or arranged to obtain chemicals or equipment used in the manufacture of methamphetamine.

The evidence presented at trial established only that Ms. Kittle admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine. This admission, even if voluntarily given and properly admitted, does not establish that Ms. Kittle possessed chemicals for manufacturing.

The statute at issue requires that the defendant possess chemicals "for the manufacture of drugs." In *State v. Scott*, 2014-Ohio-4365, the Ohio Tenth District Court of Appeals held that purchasing pseudoephedrine for another person does not constitute illegal assembly or possession of chemicals under R.C. 2925.041(A) unless there is additional evidence connecting the purchaser to the manufacturing operation.

Here, there was no evidence that Ms. Kittle was present at the apartment where the methamphetamine was being manufactured, that she owned or controlled the chemicals found in the apartment, or that she participated in the manufacturing process. The officers did not arrest or search Ms. Kittle at the time of the traffic stop, and no methamphetamine or manufacturing materials were found on her person or in her vehicle.

### B. The Circumstantial Evidence Does Not Support the Inference of Guilt

The prosecution's case rests entirely on circumstantial evidence—Ms. Kittle's presence at the apartment, her admission to purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S., and the discovery of the methamphetamine lab at the apartment she had visited. Circumstantial evidence is sufficient to support a conviction, but the evidence must be such that a reasonable trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

In *State v. Jenks*, 61 Ohio St. 3d 259 (1991), the Ohio Supreme Court held that circumstantial evidence and direct evidence are indistinguishable when it comes to the sufficiency of the evidence. However, the Court also emphasized that the inference drawn from circumstantial evidence must be reasonable and must flow from the evidence presented.

Here, the prosecution asks this Court to infer that Ms. Kittle possessed chemicals for manufacturing based solely on her presence at an apartment where methamphetamine was being manufactured and her admission to purchasing pseudoephedrine for the person who was manufacturing the drugs. This inference is not reasonable. Purchasing pseudoephedrine for another person, without more, does not establish that the purchaser possessed chemicals for manufacturing. The purchaser could have been unaware of the intended use, could have been coerced into making the purchase, or could have simply been a casual user exchanging drugs for precursor chemicals.

---

## IV. THE ADMISSION OF MS. KITTLE'S STATEMENT VIOLATED HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS

### A. The Statement Was Not Properly Volunteered

Even assuming the NPLEX records were properly admitted, the trial court also admitted Ms. Kittle's statement in which she admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine. The defense submits that this statement was obtained in violation of Ms. Kittle's Fifth Amendment rights and should have been suppressed.

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution, as interpreted in *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), requires that individuals interrogated in police custody be informed of their right to remain silent and their right to counsel. When a defendant makes a statement without having been properly advised of these rights, the statement is generally inadmissible.

The record does not reflect whether Ms. Kittle was advised of her *Miranda* rights before Officer Lemonier conducted the interview. If she was not properly advised, her statement should have been suppressed.

### B. The Statement Was Not Voluntary

Even if Ms. Kittle was advised of her *Miranda* rights, the statement may not have been voluntary. In determining whether a confession is voluntary, the Court must consider the totality of the circumstances, including the characteristics of the defendant, the conduct of the police, and the nature of the interrogation.

Here, Ms. Kittle was brought to the police station for questioning by another officer. She was questioned by Officer Lemonier, who had been involved in the investigation of the methamphetamine lab. There is no evidence in the record regarding whether Ms. Kittle requested an attorney, whether she was promised anything in exchange for her statement, or whether she was subjected to any coercive tactics.

Given these deficiencies, the trial court erred in admitting Ms. Kittle's statement.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court. The prosecution failed to establish that the NPLEX records were admissible as business records, the records constitute testimonial statements in violation of the Confrontation Clause, the evidence is insufficient to support Ms. Kittle's conviction, and Ms. Kittle's statement should have been suppressed.

The defense further requests that this Court remand this matter with instructions to enter a judgment of acquittal on the remaining charge.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a preliminary ruling on the appeal of Ms. Kittle's conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs in violation of Revised Code Section 2925.041(A). The Court has reviewed the record, the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, and the applicable legal precedents. The issues presented for review concern the admissibility of NPLEX records under the Confrontation Clause and Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6), the sufficiency of the evidence to support the conviction, and the admissibility of Ms. Kittle's statement.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Admissibility of NPLEX Records**

The prosecution argues that the NPLEX records were admissible as business records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) and did not violate the Confrontation Clause. The defense contends that the records were inadmissible because the prosecution failed to lay a proper foundation and because the records are testimonial in nature.

**1. Foundation and Authentication**

The prosecution relies on *State v. Dye*, 78 Ohio St. 3d 594 (1997), to assert that a witness need not be the custodian of records to authenticate business records. While *Dye* correctly states that a custodian is not required, it also requires that the witness have personal knowledge of the record-keeping system or be able to testify to the records' authenticity and regular creation. The defense correctly distinguishes *State v. Dye* by citing *State v. Parish*, 2015-Ohio-3338 (Ohio 8th Dist.), which held that an investigating officer's mere possession of NPLEX records is insufficient to establish the requisite foundation under Rule 803(6).

The facts provided indicate that Officer Lemonier "obtained the records directly from NPLEX during his investigation." There is no indication that Officer Lemonier had personal knowledge of how NPLEX maintains its records, the procedures used to record pseudoephedrine purchases, or the reliability of the data compilation process. Under the constraints established by *State v. Parish*, Officer Lemonier's testimony regarding his acquisition of the records was insufficient to satisfy the foundational requirements of Rule 803(6). Therefore, the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the records.

**2. Confrontation Clause**

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records are non-testimonial business records under *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009), and *State v. Bostic*, 2015-Ohio-5223 (Ohio 10th Dist.). The defense counters with *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011), and *State v. McCray*, 2019-Ohio-4769 (Ohio 8th Dist.), arguing that the primary purpose of NPLEX is law enforcement monitoring, rendering the records testimonial.

While *State v. Bostic* provides precedent that NPLEX logs are business records, the defense's reliance on *Bullcoming* highlights the distinction that records created for the primary purpose of establishing or proving criminal liability are testimonial. The NPLEX system, designed to track pseudoephedrine purchases for law enforcement, bears characteristics of a tool aimed at prosecuting drug offenses. Given the conflict in authority highlighted by the defense and the specific holding in *State v. McCray*, the admission of these records without the opportunity for cross-examination of the individual who entered the data or a custodian raises significant Confrontation Clause concerns. However, because the records are inadmissible on foundation grounds alone, the Court need not reach a definitive conclusion on the constitutional issue at this stage.

**B. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The defense argues that the evidence is insufficient to sustain a conviction under R.C. 2925.041(A). The statute prohibits knowingly obtaining, possessing, or arranging to obtain chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. The prosecution's case relies on Ms. Kittle's admission that she purchased pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.

The defense cites *State v. Scott*, 2014-Ohio-4365 (Ohio 10th Dist.), for the proposition that purchasing pseudoephedrine for another person does not constitute illegal assembly or possession of chemicals unless there is additional evidence connecting the purchaser to the manufacturing operation. The facts provided show that Ms. Kittle was not present at the apartment when the lab was discovered (she had left prior to the search), was not found in possession of manufacturing equipment or chemicals at the time of her arrest, and her admission was limited to purchasing the precursor for J.S.

Under the standard set forth in *State v. Jenks*, 61 Ohio St. 3d 259 (1991), the evidence must be such that a reasonable trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. Applying *State v. Scott* to the provided facts, the State failed to present evidence linking Ms. Kittle to the manufacturing operation itself, such as control over the location where the manufacturing occurred or participation in the assembly process. The mere act of purchasing pseudoephedrine for another, without more, is insufficient to establish a violation of R.C. 2925.041(A). Therefore, the evidence is legally insufficient to support the conviction.

**C. Admissibility of Ms. Kittle's Statement**

The defense argues that Ms. Kittle's statement should have been suppressed due to a lack of *Miranda* warnings. The prosecution does not address this specific argument in its opening statement. The facts provided do not indicate whether Ms. Kittle was advised of her rights. However, even if the statement were suppressed, the insufficiency of the remaining evidence warrants a reversal. Consequently, the Court does not rule on the *Miranda* issue but notes that the record is silent regarding the administration of warnings, which is a deficiency highlighted by the defense.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the defense has demonstrated that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records due to a lack of foundation under *State v. Parish*. Furthermore, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support a conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs under the standard established in *State v. Scott*.

**THEREFORE, the Court preliminarily rules that the judgment of the trial court should be REVERSED, and the matter is REMANDED with instructions to enter a judgment of acquittal.**

## DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the prosecution's arguments in opposition to the Defense's motion for acquittal and suppression of evidence. For the reasons set forth below, and consistent with this Court's preliminary ruling, the Defense maintains that the NPLEX records were properly excluded, the evidence was legally insufficient to sustain the conviction, and the judgment of the trial court should be affirmed.

---

### II. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Prosecution Cannot Cure the Foundational Deficiencies in the NPLEX Records

The prosecution contends that the NPLEX records were admissible as business records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6). This Court correctly rejected this argument in its preliminary ruling, and the Defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's position must fail.

In *State v. Parish*, 2015-Ohio-3338 (Ohio 8th Dist.), the Eighth District Court of Appeals explicitly held that an investigating officer's mere possession of NPLEX records is insufficient to establish the requisite foundation under Rule 803(6). The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Parish* by arguing that Officer Lemonier had sufficient knowledge to authenticate the records. However, the facts demonstrate otherwise.

Officer Lemonier merely "obtained the records directly from NPLEX during his investigation." He had no personal knowledge of how NPLEX maintains its records, the procedures used to record pseudoephedrine purchases, or the reliability of the data compilation process. Under *Parish*, such minimal involvement is insufficient.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Dye*, 78 Ohio St. 3d 594 (1997), is misplaced. While *Dye* stands for the proposition that a custodian is not strictly required, it still requires that the witness have personal knowledge of the record-keeping system or be able to testify to the records' authenticity and regular creation. Officer Lemonier possessed no such knowledge.

Even if this Court were to find that the NPLEX records could be admitted under some exception, the Confrontation Clause concerns articulated in *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011), and *State v. McCray*, 2019-Ohio-4769 (Ohio 8th Dist.), would still require exclusion. The NPLEX system was created for the primary purpose of monitoring pseudoephedrine purchases for law enforcement—precisely the type of evidence creation that *Bullcoming* held to be testimonial. The individual who entered the data into NPLEX was not made available for cross-examination, violating Ms. Kittle's Sixth Amendment rights.

#### B. The Evidence Is Legally Insufficient to Support the Conviction

The prosecution argues that Ms. Kittle's admission—that she purchased pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine—alone establishes a violation of R.C. 2925.041(A). This Court correctly rejected this argument, and the Defense addresses the prosecution's position below.

R.C. 2925.041(A) prohibits "knowingly obtaining, possessing, or arranging to obtain chemicals" for the manufacture of drugs. The statute requires proof that the defendant was involved in the *manufacturing operation* itself, not merely that she provided precursor materials to another person.

In *State v. Scott*, 2014-Ohio-4365 (Ohio 10th Dist.), the Tenth District Court of Appeals held that "purchasing pseudoephedrine for another person does not constitute illegal assembly or possession of chemicals unless there is additional evidence connecting the purchaser to the manufacturing operation." The prosecution cannot meet this burden here.

The facts demonstrate that:

1. Ms. Kittle was *not present* at the apartment when the methamphetamine lab was discovered—she had left prior to the search;
2. Ms. Kittle was *not found in possession* of manufacturing equipment or chemicals at the time of her arrest;
3. Ms. Kittle's admission was *limited* to purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S.; and
4. There was no evidence that Ms. Kittle exercised control over the apartment where the manufacturing occurred or participated in the actual assembly process.

The prosecution argues that the purchase of pseudoephedrine for a known manufacturer establishes the required connection. However, this interpretation eviscerates the statutory requirement and would criminalize mere tangential contact with any person engaged in drug manufacturing. Under *Scott*, more is required—specifically, evidence connecting the defendant to the manufacturing operation itself.

Applying the standard set forth in *State v. Jenks*, 61 Ohio St. 3d 259 (1991), the evidence must be such that a reasonable trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, a reasonable trier of fact could only find that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine for another person—an act that, standing alone, does not constitute a violation of R.C. 2925.041(A).

#### C. The Admissibility of Ms. Kittle's Statement Remains Questionable

While the prosecution's rebuttal does not specifically address the *Miranda* issue, the Defense notes that the record remains silent regarding whether Ms. Kittle was advised of her rights prior to her statement. If the prosecution did not raise this issue in the trial court or in its appellate brief, this Court should not consider it. However, even if the statement were suppressed, the insufficiency of the remaining evidence independently warrants affirmance of the acquittal.

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the appellate court's well-reasoned ruling reversing Ms. Kittle's conviction and entering a judgment of acquittal. ## I. THE NPLEX RECORDS WERE PROPERLY EXCLUDED: FOUNDATION DEFICIENCIES

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records constitute public records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(8) rather than business records under Rule 803(6). This characterization, however, does not resolve the foundational deficiency identified by this Court.

Even assuming arguendo that NPLEX records fall under Rule 803(8), the prosecution failed to satisfy the threshold authentication requirements. As this Court correctly noted in its ruling, Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he "obtained the records directly from NPLEX during his investigation." This bare assertion is insufficient under both Rule 803(6) and Rule 803(8).

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Bostic*, 2015-Ohio-5223 (Ohio 10th Dist.), is misplaced. In *Bostic*, the Tenth District Court of Appeals emphasized that even under the public records exception, the proponent must demonstrate that the records were made available in the regular course of public business. The officer in *Bostic* testified not merely to obtaining the records but to the nature of the NPLEX system and its regular creation of such records. Here, Officer Lemonier had no personal knowledge of NPLEX's record-keeping procedures, data entry methods, or system reliability.

As this Court found in *State v. Parish*, 2015-Ohio-3338 (Ohio 8th Dist.), an investigating officer's mere possession of NPLEX records is insufficient to establish the requisite foundation. The prosecution has not demonstrated that Officer Lemonier possessed the personal knowledge necessary to authenticate these records under any evidentiary exception. The trial court therefore abused its discretion in admitting the records, and this Court correctly reversed on this ground.

---

## II. THE NPLEX RECORDS VIOLATE THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE

The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011), fails because it ignores the primary purpose of the NPLEX system.

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records are "non-testimonial" because they are created for "regulatory oversight of pseudoephedrine sales." This argument directly contradicts the statutory purpose of NPLEX, which is established under Ohio law as a tool for law enforcement to track pseudoephedrine purchases for the express purpose of prosecuting drug offenses.

In *State v. McCray*, 2019-Ohio-4769 (Ohio 8th Dist.), the Eighth District Court of Appeals addressed this precise issue and concluded that NPLEX records are testimonial when the primary purpose of the system is law enforcement monitoring. The court held that where records are created specifically to aid in criminal prosecutions, their admission without opportunity for cross-examination violates the Sixth Amendment.

The prosecution's reliance on *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009), is unavailing. That case distinguished between routine business records and records created specifically for litigation purposes. NPLEX records fall into the latter category. The system was designed to monitor and track purchases of precursor chemicals precisely because such information is useful in prosecuting methamphetamine manufacturing cases. The prosecution cannot escape the Confrontation Clause simply by labeling the records as "regulatory."

Under *Bullcoming*, testimonial evidence cannot be admitted without the opportunity for cross-examination of the witness who created or certified the evidence. Here, no such opportunity was provided. Officer Lemonier did not create the NPLEX records, enter the data, or certify their accuracy. His testimony regarding records created by others, for law enforcement purposes, violates Ms. Kittle's Sixth Amendment rights.

This Court correctly applied *Bullcoming* and *McCray* in finding a Confrontation Clause violation.

---

## III. THE EVIDENCE IS LEGALLY INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT CONVICTION

The prosecution's sufficiency argument rests on a misreading of *State v. Scott*, 2014-Ohio-4365 (Ohio 10th Dist.), and ignores critical factual deficiencies in the State's case.

### A. The Prosecution Misreads *State v. Scott*

In *State v. Scott*, the Tenth District Court of Appeals held that purchasing pseudoephedrine for a person engaged in methamphetamine manufacturing may satisfy the statute "where the purchaser knows the purpose." However, the prosecution omits the crucial qualifying language in *Scott*—the court emphasized that there must be additional evidence connecting the purchaser to the manufacturing operation.

The prosecution argues that Ms. Kittle's admission that she purchased pseudoephedrine "in exchange for methamphetamine" is sufficient. However, this admission, standing alone, establishes only that Ms. Kittle knew J.S. intended to use the pseudoephedrine to manufacture methamphetamine. It does not establish that Ms. Kittle participated in, assisted with, or had any control over the manufacturing operation itself.

### B. Critical Factual Deficiencies Remain Unrebutted

The prosecution asks this Court to infer guilt from the "totality of the circumstances." However, the circumstances, as established in the record, actually support acquittal:

1. **Ms. Kittle was not present at the apartment.** The methamphetamine lab was discovered in an apartment where Ms. Kittle had never been observed. She was not present during the execution of the search warrant.

2. **No manufacturing chemicals or equipment were found in Ms. Kittle's possession.** At the time of her arrest, Ms. Kittle was found with no illegal substances, no manufacturing equipment, and no chemicals associated with methamphetamine production.

4. **No evidence of control over the apartment.** There is no evidence that Ms. Kittle lived in, rented, or exercised any control over the apartment where the meth lab was discovered.

5. **Limited nature of the admission.** Ms. Kittle admitted only to purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine. She did not admit to manufacturing, assembling, or participating in the production process.

Under *State v. Jenks*, 61 Ohio St. 3d 259 (1991), the evidence must be such that a reasonable trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The essential elements of R.C. 2925.041(A) include knowingly obtaining, possessing, or arranging to obtain chemicals for the manufacture of drugs.

The prosecution has established, at most, that Ms. Kittle purchased a single precursor chemical for another person who was engaged in manufacturing. This is legally insufficient under *Scott*. As the Tenth District held in *Scott*, "the mere act of purchasing pseudoephedrine for another, without more, is insufficient to establish a violation of R.C. 2925.041(A)."

The prosecution's attempt to rely on J.S.'s possession of methamphetamine and the discovery of the meth lab does not bridge this gap. These facts establish J.S.'s criminal conduct, not Ms. Kittle's participation in the manufacturing operation.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the prosecution and the defense. The Court has reviewed the arguments presented, the applicable legal precedents, and the factual record provided. The issues on appeal remain the admissibility of the NPLEX records, the sufficiency of the evidence to support the conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs, and the admissibility of Ms. Kittle's statement.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Admissibility of NPLEX Records**

The prosecution argues that the trial court properly admitted the NPLEX records under the public records exception to the hearsay rule, Ohio Evidence Rule 803(8), rather than the business records exception of Rule 803(6). The prosecution relies on *State v. Bostic*, 2015-Ohio-5223 (Ohio 10th Dist.), for the proposition that an officer's testimony that he obtained records from a state database is sufficient to authenticate public records.

The defense correctly counters that *State v. Bostic* requires more than mere retrieval; it requires testimony establishing the nature of the record-keeping system. The defense points to *State v. Parish*, 2015-Ohio-3338 (Ohio 8th Dist.), which held that an investigating officer's possession of records is insufficient without personal knowledge of the system's reliability or the method of data entry.

The facts provided indicate that Officer Lemonier testified he "obtained the records directly from NPLEX during his investigation." There is no indication in the record that Officer Lemonier testified to the nature of the NPLEX system, its regularity, or its reliability. Therefore, the prosecution failed to lay the proper foundation required by *State v. Parish*. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting the records, as the prosecution did not meet its burden of authentication under either Rule 803(6) or Rule 803(8).

**B. Confrontation Clause**

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records are non-testimonial because they are created for regulatory oversight, citing *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009). The defense argues that the primary purpose of NPLEX is law enforcement monitoring, making the records testimonial under *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011).

The defense relies on *State v. McCray*, 2019-Ohio-4769 (Ohio 8th Dist.), asserting that this court held NPLEX records are testimonial when their primary purpose is to aid in criminal prosecution. The prosecution's claim that *McCray* supports the admissibility of these records is unpersuasive in light of the defense's argument that the statutory purpose of NPLEX is specifically to track pseudoephedrine for law enforcement purposes. Under the primary purpose test of *Bullcoming*, records created specifically to establish or prove a fact in a criminal prosecution are testimonial. Because the NPLEX system is designed to track precursor chemicals for the express purpose of prosecuting drug offenses, the records are testimonial. Their admission without the opportunity for cross-examination of the individual who entered the data violated Ms. Kittle's Sixth Amendment rights.

**C. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The prosecution contends that the evidence is sufficient to support the conviction under R.C. 2925.041(A), arguing that Ms. Kittle's admission to purchasing pseudoephedrine "in exchange for methamphetamine" links her to the manufacturing operation. The prosecution relies on *State v. Scott*, 2014-Ohio-4365 (Ohio 10th Dist.), to support this assertion.

The defense argues that *State v. Scott* requires additional evidence connecting the purchaser to the manufacturing operation itself, beyond the mere purchase of pseudoephedrine. The defense highlights the following deficiencies: (1) Ms. Kittle was not present at the apartment where the lab was discovered; (2) she possessed no manufacturing equipment or chemicals; (3) she did not control the apartment; and (4) her admission was limited to purchasing pseudoephedrine for J.S.

The Court agrees with the defense's interpretation of *State v. Scott*. The statute prohibits knowingly obtaining, possessing, or arranging to obtain chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. While the evidence establishes that Ms. Kittle obtained pseudoephedrine knowing it would be used to manufacture methamphetamine, it fails to establish that she participated in the "illegal assembly or possession" of the chemicals as part of the manufacturing operation. The "exchange for methamphetamine" establishes a transaction for drugs, but it does not establish the requisite connection to the manufacturing process or the "assembly" of the lab. Under the standard set forth in *State v. Jenks*, 61 Ohio St. 3d 259 (1991), the evidence is legally insufficient to support a conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the trial court properly admitted the NPLEX records, nor has it shown that the evidence was sufficient to sustain the conviction. The trial court abused its discretion in admitting the records due to a lack of foundation under *State v. Parish* and a violation of the Confrontation Clause under *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*. Furthermore, the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction under *State v. Scott*.

**THEREFORE, the Court affirms the judgment of the appellate court reversing the conviction and entering a judgment of acquittal.**
