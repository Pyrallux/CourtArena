# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response to the prosecution's arguments and maintains that the judgment below should be reversed. Although the State concedes that Officer Shugrue's comment regarding the Defendant's exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent was improper, the prosecution's characterization of this error as "harmless beyond a reasonable doubt" fundamentally misapplies the governing standard and ignores the profound prejudice such comments inflict upon a defendant's constitutional rights. Furthermore, the prosecution's attempt to minimize the discovery violation concerning the breathalyzer inspection reports ignores the State's affirmative duty of disclosure under Florida law.

## I. The Fifth Amendment Violation Was Not Harmless

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

The prosecution correctly cites *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967), and *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), for the proposition that an error is harmless only if the reviewing court can conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. However, the prosecution misapplies this standard by focusing exclusively on the existence of other evidence while ignoring the qualitative nature of the prejudice inflicted by commenting on a defendant's silence.

As the United States Supreme Court established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967), prosecutorial comments on a defendant's silence violate the Fifth Amendment and the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of due process. Such comments are per se prejudicial because they penalize the defendant for exercising a constitutionally protected right. The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that comments upon a defendant's silence "are inherently prejudicial and are rarely, if ever, harmless." *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129, 1135 (Fla. 1986).

### B. The Prosecution's "Overwhelming Evidence" Argument Fails

The prosecution's primary contention—that the evidence of guilt was "overwhelming"—does not satisfy the *DiGuilio* standard. The existence of other evidence, even strong evidence, does not automatically render a constitutional violation harmless. The question is not whether sufficient evidence exists to support the verdict, but whether the improper comment "contributed" to the verdict in any manner.

The prosecution relies upon the Defendant's blood alcohol concentration of .12 percent and his refusal to perform roadside field sobriety tests. However, this evidence was not uncontroverted as the prosecution suggests. The Defense specifically challenged the admissibility and accuracy of the breathalyzer reading through the discovery violation issue. The Defendant's refusal to perform field sobriety exercises, while relevant, cannot be considered in isolation from the constitutional violation at issue, as such refusals may be exercising Fourth Amendment rights against unreasonable searches as well as Fifth Amendment rights.

Moreover, the prosecution's characterization of the evidence as "overwhelming" ignores the critical fact that the State bore the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The appellate court below acknowledged that "career officers find it necessary to support the prosecutor in proving the State's case" and that such conduct cannot be "condone[d]" even when it leads to loss of the case. This acknowledgment by the court demonstrates recognition that officer testimony carries significant weight with juries, making comments on silence particularly prejudicial.

### C. The Error Was Not Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The prosecution cannot demonstrate beyond a reasonable doubt that Officer Shugrue's comment did not influence the jury. The comment directly implicated the Defendant's exercise of his Fifth Amendment right, suggesting to the jury that his silence was evidence of guilt. This inference is precisely what the Fifth Amendment was designed to prevent.

In *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976), the Supreme Court held that the use of post-arrest silence for impeachment purposes violates the Due Process Clause. While *Doyle* specifically addressed impeachment, the principle extends to prosecutorial comments during the State's case-in-chief. The prejudice is compounded when such comments come from a law enforcement officer, whose testimony carries inherent credibility with jurors.

The prosecution's reliance on the appellate court's finding of harmless error is misplaced. The appellate court below applied an incorrect standard by asking merely whether the evidence was strong enough to support the verdict, rather than whether the State has proven beyond a reasonable doubt that the comment did not contribute to the verdict. The court stated it "doubt[ed] that the comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury," but doubt is insufficient under *Chapman* and *DiGuilio*. The State must affirmatively demonstrate the absence of any contribution to the verdict.

## II. The Discovery Violation Was Material

### A. The State's Duty of Disclosure

The prosecution argues that the State had no duty to disclose the breathalyzer inspection reports because the rules "do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facie case." This argument mischaracterizes the nature of the State's disclosure obligations under Florida law.

Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220 imposes upon the State an affirmative duty to disclose, upon request, "all documents, tangible objects, or photographs" within its possession or control that are material to the defense. The breathalyzer inspection reports fall squarely within this category. The defendant in any DUI case is entitled to challenge the accuracy and reliability of the testing equipment, and such challenge necessarily requires access to maintenance and inspection records.

The prosecution's argument that disclosure is required only "upon such challenge" fundamentally misunderstands the discovery process. The purpose of discovery is to enable the defendant to prepare his defense, not merely to respond to the State's case. A defendant cannot effectively challenge the breathalyzer's accuracy without first knowing what inspection records exist.

### B. The Prejudice to the Defense

The Defense was deprived of potentially exculpatory evidence regarding the condition and calibration of the breathalyzer machine. Without access to the monthly inspection reports, the Defense could not determine whether the machine was properly maintained, whether it was functioning correctly at the time of the defendant's test, or whether any irregularities existed in the testing process.

The prosecution's argument that the defendant must first "challenge the condition of the equipment" before the State must produce documentation puts the defendant in an impossible position. The defendant cannot make an informed challenge without first knowing what documentation exists. This circular reasoning eviscerates the discovery rules and deprives defendants of their due process rights.

## III. The Court Costs Issue

The State has conceded error on this point, and the Defense agrees with the appellate court's decision to remand for vacatur of the order taxing costs. However, this concession underscores the prosecution's overall weakness in this case and supports the Defense's position that the remaining errors require reversal.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this honorable Court reverse the judgment below and remand for a new trial. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate beyond a reasonable doubt that Officer Shugrue's improper comment on the defendant's Fifth Amendment right to remain silent did not contribute to the verdict. The discovery violation deprived the defendant of potentially exculpatory evidence. The cumulative effect of these errors denied the defendant a fair trial.

The Defense further requests that, in the event this Court affirms the conviction, it nonetheless reverse the order taxing costs and remand for appropriate proceedings consistent with the State's concession of error.

Respectfully submitted,

**DEFENSE**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Introduction**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the conviction of the Defendant for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s silence; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third point. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the case background, and the applicable legal standards.

**II. Analysis of the Fifth Amendment Violation**

The Defense argues that the trial court committed reversible error when Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. The State concedes that the comment was improper but argues the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

The legal standard for determining whether a constitutional error is harmless was established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967), and adopted by the Florida Supreme Court in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986). Under *DiGuilio*, the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The Defense correctly notes that comments on a defendant's silence are inherently prejudicial. See *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976).

However, the Court must look to the totality of the evidence presented at trial to determine if the State met this burden. The record indicates that the Defendant refused to perform roadside sobriety tests and provided a breathalyzer reading of .12. Under Section 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding .10 establishes prima facie evidence that the Defendant was under the influence of alcohol.

While the Defense argues that the evidence was not "uncontroverted" due to the discovery issue, the physical evidence of intoxication remains substantial. The Prosecution correctly argues that the scientific evidence of a .12 blood alcohol content provides overwhelming proof of impairment. The improper comment by Officer Shugrue, while unfortunate and constitutionally infirm, is outweighed by the direct physical evidence linking the Defendant to the crime. The State has satisfied its burden under *Chapman* and *DiGuilio* to show that the error did not contribute to the verdict. Therefore, the error is deemed harmless.

**III. Analysis of the Discovery Violation**

The Defense contends that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to provide the monthly inspection reports for the breathalyzer machine, citing Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220. The Defense argues that the State has an affirmative duty to disclose all material documents, and that the State's requirement that the Defendant first challenge the equipment creates an impossible Catch-22.

The Court must look to the specific statutory and administrative framework governing breathalyzer testing in Florida. The relevant administrative rule, § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules, dictates the procedures for administering breathalyzer tests. As noted in the case background, these rules do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to establish a prima facie case. Rather, the documentation serves as a predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment.

The Defense's reliance on Rule 3.220, while generally correct regarding the duty to disclose, cannot override the specific requirements of the administrative rules governing DUI proceedings. The State's duty to produce the inspection reports is triggered upon a challenge to the equipment's condition. Because the rules do not require the State to prove the machine's fitness as part of its prima facie case, the failure to produce the reports absent such a challenge does not constitute a discovery violation warranting reversal. The State's interpretation of the procedural requirements is consistent with the applicable administrative rules.

**IV. Court Costs**

The State has properly conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs on the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Due process requires that a defendant be given an opportunity to be heard before being adjudicated liable for court costs. Accordingly, the order taxing costs must be vacated.

**V. Conclusion**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the trial court's error regarding the officer's comment was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt in light of the overwhelming evidence of the Defendant's impairment. Furthermore, the State did not commit a discovery violation under the specific administrative rules governing breathalyzer testing. The State's concession on the issue of court costs is accepted.

**ORDERED AND ADJUDGED:**

1.  The judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED** as to the conviction.
2.  The cause is **REMANDED** to the Trial Court to vacate the Order Taxing Costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. Introduction**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by the Prosecution and the Defense. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third point. The Court has reviewed the arguments presented, the case background, and the applicable legal standards.

**II. Analysis of the Fifth Amendment Violation**

The Defense argues that the trial court committed reversible error when Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s silence, and that the State’s harmless error analysis is circular because it relies on the Defendant’s refusal to perform roadside tests—a form of silence—to prove the error was harmless. The Defense relies on *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976), to assert that such comments are inherently prejudicial.

The Court acknowledges the logical tension identified by the Defense. However, the Prosecution correctly distinguishes between the Defendant's silence and the independent physical evidence of guilt. The record establishes a breathalyzer reading of .12. Under Section 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding .10 establishes *prima facie* evidence that the Defendant was under the influence of alcohol. This reading constitutes objective, scientific evidence independent of the Defendant's silence or refusal to perform roadside sobriety exercises.

The standard for reviewing constitutional error is whether the State can prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict, as established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967), and adopted by the Florida Supreme Court in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986). The Prosecution has met this burden. The .12 reading is significantly above the legal limit and was the primary evidence of impairment. While the officer's comment was improper, it was a fleeting remark in the context of overwhelming scientific evidence. The State did not use the Defendant's silence to prove guilt, but rather used the breathalyzer result to demonstrate that the improper comment could not have affected the jury's verdict. Therefore, the error is deemed harmless.

**III. Analysis of the Discovery Violation**

The Defense contends that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to provide the monthly inspection reports for the breathalyzer machine, arguing that the administrative rules create a "Catch-22" where a defendant cannot challenge the equipment without first seeing the reports. The Defense cites Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220 to support a broad disclosure obligation.

The Court is bound by the specific statutory and administrative framework provided in the case background. The background facts state that the State's duty is governed by § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules. These rules "do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facia case." Rather, the documentation serves as a predicate for admissibility "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment." The background further indicates that "Upon such challenge, there is a duty placed upon the State to show the documentation required by the rules."

While the Defense argues that this procedure is fundamentally unfair, the Court must adhere to the explicit language of the rules as interpreted in the provided background. The background facts conclude that "For these reasons there was no discovery violation," implying that the threshold for triggering the duty under the administrative rules was not met or that the challenge was insufficient to require the documentation for a *prima facie* case. The broad discovery obligations of Rule 3.220 cannot override the specific procedural requirements of the administrative rules governing breathalyzer testing in this context. Therefore, the State did not commit a discovery violation, and the breathalyzer reading remains valid and unimpeached evidence.

**IV. Court Costs**

The State has properly conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs on the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Due process requires that a defendant be given an opportunity to be heard before being adjudicated liable for court costs. Accordingly, the order taxing costs must be vacated.

**V. Conclusion**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the trial court's error regarding the officer's comment was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt in light of the overwhelming evidence of the Defendant's impairment, specifically the .12 blood alcohol content. Furthermore, the State did not commit a discovery violation, as the administrative rules governing breathalyzer testing do not require the production of inspection reports to establish a *prima facie* case absent a specific challenge to the equipment's condition that triggers the duty. The State's concession on the issue of court costs is accepted.

**ORDERED AND ADJUDGED:**

1.  The judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED** as to the conviction.
2.  The cause is **REMANDED** to the Trial Court to vacate the Order Taxing Costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
