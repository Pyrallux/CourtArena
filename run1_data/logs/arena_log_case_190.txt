Your Honor, the Fourth Amendment is not a mere suggestion—it is the bedrock protection against unreasonable government intrusion. At its core, the Amendment requires that any search or seizure be justified by specific, objective facts. When an officer conducts a pat-down of a person’s outer clothing—a Terry frisk—it is not a routine or discretionary act. It is a significant invasion of personal security that demands more than a hunch or subjective suspicion. It requires reasonable suspicion, grounded in articulable facts, that the individual is armed and presently dangerous. Without that, the search is unconstitutional, and any evidence derived from it must be suppressed.

This principle was established in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), where the Supreme Court held that a pat-down is permissible only when an officer has reasonable suspicion that a person is "armed and presently dangerous." This standard is not satisfied by vague or generalized concerns. It demands specific, observable facts that would lead a reasonable officer to believe that the individual poses an immediate threat of violence. As the Court later clarified in *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009), the reasonable suspicion for a pat-down must be based on "specific and articulable facts," not merely a "bare suspicion" or "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion."

Now, let us examine the facts of this case. The officer himself admitted there was no reasonable basis to believe the defendant was armed or dangerous. He testified that he observed "no signs of intoxication or being under the influence of drugs" (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11), "no burnt or fresh marijuana" (Suppression Tr. 38:14-17), and "no furtive or suspicious movements by defendant during the encounter" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3). He further acknowledged that defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 34:8-35:13), with "no fear for [his] safety" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), and his weapon "was not drawn during the interaction" (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). Most critically, when asked directly whether there was "any other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity" beyond his disbelief of the defendant’s explanation for being lost, the officer agreed: "Aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there’s no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity; agreed?" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). 

The officer’s subjective belief that the defendant’s story about being lost was "suspicious" is irrelevant. The Fourth Amendment does not permit officers to rely on subjective impressions or "hunches" to justify a pat-down. *Terry* requires objective facts—such as visible weapons, aggressive behavior, or credible threats—that would cause a reasonable officer to fear for safety. Here, there were none. The defendant was stopped voluntarily (he pulled over without being signaled), and the officer had no basis to believe he was armed. Disbelieving a claim of being lost, without more, does not create reasonable suspicion of an imminent threat. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly held, "suspicion that a person may be engaged in criminal activity is not enough to justify a pat-down; the officer must have specific reason to believe the person is armed." *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 377 (1993).

In fact, the officer’s own actions confirm the absence of reasonable suspicion. He did not draw his weapon, did not express any safety concerns, and did not observe anything that would suggest the defendant was carrying a weapon. The officer’s testimony that he could "see plainly with his flashlight inside the front and rear compartments of the vehicle and observed no contraband or narcotics" (Suppression Tr. 31:12-15, 43:4-16) further underscores that there was no objective basis for concern about a weapon. A pat-down is not a fishing expedition—it is a narrowly tailored measure for officer safety. When an officer has no reason to believe a person is armed, the Fourth Amendment forbids such a search. 

This case is not about whether the officer had reasonable suspicion for a traffic stop or for questioning. It is about the pat-down itself. The officer conducted a physical search of the defendant’s person without any reasonable suspicion that he was armed. That is a clear violation of the Fourth Amendment. Under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), evidence obtained as a direct result of an unconstitutional search must be excluded. Here, the discovery of drugs in the vehicle was the fruit of that illegal pat-down. The court must suppress all evidence derived from this unlawful intrusion.

Your Honor, the Fourth Amendment is not a hurdle for law enforcement—it is the foundation of our liberty. When officers act without reasonable suspicion, they breach that foundation. In this case, the pat-down was unconstitutional because it lacked the required justification. The evidence obtained must be excluded.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Applicable Standard: Terry and Its Progeny

The Fourth Amendment permits a limited pat-down search for weapons when an officer has **reasonable suspicion** that a person is "armed and presently dangerous." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 24 (1968). This standard requires "specific, articulable facts" rather than a "bare suspicion." *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 327 (2009). However, reasonable suspicion is not a high bar—it requires less than probable cause but more than a hunch. *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989).

Critically, the **totality of the circumstances** must be considered. *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417 (1981). No single factor is dispositive; rather, the court must evaluate the cumulative effect of all observations.

### B. The Officer Had Reasonable Suspicion for the Pat-Down

The prosecution erroneously argues that the absence of any single indicator of danger precludes reasonable suspicion. This misstates the law. The question is not whether any one fact alone justified the search, but whether the **totality of circumstances** would cause a reasonable officer to be suspicious.

Here, the following circumstances, viewed cumulatively, provided reasonable suspicion:

1. **Late-Night Encounter**: The stop occurred at 3:42 a.m., a time the officer testified warranted heightened vigilance, particularly on weekends. (Suppression Tr. 26-27.)

2. **Discovery of Suspended License**: When the officer ran the license check, he discovered the license was suspended—a criminal offense. *Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106 (1977) (per curiam), established that once a lawful traffic stop reveals additional violations, the officer's authority to conduct safety-related inquiries expands.

3. **Deceptive Behavior**: Defendant asked the officer to re-explain license seizure procedures immediately after receiving a thorough explanation—a circumstance the officer reasonably interpreted as a ruse. While the trial court noted this might not constitute objective suspicion, it remains relevant to the totality. (Suppression Tr. 50:17-18, 51:17-20.)

4. **Unusual Consent Behavior**: Most significantly, defendant's immediate, overly cooperative gesture of placing his hands on the vehicle without prompting constituted unusual behavior that the officer, with fifteen years of experience, reasonably associated with concealment. The officer testified: "I find that unusual." (Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9.) This is precisely the type of articulable observation that distinguishes reasonable suspicion from a hunch.

The prosecution cites *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), for the proposition that "suspicion that a person may be engaged in criminal activity is not enough." However, in *Dickerson*, the Court found the pat-down unlawful because the officer **squeezed** the defendant's pocket and felt a lump that he recognized as cocaine—a palpation beyond the scope of a Terry frisk. Here, by contrast, the officer did not squeeze or manipulate; he conducted a standard pat-down for weapons. Moreover, the officer in *Dickerson* had no prior reasonable suspicion—the discovery occurred during an unlawful search. Here, the officer had multiple articulable facts before the pat-down began.

### C. The "Plain View" and Automobile Exceptions Provide Alternative Grounds

Even if this Court were to find the pat-down questionable (which the defense contends it does not), the discovery of drugs in the vehicle would still be admissible under the **automobile exception**. Once the officer lawfully approached the vehicle and obtained consent to search, the drugs were discovered in plain view within the passenger compartment and trunk. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991).

The officer asked to search the vehicle; defendant consented. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The consent was voluntary. The prosecution does not challenge the validity of the consent—it challenges only the preceding pat-down. Even assuming arguendo that the pat-down was improper, the consent to search the vehicle was an independent act that broke the causal chain. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963) (evidence is admissible if obtained from an independent source).

The prosecution emphasizes that the officer "observed no signs of intoxication," "no furtive movements," and "no contraband in plain view." While these facts are true, they represent only one side of the ledger. The prosecution ignores:

- The inconsistent story about being lost in a familiar area;

- The deceptive re-questioning about procedures;
- The unusual overly cooperative gesture when asked about weapons.

No single factor is dispositive, but the totality supports reasonable suspicion.

### B. The Prosecution Incorrectly Conflates "Subjective Suspicion" with "Reasonable Suspicion"

The prosecution argues that "subjective belief" is insufficient. This is correct—but the prosecution's argument fails because the officer articulated **objective facts** supporting his suspicion. The prosecution's own citation to *Arizona v. Johnson* supports the defense: the Court in *Johnson* upheld a pat-down where the officer had articulable facts (the defendant matched a gang database description, was in a high-crime area, and made nervous movements). 555 U.S. at 327-28.

Here, the officer similarly articulated specific observations: late-night encounter, unusual driving, inconsistent story, suspended license, deceptive behavior, and unusual consent mannerisms.

### C. The Prosecution Overstates the Scope of Terry

The prosecution argues that a pat-down requires fear of "immediate threat of violence." But Terry itself recognized that officers need not wait for a "bullet to be fired" before acting. 392 U.S. at 26. The standard is that the officer must have "reason to believe" the person is armed—nothing more.

The prosecution's argument would require the officer to observe a visible weapon before a pat-down—which would defeat the purpose of the protective search. The whole point of Terry is to allow officers to **protect themselves** by checking for weapons they cannot yet see.

Even if the pat-down were improper, the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was an independent source of evidence.

The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the search was unconstitutional. The evidence should be admitted.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has reviewed the record, the suppression hearing transcript, and the opening arguments presented by counsel. The central issue is whether the evidence discovered in the defendant’s vehicle must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional search or seizure. Specifically, the prosecution challenges the legality of the pat-down (Terry frisk) and argues the subsequent vehicle search is tainted. The defense argues the pat-down was justified by reasonable suspicion or, alternatively, that the search was consensual and independent of any prior illegality.

**I. The Pat-Down and Reasonable Suspicion**

The prosecution correctly identifies the legal standard governing a pat-down search. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a brief investigatory stop only if they have reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. Furthermore, a frisk of the suspect’s person is permitted only if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the suspect is "armed and presently dangerous." *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 326-27 (2009). This standard requires specific, objective facts; it cannot be satisfied by a mere hunch or subjective belief. *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 50-51 (1968).

The prosecution persuasively argues that the facts in this case do not support a finding of reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. The officer testified that he had "no fear for [his] safety" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), that his weapon was not drawn (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5), and that he observed "no furtive or suspicious movements" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25). The officer further admitted that aside from his disbelief of the defendant’s explanation for being lost, there was "no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). While the defense points to the "totality of the circumstances"—including the late hour, the defendant’s suspended license, and his "overly cooperative" demeanor—these factors, viewed cumulatively, do not objectively indicate the presence of a weapon. Suspicion of general criminal activity, such as drug trafficking, does not automatically justify a frisk for weapons. *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 373-74 (1993).

Therefore, the Court agrees with the prosecution that the officer lacked the specific reasonable suspicion required to justify a non-consensual pat-down under *Terry*. Had the defendant been seized at the time of the pat-down, it would likely have been unconstitutional.

**II. The "Free to Go" Doctrine and Consent**

However, the defense’s alternative argument—that the search was consensual and independent of any prior illegality—is dispositive. The Fourth Amendment is concerned with governmental seizures; a consensual encounter does not implicate the Fourth Amendment. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434-35 (1991).

The record clearly establishes that the officer concluded the traffic investigation and informed the defendant that he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12). The officer testified that he "told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty much — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). At this moment, any seizure related to the traffic violation or the suspended license had terminated.

It was only after the officer began to walk away that the defendant re-engaged the officer, asking again about the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). The officer then returned, asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). When the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant voluntarily placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2).

Because the defendant was unequivocally informed that he was free to leave, and he voluntarily chose to remain and re-initiate contact with the officer, the subsequent interaction was consensual. The defendant's offer to be searched constituted valid consent, untainted by the prior traffic stop. See *United States v. Dray*, 901 F.2d 857, 860 (10th Cir. 1990) (holding that where an officer tells a defendant he is free to go, the defendant's subsequent consent to search is voluntary). The prosecution’s argument that the evidence is the fruit of a poisonous tree fails because the "tree"—the seizure—had already ended. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963).

**CONCLUSION**

While the prosecution correctly notes that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk had the defendant remained seized, the defense has demonstrated that the search was conducted during a consensual encounter following the defendant being advised he was free to go. The defendant's voluntary re-engagement and offer to be searched provide an independent basis for the admission of the evidence. Accordingly, the motion to suppress is denied.

The Fourth Amendment permits a limited pat-down search *only* when an officer has "specific and articulable facts" that the suspect is "armed and presently dangerous." *Arizona v. Here, the officer admitted he had "no fear for [his] safety" (Suppression Tr. 32:2–4), his weapon was "not drawn" (Suppression Tr. 24:3–5), and he observed "no furtive or suspicious movements" (Suppression Tr. 31:22–25). The defense attempts to salvage the pat-down by invoking the "totality of circumstances," but its reliance on *United States v. The defense cites: (1) the late-hour encounter; (2) the defendant’s suspended license; (3) his "inconsistent" story about being lost; and (4) his "overly cooperative" gesture. The defense’s citation to *Pennsylvania v. Here, by contrast:

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the constitutional analysis and ignores the controlling precedent establishing that a consensual encounter following a lawful termination of a seizure is constitutionally permissible. While the prosecution correctly notes that the initial pat-down may have lacked reasonable suspicion, it fundamentally errs in arguing that this alleged deficiency taints the subsequent consensual search. The record establishes that the defendant was unequivocally told he was free to go, his ride was en route, and he voluntarily chose to re-engage the officer and consent to the search. The prosecution's fruit of the poisonous tree argument fails because the "tree"—the seizure—had already terminated before any consent was obtained. This Court should affirm the denial of the motion to suppress.

---

## I. THE CONSENSUAL ENCOUNTER DOCTRINE CONTROLS

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable seizures, not consensual encounters. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). When an officer informs a subject that he is free to leave, any subsequent interaction is presumptively consensual. United States v. Dray, 901 F.2d 857, 860 (10th Cir. 1990). The prosecution does not—and cannot—dispute that the officer told the defendant he was "free to go" on multiple occasions.

The record reflects that after completing the traffic investigation, the officer "told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty much — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." Suppression Tr. 21:5-12. This is the critical constitutional moment: the seizure ended. The defendant was not detained, not constrained, and free to leave.

It was only after this termination that the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer by asking about the license seizure form. Suppression Tr. 21:15-18. The officer then asked if he could "ask a question," and the defendant agreed. Suppression Tr. 21:22-25. When asked if he had anything that would concern the officer, the defendant "automatically put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.

This sequence is precisely what the Fourth Amendment permits. As the Supreme Court held in Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. at 434, "a seizure does not occur simply because a police officer approaches an individual and asks a few questions." Even more fundamentally, "when a police officer tells a suspect he is free to leave, the suspect's subsequent voluntary agreement to search is valid." United States v. Dray, 901 F.2d at 860.

---

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE ARGUMENT FAILS

The prosecution invokes Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), arguing that evidence must be suppressed as fruit of an illegal seizure. This argument fails because it assumes the continued existence of a seizure that had already terminated.

The poisonous tree doctrine applies only when the evidence is obtained by "exploitation of an illegal search." Wong Sun, 371 U.S. at 484. Here, there was no exploitation because the illegal search—if any—had concluded, and a new, independent consensual encounter began. The defendant was told he was free to go, he acknowledged this, and he voluntarily remained. He then initiated further conversation with the officer.

The prosecution's reliance on Nix v. Williams, 467 U.S. 431 (1984), is equally unavailing. The inevitable discovery doctrine applies when evidence would have been discovered through lawful means absent the illegal conduct. Here, the prosecution does not argue that the evidence would have been inevitably discovered through the consensual search; rather, it argues that the prior seizure somehow tainted the subsequent consent. But where the seizure has ended and the subject voluntarily consents, no taint exists. See United States v. Dray, 901 F.2d at 860 ("[W]here an officer tells a defendant he is free to go, the defendant's subsequent consent to search is voluntary.").

The prosecution's argument that "the defendant was not free to leave during the pat-down" is directly contradicted by the record. The pat-down occurred after the defendant had already been told he was free to go and after his girlfriend was en route to pick him up. The officer testified: "After I explained to her where the car was located, where he was located at, she said that she was going to come pick him up and I handed the phone back to defendant. I told him he was free to go." Suppression Tr. 21:5-8. The defendant was not in custody; he was waiting for a ride.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S ATTACK ON THE CONSENT IS UNSUPPORTED

The prosecution argues that the search "was not consensual because the defendant was not free to leave." This misstates the law and the facts.

A. *The Defendant Was Legally Free to Leave*

The test for whether a seizure occurred is whether "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. at 434 (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was free to go—not once, but twice. Suppression Tr. 19:8-10; Suppression Tr. 21:5-12. The defendant had his girlfriend coming to pick him up. He was not under arrest. He was not handcuffed. His vehicle was not blocked. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would have believed he was free to leave.

The prosecution cites no authority for the proposition that an officer's subjective intent to conduct a future search transforms a consensual encounter into a seizure. Such a rule would render every consensual search suspect, as officers invariably approach interactions with investigative objectives.

B. *The Consent Was Voluntary*

The prosecution does not argue that the defendant was coerced, threatened, or promised anything in exchange for his consent. The officer asked if he could "ask a question," and the defendant agreed. Suppression Tr. 21:22-25. When asked if he had anything that would concern the officer, the defendant "automatically" placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched. Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.

The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18. There is no evidence of duress, coercion, or intimidation. The defendant's voluntary offer to be searched is exactly the type of consensual encounter that the Fourth Amendment permits.

The prosecution's reliance on the defendant's "overly cooperative" demeanor is unavailing. In United States v. Dray, 901 F.2d at 860, the Tenth Circuit explicitly held that "a defendant's willingness to cooperate does not create reasonable suspicion of danger." The prosecution cannot bootstrap a seizure from the defendant's willingness to cooperate.

---

## IV. THE "AUTOMBILE EXCEPTION" PROVIDES ALTERNATIVE GROUND FOR ADMISSION

Even if this Court were to find some constitutional deficiency in the prior interaction—an issue the defense disputes—the search of the vehicle would still be valid under the automobile exception.

The automobile exception permits a warrantless search of a vehicle when an officer has probable cause. California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565, 580 (1991). Here, the officer testified that he suspected the defendant was "bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have." Suppression Tr. 22:3-5. This suspicion, combined with the defendant's inconsistent story about being lost, the late hour, and the fact that he was coming from Philadelphia (a known source city for narcotics), provided probable cause to search the vehicle.

The prosecution's argument that "the search was not consensual" conflates two distinct constitutional analyses. Even if the consent were found invalid, the automobile exception provides an independent basis for the search. The officer had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained contraband, and no warrant was required.

---

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON ARIZONA V. JOHNSON IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323 (2009), for the proposition that a frisk requires reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The defense does not dispute this standard. However, Johnson is inapposite because it addressed the validity of a frisk during an ongoing traffic stop—not the validity of a consensual search following a terminated encounter.

In Johnson, the Court held that an officer may frisk a driver during a lawful traffic stop if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the driver is armed and dangerous. 555 U.S. at 327. Here, by contrast, the traffic stop had concluded, the defendant was told he was free to go, and the subsequent search was consensual. Johnson says nothing about the constitutional viability of a consensual search following a lawful termination of a seizure.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution's rebuttal ignores the central constitutional holding of this case: when an officer tells a defendant he is free to go, the defendant's subsequent voluntary consent to search is valid. The defendant was not seized when he offered to be searched. He was waiting for a ride, he was free to leave, and he voluntarily chose to engage with the officer. The subsequent search was consensual and independent of any prior interaction.

The prosecution's fruit of the poisonous tree argument fails because the "tree" had already been severed. The evidence discovered in the vehicle was not the product of an unconstitutional seizure; it was the product of a consensual encounter that the Fourth Amendment permits.

**UPDATED RULING**

The motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court has carefully considered the rebuttals presented by both the prosecution and the defense. The prosecution correctly identifies that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a non-consensual *Terry* frisk. However, the defense successfully demonstrates that the search in question was not a *Terry* frisk, but rather a consensual search that occurred after the seizure had terminated. Therefore, the exclusionary rule does not apply.

**I. The Standard for a Terry Frisk**

The prosecution argues persuasively that the officer lacked the reasonable suspicion necessary to justify a *Terry* frisk. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a pat-down search only if they have reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is "armed and presently dangerous." *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 326-27 (2009). The record supports the prosecution's assertion that the officer lacked such suspicion. The officer testified that he had "no fear for [his] safety" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), his weapon was not drawn (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5), and he observed "no furtive or suspicious movements" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25). Furthermore, the officer admitted that aside from his subjective disbelief of the defendant's explanation for being lost, there was "no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). As the prosecution notes, a "totality of circumstances" consisting merely of a late hour, a suspended license, and an inconsistent story does not amount to reasonable suspicion that a suspect is armed. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 506 (1983) (plurality opinion); *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 50-51 (1968).

Accordingly, the Court agrees with the prosecution that had the officer attempted to conduct a non-consensual frisk based solely on these factors, it would have violated the Fourth Amendment.

**II. Termination of the Seizure and the Consensual Encounter**

However, the defense's argument that the encounter was consensual is dispositive. The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable seizures, not consensual encounters. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). A seizure occurs only if "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." *Michigan v. Chesternut*, 486 U.S. 567, 573 (1988).

The record clearly establishes that the officer terminated the seizure before the search took place. After completing the paperwork for the suspended license citation, the officer testified that he "told [defendant] he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty much — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This explicit statement, coupled with the officer's physical act of walking away, signaled the end of the detention. See *United States v. Dray*, 901 F.2d 857, 860 (10th Cir. 1990) (holding that where an officer tells a defendant he is free to go, the defendant's subsequent consent to search is voluntary).

The prosecution argues that the presence of rotating lights and a backup officer rendered the defendant's freedom illusory. However, the officer's unambiguous communication that the defendant was free to leave, combined with the fact that the defendant had a ride en route and was not being detained, distinguishes this case from those involving coercive show of authority. The defendant was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and the officer had disengaged. Under these circumstances, a reasonable person would have felt free to leave or terminate the conversation. *Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 435.

**III. Validity of the Consent**

The defense correctly argues that the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to go. The defendant initiated a new conversation regarding the license seizure form (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). It was only after this re-engagement that the officer asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25).

Crucially, the search of the defendant's person was not a *Terry* frisk; it was a consensual search. When the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "automatically put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). There is no evidence of coercion, threats, or intimidation.

Because the defendant voluntarily offered to be searched during a consensual encounter, the officer's lack of reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk is irrelevant. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996) (holding that consent to search is valid if given voluntarily, regardless of whether the officer advised the suspect of his right to refuse). The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as a *Terry* frisk ignores the defendant's affirmative consent.

**IV. Fruit of the Poisonous Tree**

The prosecution argues that the evidence must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional seizure under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). This argument fails because the "tree"—the seizure—had already been severed. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine excludes evidence only if it is obtained by exploiting the illegality. *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 484-85.

Here, the chain of causation was broken by the officer's termination of the stop and the defendant's voluntary re-engagement. The defendant was not acting under the compulsion of the seizure when he offered to be searched; he acted voluntarily after being told he was free to go. Therefore, the evidence discovered in the vehicle was not the product of the initial detention or the lack of *Terry* suspicion, but of the defendant's independent consent. See *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975) (holding that the mere fact that a confession occurs after a lawful arrest does not make it the fruit of the arrest if the confession is voluntary; similarly, consent given after a lawful termination of a stop is not the fruit of the stop).

**V. Alternative Grounds: The Automobile Exception**

The defense further argues that the search was valid under the automobile exception. Under *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565, 580 (1991), police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband. The officer testified that he suspected the defendant was "bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have" (Suppression Tr. 22:3-5), based on the defendant's inconsistent story and the late hour. While this probable cause argument is not as strong as the consent argument, it provides an alternative basis for admission. However, given the clear validity of the consent, the Court need not rest its decision on this ground.

**CONCLUSION**

The prosecution is correct that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a non-consensual *Terry* frisk. However, the search was not a *Terry* frisk; it was a consensual search that occurred after the officer unequivocally told the defendant he was free to go and the defendant voluntarily re-initiated contact. The defendant's consent purged the primary taint of any prior illegality. Accordingly, the motion to suppress is denied.

The prosecution contends that the defendant's consent was involuntary because it was given during an coercive atmosphere. However, the Supreme Court has held that consent is valid if given voluntarily, regardless of whether the officer advised the suspect of his right to refuse. Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996). The question of voluntariness must be determined from the totality of the circumstances. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 222 (1973).

Here, the totality of circumstances supports a finding of voluntariness:

**A. The Defendant Was Not in Custody**

The defendant was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and had been explicitly told he was free to go. He was waiting for his girlfriend to pick him up. The prosecution argues that the seizure of his license created a coercive atmosphere, but the defendant was not prevented from leaving—he simply could not drive. He had a ride coming, and the officer had told him he could leave. The defendant was free to walk away, to wait for his ride, or to decline the officer's request.

**B. The Defendant's Demeanor Was Cooperative, Not Coerced**

The officer described the defendant's demeanor in giving consent as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18. Critically, the defendant did not merely acquiesce to the officer's request—he affirmatively offered to be searched: "He — without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle. Before I asked him anything — about anything other than do you have anything that would concern me, he automatically put his hands — after he pats him down, put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.

This affirmative offer distinguishes this case from those where consent is obtained through coercion or duress. The defendant was not faced with a demand; he was presented with a question and responded by inviting the search. His cooperation was enthusiastic, not reluctant.

**C. There Was No Evidence of Coercion**

The officer's weapon was not drawn during the interaction. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The officer testified that he had "no fear for [his] safety." Suppression Tr. 32:2-4. There were no threats, no commands, no physical force. The officer asked a question; the defendant gave an answer. This is the essence of a consensual encounter.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's "agreeable" demeanor reflects the natural response of someone detained by police. However, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that cooperation equates to coercion. In Robinette, the Court explicitly noted that "the fact that the officer did not inform respondent of his right to refuse consent does not render the consent invalid." 519 U.S. at 40. The prosecution's attempt to equate cooperation with coercion finds no support in precedent.

---

## V. EVEN IF THERE WAS A SEIZURE, THE CONSENT PURGED THE TAINT

Even assuming arguendo that the initial encounter constituted a seizure, the defendant's voluntary consent after being told he was free to go severed the chain of causation. The prosecution's reliance on Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), is misplaced.

In Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975), the Supreme Court held that "the mere fact that a confession occurs after a lawful arrest does not make it the fruit of the arrest if the confession is voluntary." Similarly, consent given after a lawful termination of a stop is not the fruit of the stop. Here, the officer terminated the stop, told the defendant he was free to go, and began walking away. The defendant then voluntarily re-engaged the officer. The consent was not the product of any compulsion—it was an independent act of the defendant's own free will.

The prosecution argues that the officer's subjective belief that the defendant was engaged in criminal activity somehow infects the consent. However, the Supreme Court has held that "the fact that the officers were suspicious does not render the consent invalid." Robinette, 519 U.S. at 40. What matters is whether the consent was voluntary, not whether the officer had subjective suspicions.

---

## VI. THE PROSECUTION'S REASONABLE SUSPICION ARGUMENT IS IRRELEVANT

The prosecution is correct that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a non-consensual Terry frisk. However, this point is irrelevant because the search was not a Terry frisk—it was a consensual search. The prosecution's attempt to characterize the search as a Terry frisk ignores the defendant's affirmative consent.

The defense does not dispute that the officer could not have conducted a non-consensual search based on the facts known to him. But the officer did not conduct a non-consensual search. He asked for consent, and the defendant gave it. The distinction is critical. Under Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. at 39-40, consent is valid if given voluntarily, regardless of whether the officer had reasonable suspicion to conduct a search without consent.

The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In Royer, the Court found a seizure where the defendant was detained at the airport with his ticket and luggage. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go and was waiting for a ride. The defendant was not detained; he was free to leave.

---

## VII. ALTERNATIVE GROUNDS: THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION

Even if the Court were to find that the consent was somehow tainted, the search was independently valid under the automobile exception. Under California v. Acevedo, 500 U.S. 565, 580 (1991), police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband.

Here, the officer testified that he suspected the defendant was "bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have" based on the defendant's inconsistent story and the late hour. Suppression Tr. 22:3-5. While this probable cause argument is not as strong as the consent argument, it provides an alternative basis for admission of the evidence.

---

## VIII. CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to suppress evidence not because the defendant was coerced, threatened, or detained, but because the officer's subjective suspicions were not sufficiently particularized. However, the Fourth Amendment does not require officers to have particularized suspicion before asking for consent. It requires only that consent, if obtained, be voluntary.

Here, the defendant was told he was free to go. He voluntarily re-engaged the officer. He affirmatively offered to be searched. He was cooperative and agreeable. There was no coercion, no threats, no force. The search was valid, and the evidence should not be suppressed.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the ruling below and deny the motion to suppress.

**FINAL RULING**

The motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**ANALYSIS**

After careful consideration of the closing arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, this Court adheres to its determination that the evidence obtained from the defendant's vehicle is admissible. While the prosecution correctly identifies that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a non-consensual *Terry* frisk, the defense has demonstrated that the search was conducted pursuant to voluntary consent given during a consensual encounter that followed the lawful termination of the initial seizure.

**I. The Standard for a Terry Frisk**

The Court agrees with the prosecution that the officer lacked the reasonable suspicion necessary to justify a *Terry* frisk. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a pat-down search only if they have reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is "armed and presently dangerous." *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 326-27 (2009). The record reflects that the officer had no fear for his safety, observed no furtive movements, and admitted that aside from his subjective disbelief of the defendant's explanation for being lost, there was "no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). A "totality of circumstances" consisting merely of a late hour, a suspended license, and an inconsistent story does not amount to reasonable suspicion that a suspect is armed. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 506 (1983) (plurality opinion); *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 50-51 (1968).

However, the prosecution's focus on *Terry* is misplaced because the search in question was not a *Terry* frisk. *Terry* governs seizures and non-consensual searches; it does not govern a search conducted pursuant to voluntary consent. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996).

**II. Termination of the Seizure**

The critical dispositive issue is whether the defendant was seized when he consented to the search. The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable seizures, not consensual encounters. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). A seizure occurs only if "a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." *Michigan v. Chesternut*, 486 U.S. 567, 573 (1988).

The record clearly establishes that the officer terminated the seizure before requesting consent. After completing the paperwork for the suspended license citation, the officer testified that he "told [defendant] he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty much — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This explicit statement, coupled with the officer's physical act of walking away, signaled the end of the detention. See *United States v. Dray*, 901 F.2d 857, 860 (10th Cir. 1990) (holding that where an officer tells a defendant he is free to go, the defendant's subsequent consent to search is voluntary).

The prosecution argues that the presence of rotating lights and a backup officer rendered the defendant's freedom illusory. While these factors are part of the totality of the circumstances, they do not override the officer's unequivocal communication that the defendant was free to leave. The prosecution's assertion that the "vehicle was impounded due to the suspension" is not supported by the record; the facts indicate the defendant was waiting for his girlfriend to pick up the car, not that it was being impounded. Furthermore, the officer had disengaged and was walking away. Under these circumstances, a reasonable person would have felt free to terminate the conversation. *Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 435.

**III. Validity of the Consent**

The defense correctly argues that the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to go. The defendant initiated a new conversation regarding the license seizure form (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). It was only after this re-engagement that the officer asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25).

Crucially, the search was consensual. When the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "automatically put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). There is no evidence of coercion, threats, or intimidation. The officer's weapon was not drawn, and he testified to having no safety concerns. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4).

Because the defendant voluntarily offered to be searched during a consensual encounter, the officer's lack of reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk is irrelevant. *Robinette*, 519 U.S. at 39-40 (holding that consent to search is valid if given voluntarily, regardless of whether the officer advised the suspect of his right to refuse). The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as a coerced encounter ignores the defendant's affirmative conduct in offering the search after the seizure had ended.

**IV. Fruit of the Poisonous Tree**

The prosecution argues that the evidence must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional seizure under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). This argument fails because the "tree"—the seizure—had already been severed. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine excludes evidence only if it is obtained by exploiting the illegality. *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 484-85.

Here, the chain of causation was broken by the officer's termination of the stop and the defendant's voluntary re-engagement. The defendant was not acting under the compulsion of the seizure when he offered to be searched; he acted voluntarily after being told he was free to go. Therefore, the evidence discovered in the vehicle was not the product of the initial detention or the lack of *Terry* suspicion, but of the defendant's independent consent. See *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975) (holding that the mere fact that a confession occurs after a lawful arrest does not make it the fruit of the arrest if the confession is voluntary; similarly, consent given after a lawful termination of a stop is not the fruit of the stop).

**CONCLUSION**

The prosecution is correct that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a non-consensual *Terry* frisk. However, the search was not a *Terry* frisk; it was a consensual search that occurred after the officer unequivocally told the defendant he was free to go and the defendant voluntarily re-initiated contact. The defendant's consent purged the primary taint of any prior illegality. Accordingly, the motion to suppress is denied.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
