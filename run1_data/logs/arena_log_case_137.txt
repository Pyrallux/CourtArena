# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court's judgment should be reversed. While the plaintiff has characterized this case as one involving a heightened duty of care owed by a pipeline operator, the plaintiff has fundamentally mischaracterized the applicable legal standards and ignored critical evidence regarding the plaintiff's driver's own negligence. The defense will establish that: (1) the plaintiff's driver assumed the risk of encountering the detour road conditions; (2) the driver failed to exercise ordinary care for his own safety; and (3) the defendant's conduct, while perhaps imperfect, was not the proximate cause of the accident.

## I. The Plaintiff's Driver Assumed the Risk of Traveling on an Unfamiliar Detour Road

The plaintiff contends that the driver's unfamiliarity with the road entitled him to assume its safety. However, this argument ignores the well-established principle that a traveler on a public roadway must exercise ordinary care for his own safety, particularly when traversing roads that are obviously constructed as detours or temporary routes.

As the Texas Supreme Court held in *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927), a traveler cannot blindly rely upon the assumption of safety when entering upon a road that a reasonable person would recognize as requiring special caution. The evidence reflects that the detour road was "narrow" and led to a "culvert" that was visibly "crumpled and bent." These conditions should have alerted a reasonable driver to exercise heightened caution.

The driver of the plaintiff's truck chose to proceed upon a narrow dirt detour road without exercising any apparent caution for his own safety. This constitutes a failure to exercise ordinary care that defeats any claim of negligence against the defendant.

## II. The Plaintiff's Driver Was Negligent in Failing to Observe Obvious Road Conditions

The defense submits that the evidence conclusively establishes the plaintiff's driver's negligence. A driver who proceeds without adequate observation of road conditions fails to exercise the degree of care that a reasonably prudent person would exercise under similar circumstances.

In *Dallas Railway & Terminal Co. v. Garner*, 180 S.W.2d 278, 281 (Tex. 1944), the Texas Supreme Court recognized that a driver's failure to observe obvious conditions constitutes negligence as a matter of law. The evidence shows that the driver was traveling on an unfamiliar road, reached the "end of the pavement," and proceeded onto a narrow dirt detour. A driver exercising ordinary care would have reduced speed, examined the road conditions more carefully, or sought an alternate route.

The plaintiff's argument that the driver "had the right to assume" the road was safe ignores the obvious visual cues present at the scene. The crumpled and bent condition of the culvert was visible and apparent. The narrowness of the detour road was similarly observable. The driver proceeded without exercising due care for his own safety.

## III. The Defendant's Alleged Violations Were Not the Proximate Cause of the Accident

Even accepting the plaintiff's characterization of the defendant's conduct, the evidence does not establish proximate cause. The plaintiff must prove that the defendant's alleged negligence was a "but for" cause of the accident and that the type of harm was foreseeable.

In *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev'd on other grounds), the court held that for negligence to be actionable, the plaintiff must establish that the defendant could have reasonably foreseen the particular consequences that resulted. The defendant cannot be held liable for consequences that were not reasonably foreseeable at the time of the alleged negligent conduct.

Here, the evidence shows that the pipeline was struck because the driver failed to properly navigate the culvert. The driver "missed the narrow culvert" or "slipped off of it." This failure to navigate a visible culvert properly is not a foreseeable consequence of pipeline construction. The defendant could not have foreseen that a driver would fail to observe and properly traverse a visible culvert and instead strike a pipeline located therein.

## IV. The Defense Challenges the Application of the Heightened Duty Standard

While the plaintiff invokes the "highest degree of care" standard from *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, this standard applies to the *operation* of pipelines, not merely to their construction. The cited case itself recognizes that the duty extends to practical operation considerations. The defendant in this case was not operating a pipeline at the time of the accident; the pipeline had been constructed and was in existence.

Moreover, the plaintiff has failed to establish that the defendant's construction practices deviated from industry standards or that alternative construction methods were practical or required under the circumstances. The mere fact that the pipeline was constructed in a particular location does not establish negligence when the plaintiff driver failed to exercise ordinary care in traversing the area.

The plaintiff also erroneously conflates knowledge of the culvert's existence with foreseeability of this specific accident. In *Williams v. General Motors Corporation*, 393 S.W.2d 214, 218 (Tex. 1965), the Texas Supreme Court held that foreseeability requires knowledge that *some* injury might occur, not knowledge of the *specific* manner in which injury would occur. The defendant could not have foreseen that a driver would fail to properly navigate a visible culvert and strike the pipeline.

## V. The Trial Court Erred in Failing to Apply Comparative Fault Principles

The defense submits that the trial court erred in failing to consider the comparative fault of the plaintiff's driver. Under Texas law, a plaintiff's recovery is barred or reduced in proportion to his own negligence.

In *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984), the Texas Supreme Court adopted a comparative fault system that considers the negligence of all parties. The evidence overwhelmingly establishes that the plaintiff's driver failed to exercise ordinary care by: (1) proceeding on an unfamiliar road without adequate observation; (2) failing to reduce speed when approaching a narrow culvert; (3) failing to maintain proper control of his vehicle; and (4) failing to yield to obvious road hazards.

Any judgment in favor of the plaintiff must be reduced or barred entirely by the driver's comparative fault.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and render judgment in favor of the defendant, or in the alternative, remand for a new trial on the issues of comparative fault and proximate cause.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a judgment entered against the defendant for damages resulting from a pipeline rupture. The central issue is whether the defendant breached its duty of care in constructing the pipeline and whether the plaintiff’s driver was contributorily negligent as a matter of law. Based on the record provided and the applicable legal standards, the Court finds the arguments presented by the prosecution to be persuasive and legally sound, while the defense's attempts to shift liability to the driver are unsupported by the facts and binding precedent.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Heighted Duty of Care and Breach Thereof**

The defendant, as an operator of a natural gas pipeline, is subject to a heightened standard of care. As established in *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, 104 S.W.2d 516, 518 (Tex. Civ. App.—Waco 1937, writ refused), entities transporting natural gas must exercise the "highest degree of care consistent with practical operation" to prevent harm. The evidence confirms that the defendant failed to meet this obligation. The defendant constructed the pipeline within the ditch rather than the required two feet back, failed to provide the mandated eighteen inches of earth cover, and ran the pipe through the culvert without a protective casing.

The defense attempts to distinguish *Gulf Pipe Line Co.* by arguing the duty applies only to "operation" and not "construction." This argument is without merit. The safe construction of a pipeline is intrinsic to the safe operation of a hazardous system. Furthermore, the defendant’s knowledge that the culvert was narrow, used by the public, and frequently struck by vehicles placed a duty on the defendant to mitigate the obvious risks associated with placing an exposed pipe in that exact location. As noted in *Southern Pacific Co. v. Davenport*, 147 Tex. 529, 533, 218 S.W.2d 100, 102 (1949), operators must anticipate public use of adjacent roadways and take reasonable precautions against foreseeable risks. The defendant’s failure to encase the pipe or provide warnings, despite knowing vehicles regularly slipped off the culvert, constitutes a clear breach of this duty.

**B. Foreseeability and Proximate Cause**

The defense argues that the driver's collision with the pipeline was not a foreseeable consequence of the defendant's conduct, citing *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev'd on other grounds). This argument ignores the specific facts of the record. The defendant had actual knowledge that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it because of the crumpled and bent condition." By placing an unprotected pipe directly within this known impact zone, the defendant made the resulting damage a foreseeable probability. Under the principles discussed in *Williams v. General Motors Corporation*, 393 S.W.2d 214, 218 (Tex. 1965), foreseeability does not require predicting the exact manner of the injury, only that some injury is likely. Here, the injury was the direct result of the specific risk the defendant created and ignored.

**C. Contributory Negligence and Assumption of Risk**

The defense contends that the plaintiff’s driver was negligent for failing to observe the "obvious" condition of the culvert and assumed the risk of traveling on a detour, relying on *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927) and *Dallas Railway & Terminal Co. v. Garner*, 180 S.W.2d 278, 281 (Tex. 1944). However, this analysis conflates the visibility of the culvert with the visibility of the hidden danger.

The record establishes that the driver was unfamiliar with the road and that the pipeline was hidden inside the culvert. Under *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith*, 105 Tex. 244, 248, 147 S.W. 517, 518 (1912), a driver has the right to assume a public road is safe for travel in the absence of warning signs. The driver was not required to inspect for hidden instrumentalities, such as a gas pipe running through a drainage structure. The trial judge’s finding that the record does not support contributory negligence is binding on this Court absent clear error, which the defense has not demonstrated. The driver's reliance on the apparent safety of the road was reasonable, and the defense's assertion that he should have anticipated a hidden pipeline inside the culvert is speculative and contrary to established law.

**D. Comparative Fault**

Finally, the defense argues for the application of comparative fault principles under *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984). While comparative fault is a valid legal concept, it requires a finding of negligence on the part of the plaintiff. As the evidence does not support a finding of contributory negligence against the driver, the defense's request to reduce or bar recovery based on comparative fault must fail.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has demonstrated that the defendant breached its heightened duty of care by constructing the pipeline in a negligent manner and failing to protect a foreseeable hazard. The defense's arguments regarding the driver's negligence are unpersuasive, as they require the driver to have discovered a hidden danger that the defendant itself created and failed to mark. The trial court’s findings are consistent with the evidence and the applicable precedents of *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, *Southern Pacific Co. v. Davenport*, and *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith*.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling affirms the judgment of the trial court. The defendant's liability stands, and the defense's motion for reversal is denied.

## REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT

The defense's arguments mischaracterize the legal standards, ignore controlling precedent, and distort the factual record. We concur fully with the trial court’s conclusion that the defendant breached its heightened duty of care, and the defense’s attempt to shift liability to the driver is legally and factually unsound. Each of the defense’s contentions fails under binding precedent and the undisputed evidence.

### I. The Driver Did Not Assume the Risk of Traveling on the Detour Road

The defense’s reliance on *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927), is misplaced. That case does not stand for the proposition that a driver must exercise heightened caution on *any* detour road. Instead, it emphasizes that a traveler must exercise ordinary care when encountering *known hazards*. Here, the evidence confirms the pipeline was entirely hidden within the culvert—no part of it was visible to the driver. As established in *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith*, 105 Tex. 244, 248, 147 S.W. 517, 518 (1912), a driver has the right to assume a public road is safe for travel in the absence of warning signs or visible hazards. The driver’s unfamiliarity with the road did not impose a duty to inspect for hidden instrumentalities; the law presumes public roads are safe unless defects are apparent. The crumpled and bent condition of the culvert’s exterior does not equate to visibility of the pipeline buried within it. The defense conflates the culvert’s structural condition with the pipeline’s location—a distinction critical to the *Smith* standard. The trial court correctly held the driver’s assumption of safety was reasonable.

### II. The Driver Did Not Negligently Fail to Observe "Obvious" Conditions

The defense’s citation of *Dallas Railway & Terminal Co. v. Garner*, 180 S.W.2d 278, 281 (Tex. 1944), misapplies the law. *Garner* addresses negligence arising from *visible* hazards, such as open trenches or obvious obstructions. Here, the pipeline was not visible—it was concealed inside the culvert, as the trial court found. The defense’s assertion that the pipeline’s location was "obvious" contradicts the record. The driver was not required to search for hidden dangers; the law imposes no duty to inspect for concealed utilities. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Davenport*, 147 Tex. 529, 533, 218 S.W.2d 100, 102 (1949), confirms that operators must anticipate public use of adjacent roadways and take reasonable precautions against foreseeable risks. The defendant’s failure to mark or protect the pipeline—despite knowing vehicles regularly struck the culvert—created the hidden hazard. The driver’s failure to see what was not there cannot constitute negligence.

### III. The Defendant’s Conduct Was the Proximate Cause of the Accident

The defense’s reliance on *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev’d on other grounds), ignores the core principle of foreseeability. *Dowling* does not require the defendant to foresee the *exact sequence* of events, only that the general risk of harm was foreseeable. The defendant had actual knowledge that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it because of the crumpled and bent condition." *Williams v. General Motors Corporation*, 393 S.W.2d 214, 218 (Tex. 1965), holds that foreseeability requires knowledge that *some* injury might occur—not the precise manner. Here, the defendant placed the pipeline directly within the known impact zone of a narrow, structurally compromised culvert used by the public. The resulting rupture was the direct consequence of that specific risk. As *Southern Pacific Co. v. Davenport* makes clear, operators must take precautions against dangers they know are likely to occur. The defense’s attempt to characterize the accident as unforeseeable is contradicted by the defendant’s own knowledge of prior collisions.

### IV. The Heightened Duty Applies to Construction, Not Just Operation

The defense’s argument that *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, 104 S.W.2d 516, 518 (Tex. Civ. App.—Waco 1937, writ refused), only applies to "operation" is legally incorrect. *Gulf Pipe Line Co.* explicitly holds that entities transporting natural gas must exercise the "highest degree of care consistent with practical operation" in *all phases* of pipeline development, including construction. Safe construction is intrinsic to safe operation; a pipeline that is negligently built cannot be operated safely. The defendant’s violation of construction standards—placing the pipe within the ditch instead of two feet back, failing to provide eighteen inches of earth cover, and running it through the culvert without protective casing—directly created the hazard. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Davenport* further confirms that operators must anticipate public use of adjacent infrastructure and mitigate risks during construction. The defense’s artificial distinction between "construction" and "operation" has been uniformly rejected by Texas courts.

### V. Comparative Fault Does Not Apply

The defense’s invocation of *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984), fails because comparative fault requires a finding of plaintiff negligence. The trial court correctly determined the record does not support contributory negligence. As *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith* establishes, a driver is entitled to assume a road is safe without inspecting for hidden dangers. The driver’s unfamiliarity with the road does not transform the defendant’s hidden hazard into the driver’s fault. The defense’s speculation that the driver should have "observed" the pipeline—when it was concealed—has no basis in law or evidence. Without a finding of negligence by the driver, comparative fault is inapplicable.

### Conclusion

The defense’s arguments rely on misreadings of precedent and factual distortions. The defendant’s violations of construction standards, coupled with its knowledge of the culvert’s dangerous conditions, breached its heightened duty of care. The driver reasonably assumed the road was safe, as required by *Smith*, and had no duty to discover a hidden pipeline. The accident was a foreseeable consequence of the defendant’s own actions. We concur with the trial court’s ruling that the defendant is liable, and the defense’s motion for reversal must be denied.

## I. The Driver's Duty to Exercise Ordinary Care on an Unfamiliar Road

The plaintiff incorrectly asserts that the driver's unfamiliarity with the road absolved him of any duty to exercise caution. This misreads *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith*, 105 Tex. 244, 248, 147 S.W. 517, 518 (1912). While *Smith* establishes that drivers may assume roads are generally safe, this presumption does not extend to conditions that are actually known to the traveler or that would be apparent to a reasonable person exercising ordinary care. As the court in *Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Bigham*, 190 S.W.2d 412, 415 (Tex. 1945), held, a driver cannot close his eyes to obvious dangers and must exercise ordinary care in light of circumstances known or that should be known to him.

The record establishes that the culvert was visibly "crumpled and bent" — a condition that was obvious to any observer. The defendant had knowledge that "vehicles either missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it because of the crumpled and bent condition." This visible structural deterioration should have alerted the driver to exercise heightened caution. The driver's failure to slow down, inspect the condition, or proceed with ordinary care when approaching a visibly damaged structure constitutes negligence per se. The plaintiff cannot rely on *Smith* to bootstrap immunity when the driver himself observed or should have observed conditions indicating potential danger.

## II. The Driver Assumed the Risk of Traveling the Detour Road

The plaintiff's attempt to distinguish *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927), is unavailing. In *American Statesman*, the court held that travelers who voluntarily proceed over known dangerous conditions assume the risk of injury. Here, the driver voluntarily selected the detour route. The record confirms that the detour was a "narrow road" designated for reaching the paralleling main highway. The driver exercised choice in selecting this route.

Moreover, the defense in *Dallas Railway & Terminal Co. v. Garner*, 180 S.W.2d 278, 281 (Tex. 1944), correctly stands for the proposition that a traveler who encounters an obstruction or hazardous condition must exercise care commensurate with the danger. The "crumpled and bent condition" of the culvert was a visible warning that the structure was compromised. The driver proceeded without exercising due care, thereby assuming the risk of hidden dangers that might exist within or around the damaged structure.

## III. Proximate Cause and Foreseeability Remain Unestablished

The plaintiff conflates general knowledge of culvert conditions with foreseeability of the specific injury. Under *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev'd on other grounds), proximate cause requires that the injury be a foreseeable result of the defendant's conduct, not merely that some injury might occur. The defendant knew that vehicles occasionally struck the culvert — not that vehicles would strike a pipeline running through it. The plaintiff's reasoning improperly extends foreseeability to encompass any conceivable harm that might flow from the presence of a culvert, rather than the specific injury that occurred.

The defendant cannot be held liable for failing to anticipate the precise configuration of a pipeline that was installed in violation of construction requirements. If the pipeline had been properly constructed two feet back from the ditch and covered with eighteen inches of earth, no accident would have occurred regardless of the driver's conduct. The negligence of the driver in striking the culvert — if that is what occurred — constitutes an independent intervening cause that breaks the chain of causation.

The defense does not dispute that pipeline operators owe a heightened duty of care. Gipson*, 104 S.W.2d 516, 518 (Tex. Civ. App.—Waco 1937, writ refused), recognized, the duty to exercise the highest degree of care is matched by the expectation that the public will exercise ordinary care when utilizing adjacent infrastructure.

The plaintiff incorrectly assumes that the defendant's heightened duty somehow creates a strict liability standard. This is not the law. The driver still bears responsibility for exercising ordinary care. The trial court's finding that "the record does not support the facts alleged by the defendant as constituting the driver's contributory negligence" is clearly erroneous when the record establishes that the driver approached a visibly damaged structure without exercising due care.

## V. Comparative Fault Applies Regardless of Complete Contributory Negligence

The defense's invocation of *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984), is correct in principle but incomplete. Under Texas comparative fault principles, the driver's negligence, if any, should be assessed and apportioned regardless of whether it rises to the level of complete contributory negligence. The plaintiff's argument that comparative fault "requires a finding of plaintiff negligence" misapprehends the doctrine. To the contrary, comparative fault applies precisely because the plaintiff may bear partial responsibility for the damages.

The driver chose to travel an unfamiliar detour road, approached a visibly damaged culvert without exercising due care, and struck a pipeline. Even if the pipeline's location was not visible, the damaged condition of the culvert should have prompted caution. Under *Duncan*, the driver's negligence, if found, should reduce the plaintiff's recovery proportionally rather than bar it entirely.

## VI. The Defendant's Knowledge of Prior Incidents Does Not Establish Liability for This Specific Accident

The plaintiff repeatedly emphasizes that the defendant knew vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it." However, the record does not establish that any vehicle previously struck the pipeline itself. The defendant's knowledge of prior collisions with the culvert structure does not establish foreseeability of damage to a pipeline running through the culvert — particularly when the defendant may have reasonably believed that the pipeline was adequately protected or that drivers would exercise care to avoid the structure entirely.

The plaintiff's foreseeability argument improperly bootstraps general knowledge of culvert collisions into foreseeability of pipeline damage. Under *Williams v. Here, the "general type of harm" that the defendant could foresee was damage to the culvert from vehicle collisions, not damage to a pipeline that the defendant may have reasonably believed was properly protected or located.

The plaintiff's rebuttal fails to address the driver's reciprocal duty of ordinary care when approaching a visibly damaged structure. The driver chose to travel the detour road, encountered a culvert in "crumpled and bent" condition, and proceeded without exercising due care. The defendant's heightened duty does not eliminate the driver's duty to exercise ordinary care. The preliminary ruling incorrectly absolves the driver of any negligence and erroneously applies the assumption of risk doctrine. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the preliminary ruling and remand for a new trial on the issues of contributory negligence and comparative fault.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a reconsideration of the preliminary ruling in light of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue remains whether the defendant breached its duty of care in constructing the pipeline and whether the plaintiff’s driver was contributorily negligent as a matter of law. The prosecution’s rebuttal successfully demonstrates that the defendant’s negligence was the sole proximate cause of the damages, and the trial court’s findings regarding the driver’s conduct are supported by the record.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Driver’s Duty Regarding Visible vs. Hidden Dangers**

The defense argues, citing *Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Bigham*, 190 S.W.2d 412, 415 (Tex. 1945), that the driver had a duty to exercise ordinary care upon observing the "crumpled and bent" condition of the culvert. The defense contends that this visible defect should have alerted the driver to a dangerous condition, thereby negating the presumption of safety established in *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith*, 105 Tex. 244, 248, 147 S.W. 517, 518 (1912).

The Court is not persuaded by this distinction. While the culvert’s condition may have been visible, the specific instrumentality that caused the damage—the gas pipeline—was hidden within the culvert. As the prosecution correctly notes, *Smith* holds that a traveler is not required to anticipate hidden defects. The visible disrepair of a culvert does not necessarily signal the presence of a high-pressure gas pipeline running through its center. A driver observing a damaged culvert might anticipate a rough ride or a risk of vehicle damage, but they are not charged with the knowledge that a utility company has negligently placed a hazardous, unencased pipe inside that structure.

The defense’s reliance on *Bigham* is misplaced because that case involves dangers that are apparent and directly related to the injury. Here, the injury was caused by a hidden pipe, not by the culvert structure itself. The trial judge’s finding that the driver was not contributorily negligent is not clearly erroneous, as the record does not support the conclusion that the driver should have foreseen the presence of the pipeline.

**B. Assumption of Risk and the Detour Road**

The defense argues that the driver assumed the risk by voluntarily choosing the detour road, relying on *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927). This argument fails to account for the nature of the risk assumed. Traveling on a detour road does not constitute an assumption of the risk of encountering a negligently constructed, unmarked, and hidden gas pipeline. The driver had a right to assume, as per *Smith*, that the road was safe for its intended purpose. The absence of warning signs regarding the pipeline, coupled with the hidden nature of the pipe, precludes a finding of assumption of risk. The driver’s choice to use a public detour does not amount to a voluntary acceptance of the defendant’s hidden safety violations.

**C. Proximate Cause and Foreseeability**

The defense contends that the defendant could not have foreseen the specific injury of a pipeline rupture, citing *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev'd on other grounds). They argue that foreseeability extends only to damage to the culvert, not the pipe within it.

This argument ignores the defendant’s actual knowledge. The defendant knew that vehicles frequently missed the narrow culvert or slipped off it. By placing a fragile, unencased gas pipe directly within the path of those known mishaps, the defendant created a foreseeable risk of pipeline rupture. As established in *Williams v. General Motors Corporation*, 393 S.W.2d 214, 218 (Tex. 1965), foreseeability requires only that the general type of harm be foreseeable, not the precise sequence of events. It is entirely foreseeable that a vehicle striking a culvert containing a gas pipe would rupture that pipe. The defendant’s knowledge of prior incidents with the culvert makes the damage to the pipeline a foreseeable consequence of its negligent construction.

**D. The Scope of the Heightened Duty of Care**

The defense attempts to limit the application of *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, 104 S.W.2d 516, 518 (Tex. Civ. App.—Waco 1937, writ refused), to the "operation" phase, arguing that the "construction" phase is distinct. The prosecution correctly rebuts this by noting that safe construction is intrinsic to safe operation. A pipeline constructed in violation of safety standards—specifically, lacking the required cover and casing—is inherently unsafe from the moment it is placed in the ground. The "highest degree of care" mandated by *Gulf Pipe Line Co.* applies to the entirety of the defendant's activities regarding the pipeline. The defendant’s failure to adhere to construction specifications was a direct breach of this duty.

**E. Comparative Fault**

Finally, the defense argues for the application of comparative fault under *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984). However, comparative fault requires a finding of negligence on the part of the plaintiff. As discussed above, the driver’s failure to navigate a "crumpled" culvert does not equate to negligence regarding a hidden pipeline. The trial court found that the record does not support the facts alleged by the defendant as constituting contributory negligence. Because the defense has not demonstrated that the driver breached a duty of care, the doctrine of comparative fault is inapplicable.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s rebuttal fails to overcome the weight of the evidence and controlling precedent. The driver was not required to anticipate a hidden danger inside a visible but unrelated defect. The defendant’s knowledge of the risks associated with the culvert, combined with its failure to construct the pipeline according to safety standards, establishes liability. The prosecution’s rebuttal successfully affirms that the defendant’s conduct was the proximate cause of the accident and that the driver is not chargeable with contributory negligence.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. The defendant’s liability stands, and the defense’s motion for reversal is denied.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in opposition to the plaintiff's claims and in support of our position that the judgment below should be reversed. Despite the Court's detailed ruling, the Defense maintains that fundamental legal principles regarding proximate cause, contributory negligence, and the scope of duty have not been properly applied to the facts of this case. We respectfully urge this Court to reconsider the evidence and apply the correct legal standards.

## I. THE DRIVER'S CONDUCT CONSTITUTES CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE AS A MATTER OF LAW

The evidence presented at trial establishes that the driver of the plaintiff's truck approached a visibly damaged, "crumpled and bent" culvert without exercising appropriate caution. This visible defect should have alerted the driver to the need for heightened vigilance. In *Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Bigham*, 190 S.W.2d 412, 415 (Tex. 1945), the Texas Supreme Court held that a traveler who encounters an obviously dangerous condition must exercise ordinary care to avoid injury. The Court in *Bigham* specifically noted that the duty to exercise care arises when dangers are apparent and observable.

Here, the culvert's damaged condition was visible and obvious. The defendant had knowledge that vehicles "either missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it because of the crumpled and bent condition of the culvert." This knowledge is imputed to the plaintiff through the doctrine of constructive notice—the driver should have recognized the risk inherent in traversing such a damaged structure. The Defense contends that the trial court's finding that the record does not support contributory negligence is clearly erroneous because the driver proceeded without adequate caution upon observing a plainly defective infrastructure element.

The prosecution argues that the driver had no duty to anticipate hidden pipeline hazards. However, this argument conflates two distinct duties. Even if the driver had no duty to anticipate the specific hidden gas pipeline, the driver certainly had a duty to exercise ordinary care when approaching a visibly damaged culvert. The driver's failure to slow down, inspect the crossing, or take precautions when confronted with a damaged structure constitutes a breach of that duty.

## II. THE DOCTRINE OF ASSUMPTION OF RISK PRECLUDES RECOVERY

The driver voluntarily chose to traverse the narrow detour road, knowing or having the opportunity to observe its condition. In *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927), the Texas Supreme Court held that a traveler who voluntarily assumes a known risk cannot recover for injuries resulting from that risk. The Defense argues that the driver, by electing to use the detour road rather than seeking an alternative route, assumed the inherent risks of that deteriorated pathway.

While the prosecution correctly notes that assumption of risk requires knowledge of the specific risk, the driver in this case had at minimum constructive knowledge that the detour road was in poor condition. The "crumpled and bent" condition of the culvert was visible, and the driver proceeded despite this obvious defect. This voluntary choice to traverse a known defective pathway constitutes assumption of risk sufficient to bar recovery or, at minimum, to invoke comparative fault principles.

## III. THE DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT WAS NOT THE PROXIMATE CAUSE OF THE DAMAGES

Even accepting that the defendant may have violated certain construction requirements, the driver's independent action of striking the culvert constitutes an intervening cause that breaks the chain of proximate causation. In *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev'd on other grounds), the court held that for liability to attach, the defendant's conduct must be the proximate cause of the injury, meaning the injury must be a natural and probable consequence of the defendant's actions.

The Defense maintains that the precise injury—the rupture of a gas pipeline within a culvert struck by a vehicle—was not a foreseeable consequence of the defendant's construction methods. The defendant could foresee, at most, damage to the culvert structure itself, not the catastrophic rupture of a pipeline contained within it. The driver's decision to traverse the damaged culvert at sufficient speed to cause damage was an independent, intervening act that severs the causal chain.

Furthermore, the prosecution's reliance on *Williams v. General Motors Corporation*, 393 S.W.2d 214, 218 (Tex. 1965), is misplaced. While *Williams* stands for the proposition that foreseeability requires only that the general type of harm be foreseeable, the harm in *Williams* was directly related to the defendant's conduct. Here, the harm resulted from the driver's independent decision to traverse a damaged structure. The defendant did not place the driver in a position where striking the culvert was inevitable; rather, the driver's own navigation choices led to the collision.

## IV. THE HEIGHTENED DUTY OF CARE DOES NOT EXTEND TO CONSTRUCTION PHASE

The prosecution invokes *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, 104 S.W.2d 516, 518 (Tex. Civ. App.—Waco 1937, writ refused), for the proposition that the "highest degree of care" applies to pipeline construction. The Defense respectfully disagrees with this broad interpretation.

The *Gipson* case and its progeny address the duty owed during the *operation* of pipelines—specifically, the transportation of gas through existing infrastructure. The construction of pipeline infrastructure is subject to different standards and regulatory frameworks. The defendant's duty to construct the pipeline in compliance with administrative regulations is distinct from the operational duty of care addressed in *Gipson*. The prosecution's attempt to conflate these distinct phases should be rejected.

Moreover, even assuming *Gipson* applies, the defendant cannot be held liable for violations that did not proximately cause the damages. The alleged construction defects—improper burial depth, failure to encase the pipe—would only result in liability if those defects were the direct cause of the injury. Here, the injury resulted from the driver's physical impact with the culvert, not from any latent defect in burial depth or encasement.

## V. COMPARATIVE FAULT APPLIES TO REDUCE OR BAR RECOVERY

Under *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984), Texas adopted a system of comparative fault that apportion damages according to each party's degree of fault. Even if the defendant is found to have been negligent, the driver's failure to exercise ordinary care when approaching the damaged culvert constitutes negligence that should reduce the plaintiff's recovery.

The trial court erred in finding that comparative fault is inapplicable. The record demonstrates that the driver was unfamiliar with the road, yet proceeded without reducing speed or exercising caution upon encountering a visibly defective structure. This conduct constitutes negligence that contributed to the damages. Under *Duncan*, the plaintiff's recovery should be reduced proportionally, or potentially barred entirely if the driver's fault equals or exceeds the defendant's.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and render judgment in favor of the defendant, or in the alternative, remand the case for a new trial on the issues of contributory negligence and comparative fault. The driver had a duty to exercise care when approaching a visibly damaged culvert, and his failure to do so constitutes contributory negligence that breaks the chain of proximate causation or, at minimum, reduces the plaintiff's recovery under comparative fault principles.

The Defense avers that the prosecution has not overcome the fundamental legal defects in its case, and that justice requires reversal of the judgment below.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final determination following the submission of closing arguments by both the prosecution and the defense. The core issue remains whether the defendant is liable for the damages resulting from the rupture of the gas pipeline, and whether the plaintiff’s driver is barred from recovery due to contributory negligence or assumption of risk. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, the applicable legal standards, and the factual record, the Court finds that the defense’s arguments, while skillfully articulated, are ultimately unpersuasive in light of the controlling precedent. The prosecution has successfully demonstrated that the defendant breached its duty of care and that the driver’s conduct does not constitute contributory negligence as a matter of law.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Distinction Between Visible Defects and Hidden Dangers**

The defense argues vigorously that the driver was contributorily negligent because he failed to exercise ordinary care upon observing the "crumpled and bent" condition of the culvert. Relying on *Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Bigham*, 190 S.W.2d 412, 415 (Tex. 1945), the defense contends that the visible nature of the culvert's damage imposed a duty on the driver to anticipate danger.

However, this argument conflates the visible defect with the actual instrumentality of injury. The injury was not caused by the culvert itself, but by the gas pipeline running *through* it. As established in *Houston & Texas Central Railway Co. v. Smith*, 105 Tex. 244, 248, 147 S.W. 517, 518 (1912), a traveler upon a public highway is not required to anticipate hidden defects or dangers. While the driver may have seen the damaged culvert, he had no reason to anticipate that a high-pressure gas pipeline was hidden within it. The specific danger that caused the damage—the unencased pipe—was not visible or apparent. Therefore, the driver’s failure to navigate the culvert with extreme caution does not constitute negligence regarding the hidden pipeline. The trial court’s finding that the record does not support contributory negligence is affirmed.

**B. Assumption of Risk Regarding the Detour**

The defense further argues that the driver assumed the risk by choosing the detour road, citing *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. American Statesman*, 292 S.W. 204, 207 (Tex. 1927). The doctrine of assumption of risk requires that the plaintiff voluntarily encounters a known risk. The driver here was unfamiliar with the road and had no knowledge of the pipeline's existence. The "crumpled" condition of the culvert does not equate to knowledge of a hidden gas line. The driver had a right to assume, in the absence of warning signs, that the road was safe for its intended purpose. *Smith*, 105 Tex. at 248. Consequently, the driver did not voluntarily assume the risk of a pipeline rupture.

**C. Proximate Cause and Foreseeability**

The defense contends that the defendant’s conduct was not the proximate cause of the damages, arguing that the rupture was not a foreseeable consequence of the construction methods. They rely on *Dowling v. NADW Marketing*, 631 S.W.2d 772, 776 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1982, rev'd on other grounds), to suggest the driver’s act was an intervening cause.

This argument is contradicted by the defendant’s own knowledge. The evidence shows the defendant knew that vehicles frequently missed or slipped off the narrow culvert. By placing an unencased, fragile gas pipe directly within the path of these foreseeable mishaps, the defendant created a zone of danger. Under *Williams v. General Motors Corporation*, 393 S.W.2d 214, 218 (Tex. 1965), foreseeability requires only that the general type of harm be foreseeable. It was entirely foreseeable that a vehicle striking the culvert would damage the contents within it. The defendant’s decision to run the pipe through the culvert without protection, knowing the culvert's condition and usage, establishes the requisite proximate cause.

**D. The Scope of the Duty of Care**

The defense attempts to limit the "highest degree of care" mandated by *Gulf Pipe Line Co. v. Gipson*, 104 S.W.2d 516, 518 (Tex. Civ. App.—Waco 1937, writ refused), to the operational phase of the pipeline. The Court rejects this artificial distinction. The safety of a gas pipeline is inextricably linked to its construction. A pipeline that is improperly buried and unencased is unsafe from the moment it is installed. The "highest degree of care" applies to the construction of inherently dangerous instrumentalities just as it does to their operation. The defendant’s failure to adhere to safety specifications during construction was a direct breach of this duty.

**E. Comparative Fault**

Finally, the defense invokes *Duncan v. Cessna Aircraft Co.*, 665 S.W.2d 414, 428 (Tex. 1984), to argue for comparative fault. However, comparative fault is predicated on the existence of negligence on the part of the plaintiff. As established in the analysis of contributory negligence, the driver’s failure to anticipate a hidden danger within a visible defect does not constitute negligence. Because the driver was not negligent, the principles of comparative fault do not apply.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense’s closing arguments fail to overcome the weight of the evidence and the controlling legal authority. The defendant breached its heightened duty of care by constructing the pipeline in a negligent manner, creating a foreseeable risk of harm. The plaintiff’s driver, confronted with a hidden danger, had no duty to anticipate the specific hazard that caused the injury. The trial court’s findings are supported by the record and the law.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed. The defendant’s liability stands, and the defense’s motions for reversal or remand are denied.
