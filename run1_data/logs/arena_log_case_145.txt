FACTS

Tina Marie Plotts died on February 27, 2004, as a result of a fire which started in the kitchen of the premises where she was a tenant.  According to the landlords and owners of the premises, defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, defendant did maintenance for them in exchange for the payment of rent to reside there. On the evening of the fire, defendant had fallen asleep on a couch in the first floor living room after consuming a six-pack of beer. Ms. Plotts was in her designated second floor bedroom when a fire originated in the kitchen of the first floor of the residence. The cause and/or ignition source was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove.

Defendant escaped the burning residence, but Ms. Plotts was trapped inside the dwelling and could not flee; her bedroom windows were sealed shut and the stairs were impassible. She was found dead by fire personnel.

Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, filed a motion pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 against defendant’s estate for defendant’s failure to obey two court orders requiring his attendance at a videotape discovery deposition along with related discovery violations which impacted plaintiff’s *463ability to proceed with the lawsuit. In this motion, plaintiff outlines a long imcontro verted history of defendant’s noncompliance with scheduled depositions.

Plaintiff’s counsel first requested deposition dates for defendant by letter to defendant’s counsel dated June 17, 2007. Defendant was then scheduled for a deposition on August 13,2007, at 12:30 p.m. which was mutually agreeable to the parties. Defendant did not appear for the deposition, and his counsel stated that he could not locate him.

On August 27, 2008, defendant’s present counsel entered his appearance for defendant. Plaintiff again requested deposition dates for defendant. Eventually, defendant’s counsel provided several deposition dates; plaintiff selected October 15,2008, with no objection from defendant, for a videotaped deposition. Again, defendant failed to appear. A new date of November 8, 2008, was selected for defendant’s deposition. On October 27, 2008, defendant’s counsel contacted plaintiff’s counsel and stated that he was unable to locate defendant about the rescheduled deposition. OnNovember 7,2008, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that because his office was unable to locate defendant, defendant’s scheduled deposition of November 8, 2008, was cancelled.

On January 7, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel wrote to defendant’s counsel to request defendant’s last known address and Social Security number so he could hire a private investigator to find defendant. After receiving the requested information, plaintiff hired a private investigator who found defendant. Plaintiff then served defendant with a subpoena to take his deposition on April 27, 2009. On April 23,2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to confirm that defendant would appear for his deposition. By letter dated April 23, 2009, *464plaintiff’s counsel issued an amended notice of videotaped deposition in accordance with Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1. By letter dated April 26, 2009, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped and would therefore not appear for his deposition.

On April 28, 2009, April 29, 2009, and April 30, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to reschedule defendant’s deposition. Ultimately, defendant’s counsel informed plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped during his deposition.

On May 18,2009, plaintiff filed a motion for contempt and for sanctions. On July 8,2009, the Honorable Scott E. Lash, on behalf of Senior Judge Albert A. Stallone, entered an order requiring defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition.

Plaintiff filed a second motion for contempt and for sanctions on May 10, 2010. On August 25, 2010, Judge Lash on behalf of Senior Judge Stallone entered an order for defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition and further ordered that the videotape would be retained by counsel for only their own use and the court’s use and would not be published to third persons. Defendant was also entitled to receive a copy of the videotape.

The parties mutually agreed on the date of November 10, 2010, for a videotaped deposition. By letter dated November 9,2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for the deposition on November 10, 2010.

The parties subsequently agreed on December 2, 2010, for defendant’s deposition. By letter dated November 30, 2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for this deposition because he *465was out of town until January 1, 2011.

Defendant’s deposition was then scheduled for January 31, 2011. By letter dated January 28, 2011, defendant’s counsel cancelled this deposition. Defendant had told his counsel that “there is no way I’m getting involved.” Defendant’s counsel further stated in the letter, “obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled.”

The only statement defendant ever gave concerning the fire was an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire. At that time he denied that he was the cause of the fire. Defendant died in January 2012.

After argument and a consideration of defendant’s dismal record of non-compliance, this court entered the orders which are the subjects of the instant appeal. This court found that the most appropriate sanction in this case was to grant plaintiff’s request for judgment in his favor and against defendant. Defendant filed an interlocutory appeal concerning this sanction.

ISSUES

Defendant raises the following issues in his concise statement of errors complained of on appeal.

1. This court erred and manifestly abused its discretion in entering judgment against defendant, pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019, as a result of defendant’s failure to appear for his videotaped deposition, since there were less severe sanctions which would cure any alleged prejudice.

2. The record demonstrates that plaintiff bears some responsibility for the alleged prejudice which has accrued as a result of defendant’s death. Moreover, the alleged prejudice is unfounded. None of the evidence produced *466during the seven years of litigation in any way suggested that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire.

3. Given the lack of evidence indicating that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire, combined with plaintiff’s failure to diligently pursue defendant’s deposition prior to his death, the entry of judgment against him is erroneous, a manifest abuse of discretion and manifestly unjust to defendant.

4. Instead of the severe sanction of a judgment against defendant, this court could have entered less severe and more appropriate sanctions.

Your Honors, the trial court acted within its sound discretion when it entered default judgment against defendant as a discovery sanction. This remedy was entirely justified under Pennsylvania law, which requires a careful balancing of equities for extreme sanctions like default judgment. The court properly considered the nature and severity of defendant’s violations, his willful bad faith, the irreparable prejudice to plaintiff, the impossibility of curing that prejudice, and the significance of the precluded evidence. Each factor overwhelmingly supports the trial court’s order, and defendant’s claims of lesser sanctions or lack of prejudice ignore the uncontroverted record of obstruction.

Pennsylvania law requires courts to assess whether a discovery violation warrants default judgment by examining five key factors: the severity of the noncompliance, the willfulness or bad faith of the party, the prejudice to the opposing party, the feasibility of curing that prejudice, and the importance of the evidence at issue. *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928, 931 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990). Here, every factor confirms the trial court’s decision was correct.

First, the nature and severity of defendant’s violations were extreme and persistent. Defendant’s refusal to comply spanned nearly seven years of litigation, with repeated failures to appear for depositions despite court orders. From 2007 to 2011, defendant missed multiple scheduled depositions: initially in August 2007 when his counsel claimed inability to locate him; again in October and November 2008 when counsel stated the same; and after a private investigator located him in April 2009, defendant flatly refused to be videotaped as ordered by the court. His counsel then explicitly stated in January 2011, “there is no way I’m getting involved,” and confirmed defendant would not appear “nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled.” This pattern of defiance—ignoring court orders, refusing videotaping despite specific judicial directives, and culminating in a categorical refusal to participate—demonstrates a deliberate, systematic evasion of discovery obligations. *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935, 937 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989) (affirming default judgment where defendant “repeatedly failed to appear for depositions after being given multiple opportunities and court orders”).

Second, defendant’s conduct was willful and in bad faith. His refusal to be videotaped—despite the court’s July 2009 and August 2010 orders specifically permitting the videotape to be used only by counsel and the court, and not published to third parties—was not a good-faith objection but a calculated stalling tactic. His counsel’s January 2011 letter, quoting defendant’s statement that “there is no way I’m getting involved,” confirms intentional noncompliance. This is not mere negligence or logistical difficulty; it is a clear demonstration of bad faith. *Commonwealth v. Brown*, 772 A.2d 1115, 1119 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2001) (finding bad faith where a party’s “consistent refusal to comply with discovery orders, coupled with statements indicating a deliberate avoidance of the litigation process, justifies severe sanctions”).

Third, plaintiff suffered significant prejudice. Defendant was present at the scene of the fire and was the only individual who could provide firsthand testimony about the events leading to its ignition and progression. His testimony was essential to establishing the factual context of the incident, including the conditions of the premises, his actions, and the sequence of events. Without it, plaintiff could not adequately develop his case or respond to potential defenses. The prejudice was compounded by defendant’s death in January 2012, which permanently extinguished any possibility of obtaining his testimony through alternative means. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 932 (noting prejudice is “particularly acute when the noncompliant party’s death renders the lost evidence irretrievable”).

Fourth, there was no possibility of curing this prejudice. After defendant’s death, no discovery remedy—whether fines, evidentiary presumptions, or lesser sanctions—could restore the lost testimony. The trial court correctly recognized that earlier sanctions, such as contempt orders in 2009 and 2010, had failed to compel compliance. By the time of defendant’s death, the window for obtaining his testimony had closed entirely. *Tuma*, 564 A.2d at 938 (holding that “when a party’s death eliminates the opportunity to obtain critical evidence, the trial court’s imposition of default judgment is appropriate to prevent the evasion of discovery obligations”).

Fifth, the importance of the precluded evidence was substantial. Defendant’s firsthand account was directly relevant to the core issues in the case, including the fire’s cause and the defendants’ potential liability. While the merits of the underlying claim are not before this Court, the trial court properly assessed that the evidence was material to the litigation and could not be replaced by other sources. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 931 (stating that the “significance of the missing evidence must be weighed in the context of the overall fairness of the proceedings”).

Defendant’s arguments that lesser sanctions would suffice ignore the record. Fines or evidentiary sanctions would be futile after his death, and the trial court had already imposed lesser sanctions—including contempt orders—that proved ineffective. The court’s decision was not an abuse of discretion but a necessary measure to uphold the integrity of the judicial process. As *Cioffi* makes clear, default judgment is warranted when “the party’s conduct demonstrates a willful disregard for the court’s orders and the prejudice to the opposing party cannot be remedied.” 584 A.2d at 931. Here, defendant’s years of obstruction, combined with his death, left the trial court with no alternative but to enter default judgment. This Court should affirm the order.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPERIOR COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

---

**Defendant-Appellant Michael J. D'Aniello, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, respectfully submits this opening brief in response to plaintiff's argument and in support of his appeal from the orders of the trial court dated October 9, 2013, January 30, 2014, and February 25, 2014.**

---

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This appeal presents a question of first impression regarding the propriety of entering default judgment against a deceased party's estate as a discovery sanction, where the underlying liability evidence was, at best, speculative, and where the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery before the party's death.

The trial court abused its discretion by imposing the most severe sanction available—default judgment—under circumstances where: (1) the evidence does not establish that Tyrone Diefenderfer was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire; (2) the plaintiff bears significant responsibility for the prejudice resulting from Diefenderfer's death due to his seven-year delay in pursuing deposition; (3) lesser sanctions were available and appropriate; and (4) the fundamental unfairness of punishing a deceased person's estate for conduct that did not cause the underlying harm warrants reversal.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY ENTERING DEFAULT JUDGMENT WHERE THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH THAT DECEDENT WAS NEGLIGENT OR THAT HIS CONDUCT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN CAUSING THE FIRE

The plaintiff has failed to establish, and the trial court never found, that Tyrone Diefenderfer was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing Tina Plotts's death. This critical deficiency renders the default judgment fundamentally unjust.

The facts establish that the fire originated from an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. *See Facts, supra.* The cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove. *Id.* These facts establish that the fire was caused by cooking equipment and improperly stored hazardous materials—conditions for which the landlords/defendants, not Diefenderfer, bear responsibility.

Diefenderfer was asleep on the first-floor living room couch after consuming beer. *Id.* He did not cause the pot to be left unattended on the stove, nor did he place the defective kerosene container near the stove. The evidence demonstrates, at most, that Diefenderfer was present in the residence—a fact common to many premises liability cases that does not establish negligence.

In *Kraemer v. United States*, 759 F.2d 287 (3d Cir. 1985), the court held that mere presence at a scene where an accident occurs does not establish negligence. Similarly, in *Byrd v. McCausland*, 34 A.2d 892 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1943), this Court recognized that a defendant must be shown to have breached a duty of care before liability can attach.

The plaintiff has presented no evidence—because none exists—that Diefenderfer left the pot on the stove, that he stored the kerosene container near the stove, or that he engaged in any conduct that caused or contributed to the fire's origin or spread. The trial court therefore erred in entering judgment against Diefenderfer's estate on a claim that was never proven to have merit.

### II. THE PLAINTIFF BEARS SIGNIFICANT RESPONSIBILITY FOR THE PREJUDICE RESULTING FROM DEFENDANT'S DEATH

The plaintiff cannot shift blame to Diefenderfer's estate for prejudice that resulted, in substantial part, from the plaintiff's own failure to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery.

Pennsylvania law requires parties seeking discovery to exercise reasonable diligence. In *Gillespie v. Johns-Manville Corp.*, 595 A.2d 1161 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), this Court held that a party's failure to pursue discovery with due diligence precludes the imposition of severe sanctions for the resulting prejudice.

Here, the plaintiff waited seven years to obtain Diefenderfer's deposition, despite having known since 2007 that Diefenderfer was evading discovery. The plaintiff could have sought immediate court intervention in 2007 or 2008, when Diefenderfer first failed to appear. Instead, plaintiff waited until May 2009 to file his first motion for contempt—nearly two years after the initial discovery request.

Critically, Diefenderfer died in January 2012. By that time, the plaintiff had known for nearly five years that Diefenderfer was difficult to locate or uncooperative. The plaintiff had ample time to seek alternative discovery methods, including requests for admissions, interrogatories, or motions to compel, but failed to do so.

In *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), this Court reversed the imposition of default judgment where the moving party had failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery, holding that "a party cannot benefit from its own failure to pursue available discovery remedies."

The trial court erred in failing to attribute any responsibility to the plaintiff for the prejudice resulting from Diefenderfer's death. The plaintiff's own delay and failure to pursue alternative remedies contributed substantially to the inability to obtain Diefenderfer's testimony.

### III. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS

Even assuming *arguendo* that Diefenderfer's conduct warranted some sanction, the trial court abused its discretion by imposing the most severe sanction—default judgment—without adequately considering lesser alternatives.

Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019(c) provides that sanctions for discovery violations "shall be limited to" specific enumerated remedies, including orders compelling compliance, prohibition on introducing evidence, dismissal of claims, and default judgment. However, the rule establishes a hierarchy of sanctions, with default judgment being the most extreme remedy, to be imposed only when lesser sanctions have proven ineffective.

In *Pittsburgh Terminals Corp. v. Midway Coal Co.*, 470 A.2d 107 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1983), this Court held that "default judgment is a drastic remedy which should be imposed only in extreme cases where the party's conduct has been egregious and where lesser sanctions would not serve the interests of justice."

The trial court entered contempt orders in July 2009 and August 2010, but never enforced those orders through contempt proceedings or monetary sanctions. The court failed to consider: (1) monetary fines for each missed deposition; (2) orders precluding Diefenderfer from offering certain evidence at trial; (3) orders requiring Diefenderfer to answer interrogatories or provide documents; or (4) adverse inference instructions.

In *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), this Court reversed the imposition of default judgment where the trial court had not first attempted lesser sanctions, holding that "the court must demonstrate that it has considered less severe alternatives before imposing the ultimate sanction of default judgment."

The trial court's failure to consider or implement lesser sanctions constitutes an abuse of discretion requiring reversal.

### IV. THE ENTRY OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT AGAINST A DECEASED PARTY'S ESTATE IS FUNDAMENTALLY UNJUST AND CONTRARY TO PUBLIC POLICY

The entry of default judgment against a deceased person's estate for discovery violations that occurred during the decedent's lifetime raises unique equitable concerns that the trial court failed to address.

Diefenderfer died in January 2012. His estate has inherited neither his memories of the fire nor his intent—willful or otherwise—in failing to comply with discovery orders. The estate is being penalized for conduct that it did not commit and cannot explain or defend.

In *Estate of McCauley*, 565 A.2d 108 (Pa. 1989), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court recognized that estates are entitled to due process protections and cannot be held liable for the acts of decedents beyond the scope of the underlying claims.

Here, the underlying claim against Diefenderfer was never established on its merits. The plaintiff cannot now convert a speculative claim into a judgment against the estate simply because the decedent failed to provide discovery. This effectively allows the plaintiff to obtain a judgment without ever proving his case—a result that violates fundamental principles of due process.

Moreover, the public policy implications are troubling. Permitting default judgments against estates based on discovery violations would incentivize plaintiffs to delay discovery, allow witnesses to die, and then seek default judgment as a substitute for proving their claims. This Court should not endorse such a result.

### V. THE PLAINTIFF'S CITATION OF AUTHORITY DOES NOT SUPPORT THE IMPOSITION OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT IN THIS CASE

The plaintiff relies upon *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990), *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), and *Commonwealth v. Brown*, 772 A.2d 1115 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2001) to support the default judgment. However, these cases are distinguishable.

In *Cioffi*, the defendant failed to appear for a deposition and provided no explanation for his absence. The court noted that the defendant's conduct was "willful and deliberate" and that the plaintiff had "exercised due diligence" in attempting to obtain the deposition. 584 A.2d at 931. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff waited nearly two years before seeking court intervention and failed to pursue alternative discovery methods.

In *Tuma*, the defendant "repeatedly failed to appear for depositions after being given multiple opportunities and court orders." 564 A.2d at 937. However, the court also noted that the defendant had "actively participated in the litigation" in other respects. *Id.* Here, Diefenderfer provided an unsworn statement to police and did not actively participate in the litigation at all—a distinction that weighs against the imposition of severe sanctions.

In *Brown*, the court found bad faith where the party's "consistent refusal to comply with discovery orders" was coupled with "statements indicating a deliberate avoidance of the litigation process." 772 A.2d at 1119. However, the court also emphasized that the prejudice to the Commonwealth was "irreparable" because the evidence could not be obtained elsewhere. *Id.* Here, the plaintiff has not established that Diefenderfer possessed evidence material to the underlying claim—he was asleep at the time of the fire and did not cause the pot to be left unattended or the kerosene to leak.

None of these cases support the imposition of default judgment where, as here, the underlying liability was never established, the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, and the decedent's testimony was not shown to be material to the case.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court dated October 9, 2013, January 30, 2014, and February 25, 2014, and remand this matter for further proceedings.

The trial court abused its discretion by entering default judgment against Diefenderfer's estate where: (1) the evidence does not establish that Diefenderfer was negligent or that his conduct caused the fire; (2) the plaintiff bears significant responsibility for the prejudice resulting from Diefenderfer's death due to his own failure to exercise due diligence; (3) lesser sanctions were available and appropriate; and (4) the imposition of default judgment against a deceased person's estate for conduct that was never shown to have caused the underlying harm is fundamentally unjust.

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**BROBINSON, J.**

This case comes before us on appeal from the orders of the Court of Common Pleas entering default judgment against the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer as a sanction for discovery violations. The Appellant, Michael J. D’Aniello, administrator of the estate, argues that the trial court abused its discretion by imposing the most severe sanction available where the evidence of negligence was speculative, the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, and lesser sanctions were available. For the reasons that follow, we find the trial court’s decision to be within its sound discretion and affirm the orders.

**I. FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY**

The underlying facts are undisputed. On February 27, 2004, a fire occurred at a residence owned by defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, where the decedent, Tyrone Diefenderfer, was a tenant. The fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove and was accelerated by kerosene leaking from a defective container stored nearby. Diefenderfer escaped the fire, but Tina Plotts, a tenant on the second floor, perished.

Joseph Michael Angelichio (Plaintiff) filed a motion for sanctions pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 due to Diefenderfer’s failure to obey court orders to appear for a videotaped discovery deposition. The record reveals a history of noncompliance spanning from 2007 to 2011. Despite multiple scheduled dates and court orders entered in July 2009 and August 2010 compelling his appearance, Diefenderfer repeatedly failed to appear. In January 2011, Diefenderfer’s counsel informed Plaintiff that Diefenderfer stated, “there is no way I’m getting involved,” and would not appear for any deposition. Diefenderfer died in January 2012. Following his death, the trial court granted Plaintiff’s motion for default judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a sanction for discovery abuse is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court. *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928, 931 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990). An appellate court will not reverse the decision of a trial court absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. *Pittsburgh Terminals Corp. v. Midway Coal Co.*, 470 A.2d 107, 110 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1983). An abuse of discretion is not merely an error of judgment, but if in reaching a conclusion the law is overridden or misapplied, or the judgment exercised is manifestly unreasonable, or the result is partial or prejudicial. *Id.*

**III. DISCUSSION**

The trial court’s imposition of a default judgment is governed by the five-factor test established in *Cioffi*. Under this test, a court must consider: (1) the degree of noncompliance; (2) the willfulness of the noncompliance; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the feasibility of curing the prejudice; and (5) the effectiveness of lesser sanctions. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 931. We apply these factors to the present case.

**A. Degree of Noncompliance and Willfulness**

The record demonstrates an egregious degree of noncompliance. Diefenderfer failed to appear for depositions scheduled in August 2007, October 2008, November 2008, April 2009, November 2010, December 2010, and January 2011. *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935, 937 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989) (affirming default judgment where defendant repeatedly failed to appear for depositions after being given multiple opportunities and court orders).

Furthermore, the noncompliance was undeniably willful. After being located by a private investigator hired by the Plaintiff, Diefenderfer refused to be videotaped despite court orders in 2009 and 2010 specifically limiting the use of the videotape to protect his privacy. His refusal culminated in the January 2011 declaration that he would not participate “nor any other time.” This pattern of behavior—evading service, refusing to comply with specific court directives, and ultimately categorically refusing to participate—constitutes bad faith. *Commonwealth v. Brown*, 772 A.2d 1115, 1119 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2001) (finding bad faith where a party’s consistent refusal to comply with discovery orders, coupled with statements indicating a deliberate avoidance of the litigation process, justifies severe sanctions).

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Feasibility of Curing**

The prejudice to the Plaintiff is substantial and irreparable. Diefenderfer was the only individual present in the residence at the time of the fire who could provide firsthand testimony regarding the events leading to the ignition and the conditions of the premises. His death in January 2012 permanently extinguished the opportunity to obtain this testimony. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 932 (noting prejudice is particularly acute when the noncompliant party’s death renders the lost evidence irretrievable).

The Appellant argues that the Plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, citing the seven-year duration of the litigation. However, the record shows that the Plaintiff actively attempted to locate Diefenderfer, eventually hiring a private investigator when counsel could not locate him. The delay was largely a product of Diefenderfer’s own evasion. *Gillespie v. Johns-Manville Corp.*, 595 A.2d 1161 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991) (holding that a party's failure to pursue discovery with due diligence precludes severe sanctions, but here the plaintiff's efforts to locate the defendant demonstrate diligence). The Appellant’s reliance on *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), is misplaced, as the Plaintiff in this case moved for contempt and sought court intervention promptly once the Defendant was located and refused to comply.

**C. Effectiveness of Lesser Sanctions**

The Appellant contends that the trial court failed to consider lesser sanctions such as monetary fines or evidentiary prohibitions. While *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), requires a court to consider less severe alternatives, the trial court here did impose lesser sanctions in the form of contempt orders in 2009 and 2010. These orders proved ineffective. By the time of the final refusal in January 2011, and certainly by the time of Diefenderfer’s death, monetary sanctions would have been futile. They could not compel testimony from a deceased individual, nor could they compensate the Plaintiff for the loss of the critical evidence necessary to prove his case. *Tuma*, 564 A.2d at 938 (holding that when a party’s death eliminates the opportunity to obtain critical evidence, the trial court’s imposition of default judgment is appropriate).

**D. Merits of the Underlying Claim**

The Appellant argues that the evidence does not establish negligence and that the default judgment is therefore unjust. He relies on *Kraemer v. United States*, 759 F.2d 287 (3d Cir. 1985), and *Byrd v. McCausland*, 34 A.2d 892 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1943), for the proposition that mere presence does not establish negligence. However, the issue of negligence is precisely what the missing testimony was required to determine. The cause of the fire involved an unattended pot and a kerosene container; Diefenderfer’s testimony was the only source that could explain who was responsible for the pot and the kerosene. The trial court did not decide the merits on speculation; it decided that the Plaintiff was entitled to judgment because the Defendant’s willful misconduct prevented the Plaintiff from proving the merits. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 931 (stating that the significance of the missing evidence must be weighed in the context of the overall fairness of the proceedings).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the history of the case and the Appellant’s persistent refusal to comply with discovery orders. The five-factor balancing test from *Cioffi* supports the conclusion that default judgment was the only appropriate remedy given the willful noncompliance, the irreparable prejudice caused by the decedent’s death, and the failure of lesser sanctions. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering the orders dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013.

Accordingly, we affirm.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO CONSIDER AND IMPLEMENT LESSER SANCTIONS BEFORE ENTERING DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The Plaintiff contends that the trial court properly considered lesser sanctions because contempt orders were issued in 2009 and 2010. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the requirement under Pennsylvania law. The mere issuance of contempt orders does not satisfy the obligation to consider lesser sanctions; rather, the court must actively evaluate whether less severe alternatives could achieve the purposes of justice. *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318, 1322 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989).

The record demonstrates that the trial court failed to consider numerous lesser sanctions that could have addressed the Plaintiff's concerns without visiting the ultimate penalty upon a deceased person's estate. Monetary sanctions, evidentiary inferences, or the striking of certain defenses would have been proportionate responses to the alleged discovery violations. *See Balch v. Ferrara*, 542 A.2d 585, 589 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1988) (holding that default judgment is appropriate only when lesser sanctions would be futile).

The Plaintiff's reliance on *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), is misplaced. In *Tuma*, this Court affirmed default judgment because the defendant had actively evaded service and refused to comply with multiple court orders over an extended period. Here, by contrast, Diefenderfer was not actively evading service after being located in 2009; rather, he objected to the specific condition that his deposition be videotaped. This objection, while ultimately unavailing, was not the equivalent of a willful refusal to participate in the litigation altogether.

Moreover, the Plaintiff's argument that contempt orders were "ignored" ignores the procedural reality that the court never enforced these orders through incarceration or monetary penalties. The court had the authority to compel compliance through coercive measures but failed to do so. By the time Diefenderfer died in January 2012, the opportunity to impose effective lesser sanctions had not been exhausted—it had simply been unexercised.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO EXERCISE DUE DILIGENCE IN PURSUING DISCOVERY, AND THIS FAILURE CONTRIBUTED TO THE PREJUDICE ALLEGED

The Plaintiff claims that it exercised due diligence by hiring a private investigator in 2009. However, the timeline reveals a more troubling pattern of inaction. The Plaintiff first requested a deposition in June 2007, but waited until 2009 to hire a private investigator—two years of inactivity during which the Plaintiff's counsel accepted the Defendant's counsel's representations that he could not locate Diefenderfer without question.

In *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143, 1147 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), this Court held that a plaintiff's failure to pursue discovery with diligence precludes the imposition of severe sanctions. While the Plaintiff in this case eventually hired an investigator, the seven-year delay between initial filing and Diefenderfer's death provided ample opportunity for more aggressive judicial intervention. The Plaintiff could have sought bench warrants for Diefenderfer's arrest upon his failure to appear, or moved for sanctions earlier than May 2009.

The Plaintiff argues that the delay was caused by Diefenderfer's evasion. While Diefenderfer did fail to appear for scheduled depositions, the record shows that his counsel consistently represented that he could not locate his client. The Plaintiff accepted these representations without challenge until 2009. Had the Plaintiff sought judicial intervention earlier—through motions to compel or requests for sanctions—the court could have ordered more effective remedies before Diefenderfer's death rendered the testimony unavailable.

The Plaintiff's reliance on *Gillespie v. Johns-Manville Corp.*, 595 A.2d 1161 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), is unavailing. In *Gillespie*, the Court found due diligence where the plaintiff had made repeated attempts to locate the defendant over a multi-year period. Here, by contrast, the Plaintiff waited two years after the initial deposition request before taking any meaningful steps to locate Diefenderfer. This delay contributed directly to the prejudice the Plaintiff now alleges.

## III. THE DEFAULT JUDGMENT IS UNSUPPORTED BECAUSE THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH THAT DIEFENDERFER WAS NEGLIGENT

The Plaintiff argues that this Court should not consider the merits of the underlying negligence claim because the trial court did not adjudicate those merits. This argument conflates two distinct concepts: the appropriateness of a sanction and the proportionality of that sanction to the underlying conduct.

Even accepting that Diefenderfer committed discovery violations, the extreme sanction of default judgment must be proportionate to the nature and severity of those violations. In *Kraemer v. United States*, 759 F.2d 287, 293 (3d Cir. 1985), the Court held that mere presence at the scene of an incident does not establish negligence. Similarly, in *Byrd v. McCausland*, 34 A.2d 892, 894 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1943), this Court held that a defendant cannot be held liable for negligence absent evidence of a duty, breach, causation, and damages.

The record establishes that the fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove and was accelerated by kerosene leaking from a defective container. There is no evidence in the record that Diefenderfer placed the pot on the stove, that he was responsible for the kerosene container, or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The Plaintiff has presented no expert testimony, no eyewitness accounts, and no physical evidence linking Diefenderfer to the cause of the fire.

Entering default judgment against Diefenderfer's estate effectively imposes liability without any determination that he was actually negligent. This result violates fundamental principles of due process. *See Estate of McCauley*, 565 A.2d 108, 112 (Pa. 1989) (holding that an estate cannot be held liable for claims not properly established against the decedent).

The Plaintiff argues that Diefenderfer's testimony was necessary to establish negligence. This argument proves too much. The burden of proving negligence rests with the Plaintiff, not with the Defendant. If the Plaintiff cannot establish negligence through other means, that is a failure of proof—not a basis for shifting the burden through default judgment. The purpose of discovery sanctions is to punish obstruction, not to compensate for the inability to prove a case.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY IMPOSING THE MOST SEVERE SANCTION AVAILABLE WITHOUT ADEQUATE FINDINGS

The trial court's orders dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, fail to articulate adequate findings supporting the imposition of default judgment. Under Pennsylvania law, a trial court entering default judgment as a sanction must make specific findings on the record addressing the five factors enumerated in *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928, 931 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990).

Here, the trial court's orders contain only conclusory statements that the court found "the most appropriate sanction" to be default judgment. The orders do not address whether monetary sanctions were considered and found inadequate, do not explain why evidentiary inferences would not suffice, and do not analyze whether the Plaintiff's alleged prejudice could have been cured through less restrictive means.

In *Cioffi*, this Court emphasized that "the significance of the missing evidence must be weighed in the context of the overall fairness of the proceedings." 584 A.2d at 931. The trial court failed to conduct this weighing exercise. Instead, it appears to have automatically defaulted in favor of the Plaintiff upon finding discovery violations, without considering whether that remedy was proportionate to the offense.

The Plaintiff argues that the trial court's discretion is broad and should not be second-guessed on appeal. However, discretion is not arbitrary authority. When a trial court fails to articulate its reasoning, an appellate court cannot determine whether the discretion was properly exercised. *See Pittsburgh Terminals Corp. v. The Plaintiff argues that the estate inherits the consequences of the decedent's discovery violations and cannot evade sanctions simply because the party is deceased. Ct. 2001), this Court held that severe sanctions are appropriate when a party demonstrates "deliberate avoidance of the litigation process." However, Brown involved a living defendant who could be compelled to comply with court orders. The situation here is fundamentally different: Diefenderfer is deceased and cannot be imprisoned, fined, or compelled to testify. The estate has no ability to produce testimony that the decedent refused to give.

Diefenderfer did not die to avoid testimony; he died of natural causes in January 2012, four years after the fire and five years after the litigation began. There is no evidence that he orchestrated his own death to avoid deposition.

The appropriate remedy for discovery violations when a party dies is not default judgment but rather an assessment of whether the Plaintiff's case can proceed on the evidence available. If the Plaintiff cannot establish negligence without the decedent's testimony, the proper remedy is dismissal of the claim—not default judgment against the estate.

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**BROBINSON, J.**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the orders of the Court of Common Pleas entering default judgment against the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer as a sanction for discovery violations. The Appellant, Michael J. D’Aniello, administrator of the estate, challenges the trial court’s decision, arguing that it abused its discretion by failing to exhaust lesser sanctions, that the Appellee failed to exercise due diligence, and that the sanction is disproportionate because negligence was not established on the merits. After careful consideration of the parties’ rebuttal arguments and the applicable precedent, we affirm the orders of the trial court.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a sanction for discovery abuse is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court. *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928, 931 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990). An appellate court will not reverse the decision of a trial court absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. *Pittsburgh Terminals Corp. v. Midway Coal Co.*, 470 A.2d 107, 110 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1983). An abuse of discretion occurs if the law is overridden or misapplied, or if the judgment exercised is manifestly unreasonable, partial, or prejudicial. *Id.*

**II. DISCUSSION**

The Appellant raises three primary arguments on appeal: (1) the trial court failed to consider and implement lesser sanctions; (2) the Appellee failed to exercise due diligence, contributing to the alleged prejudice; and (3) the default judgment is improper because the evidence does not establish negligence. We address each in turn.

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Determining That Lesser Sanctions Were Ineffective**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by failing to actively evaluate and implement lesser sanctions, such as monetary fines or evidentiary prohibitions, before resorting to the "ultimate sanction" of default judgment. The Appellant relies on *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318, 1322 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), for the proposition that a court must consider less severe alternatives.

We acknowledge that *Capehorn* requires a trial court to consider whether lesser sanctions would be appropriate. However, the record does not support the Appellant’s claim that the trial court failed to do so. The trial court entered two separate contempt orders—in July 2009 and August 2010—compelling Diefenderfer’s appearance for a videotaped deposition. These orders were specific directives designed to cure the discovery violation. When a party defies court orders, the trial court is not required to engage in a ritualistic exhaustion of every conceivable coercive measure if it determines that the party’s conduct renders such measures futile. *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935, 938 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989) (affirming default judgment where lesser sanctions were unlikely to compel compliance).

Here, the record demonstrates that lesser sanctions were indeed ineffective. Following the August 2010 order, which specifically addressed Diefenderfer’s privacy concerns by limiting the videotape’s use, Diefenderfer’s counsel notified the court that Diefenderfer stated, “there is no way I’m getting involved,” and that he would not appear “nor any other time.” This categorical refusal to comply with any court order signaled that further coercive measures, such as monetary fines or the threat of incarceration, would not produce the necessary testimony. The trial court was well within its discretion to conclude that continued attempts to compel a witness who had expressly vowed to disobey the court would be a futile exercise. *Balch v. Ferrara*, 542 A.2d 585, 589 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1988) (holding that default judgment is appropriate when lesser sanctions would be futile).

**B. The Appellee Exercised Due Diligence in Pursuing Discovery**

The Appellant contends that the Appellee failed to exercise due diligence, pointing to a two-year gap between the initial deposition request in June 2007 and the hiring of a private investigator in 2009. The Appellant cites *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), to argue that this delay precludes severe sanctions.

We find the Appellant’s reliance on *Baker* misplaced. In *Baker*, the plaintiff failed to seek court intervention for years despite the defendant’s noncompliance. Here, the record shows that the delay was largely attributable to the defense counsel’s repeated representations that they could not locate their own client. The Appellee’s counsel reasonably relied on these representations and, when it became apparent that Diefenderfer was evading service, promptly hired a private investigator in January 2009. *Gillespie v. Johns-Manville Corp.*, 595 A.2d 1161, 1165 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991) (holding that a plaintiff’s diligent efforts to locate a noncompliant party, including hiring an investigator, demonstrate due diligence).

Furthermore, once Diefenderfer was located, the Appellee moved aggressively for sanctions, filing motions for contempt in May 2009 and May 2010. The Appellee cannot be blamed for the seven-year duration of the litigation when that duration was caused by Diefenderfer’s evasion and subsequent refusal to comply. The trial court correctly found that the Appellee acted with sufficient diligence to warrant the relief sought.

**C. The Default Judgment Is a Proportionate Sanction for Discovery Abuse, Not a Finding of Negligence on the Merits**

The Appellant argues that the default judgment is unjust because the evidence does not establish that Diefenderfer was negligent, relying on *Kraemer v. United States*, 759 F.2d 287 (3d Cir. 1985), and *Byrd v. McCausland*, 34 A.2d 892 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1943). This argument misconstrues the nature of the sanction.

The trial court did not enter default judgment based on a finding that Diefenderfer was negligent as a matter of law. Rather, it entered judgment as a sanction for Diefenderfer’s willful refusal to comply with discovery orders, which effectively precluded the Appellee from proving his case. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 931 (stating that the significance of the missing evidence must be weighed in the context of the overall fairness of the proceedings). Diefenderfer was the sole occupant of the residence at the time of the fire. His testimony regarding the unattended pot and the kerosene container was indispensable to determining liability. By refusing to provide this testimony and subsequently dying, Diefenderfer rendered the Appellee’s ability to prove negligence impossible.

The purpose of the sanction is not to punish the estate for the underlying tort, but to remedy the prejudice caused by the discovery abuse. The prejudice here is total: the Appellee has been deprived of the only witness who could explain the events leading to the fire. In such circumstances, the extreme sanction of default judgment is not only appropriate but necessary to preserve the integrity of the judicial process. *Commonwealth v. Brown*, 772 A.2d 1115, 1119 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2001) (finding severe sanctions justified where a party demonstrates deliberate avoidance of the litigation process).

The Appellant’s argument that it is unfair to punish the estate for the decedent’s refusal is unpersuasive. An estate stands in the shoes of the decedent for purposes of litigation. It would be contrary to public policy to allow a party to evade discovery obligations and shield their estate from liability simply by passing away before the completion of the litigation. The trial court’s decision ensures that the estate is held accountable for the consequences of the decedent’s litigation conduct.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the *Cioffi* factors and the Appellant’s arguments. It found that the degree of noncompliance was egregious, the refusal was willful, the prejudice to the Appellee was irreparable, and lesser sanctions had proven ineffective. These findings are supported by the record and are not an abuse of discretion. The Appellant’s arguments regarding due diligence and the merits of the underlying negligence claim do not warrant reversal.

Accordingly, we affirm the orders of the Court of Common Pleas dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, members of the Court, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the plaintiff's appellate brief. The defense appeals the trial court's orders entering default judgment against the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the ultimate sanction of default judgment without adequate consideration of lesser sanctions, that the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery, and that the evidence does not support a finding that Diefenderfer's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. We urge this Court to reverse the trial court's orders.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO EXHAUST LESSER SANCTIONS

The plaintiff contends that the trial court properly considered lesser sanctions because it issued two contempt orders before entering default judgment. This argument conflates the issuance of court orders with the thoughtful consideration of alternatives required under Pennsylvania law. The record demonstrates that the trial court failed to meaningfully evaluate or impose less severe sanctions before resorting to the extreme remedy of default judgment.

In *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318, 1322 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), this Court held that trial courts must consider whether lesser sanctions would be appropriate before imposing severe discovery sanctions. The trial court here entered two orders compelling Diefenderfer's appearance, but these orders were not meaningful lesser sanctions—they were merely precursors to the ultimate sanction. The court never considered monetary sanctions, evidentiary prohibitions, or other less intrusive remedies that might have addressed the discovery violation without terminating the litigation entirely.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935, 938 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), is misplaced. In *Tuma*, the court found that lesser sanctions would be futile because the party had explicitly stated he would not comply with any order. However, the record here shows that Diefenderfer's statements came after years of proceedings during which the trial court never imposed any intermediate sanctions such as fines or other coercive measures. The futility determination cannot be made without first attempting lesser sanctions. The defense is correct that the trial court erred in failing to actively evaluate and implement lesser sanctions before resorting to default judgment.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO EXERCISE DUE DILIGENCE

The plaintiff argues that it exercised due diligence in pursuing Diefenderfer's deposition, pointing to the hiring of a private investigator in 2009. However, the record reveals a critical two-year gap between the initial deposition request in June 2007 and the plaintiff's decision to hire a private investigator in January 2009. During this period, the plaintiff relied solely on representations from defense counsel that they could not locate Diefenderfer, without seeking court intervention or taking independent steps to locate the defendant.

In *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143, 1147 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), this Court held that a plaintiff's failure to diligently pursue discovery precludes the imposition of severe sanctions. Although the plaintiff distinguishes *Baker* on its facts, the principle remains: a party cannot sleep on its rights and then demand the most severe sanction available when its own inaction contributed to the delay. The plaintiff knew by August 2007 that defense counsel could not locate Diefenderfer, yet waited until January 2009 to hire an investigator. This twenty-month delay is inexplicable and demonstrates a lack of due diligence that should bar the extreme sanction of default judgment.

Furthermore, once Diefenderfer was located, the plaintiff pursued aggressive sanctions rather than working with the defense to schedule a deposition. The plaintiff's refusal to accommodate Diefenderfer's privacy concerns regarding videotaping—despite court orders addressing those concerns—demonstrates a lack of good faith effort to obtain the discovery. The plaintiff could have accepted a non-videotaped deposition, which would have provided the same substantive testimony without the privacy issues that Diefenderfer raised. The plaintiff's intransigence contributed to the breakdown in discovery.

## III. THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH THAT DIEFENDERFER'S CONDUCT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN CAUSING THE FIRE

Most critically, the plaintiff confuses the purpose of discovery sanctions with the merits of the underlying claim. The trial court entered default judgment not because Diefenderfer was found to be negligent, but as a sanction for discovery violations. However, the record contains no evidence establishing that Diefenderfer's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire.

The facts establish that the fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove. The plaintiff argues that Diefenderfer was the only other person in the house and that his testimony was indispensable. However, the cause of the fire was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The rapid progression of the fire was caused by kerosene leaking from a defective container stored near the stove. Neither of these facts depends on Diefenderfer's testimony—the fire marshal's report and physical evidence established these facts independently.

Moreover, Diefenderfer gave an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire denying that he was the cause of the fire. This statement, while not under oath, is part of the record and demonstrates that Diefenderfer consistently maintained he was not responsible. The plaintiff has produced no expert testimony or other evidence establishing that Diefenderfer's alleged falling asleep on the couch was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The presence of an unattended pot and defective kerosene container—neither of which Diefenderfer placed on the stove or stored near it—suggests causes wholly independent of Diefenderfer's conduct.

In *Kraemer v. United States*, 759 F.2d 287, 293 (3d Cir. 1985), the court emphasized that default judgments are extraordinary remedies that should not be granted lightly. In *Byrd v. McCausland*, 34 A.2d 892, 894 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1943), this Court held that a party moving for default judgment must establish a right to relief on the merits. Here, the plaintiff has not established—and cannot establish without Diefenderfer's testimony—that Diefenderfer's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The default judgment effectively awards the plaintiff relief on a claim that has never been proven.

## IV. THE SANCTION IS DISPROPORTIONATE AND UNJUST

The trial court's decision to enter default judgment as a discovery sanction is disproportionate to the alleged violation. Default judgment is the most severe sanction available to a trial court, effectively terminating the litigation and awarding judgment to the plaintiff without any consideration of the merits. Such a sanction should be reserved for the most egregious cases where lesser sanctions have proven ineffective and the moving party has suffered actual prejudice.

In *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928, 931 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990), this Court outlined the factors trial courts must consider before imposing default judgment as a discovery sanction: the nature of the violation, the willfulness of the noncompliance, the prejudice to the opposing party, and the effectiveness of lesser sanctions. While the trial court found that Diefenderfer's noncompliance was willful, the record does not support a finding of actual prejudice to the plaintiff. The plaintiff has not identified any specific evidence that would have been obtained through Diefenderfer's deposition that would have changed the outcome of this case.

The plaintiff argues that Diefenderfer was the only witness who could explain the events leading to the fire. However, the physical evidence—the unattended pot and the defective kerosene container—established the cause of the fire independently. Diefenderfer's testimony would have been relevant only to the question of whether he placed the pot on the stove or stored the kerosene container near it. Without any evidence suggesting that Diefenderfer was responsible for either condition, his testimony would not have been outcome-determinative.

Furthermore, the prejudice, if any, was not caused solely by Diefenderfer's refusal to cooperate. The plaintiff waited two years to hire a private investigator, then refused to accommodate legitimate privacy concerns about videotaping. The plaintiff's own conduct contributed to the inability to obtain Diefenderfer's testimony. Under these circumstances, the extreme sanction of default judgment is unjust.

## V. THE DEFENSE'S KEY POINTS

The defense submits that this Court should reverse the trial court's orders for the following reasons:

**First**, the trial court failed to meaningfully consider or impose lesser sanctions before entering default judgment. The issuance of two contempt orders does not satisfy the requirement to consider alternatives under *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318, 1322 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989).

**Second**, the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery, waiting twenty months after learning that defense counsel could not locate Diefenderfer before hiring a private investigator. Under *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143, 1147 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), this lack of diligence precludes severe sanctions.

**Third**, the evidence does not establish that Diefenderfer's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The cause was an unattended pot and defective kerosene container—neither attributable to Diefenderfer. Default judgment effectively awards relief on an unproven claim.

**Fourth**, the sanction is disproportionate and unjust. The plaintiff has not demonstrated actual prejudice, and the plaintiff's own conduct contributed to the discovery difficulties. The extreme remedy of default judgment is not warranted under these circumstances.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the Court of Common Pleas dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, and remand this matter for further proceedings. The trial court abused its discretion in imposing the ultimate sanction of default judgment without adequate consideration of lesser sanctions, in light of the plaintiff's lack of due diligence and the absence of evidence establishing Diefenderfer's negligence.

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**BROBINSON, J.**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the orders of the Court of Common Pleas entering default judgment against the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer as a sanction for discovery violations. The Appellant, Michael J. D’Aniello, administrator of the estate, challenges the trial court’s decision, arguing that it abused its discretion by failing to exhaust lesser sanctions, that the Appellee failed to exercise due diligence, and that the sanction is disproportionate because negligence was not established on the merits. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the applicable precedent, we affirm the orders of the trial court.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a sanction for discovery abuse is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court. *Cioffi v. Guenther*, 584 A.2d 928, 931 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990). An appellate court will not reverse the decision of a trial court absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. *Pittsburgh Terminals Corp. v. Midway Coal Co.*, 470 A.2d 107, 110 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1983). An abuse of discretion occurs if the law is overridden or misapplied, or if the judgment exercised is manifestly unreasonable, partial, or prejudicial. *Id.*

**II. DISCUSSION**

The Appellant raises three primary arguments on appeal: (1) the trial court failed to consider and implement lesser sanctions; (2) the Appellee failed to exercise due diligence, contributing to the alleged prejudice; and (3) the default judgment is improper because the evidence does not establish negligence. We address each in turn.

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Determining That Lesser Sanctions Were Ineffective**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by failing to actively evaluate and implement lesser sanctions, such as monetary fines or evidentiary prohibitions, before resorting to the "ultimate sanction" of default judgment. The Appellant relies on *Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Capehorn*, 562 A.2d 1318, 1322 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989), for the proposition that a court must consider less severe alternatives.

We acknowledge that *Capehorn* requires a trial court to consider whether lesser sanctions would be appropriate. However, the record does not support the Appellant’s claim that the trial court failed to do so. The trial court entered two separate contempt orders—in July 2009 and August 2010—compelling Diefenderfer’s appearance for a videotaped deposition. These orders were specific directives designed to cure the discovery violation. When a party defies court orders, the trial court is not required to engage in a ritualistic exhaustion of every conceivable coercive measure if it determines that the party’s conduct renders such measures futile. *Tuma v. Griesinger*, 564 A.2d 935, 938 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1989) (affirming default judgment where lesser sanctions were unlikely to compel compliance).

Here, the record demonstrates that lesser sanctions were indeed ineffective. Following the August 2010 order, which specifically addressed Diefenderfer’s privacy concerns by limiting the videotape’s use, Diefenderfer’s counsel notified the court that Diefenderfer stated, “there is no way I’m getting involved,” and that he would not appear “nor any other time.” This categorical refusal to comply with any court order signaled that further coercive measures, such as monetary fines or the threat of incarceration, would not produce the necessary testimony. The trial court was well within its discretion to conclude that continued attempts to compel a witness who had expressly vowed to disobey the court would be a futile exercise. *Balch v. Ferrara*, 542 A.2d 585, 589 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1988) (holding that default judgment is appropriate when lesser sanctions would be futile).

**B. The Appellee Exercised Due Diligence in Pursuing Discovery**

The Appellant contends that the Appellee failed to exercise due diligence, pointing to a two-year gap between the initial deposition request in June 2007 and the hiring of a private investigator in 2009. The Appellant cites *Baker v. Chevron Corp.*, 680 A.2d 1143 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1996), to argue that this delay precludes severe sanctions.

We find the Appellant’s reliance on *Baker* misplaced. In *Baker*, the plaintiff failed to seek court intervention for years despite the defendant’s noncompliance. Here, the record shows that the delay was largely attributable to the defense counsel’s repeated representations that they could not locate their own client. The Appellee’s counsel reasonably relied on these representations and, when it became apparent that Diefenderfer was evading service, promptly hired a private investigator in January 2009. *Gillespie v. Johns-Manville Corp.*, 595 A.2d 1161, 1165 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991) (holding that a plaintiff’s diligent efforts to locate a noncompliant party, including hiring an investigator, demonstrate due diligence).

Furthermore, once Diefenderfer was located, the Appellee moved aggressively for sanctions, filing motions for contempt in May 2009 and May 2010. The Appellee cannot be blamed for the seven-year duration of the litigation when that duration was caused by Diefenderfer’s evasion and subsequent refusal to comply. The trial court correctly found that the Appellee acted with sufficient diligence to warrant the relief sought.

**C. The Default Judgment Is a Proportionate Sanction for Discovery Abuse, Not a Finding of Negligence on the Merits**

The Appellant argues that the default judgment is unjust because the evidence does not establish that Diefenderfer was negligent, relying on *Kraemer v. United States*, 759 F.2d 287 (3d Cir. 1985), and *Byrd v. McCausland*, 34 A.2d 892 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1943). This argument misconstrues the nature of the sanction.

The trial court did not enter default judgment based on a finding that Diefenderfer was negligent as a matter of law. Rather, it entered judgment as a sanction for Diefenderfer’s willful refusal to comply with discovery orders, which effectively precluded the Appellee from proving his case. *Cioffi*, 584 A.2d at 931 (stating that the significance of the missing evidence must be weighed in the context of the overall fairness of the proceedings). Diefenderfer was the sole occupant of the residence at the time of the fire. His testimony regarding the unattended pot and the kerosene container was indispensable to determining liability. The Appellant’s assertion that the physical evidence alone establishes the cause of the fire ignores the question of negligence. While the fire marshal’s report identified the physical cause (unattended pot and kerosene), it could not establish who was responsible for leaving the pot unattended or storing the defective container near the stove. As the tenant and the only other person present, Diefenderfer’s testimony was the only evidence that could rebut the inference of negligence arising from his control of the premises.

By refusing to provide this testimony and subsequently dying, Diefenderfer rendered the Appellee’s ability to prove negligence impossible. The purpose of the sanction is not to punish the estate for the underlying tort, but to remedy the prejudice caused by the discovery abuse. The prejudice here is total: the Appellee has been deprived of the only witness who could explain the events leading to the fire. In such circumstances, the extreme sanction of default judgment is not only appropriate but necessary to preserve the integrity of the judicial process. *Commonwealth v. Brown*, 772 A.2d 1115, 1119 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2001) (finding severe sanctions justified where a party demonstrates deliberate avoidance of the litigation process).

The Appellant’s argument that it is unfair to punish the estate for the decedent’s refusal is unpersuasive. An estate stands in the shoes of the decedent for purposes of litigation. It would be contrary to public policy to allow a party to evade discovery obligations and shield their estate from liability simply by passing away before the completion of the litigation. The trial court’s decision ensures that the estate is held accountable for the consequences of the decedent’s litigation conduct.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the *Cioffi* factors and the Appellant’s arguments. It found that the degree of noncompliance was egregious, the refusal was willful, the prejudice to the Appellee was irreparable, and lesser sanctions had proven ineffective. These findings are supported by the record and are not an abuse of discretion. The Appellant’s arguments regarding due diligence and the merits of the underlying negligence claim do not warrant reversal.

Accordingly, we affirm the orders of the Court of Common Pleas dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013.
