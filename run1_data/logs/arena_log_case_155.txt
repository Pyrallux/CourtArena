**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, the district court committed reversible error in two fundamental respects: first, by recognizing a Bivens remedy for substantive due process claims in this novel context, and second, by denying qualified immunity to Secretary Rumsfeld. This Court should reverse because Bivens claims are disfavored outside the original Fourth Amendment context, and the law was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct.  

**First, the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy.** Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), established a narrow cause of action for Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents. Since then, the Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against extending Bivens to new contexts. As the Court held in Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007), "we have never extended Bivens to any new context or new category of defendants," and courts must "exercise great caution" before doing so. The Court in Wilkie emphasized that "the Constitution does not require Congress to create a remedy for every constitutional violation, and courts should not create new Bivens remedies in the absence of congressional direction." Here, the district court extended Bivens to substantive due process claims in a detention context that has never been recognized before. There are special factors counseling hesitation—namely, the potential for judicial interference with executive branch decision-making in areas where Congress has not provided a statutory remedy. This alone is sufficient to bar extension of Bivens.  

**Second, even if a Bivens remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.** Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process claims is inherently vague and fact-specific. As the Supreme Court explained in County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998), "only the most egregious official conduct" shocks the conscience. There is no prior precedent that clearly establishes that the specific practices alleged—such as solitary confinement, denial of counsel, and physical mistreatment—violate substantive due process in this context. As this Court held in Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002), "the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." Without such clear precedent, Secretary Rumsfeld could not have known his actions were unconstitutional, and qualified immunity bars the claim.  

In sum, the district court’s decision to recognize a Bivens remedy in this novel context and deny qualified immunity contravenes Supreme Court precedent. For these reasons, we respectfully request that this Court reverse and dismiss Doe’s claims.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS

**DEFENSE OPENING BRIEF OF APPELLEE JOHN DOE**

---

Your Honor, the appellee's response addresses the two issues presented in this interlocutory appeal: (1) whether the district court properly denied Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss on the basis that a Bivens remedy is available for substantive due process claims arising from military detention, and (2) whether Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity at this pleading stage. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED THE MOTION TO DISMISS ON THE BIVENS CLAIM

### A. The Factual Allegations Establish a Novel Yet Viable Bivens Context

The complaint, accepted as true at this stage, establishes that John Doe, a United States citizen, was detained by United States military personnel in Iraq for nine months without formal charges. During his detention, Doe alleges he was subjected to: (1) solitary confinement for three months; (2) interrogation while blindfolded and hooded; (3) denial of requests for an attorney; (4) physical mistreatment including being kicked in the back and choked; (5) exposure to extreme temperatures; (6) deprivation of sleep; and (7) placement in a cell with hostile detainees who were encouraged to attack him. These allegations describe a comprehensive pattern of mistreatment that, if proven, would constitute the most egregious violations of constitutional rights.

The district court correctly identified this as a substantive due process claim arising from military detention of a United States citizen—a context in which the constitutional right to liberty without due process of law is at its strongest. Unlike the commercial or regulatory contexts in which the Supreme Court has declined to extend Bivens, here we have direct governmental intrusion upon the most fundamental of all constitutional rights: freedom from unlawful imprisonment.

### B. The District Court Correctly Applied the Bivens Framework

The Supreme Court in Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), recognized an implied cause of action for damages against federal officers for Fourth Amendment violations. While the Court has expressed caution about extending Bivens to new contexts, it has never categorically barred such extensions. In Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14, 18-19 (1980), the Court explicitly held that Bivens remedies are available unless "special factors counseling hesitation" are present.

The special factors analysis requires this Court to consider whether: (1) there is an alternative remedial scheme available; (2) the case involves military discretion; and (3) congressional silence indicates reluctance to create a remedy. Here, these factors do not counsel against a Bivens remedy.

**First**, no adequate alternative remedial scheme exists.** The Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd, does not provide a private cause of action, as the district court correctly held. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F Supp.2d 94, 104-05 (D.D.C. 2011). The Military Commissions Act similarly does not provide a civil remedy for a citizen detained domestically. Thus, without a Bivens remedy, Doe would have no judicial avenue to seek compensation for his alleged constitutional injuries.

**Second**, while military discretion is involved, it is not absolute.** The Supreme Court in Department of Navy v. Egan, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), recognized executive discretion in military matters, but this discretion does not extend to violating the constitutional rights of United States citizens. As the Court held in Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2, 125-26 (1866), even during wartime, civilian courts retain jurisdiction over claims that military officials have violated fundamental rights. The detention of a U.S. citizen without charges or access to counsel presents precisely the type of constitutional violation that civilian courts must be permitted to address.

**Third**, congressional silence does not indicate reluctance here.** In FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471, 484 (1994), the Court noted that congressional silence may sometimes counsel hesitation, but only when Congress has considered and rejected providing a remedy. Here, Congress has not considered the specific question of whether Bivens remedies should be available for substantive due process claims arising from military detention of U.S. citizens. The absence of legislation is not the equivalent of congressional rejection.

### C. The Cases Cited by Secretary Rumsfeld Are Distinguishable

Secretary Rumsfeld's reliance on Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537 (2007), is misplaced. In Wilkie, the plaintiff alleged that a federal agency official coerced a settlement through extortionate threats. The Court declined to extend Bivens, emphasizing that the case involved "a dispute over private property" rather than a direct constitutional violation. Id. at 550. Here, by contrast, Doe's claim involves the most fundamental of constitutional rights—the right to liberty itself. The distinction between property disputes and liberty interests is constitutionally significant.

Similarly, in Bush v. Lucas, 462 U.S. 367 (1983), the Court declined to create a Bivens remedy for a federal employee's First Amendment retaliation claim, noting that Congress had provided an extensive remedial scheme through the civil service laws. No analogous comprehensive remedial scheme exists here for detainees alleging constitutional violations.

The district court correctly noted that this Circuit's precedent in Abu Ali v. Rumsfeld, 326 F. App'x 1 (D.C. Cir. 2009), is not controlling because the plaintiff in that case was an enemy combatant detained abroad. Doe, by contrast, is a United States citizen detained in U.S. military facilities. The constitutional rights of citizens are fundamentally different from those of enemy combatants, and this distinction was properly recognized by the district court.

---

## II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS NOT ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY AT THIS STAGE

### A. The Pleaded Facts, Accepted as True, Establish a Violement of a Clearly Established Right

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). At the motion to dismiss stage, the question is whether the complaint pleads facts that, if proven, would establish a violation of a clearly established right. The Court does not weigh evidence or resolve factual disputes.

The district court correctly held that Doe has alleged a violation of his substantive due process right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience." As the Supreme Court established in County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998), "only the most egregious official conduct" shocks the conscience. The complaint alleges conduct that meets this demanding standard.

Doe was detained for nine months without formal charges. He was held in solitary confinement for three months. He was blindfolded, hooded, and subjected to extreme temperatures and sleep deprivation. He was physically assaulted—kicked in the back and choked. He was denied access to an attorney. He was placed in a cell with hostile detainees whom guards encouraged to attack him. These allegations, taken together, describe conduct that far exceeds the threshold for substantive due process violations.

### B. The Right Was Clearly Established in 2005

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the contours of substantive due process were not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. This argument fails for several reasons.

**First**, the right to be free from arbitrary governmental detention without due process of law is among the most clearly established constitutional rights.** The Fifth Amendment's guarantee that no person shall be deprived of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law" has been fundamental since the founding. A reasonable government official would understand that detaining a U.S. citizen for nine months without charges or access to counsel would violate this right.

**Second**, the "shock the conscience" standard, while fact-intensive, provides clear notice when conduct crosses the line.** In Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002), the Court held that "the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." Here, the alleged conduct—nine months of solitary confinement, physical abuse, denial of counsel, and placement with hostile detainees—would be understood by any reasonable official to violate constitutional rights.

**Third**, even if some uncertainty existed regarding the application of substantive due process to military detention, Secretary Rumsfeld's own conduct was so egregious that it falls squarely within clearly established violations.** The denial of access to counsel for nine months, combined with physical mistreatment, is not a close case. As the Court noted in Hope, 536 U.S. at 745, "a reasonable officer would conclude that a severe pain lockup" violates the Eighth Amendment. Similarly, a reasonable officer would conclude that the treatment alleged here violates substantive due process.

### C. The Individual Capacity Claim Against Secretary Rumsfeld Is Properly Pleaded

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that he cannot be held personally liable because he did not personally participate in the alleged misconduct. This argument fails. In Bivens actions, a supervisor may be held liable under certain circumstances. In Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 676 (2009), the Court held that supervisory liability is available where the supervisor "knew of, and acquiesced in, the unconstitutional conduct."

The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld was responsible for the policies and practices that led to Doe's detention and mistreatment. Doe alleges that the treatment he received was the result of policies authorized at the highest levels of the Department of Defense. These allegations are sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. The district court correctly held that Doe "sufficiently pled facts to support a claim that Secretary Rumsfeld violated that right." Doe, 800 F Supp.2d at 115.

---

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the appellee respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court correctly held that a Bivens remedy is available for substantive due process claims arising from the military detention of a United States citizen, and that Doe has pleaded sufficient facts to survive a qualified immunity defense at this pleading stage.

The alternative remedy—allowing government officials to detain United States citizens without due process of law and without any judicial remedy for constitutional violations—would eviscerate the fundamental protections embodied in the Fifth Amendment. The Framers did not intend for constitutional rights to be contingent upon congressional action, and this Court should not create a categorical bar to judicial review of the most fundamental of all constitutional rights: liberty.

Respectfully submitted,

**APPELLEE JOHN DOE**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the interlocutory appeal, the Court reverses the order of the district court.**

The district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens* for the Appellee’s substantive due process claims and further erred in denying Appellant Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion for qualified immunity. While the Appellee’s allegations describe harsh treatment, the legal framework for extending *Bivens* actions to the context of military detention in a war zone, and the application of qualified immunity to high-ranking officials in such contexts, precludes the continuation of this action at this time.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity and the legal sufficiency of a *Bivens* claim. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir. 2008). In evaluating the motion to dismiss, the Court accepts the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construes reasonable inferences in the Appellee’s favor. See *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009).

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the implication of a private cause of action for damages against federal officers for constitutional violations—first recognized in *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971)—is a "disfavored" judicial undertaking. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The Court has emphasized that it "has never extended *Bivens* to any new context or new category of defendants," and lower courts are instructed to exercise great caution before doing so. *Wilkie*, 551 U.S. at 550.

To determine whether a *Bivens* remedy should be extended, a court must consider whether there are "special factors counselling hesitation" in the absence of affirmative action by Congress. *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 18 (1980). The Appellant correctly identifies that the military detention context presents such special factors. The detention of individuals in a combat zone involves national security and military discretion that are "the province of the political branches." *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988).

The Appellee argues that the absence of an alternative remedial scheme and the fundamental nature of the liberty interest at stake weigh in favor of implying a remedy. While the Court acknowledges the Appellee’s argument that the Detainee Treatment Act does not provide a private cause of action, the presence of "special factors" is not defeated solely by the lack of a statutory alternative. The implication of a *Bivens* action in this context would require the judiciary to intrude upon the "distinctively military" realm, specifically regarding the detention and interrogation policies employed during active hostilities in Iraq. *Wilkie*, 551 U.S. at 550. The separation-of-powers implications and the risk of interfering with military judgment constitute "special factors counselling hesitation" that are sufficient to preclude the creation of a new *Bivens* remedy in this context. Consequently, the district court erred in implying a cause of action where Congress has not authorized such a suit and where military discretion is heavily implicated.

**III. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even assuming arguendo that a *Bivens* remedy could be implied, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless the official violated a "clearly established statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). The Appellee bears the burden of showing that the right was clearly established at the time of the alleged violation. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730, 739 (2003).

The Appellee relies on the "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process violations. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998). However, the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and does not automatically provide fair warning to officials that their conduct is unlawful. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846 n.8. The Appellee alleges severe mistreatment, including solitary confinement, physical abuse, and denial of counsel. While these allegations are disturbing, the question is whether existing law in 2005 clearly established that such treatment of a U.S. citizen detained as a security threat in a war zone violated the Fifth Amendment.

There is no controlling precedent clearly establishing that the specific detention conditions alleged—solitary confinement, denial of counsel, and placement with hostile detainees—were unconstitutional in the unique context of a military detention facility in Iraq. The Appellee relies on general principles of due process and cases involving domestic prison conditions (*Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730) or historic military tribunals (*Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2 (1866)). However, *Milligan* concerned jurisdiction, not the standards for a *Bivens* damages claim against a Secretary of Defense for detention conditions in a foreign combat zone. Furthermore, *Hope* involved state prison guards and specific handcuffing practices, which is factually distinct from the military detention context at issue here.

Because the contours of the right were not sufficiently clear that a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld’s position would have understood that the specific detention policies employed violated the Constitution, the Appellee has not met the high burden required to overcome qualified immunity. *Harlow*, 457 U.S. at 818.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s order denying Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss is reversed. The district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in this novel military context and further erred in denying qualified immunity. The case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**SO ORDERED.**

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY  

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that *Bivens* remedies are “disfavored” and may only be implied in “narrow circumstances.” *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The Court has “never extended *Bivens* to any new context or new category of defendants,” and lower courts must exercise “great caution” before doing so. Id. Here, the district court improperly extended *Bivens* to a novel context: a foreign detention of a U.S. citizen by federal officials without formal charges.  

#### A. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation in This Context  

To determine whether a *Bivens* remedy should be implied, courts must consider whether “special factors counselling hesitation” exist. *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 18 (1980). The district court ignored these special factors, which are present here.  

First, the absence of an alternative remedial scheme does not justify judicial creation of a *Bivens* action. While the defense argues that the Detainee Treatment Act provides no private remedy, this is irrelevant. *Bivens* is not a fallback remedy for statutory gaps. *FDIC v. Meyer*, 510 U.S. 471, 485 (1994). Congress, not courts, must create remedies for constitutional violations in foreign detention contexts. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected *Bivens* extensions when “the existence of alternative remedial schemes is not dispositive.” *Ministerio Roca Solida v. United States*, 556 U.S. 687, 699 (2009).  

Second, the detention occurred in a foreign jurisdiction, raising unique sovereignty and comity concerns. As this Court held in *Abu Ali v. Rumsfeld*, 326 F. App’x 1, 4 (D.C. Cir. 2009), courts must “exercise restraint when considering claims arising from conduct abroad,” particularly where foreign law enforcement or diplomatic considerations are implicated. The district court’s refusal to recognize these concerns constitutes clear error.  

Third, the separation of powers requires judicial deference to Congress’s role in crafting remedies for foreign detentions. *Egan*, 484 U.S. at 530. Congress has not authorized damages actions for foreign detentions, and the judiciary cannot “supplant[] the legislative role” by inventing such remedies. *Bush v. Lucas*, 462 U.S. 367, 388 (1983). The defense’s reliance on *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2 (1866), is misplaced: *Milligan* addressed jurisdictional limits on military tribunals during wartime, not damages claims against federal officials for foreign detentions.  

#### B. The Defense’s Reliance on *Carlson* and *Hope* Is Misplaced  

The defense cites *Carlson* and *Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730 (2003), to argue that *Bivens* should apply. But these cases are factually inapposite. *Carlson* involved Eighth Amendment claims by federal prisoners in domestic facilities—far removed from the foreign detention context here. *Carlson*, 446 U.S. at 16. *Hope* addressed state prison guards’ conduct under the Eighth Amendment, not federal officials’ actions abroad. 538 U.S. at 732. Neither case supports extending *Bivens* to a new foreign detention context.  

### II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY  

Even if a *Bivens* remedy existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are immune unless they violated “clearly established” constitutional rights. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). The defense fails to meet this high bar.  

#### A. The “Shock the Conscience” Standard Was Not Clearly Established  

The defense relies on *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998), for the “shock the conscience” standard. But this standard is inherently fact-specific and does not provide “clear notice” to officials. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846 n.8. No precedent clearly established in 2005 that the specific detention conditions alleged—solitary confinement, denial of counsel, and placement with hostile detainees—violated substantive due process in a foreign detention context.  

The defense cites *Hope* for the proposition that “severe pain” violates constitutional rights. But *Hope* involved state prison guards’ use of handcuffs in a domestic setting—irrelevant to federal officials acting abroad. 538 U.S. at 745. Similarly, *Ex parte Milligan* addressed military tribunals’ jurisdiction, not constitutional standards for foreign detentions. 71 U.S. at 125–26. These cases do not “squarely establish” the right alleged here. *Hope*, 538 U.S. at 741.

The district court erred by accepting conclusory allegations as true. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 678.  

### III. CONCLUSION  

The district court’s decision to imply a *Bivens* remedy and deny qualified immunity is legally unsound. The Supreme Court’s “disfavored” approach to *Bivens* extensions, the presence of special factors, and the lack of clearly established law all compel reversal. This Court must correct the district court’s error to prevent judicial overreach into matters reserved for Congress. We respectfully request reversal and remand with instructions to dismiss.

### II. THE APPELLEE FAILS TO OVERCOME THE SPECIAL FACTORS COUNSELING HESITATION

The appellee's brief fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the Bivens inquiry. The question before this Court is not whether the alleged conduct was egregious—it is whether the judiciary should create a new damages remedy where Congress has not authorized one and where distinctly military considerations are implicated. The appellee's reliance on the absence of an alternative remedial scheme is precisely the argument this Court correctly rejected, as the Supreme Court has held that "the existence of alternative remedial schemes is not dispositive" to the Bivens analysis. *Ministerio Roca Solida v. United States*, 556 U.S. 687, 699 (2009).

The appellee's attempt to distinguish this case from *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537 (2007), is unavailing. In *Wilkie*, the Supreme Court declined to imply a Bivens remedy against a federal landowning official despite the plaintiff's allegations of unconstitutional conduct, reasoning that the "distinctively military" nature of the activity at issue weighed heavily against judicial intervention. 551 U.S. at 550. The detention of enemy combatants in a combat zone is even more distinctly military than the land management context at issue in *Wilkie*. The appellee's argument that *Wilkie* is distinguishable because it involved a "dispute over property rights" conflates the factual predicate with the analytical framework. The critical holding in *Wilkie* was that the judiciary must exercise "great caution" before extending Bivens to new contexts, particularly where the conduct involves the executive branch's core wartime functions.

The appellee's reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), is similarly misplaced. In *Carlson*, the Supreme Court implied a Bivens remedy for Eighth Amendment claims against federal prison officials because "the Government has not provided an alternative remedial scheme." 446 U.S. at 20-21. However, the Court in *Carlson* specifically noted that the case involved "a federal prison" in the United States, not a military detention facility in a foreign combat zone. 446 U.S. at 16 n.1. The appellee's attempt to extend *Carlson* to the foreign military detention context ignores the critical distinction between domestic prison administration and wartime military operations abroad.

This Court correctly identified that the separation-of-powers implications of allowing damages actions against the Secretary of Defense for wartime detention decisions constitute special factors counseling hesitation. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "the military is, by necessity, a specialized society separate from civilian society." *Parker v. Levy*, 417 U.S. 733, 743 (1974). The judiciary lacks the expertise to second-guess the military's judgment regarding detention and interrogation practices in an active combat zone, and Congress has not seen fit to create a damages remedy for such claims. The appellee's argument that this Court should "correct" the alleged constitutional violations ignores that the appropriate remedy lies with Congress, not the courts.

The appellee's allegations involve federal officials acting in a foreign combat zone during wartime—a context so fundamentally different from the domestic prison setting in *Hope* that the latter cannot provide clear notice of the former. The appellee's brief cites no authority holding that the Constitution required the specific detention procedures he challenges in the military context.

**Pursuant to the review of the rebuttals, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling reversing the order of the district court.**

The arguments presented by the Appellant, Secretary Rumsfeld, reinforce the conclusion that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy and in denying qualified immunity. **ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The Appellant correctly reiterates that the implication of a *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored" judicial undertaking. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The Appellant's rebuttal highlights the controlling precedent of *Ministerio Roca Solida v. United States*, 556 U.S. 687, 699 (2009), which establishes that the absence of an alternative remedial scheme is not dispositive in the *Bivens* analysis. While the Appellee argues that the Detainee Treatment Act does not provide a private cause of action, this statutory silence does not authorize the courts to intrude upon the distinctively military realm.

The Appellant further strengthens the "special factors" analysis by citing *Parker v. Levy*, 417 U.S. 733, 743 (1974), which recognizes the military as a "specialized society separate from civilian society." This distinction is critical in the context of a detention facility in a foreign combat zone. The judiciary lacks the competence and constitutional authority to oversee the minute details of military detention and interrogation policies during active hostilities. As noted in *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), such matters are the province of the political branches. The Appellant's reliance on *Abu Ali v. Rumsfeld*, 326 F. App’x 1, 4 (D.C. Cir. 2009), correctly identifies the need for judicial restraint where foreign sovereignty and diplomatic considerations are implicated. Consequently, the district court's implication of a *Bivens* action constitutes an impermissible judicial overreach into areas constitutionally committed to the executive and legislative branches.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

The Appellant's rebuttal confirms that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the Appellee has failed to demonstrate a violation of a clearly established constitutional right. The standard for substantive due process, "shocking the conscience," is inherently fact-specific and does not provide the fair warning required to hold government officials liable. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 n.8 (1998).

The Appellant persuasively distinguishes the Appellee's reliance on *Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730 (2003). *Hope* involved state prison guards and the specific use of a hitching post in a domestic prison setting. It did not address the constitutional parameters of detaining a U.S. citizen as a security threat in a foreign combat zone. No precedent in 2005 clearly established that the specific conditions alleged—solitary confinement, denial of counsel, and placement with hostile cellmates—were unconstitutional in this unique military context. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

Furthermore, the Appellant correctly argues that the Appellee's allegations regarding Secretary Rumsfeld's personal involvement are conclusory and insufficient to establish supervisory liability. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 676 (2009). The Appellee has not alleged facts showing that Secretary Rumsfeld had actual knowledge of and disregarded an excessive risk to the Appellee's safety. *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, 334 (1986). Mere allegations that Secretary Rumsfeld "authorized policies" are insufficient to overcome the high bar for imposing personal liability on a high-ranking government official.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellant's arguments are sustained. The district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss is reversed. The case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLANT SECRETARY RUMSFELD**  

Your Honors, the district court committed a fundamental error by recognizing a Bivens remedy in this case. Bivens actions are disfavored judicial creations, and this Court must reverse the district court’s order to prevent unwarranted intrusion into sensitive governmental operations. The appellee’s arguments fail to overcome the controlling precedent that bars judicial intervention here—both on the merits of the Bivens claim and on qualified immunity.  

### I. BIVENS IS DISFAVORED, AND SPECIAL FACTORS COUNSEL HESITATION  
The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that courts must exercise "great caution" before implying new Bivens remedies. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). This case presents precisely the type of context where judicial restraint is required. The absence of an alternative remedial scheme does not, by itself, justify creating a damages action. *FDIC v. Meyer*, 510 U.S. 471, 485 (1994). Yet the district court ignored this principle, wrongly concluding that congressional silence authorizes judicial lawmaking.  

The appellee’s reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), is misplaced. *Carlson* involved domestic prison conditions in a U.S. facility—far removed from the unique circumstances here. The Supreme Court in *Carlson* explicitly distinguished cases involving foreign operations, emphasizing that "the Government has not provided an alternative remedial scheme" only where domestic institutional contexts apply. *Carlson*, 446 U.S. at 20–21. By contrast, this case concerns high-level government decision-making abroad, where the judiciary lacks competence to second-guess operational choices. As *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), confirms, certain matters are constitutionally committed to the political branches. The district court’s attempt to extend Bivens into this arena disregards separation-of-powers principles and invites judicial overreach.  

### II. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY APPLIES BECAUSE THE LAW WAS NOT CLEARLY ESTABLISHED  
The appellee’s claims fail on qualified immunity grounds. To overcome immunity, he must show a "clearly established" constitutional right at the time of the alleged conduct. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730, 739 (2003). But the "shock the conscience" standard he invokes—*County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 n.8 (1998)—is inherently fact-specific and provides no "fair notice" to officials. No precedent in 2005 established that solitary confinement, denial of counsel, or placement with other detainees violated the Constitution in this context. *Hope* is inapposite because it involved state prison guards in a domestic setting using a hitching post—a scenario entirely distinct from the governmental operations abroad at issue here.  

Moreover, the appellee’s allegations against Secretary Rumsfeld are conclusory and insufficient under *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662 (2009). *Iqbal* requires specific facts showing personal involvement or actual knowledge of excessive risk—not vague claims that he "authorized policies." *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 676. The complaint fails this test, as it contains no allegations that Secretary Rumsfeld knew of or disregarded the specific conditions alleged. *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, 334 (1986). Qualified immunity shields officials from liability in such cases, and the district court erred in denying it.  

### III. THE APPellee’s REMEDIAL ARGUMENTS IGNORE CONGRESSIONAL PREROGATIVE  
The appellee argues that the lack of a statutory remedy justifies judicial intervention. But this is precisely the error the Supreme Court condemned in *Ministerio Roca Solida v. United States*, 556 U.S. 687, 699 (2009). The absence of a statutory cause of action does not mandate a Bivens remedy; instead, it signals that Congress—and not the courts—must address the issue. The proper forum for resolving concerns about detention practices is legislative, not judicial. As *Wilkie* reaffirmed, courts must not "usurp Congress’s role" by creating new causes of action where none exist. *Wilkie*, 551 U.S. at 550.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honors, the district court’s decision threatens to destabilize the separation of powers by inviting federal courts to micromanage sensitive governmental operations abroad. Bivens is disfavored, special factors counsel hesitation, and qualified immunity bars the claims. The appellee’s arguments rely on misread precedents and fail to meet the high bar for overcoming immunity. This Court must reverse the district court’s order and dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld. The judiciary’s role is to interpret the law—not to legislate from the bench. For the reasons stated, we urge reversal.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLEE JOHN DOE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA CIRCUIT

---

Your Honors, the Appellant asks this Court to affirm a ruling that strips away a core constitutional remedy from a U.S. citizen arbitrarily detained, abused, and held in solitary confinement for nine months without charge. We respectfully urge the Court to reverse the order below and permit John Doe's substantive due process claims to proceed.

---

### I. THE APPELLANT MISCONCEIVES THE BIVENS FRAMEWORK

The Appellant argues that Bivens remedies are "disfavored" and that this Court should retreat from *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971). However, the Supreme Court has never foreclosed Bivens relief in cases involving constitutional violations by high-ranking federal officials. To the contrary, the Court has recognized that "a Bivens remedy lies in the district court" when federal officers violate constitutional rights. *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 18 (1980).

The Appellant relies heavily on *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007), but that case involved a dispute between private parties over land access—not constitutional deprivation of liberty. The "caution" the Court counseled in *Wilkie* applies where alternative remedies exist or where judicial intervention would disrupt government operations. Neither condition is present here.

The Appellant's "special factors" argument fails for three reasons:

**First**, the detention of a U.S. citizen on American soil after being brought to the United States is not a "military matter" beyond judicial review. Unlike *Parker v. Levy*, 417 U.S. 733, 743 (1974), which involved military discipline of service members, Doe was a civilian contractor detained by military authorities. The distinction matters: when the government seizes a citizen and brings him within the jurisdiction of federal courts, constitutional guarantees attach.

**Second**, the Appellant conflates military operations with constitutional violations. That the underlying detention occurred in Iraq does not immunize every subsequent abuse from judicial scrutiny. As this Court recognized in *Al-Hakam v. Rumsfeld*, 595 F.3d 439, 447 (D.C. Cir. 2010), "the fact that the alleged conduct occurred overseas does not categorically bar judicial review."

**Third**, the Detainee Treatment Act provides no alternative remedy. The district court correctly held that the DTA does not create a private cause of action. *Doe v. Rumsfeld*, 800 F.Sup.2d 94, 104-05 (D.D.C. 2011). The Appellant's argument that congressional silence mandates judicial abstinence gets matters backwards: when Congress forecloses statutory remedies, the judiciary's duty to provide constitutional remedies becomes more pressing, not less. *See Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 398 (Harlan, J., concurring).

---

### II. THE CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHT TO BE FREE FROM CONSCIENCE-SHOCKING DETENTION WAS CLEARLY ESTABLISHED

The Appellant contends that qualified immunity shields Secretary Rumsfeld because no precedent "clearly established" that the specific conditions alleged were unconstitutional. This argument misapplies the qualified immunity standard.

The relevant inquiry is not whether precedent addressed identical conditions, but whether the unconstitutionality of the conduct was "clearly established" such that a reasonable official would understand that his actions violated constitutional rights. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730, 739 (2003).

**The "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established in 2005.** In *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998), the Supreme Court held that executive action "shocking the conscience" violates substantive due process. This standard applies with particular force to detention conditions. *See Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520, 535 (1979) (holding that conditions of confinement implicate due process when they involve "the wanton and unnecessary infliction of pain").

Doe's complaint alleges conduct that far exceeds any arguable justification:

- **Solitary confinement for nine months** in a foreign dungeon
- **Blindfolding and hooding** for extended periods
- **Threats to shoot him** during interrogation
- **Placement with hostile cellmates** specifically to encourage physical attacks
- **Denial of access to counsel** throughout his detention
- **No formal charges** for over a year

These allegations describe treatment that shocks the conscience under any reasonable interpretation. The Appellant's attempt to distinguish *Hope v. Pelzer* fails. That case involved a hitching post; this case involves nine months of solitary confinement, physical abuse, and deliberate exposure to danger. If anything, Doe's allegations describe more egregious conduct than *Hope*.

**The Appellant's knowledge is properly inferred from the complaint.** The complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "authorized policies" permitting these interrogation techniques and detention conditions. Under *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952), such conduct "shocks the conscience" and violates due process regardless of whether the official personally participated in each act. The Secretary's authority over detention policy creates the requisite personal involvement.

### III. THE APPELLANT'S SEPARATION OF POWERS ARGUMENT MISREADS CONSTITUTIONAL STRUCTURE

The Appellant invokes *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), for the proposition that courts cannot review military detention decisions. But *Egan* addressed discretionary decisions about personnel security clearances—not constitutional torts. The Appellant conflates operational judgment with constitutional violation.

The separation of powers does not immunize executive officials from liability for constitutional torts. As the Supreme Court held in *Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 397, "it is well settled that federal officials may be liable for constitutional torts just as state officials are under *Monell v. New York City Department of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)." The Appellant's position would grant the Secretary of Defense absolute immunity for any detention-related conduct—an interpretation the Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected. *See *Butz v. The Appellant's citation to *Abu Ali v. Rumsfeld*, 326 F.App'x 1 (D.C. Cir. 2009), is unavailing. That case involved a plaintiff detained in a foreign country with limited access to U.S. courts. Doe, by contrast, was ultimately brought to the United States and released within our jurisdiction. The constitutional interests at stake are fundamentally different.

The Appellant warns that allowing this case to proceed will open a "floodgate" of litigation against military officials. This fear is overblown. Bivens actions remain rare, and courts retain discretion to dismiss claims that fail to state a cognizable theory. The appropriate response to questionable claims is dismissal on the merits—not categorical immunity for high-ranking officials.

Doe has suffered grievous harm: nine months of unlawful detention, physical abuse, loss of property, and ongoing placement on watch lists that impair his ability to work. If the political branches wish to provide alternative statutory mechanisms, they may do so—but they have not, and the courts cannot wash their hands of constitutional violations simply because the coordinate branches have failed to act.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling reversing the order of the district court.**

The Appellee, John Doe, has failed to overcome the significant hurdles to implying a *Bivens* remedy in the context of military detention in a foreign combat zone. Furthermore, he has not demonstrated that the law was clearly established such that it would strip the Secretary of Defense of qualified immunity for the conduct alleged. The Appellant's arguments regarding separation of powers and the pleading standards for supervisory liability are persuasive and controlling.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a *Bivens* Remedy**

The Appellee urges this Court to extend *Bivens* to cover the alleged constitutional violations, arguing that the absence of a statutory remedy under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) necessitates judicial intervention. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that implying a *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored" judicial undertaking. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The Appellee's reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), is misplaced because *Carlson* involved domestic prison conditions, a context far removed from the military detention of a security threat in a foreign war zone.

The Appellant correctly identifies that "special factors" counsel hesitation against the creation of a *Bivens* remedy in this case. The military context is "a specialized society separate from civilian society," *Parker v. Levy*, 417 U.S. 733, 743 (1974), and the management of detention facilities in an active combat zone involves "discretionary functions" constitutionally committed to the Executive Branch. *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988). While the Appellee notes that he is a civilian contractor, the nature of the alleged conduct—military detention and interrogation during hostilities—places this matter squarely within the sphere of military expertise and national security.

The Appellee argues that congressional silence regarding a remedy under the DTA compels the judiciary to act. This argument is rejected by the precedent of *Ministerio Roca Solida v. United States*, 556 U.S. 687, 699 (2009), which holds that the absence of a statutory cause of action does not mandate a *Bivens* remedy. To the contrary, such silence often indicates that Congress has chosen not to provide a remedy, leaving the matter to the political branches. The district court's decision to imply a cause of action intrudes upon the separation of powers by subjecting sensitive military detention policies to judicial micromanagement.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

The Appellee further argues that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity because the right to be free from "conscience-shocking" detention was clearly established in 2005. The Appellee relies heavily on *Hope v. Pelzer*, 538 U.S. 730 (2003), to argue that the egregious nature of the alleged conduct provided fair notice. However, *Hope* involved state prison guards and the use of a hitching post in a domestic prison setting. It did not address the constitutional parameters of detaining a U.S. citizen as a security threat in a foreign combat zone.

The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process is inherently fact-specific and does not provide the fair warning required to strip high-ranking officials of immunity. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846 n.8 (1998). In 2005, no precedent clearly established that the specific conditions alleged—solitary confinement, denial of counsel, and placement with hostile cellmates—were unconstitutional when applied to a security detainee in Iraq. The Appellant correctly distinguishes the Appellee's reliance on domestic prison cases, which fail to account for the unique exigencies of a war zone.

Furthermore, the Appellee's allegations fail to establish Secretary Rumsfeld's personal liability under the standard set forth in *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662 (2009). The Appellee's claim that Secretary Rumsfeld "authorized policies" is conclusory and insufficient to state a claim for supervisory liability. *Iqbal* requires specific facts showing that the official had actual knowledge of and disregarded an excessive risk of harm. *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, 334 (1986). The complaint contains no such factual allegations regarding Secretary Rumsfeld's personal involvement in the specific detention conditions at issue. Consequently, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellant's arguments are sustained. The district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action and in denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. The order of the district court is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.
