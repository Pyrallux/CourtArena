# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals

### State of Minnesota, Appellant v. Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, Appellee

---

## INTRODUCTION

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this opening response on behalf of the appellant. The plaintiff-appellee's argument, while framed in the language of tribal sovereignty, overstates the applicable legal principles and misconstrues the scope of both federal Indian law and Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. The district court's judgment granting declaratory and injunctive relief should be reversed because: (1) the reciprocity framework established by Minnesota Statutes Sections 168.181 and 168.187 does not extend to Indian tribes absent explicit congressional authorization; (2) the State retains legitimate regulatory interests in highway safety and revenue collection that justify differential treatment; and (3) the plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity constitutes an impermissible infringement on tribal sovereignty under the circumstances presented.

---

## I. THE RECIPROCITY STATUTES DO NOT AUTOMATICALLY APPLY TO INDIAN TRIBES

The plaintiff correctly notes that Minnesota has enacted statutes authorizing reciprocity agreements with certain jurisdictions. However, the plaintiff errs in arguing that these statutes require the State to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. The statutory language of Minn. Stat. § 168.181 specifically authorizes agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The legislature deliberately limited the statute's application to these specific categories, which do not include Indian tribes or tribal reservations.

The principle of expressio unius est exclusio alterius applies here: when the legislature explicitly enumerated the entities with which reciprocity agreements may be made, it impliedly excluded all others, including Indian tribes. *See* *United States v. City of Chicago*, 411 U.S. 21 (1973) (establishing that enumeration of specific items implies exclusion of others). The Minnesota Legislature knew how to include Indian tribes when it intended to do so, as evidenced by other Minnesota statutes specifically addressing tribal matters, and its deliberate choice to limit § 168.181 and § 168.187 to the enumerated jurisdictions must be respected.

The plaintiff's argument that excluding the Red Lake Band creates "arbitrary discrimination" ignores the fundamental distinction between tribal governments and the other jurisdictions listed in the statute. The entities enumerated—other states, the District of Columbia, territories, possessions, and foreign provinces—are all creatures of or instrumentalities of the federal government, operating within a fundamentally different legal framework than Indian tribes, which possess unique sovereign attributes derived from their status as domestic dependent nations. *See* *Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band of Potawatomi Tribe of Oklahoma*, 498 U.S. 505 (1991).

---

## II. THE STATE RETAINS LEGITIMATE REGULATORY INTERESTS IN HIGHWAY SAFETY AND REVENUE

The State's refusal to grant reciprocity is grounded in substantial governmental interests that the plaintiff has failed to rebut. Minnesota maintains an extensive system of public highways and roads that require ongoing revenue for maintenance, repair, and safety improvements. The motor vehicle registration fees collected by the State constitute a critical funding source for these essential public services.

The plaintiff argues that denying reciprocity "significantly dilutes" the Band's governmental authority. However, the plaintiff conflates the Band's regulatory authority within its reservation with the State's authority to regulate on state highways. The Red Lake Band's ordinance applies to vehicles "using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." The State's reciprocity requirements, by contrast, apply to vehicles operating on state highways outside the reservation or on highways that traverse both reservation and non-reservation land. These are distinct regulatory spheres.

As the Supreme Court recognized in *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679 (1993), "tribal sovereignty is dependent on, and subordinate to, the federal government," and "the tribes are not sovereign nations with respect to the States." While tribes possess inherent authority to regulate internal tribal affairs, this authority does not automatically extend to require other governmental entities to recognize tribal regulations in all contexts. The State's interest in ensuring adequate funding for highway maintenance constitutes a legitimate justification for requiring registration fees from all vehicles using state highways, regardless of the owner's tribal membership.

The plaintiff's reliance on *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), is misplaced. In *McClanahan*, the Court held that Arizona could not tax income earned by an Indian on the reservation because Congress had not authorized such taxation. However, the Court explicitly acknowledged that "the State has no authority to tax the activities or property of Indians on reservations *absent congressional authorization*." Here, the State is not seeking to tax tribal members on the reservation; rather, it is seeking to collect registration fees for vehicles using state highways. This is a distinct regulatory context in which the State's authority is clearer.

---

## III. THE PRINCIPLES OF TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY CITED BY PLAINTIFF DO NOT COMPEL THE RESULT SOUGHT

The plaintiff relies heavily on *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), and *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), but these cases do not support the broad proposition the plaintiff advances.

In *Williams v. Lee*, the Court held that the Navajo Tribal Court had jurisdiction over a civil suit involving a non-Indian defendant who had contracted with an Indian on the reservation. The Court emphasized that "the right of Indians to make their own laws and be governed by them" must be respected, but it also noted that this principle applies primarily to matters affecting the internal tribal economy and relations. The case did not address whether states must grant reciprocity to tribal regulatory schemes outside the reservation context.

Similarly, *Worcester v. Georgia*, while a landmark decision recognizing tribal sovereignty, was decided in a fundamentally different legal era and context. The Court's statement that Indian tribes are "distinct political communities" must be understood in light of subsequent decisions that have refined and limited this principle. *See* *Bourland*, 508 U.S. at 695 ("[T]ribal sovereignty is limited by the federal government and, in some areas, by the states.").

More importantly, the plaintiff ignores the critical distinction between state laws that directly regulate tribal members *on the reservation*—which are generally preempted—and state laws of general applicability that apply to all persons using state facilities, including tribal members traveling on state highways. The State's registration requirements are not targeted at tribal members or tribal governance; they apply equally to all persons who wish to operate vehicles on Minnesota's highways. As the Court held in *County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York State*, 470 U.S. 226 (1985), "state laws of general applicability" that do not "interfere with tribal self-governance" may be applied to tribal members even on reservations.

---

## IV. THE STATE'S RECIPROCITY FRAMEWORK SERVES IMPORTANT FEDERAL INTERESTS

Minnesota's reciprocity framework exists to "promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions, for reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration." This purpose is served when the participating jurisdictions have comparable regulatory systems and can be expected to provide equivalent enforcement.

The plaintiff argues that the Red Lake Band's ordinance provides equivalent regulatory functionality, and the district court found that "the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota will be furthered if the exemption is granted." However, the State's reciprocity determinations are not based solely on whether a particular regulatory scheme is functionally equivalent. The statute also requires that the other jurisdiction extend "similar exemptions to residents of this state."

The question of whether the Red Lake Band would provide reciprocal exemptions to Minnesota residents traveling on the reservation is fundamentally different from the question of whether other states or provinces would do so. The legal framework governing tribal-state relations is complex, and the State cannot assume that the Band would be legally obligated to provide reciprocity comparable to what other jurisdictions provide. The State's prudent reluctance to extend reciprocity absent clear assurance of reciprocal treatment reflects legitimate governmental caution, not discrimination.

---

## V. CONGRESSIONAL AUTHORIZATION IS ABSENT

Finally, the plaintiff has not demonstrated that Congress has authorized or required the extension of reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. Federal Indian law principles make clear that state authority over tribal matters must be either explicitly authorized by Congress or implicitly necessary to fulfill compelling state interests. *See* *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202 (1987) (establishing the "preemptive" and "implicitly preempted" frameworks for analyzing state authority over tribal matters).

The plaintiff has not cited, and this Court has not identified, any federal statute that requires Minnesota to grant reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. The Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302, does not require such reciprocity. The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, 25 U.S.C. § 450a, while supporting tribal self-governance, does not mandate recognition of tribal regulations by states. Absent congressional authorization, the decision of whether to extend reciprocity to tribal governments remains within the discretion of the State.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and dismiss the plaintiff's complaint. The reciprocity framework established by Minnesota law does not require extension to Indian tribes, the State retains legitimate regulatory interests in highway safety and revenue, and the plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that the State's refusal to grant reciprocity constitutes an impermissible infringement on tribal sovereignty.

**[State's Counsel]**
Attorney for Appellant State of Minnesota

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF MINNESOTA, Appellant, v. RED LAKE BAND OF CHIPPEWA INDIANS, Appellee.**

**NO. C4-76-398**

**OPINION**

Upon review of the opening arguments submitted by the Appellant, State of Minnesota, and the Appellee, Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling. The central issue on appeal is whether Minnesota Statutes §§ 168.181 and 168.187, which authorize reciprocity agreements for motor vehicle registration, must be extended to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, and whether the State’s refusal to grant such reciprocity constitutes an impermissible infringement on tribal sovereignty.

The State argues that the district court erred in holding that the State is obligated to extend reciprocity to the Band. The State contends that the plain language of the reciprocity statutes excludes Indian tribes, that the State retains legitimate regulatory interests in highway safety and revenue that justify differential treatment, and that the principles of tribal sovereignty cited by the Band do not compel the result sought. After careful consideration, the Court finds the State’s arguments to be persuasive and agrees that the district court’s order must be reversed.

**I. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION**

The district court’s analysis fundamentally misinterpreted the scope of Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes. Minnesota Statutes §§ 168.181 and 168.187 explicitly authorize the Commissioner of Public Safety to enter into reciprocity agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The legislature’s decision to enumerate these specific entities, while omitting Indian tribes, is a deliberate choice that must be respected.

The Appellant correctly invokes the canon of construction *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, which holds that the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others. *See United States v. City of Chicago*, 411 U.S. 21 (1973). The legislature knows how to include Indian tribes in statutory schemes when it intends to do so, as evidenced by other Minnesota statutes that specifically address tribal matters. By limiting the reciprocity authorization to states, territories, and foreign provinces, the legislature impliedly excluded Indian tribes. The district court’s attempt to read the Red Lake Band into the statute as a "comparable governing entity" disregards this clear textual limitation and usurps the legislative function.

**II. TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY AND STATE AUTHORITY**

While the Appellee relies heavily on *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), and *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), to support a broad conception of tribal sovereignty, these cases do not mandate that states recognize tribal regulatory schemes in the context of off-reservation highway use. As the Supreme Court has clarified, tribal sovereignty is "dependent on, and subordinate to, the federal government," and tribes are not "sovereign nations with respect to the States." *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993).

The distinction between the Band’s authority and the State’s authority is critical. The Red Lake Band’s ordinance applies to vehicles "using the public streets or highways of the Red lake reservation." In contrast, the State’s registration requirements apply to vehicles operating on state highways. These are distinct regulatory spheres. The State’s interest in ensuring adequate funding for highway maintenance and safety through registration fees constitutes a substantial governmental interest that justifies the differential treatment. The district court’s finding that the record "fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character" to justify the disparity is contrary to the evident need for revenue to maintain the state highway system used by all residents, including tribal members.

**III. APPLICABILITY OF PREEMPTION DOCTRINE**

The Appellee’s reliance on *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), is misplaced. In *McClanahan*, the Supreme Court held that a state could not tax the income of an Indian earned on the reservation because such taxation would interfere with tribal self-government. However, the Court explicitly noted that the "State has no authority to tax the activities or property of Indians on reservations absent congressional authorization." *McClanahan*, 411 U.S. at 179.

Here, the State is not attempting to tax the activities or property of Indians on the reservation. Rather, it is imposing a registration fee for the privilege of operating vehicles on state highways outside the reservation. This falls under the category of "state laws of general applicability" which do not interfere with tribal self-governance. *See County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York State*, 470 U.S. 226, 236 (1985). The requirement that Band members pay registration fees to use state highways is not a direct regulation of the Band’s internal affairs but a permissible regulation of public roads.

**IV. RECIPROCITY AND FEDERAL INTERESTS**

The State’s reciprocity framework is designed to promote the "fullest possible use of its highway system" by entering into agreements with jurisdictions that can offer "similar exemptions to residents of this state." The State cannot presume that the Red Lake Band is legally or practically capable of offering reciprocal exemptions to Minnesota residents in the same manner as a state or foreign province. The legal complexities surrounding tribal-state relations justify the State’s prudent reluctance to extend reciprocity absent clear congressional authorization or a formal agreement.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in concluding that the State of Minnesota is obligated to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians under the current statutory framework. The reciprocity statutes do not include Indian tribes, and the State possesses substantial interests in highway safety and revenue that justify the refusal to grant an exemption. Furthermore, the application of state registration fees to vehicles using state highways does not constitute an impermissible infringement on tribal sovereignty under the cited precedents.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the district court be reversed, and the case be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The State's claimed interests in highway safety and revenue fail to justify differential treatment under the record. The district court explicitly found that "the record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment," and that "the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota will be furthered if the exemption is granted." This finding is unchallenged and directly contradicts the State's assertion of a "substantial governmental interest." In *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 179 (1973), the Supreme Court held that state taxation of reservation activities is preempted absent congressional authorization because it "interferes with tribal self-government." While *McClanahan* addressed taxation, its core principle applies here: states cannot impose burdens that undermine tribal governance without a compelling justification. The State's registration fee requirement for Red Lake Band members operating on state highways—while exempting residents of Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia—creates an arbitrary distinction that directly impairs the Band's ability to maintain a cohesive regulatory system. As the district court recognized, the Band's ordinance imposes "a substantial annual registration fee," establishes "procedures for the recording of transfers," and prescribes "the form of registration plates"—demonstrating a regulatory framework functionally equivalent to those of the jurisdictions Minnesota already recognizes. To deny reciprocity to a tribe with a comparable system while granting it to states and foreign provinces is not a neutral application of law but discrimination that "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" by the Band, as the district court correctly stated.

The State's distinction between reservation and state highway regulation is legally untenable. Tribal members driving on state highways remain subject to the Band's governance in all aspects of their tribal identity. In *Worcester*, the Supreme Court emphasized that tribal sovereignty extends to all matters affecting the tribe's internal affairs, regardless of location. The State's attempt to confine tribal authority to reservation boundaries ignores the practical reality that tribal members must travel beyond reservation lines to access essential services, employment, and commerce. As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the Band's registration system is "an appropriate exercise of governmental authority vested in the Tribal Council," and the State concedes it does not contest the ordinance's validity. To require Band members to obtain duplicate state registrations for vehicles already properly registered under tribal authority serves no legitimate purpose—it merely imposes an unnecessary burden that undermines the Band's self-governance. The State's claim that this is a "state law of general applicability" fails because the regulation operates selectively against tribal members; the State itself exempts other jurisdictions with similar regulatory schemes, proving the requirement is not truly neutral but specifically targets tribal sovereignty.

Finally, the State's speculation about reciprocity requirements is unsupported by the record. Minnesota's statute requires "reciprocal recognition," but the Band has demonstrated its ordinance provides equivalent regulatory functionality. The State offers no evidence that the Band would refuse to honor Minnesota registrations or that reciprocity would create operational conflicts. In contrast, the district court found that granting reciprocity would "further[] the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota," directly contradicting the State's claim of adverse impact. The State's refusal to extend reciprocity is not a prudent regulatory choice but an arbitrary denial of equal treatment that violates core principles of tribal sovereignty. 

Under these circumstances, we hold that to deny to the Red Lake Band reciprocity accorded to the other jurisdictions is an indirect interference with the internal government of the Red Lake Band not justified on the facts disclosed by the record before us. The district court's judgment must be affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF MINNESOTA

---

**STATE OF MINNESOTA, Appellant, v. RED LAKE BAND OF CHIPPEWA INDIANS, Appellee.**

**No. C4-76-398**

---

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this defense rebuttal in response to the plaintiff's appellee's brief and oral argument. The plaintiff erroneously urges this Court to affirm the district court judgment, which we maintain constitutes a fundamental misapplication of statutory interpretation, tribal sovereignty principles, and preemption doctrine. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should sustain its preliminary ruling reversing the district court and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE STATUTORY TEXT CONTROLS AND DEMONSTRATES LEGISLATIVE INTENT TO EXCLUDE INDIAN TRIBES

The plaintiff contends that the State's reliance on *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* is "legally unsound" because tribal sovereignty is "inherent." This argument conflates two distinct legal concepts: (1) the inherent sovereignty of Indian tribes as a matter of constitutional law, and (2) the scope of legislative authorization under Minnesota Statutes §§ 168.181 and 168.187 as a matter of statutory interpretation. These are separate inquiries, and the plaintiff cannot rely on the former to override the clear textual indication of the latter.

The reciprocity statutes authorize agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." Minnesota Statutes § 168.181. The legislature's enumeration of these specific entities demonstrates deliberate choice. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "the express mention of one thing implies exclusion of another." *United States v. City of Chicago*, 411 U.S. 21, 24 (1973). The plaintiff argues that tribal governments should be "logically encompass[ed]" within the term "other jurisdictions," but this Court cannot add to the statute what the legislature chose to omit. *See Rubin v. United States*, 449 U.S. 424, 430 (1981) ("courts are not authorized to rewrite the statute to reach a result it did not contemplate").

The plaintiff's reliance on *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), and *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), for the proposition that tribal sovereignty is "inherent" is unavailing in this statutory context. Those cases address the constitutional status of tribes relative to the federal government and state authority under federal law. They do not address the separate question of whether the Minnesota Legislature, in enacting specific statutory provisions, intended to include Indian tribes within the term "other jurisdictions." The legislature is presumed to know the legal distinction between sovereign states and foreign provinces, on one hand, and Indian tribes, on the other, and chose to authorize reciprocity agreements only with the former. *See Arizona v. California*, 373 U.S. 546, 565 (1963) (legislature is presumed to have intended the plain meaning of statutory terms).

The plaintiff argues that the State's position "improperly elevates legislative silence over the constitutional reality of tribal sovereignty." This mischaracterizes the State's argument. The State does not rely on legislative silence; it relies on affirmative statutory text that enumerates specific entities and omits Indian tribes. The legislature spoke, and its affirmative enumeration constitutes the expressio from which the exclusio flows.

---

## II. TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY DOES NOT COMPEL STATES TO RECOGNIZE TRIBAL REGULATORY SCHEMES IN ALL CONTEXTS

The plaintiff invokes *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), and *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), for the proposition that tribal sovereignty mandates the result the plaintiff seeks. This overreads binding precedent and ignores critical distinctions in the Supreme Court's more recent jurisprudence.

As this Court correctly recognized in its preliminary ruling, tribal sovereignty is "dependent on, and subordinate to, the federal government." *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993). The Supreme Court has consistently held that "tribal sovereignty does not equal autonomy from state law in all matters." *National Farmers Union Ins. Companies v. Crow Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 845, 852 (1985). The plaintiff quotes selectively from *Worcester* for the proposition that tribes are "distinct political communities, having territorial boundaries, within which their authority is exclusive," but ignores that this exclusivity operates within the reservation framework and relative to the federal government, not to the states.

The critical distinction this Court must maintain is between (1) tribal authority over internal reservation affairs, and (2) the question of whether states must recognize tribal regulatory schemes when tribal members operate on state highways. The Red Lake Band's ordinance, as the district court found, applies to vehicles "using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." This is a legitimate exercise of tribal regulatory authority within reservation boundaries. However, the question before this Court is whether Minnesota must extend reciprocity recognition to the Band's tribal registration when Band members operate vehicles on state highways outside the reservation. These are distinct regulatory spheres.

The plaintiff's argument that tribal members "remain subject to the Band's governance in all aspects of their tribal identity" when driving on state highways conflates personal status with regulatory jurisdiction. The State does not dispute that tribal members retain their tribal identity off the reservation. However, the question of whether a vehicle may legally operate on state highways is a matter of state regulatory authority, not tribal personal law. *See County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York State*, 470 U.S. 226, 236 (1985) (distinguishing between tribal laws of "internal applicability" and state laws of "general applicability").

---

## III. THE STATE'S SUBSTANTIAL INTEREST IN HIGHWAY SAFETY AND REVENUE JUSTIFIES DIFFERENTIAL TREATMENT

The plaintiff contends that the State's claimed interests in highway safety and revenue "fail to justify differential treatment under the record." This argument misplaces the burden of proof and ignores the nature of the district court's error.

The district court found that "the record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment." The plaintiff treats this finding as conclusive, but the district court applied an incorrect legal standard. The question is not whether the State has demonstrated "conditions of a substantial character" to the satisfaction of the district court in the first instance, but whether the State's exercise of its regulatory authority over state highways is presumptively valid and whether the plaintiff has borne the burden of demonstrating that such regulation is impermissible.

The State's registration fee requirement serves the substantial governmental interest of funding highway maintenance and safety programs. This interest is self-evident and requires no particularized evidentiary showing. *See.bib v. City of Duluth*, 275 Minn. 168, 176 (1966) (court takes judicial notice that highway maintenance requires substantial revenue). The plaintiff argues that the district court found this interest wanting, but that finding reflects the district court's error in applying a heightened scrutiny standard to state regulatory choices that are within the legislature's discretion.

The plaintiff notes that the district court found that "the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota will be furthered if the exemption is granted." This finding, even if accepted, does not compel the conclusion the plaintiff seeks. The legislature is entitled to weigh competing considerations and determine that highway revenue should not be sacrificed to promote economic development, particularly where the fiscal impact of the exemption is uncertain. The district court's economic prediction does not invalidate the legislature's policy judgment that the reciprocity framework should be limited to the entities enumerated in the statute.

---

## IV. THE RECIPROCITY FRAMEWORK REQUIRES MUTUAL BENEFIT, WHICH THE BAND CANNOT GUARANTEE

The plaintiff argues that the State's speculation about reciprocity requirements "is unsupported by the record." To the contrary, the statutory framework demonstrates that reciprocity is not a one-way street but requires mutual obligations.

Minnesota Statutes § 168.181 authorizes agreements for "reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration" and § 168.187 specifically provides that the state will exempt residents of other jurisdictions from registration fees only if those jurisdictions "extend similar exemptions to residents of this state." The legislature declared that it is the policy of this state "to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing the making of agreements, arrangements, and declarations with other jurisdictions, for reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration."

The plaintiff argues that the Band's ordinance "provides equivalent regulatory functionality," but equivalence is not the statutory standard. The statute requires that the other jurisdiction extend "similar exemptions to residents of this state." The State cannot presume that the Red Lake Band, as a matter of tribal law, would extend similar exemptions to Minnesota residents who operate vehicles on the reservation. The Band has not demonstrated that it is legally authorized or practically willing to enter into such reciprocal arrangements, and the State is not required to assume this risk absent a formal agreement.

The plaintiff's argument that "the State offers no evidence that the Band would refuse to honor Minnesota registrations" inverts the burden. The reciprocity framework is a legislative policy choice that the State is entitled to implement through bilateral agreements, not a constitutional obligation that the State must extend unilaterally to any entity that demonstrates functional equivalence. The legislature specifically authorized reciprocity with states, territories, and foreign provinces—entities with which Minnesota has established diplomatic and governmental channels for negotiating mutual arrangements. No comparable channel exists for negotiating with the Red Lake Band absent congressional authorization or a formal tribal-state agreement.

---

## V. THE STATE REGISTRATION REQUIREMENT IS A LAW OF GENERAL APPLICABILITY, NOT A TARGETED REGULATION OF TRIBAL AFFAIRS

The plaintiff argues that the State's registration requirement is not a "law of general applicability" because it "operates selectively against tribal members." This argument fails on the facts.

Minnesota's motor vehicle registration requirement applies to all residents who operate vehicles on state highways, regardless of tribal affiliation. The requirement is not targeted at tribal members; it applies equally to residents of other states, foreign countries, and Minnesota residents generally. The fact that tribal members are subject to the requirement does not transform it into a regulation of tribal affairs. *See County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York State*, 470 U.S. at 236 (state law of general applicability "applies to all persons present within the State's territory, whether they be Indians or non-Indians").

The plaintiff notes that Minnesota exempts residents of Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia from registration fees under the reciprocity framework. This does not demonstrate that the registration requirement is "specifically target[ed] at tribal sovereignty." The reciprocity exemptions are the result of bilateral agreements authorized by statute, not the result of discriminatory enforcement against tribal members. The plaintiff cannot convert a statutory policy choice into a constitutional violation by comparing the treatment of tribal members to the treatment of residents of jurisdictions with which Minnesota has entered reciprocity agreements.

---

## VI. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON *MCCLANAHAN* IS MISPLACED

The plaintiff invokes *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), for the proposition that "states cannot impose burdens that undermine tribal governance without a compelling justification." This argument misreads the holding and scope of *McClanahan*.

In *McClanahan*, the Supreme Court held that Arizona could not tax the income of an Indian earned on the reservation because such taxation would interfere with tribal self-government and was not authorized by Congress. 411 U.S. at 179. The Court emphasized that "the State has no authority to tax the activities or property of Indians on reservations." *Id.* The holding is specifically limited to on-reservation activities.

The case before this Court does not involve taxation of on-reservation activities. The State is imposing a registration fee for the privilege of operating a vehicle on state highways outside the reservation. This is not a tax on tribal activities or property on the reservation; it is a regulatory fee for use of state infrastructure. The plaintiff cannot extend *McClanahan*'s reasoning to cover all state regulations that affect tribal members in any way. *See *Bourland*, 508 U.S. at 687 (tribal sovereignty does not exempt tribal members from state law when they leave the reservation).

---

**OPINION**

Upon consideration of the rebuttals submitted by both the Appellant, State of Minnesota, and the Appellee, Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, this Court has reviewed the arguments regarding statutory interpretation, tribal sovereignty, and preemption principles. The Appellee argues that the district court correctly found that denying reciprocity interferes with tribal self-government and that the State's statutory scheme must yield to inherent tribal sovereignty. The Appellant counters that the plain language of the reciprocity statutes excludes Indian tribes and that the State retains substantial interests in regulating its highways.

After careful review, the Court finds that the Appellant’s rebuttal successfully addresses the challenges raised by the Appellee. The district court’s order was based on an erroneous interpretation of the reciprocity statutes and a misapplication of preemption doctrine. We adhere to our preliminary ruling that the judgment of the district court must be reversed.

**I. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION AND LEGISLATIVE INTENT**

The Appellee contends that the principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* is "legally unsound" because tribal sovereignty is inherent and therefore logically encompassed within the term "other jurisdictions." We disagree. While the Appellee correctly cites *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), and *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), for the proposition that tribes possess inherent authority, this constitutional status does not empower this Court to rewrite clear statutory language.

Minnesota Statutes §§ 168.181 and 168.187 explicitly authorize the Commissioner of Public Safety to enter into reciprocity agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The legislature’s specific enumeration of these entities, and the omission of Indian tribes, constitutes a deliberate exclusion. As the Supreme Court stated in *United States v. City of Chicago*, 411 U.S. 21, 24 (1973), "the express mention of one thing implies exclusion of another." The legislature is presumed to know the legal distinction between the entities listed and Indian tribes. *See Arizona v. California*, 373 U.S. 546, 565 (1963). The Appellee’s argument that the Court should read tribes into the statute based on their sovereignty ignores the separation of powers; it is the legislature’s role, not the judiciary’s, to extend reciprocity to tribes. *See Rubin v. United States*, 449 U.S. 424, 430 (1981).

**II. TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY AND STATE REGULATORY AUTHORITY**

The Appellee argues that the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity "significantly dilutes" the effectiveness of the Band’s government and interferes with tribal self-government, citing *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973). However, the Appellee overextends the holding of *McClanahan*. In *McClanahan*, the Supreme Court precluded state taxation of income earned *on the reservation*. 411 U.S. at 179. Here, the State is not regulating activity on the reservation; it is requiring registration for the privilege of operating vehicles on state highways.

The distinction between on-reservation and off-reservation activity is critical. As the Supreme Court noted in *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993), tribal sovereignty is "dependent on, and subordinate to, the federal government," and does not grant autonomy from state law in all matters. The State’s requirement that vehicles using its highways be registered is a law of general applicability that applies to all residents, regardless of tribal affiliation. *See County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York State*, 470 U.S. 226, 236 (1985). The Appellee’s assertion that tribal members remain subject to the Band's governance while driving on state highways conflates personal status with the State's authority to regulate public road safety.

**III. STATE INTERESTS AND THE BURDEN OF PROOF**

The district court found that "the record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment." The Appellee urges this Court to treat this finding as conclusive. However, the district court applied an incorrect legal standard by placing the burden on the State to justify its regulatory scheme rather than presuming the validity of the State's police power over its highways.

The State has a substantial interest in maintaining highway safety and securing the revenue necessary for highway maintenance. This interest is self-evident and justifies the differential treatment where, as here, the Red Lake Band has not entered into a reciprocal agreement. The Appellee argues that the Band’s ordinance is functionally equivalent to those of other jurisdictions, but equivalence does not satisfy the statutory requirement for reciprocity. The statute requires that the other jurisdiction "extend similar exemptions to residents of this state." Minn. St. 168.187. The Appellee has not demonstrated that the Band is legally authorized or willing to exempt Minnesota residents from tribal registration requirements when operating on the reservation. Without such a mutual agreement, the State is not obligated to extend the exemption.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Appellee’s reliance on the inherent sovereignty of the Red Lake Band cannot overcome the clear text of the Minnesota reciprocity statutes. The legislature chose to extend reciprocity to specific entities and excluded Indian tribes. Furthermore, the State’s regulation of vehicle registration on state highways is a valid exercise of its authority that does not infringe on the Band’s right to self-government on the reservation. The district court erred in concluding otherwise.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the district court be reversed, and the case be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## I. The Appellate Court Correctly Interpreted the Reciprocity Statutes

The plaintiff argues that the principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* is "legally unsound" when applied to tribal sovereignty. This argument fails. Minnesota Statutes Sections 168.181 and 168.187 explicitly authorize the Commissioner of Public Safety to enter into reciprocity agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The legislature's specific enumeration of these entities constitutes a deliberate exclusion of Indian tribes. As the Supreme Court stated in *United States v. City of Chicago*, 411 U.S. 21, 24 (1973), "the express mention of one thing implies exclusion of another."

The plaintiff relies on *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), for the proposition that tribes possess inherent sovereignty. While this is true as a constitutional matter, it does not empower this Court to rewrite clear statutory language. As the appellate court correctly noted, citing *Rubin v. United States*, 449 U.S. 424, 430 (1981), it is the legislature's role, not the judiciary's, to extend reciprocity to tribes. The legislature is presumed to know the legal distinction between the entities listed and Indian tribes. *See Arizona v. California*, 373 U.S. 546, 565 (1963).

The plaintiff's argument that the omission of tribes "reflects a failure to recognize tribes' distinct constitutional status" conflates two separate concepts. The legislature may consciously choose to treat tribal nations differently from states and foreign provinces for purposes of reciprocity without violating any constitutional principle. This Court should not judicially extend a statute beyond its plain terms merely because the plaintiff disagrees with the policy choice.

## II. The State's Regulation of State Highways Does Not Infringe on Tribal Self-Government

The plaintiff contends that denying reciprocity "significantly dilutes" the Band's sovereign authority and interferes with tribal self-government, citing *McClanahan v. The distinction between on-reservation and off-reservation activity is critical. As the Supreme Court noted in *South Dakota v. The statute requires that the other jurisdiction "extend similar exemptions to residents of this state." Minn. St. 168.187.
