COURT ARENA LOG - Case 99
FACTS:
¶ 1 Pao Chang appeals a judgment, entered upon his no-contest pleas, convicting him of trafficking of a child and human trafficking, both as repeaters. He also appeals an order denying him postconviction relief. Chang contends that he is entitled to plea withdrawal based on the ineffective assistance of two of his trial attorneys. We disagree and affirm.

BACKGROUND

¶ 2 On May 2, 2014, the State filed a complaint charging Chang with three counts of trafficking of a child and a single count of human trafficking, all as repeaters. The complaint alleged that between January 1, 2013, and February 18, 2013, Chang facilitated acts of prostitution by several women, including some juvenile females.

¶ 3 On February 10, 2015, the circuit court allowed Chang's original trial attorney to withdraw from the case due to a health issue. Attorney Brian Van Ells was then appointed to represent Chang. Van Ells filed various motions on Chang's behalf, including three suppression motions, all of which the court denied. Van Ells also opposed, unsuccessfully, the State's motion in limine to admit other acts evidence.

¶ 4 On May 11, 2015, the day before Chang's trial was scheduled to begin, Van Ells informed the circuit court that a plea agreement had been reached. Van Ells then presented to the court a completed and signed plea questionnaire and waiver of rights form. Attached to this form were the terms of the plea agreement. The agreement provided that in exchange for Chang's guilty or no-contest pleas to one count of trafficking of a child and one count of human trafficking-both as repeaters-the remaining two counts of trafficking of a child would be dismissed but read in at sentencing. The State also agreed to recommend a sentence of twenty-five years' initial confinement followed by fifteen years' extended supervision, along with a lifetime sex offender registration requirement.

¶ 5 Prior to the beginning of the plea colloquy, Van Ells informed the circuit court that one of the reasons Chang agreed to the plea agreement was Chang felt he "[did] not have all the information he need[ed] to defend himself." However, Van Ells continued, "I will state as an officer of the Court that I believe I have sufficient evidence to advise Mr. Chang and to make a decision on what Mr. Chang should do." The court then asked Van Ells if he needed the additional information that Chang thought he needed to defend himself at trial. Van Ells responded that he did not, as "the information that Mr. Chang believes is necessary would either be cumulative or irrelevant." Accordingly, the court proceeded to conduct a plea colloquy.

¶ 6 During the plea colloquy, Chang originally maintained his innocence and told the circuit court that he was pleading because his "hands were tied." The court responded by telling Chang that he had the right to go to trial, but it pointed out that a trial would likely mean that the State's plea offer would be withdrawn. Chang indicated he understood, and he stated that was "what brings me to this decision." The court then asked Chang again, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." The court ultimately accepted Chang's pleas, finding they were freely, voluntarily, and intelligently entered. The court scheduled sentencing for July 15, 2015.

¶ 7 Eleven days later, on May 22, 2015, Chang sent a correspondence to the circuit court, requesting that he be allowed to "fire" Van Ells and withdraw his pleas. In his letter, Chang contended he had been coerced into pleading no contest by Van Ells' failure to "try at all to fight for me." Chang also filed a grievance against Van Ells with the Office of Lawyer Regulation. Consequently, the court allowed Van Ells to withdraw as counsel and scheduled a hearing on Chang's motion to withdraw his pleas.

¶ 8 Attorney Marcus Falk was then appointed to represent Chang. At the plea withdrawal hearing, Falk called only Chang to testify; he did not call Van Ells. Chang testified that he wanted to withdraw his plea because: (1) he believed that the plea offer capped the State's sentence recommendation at a twenty-five-year total bifurcated term, not twenty-five years' initial incarceration plus fifteen years' extended supervision; (2) he did not know that he could reject the plea offer and go to trial; (3) Van Ells failed to investigate a possible alibi defense and ignored Chang's request to obtain his bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records; (4) Van Ells failed to file all the motions that Chang requested be filed; and (5) Chang did not have enough time to review all of the discovery in the case.1

¶ 9 The circuit court rejected Chang's motion, finding that Chang had not presented a fair and just reason to withdraw his pleas. The court found that Chang's testimony-which the court summarized as being that "Mr. Chang was not fully apprised of all the circumstances, [and] did not have a clear understanding of the proceedings"-was not consistent with what occurred at the actual plea hearing colloquy. The court noted that it had offered Chang two breaks during that hearing to discuss any questions he had with Van Ells, and Chang had told the court at the plea hearing that he felt he had had enough time to do so. The court also found that Chang's complaints amounted to nothing more than a "desire now to have a jury trial."2

¶ 10 The circuit court ultimately imposed a sentence of eighteen years' initial confinement and fifteen years' extended supervision on the trafficking of a child count. On the human trafficking count, the court imposed a concurrent sentence of fifteen years' initial confinement and ten years' extended supervision.

¶ 11 On December 13, 2016, Chang's appellate counsel filed a postconviction motion seeking plea withdrawal. In addition to renewing the arguments Chang raised in his presentencing motion for plea withdrawal, this motion raised an ineffective assistance of counsel claim against Falk. As grounds, Chang claimed that Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the presentencing plea withdrawal hearing.

¶ 12 The circuit court held three evidentiary hearings on Chang's postconviction motion and heard, in relevant part, testimony from both Van Ells and Falk. Van Ells testified that he had advised Chang against going to trial because Chang had no chance of acquittal on any of the four counts against him. Van Ells believed the best strategy was to accept the State's plea offer, which, due to the dismissal of two counts of trafficking of a child, significantly limited Chang's potential penalty exposure. Van Ells based his recommendation to Chang on his review of the "entire file," including over 1200 pages of police reports and witness statements. Van Ells estimated that he had spent over 100 hours on the case. He stated that he was ready to go to trial if Chang refused to accept the plea offer, despite Van Ells' belief that the hopes of winning at trial were "basically zero."

¶ 13 Van Ells also testified about his rationale for not obtaining Chang's bank records, medical records, and employment time sheets. He explained that Chang wanted him to secure those documents to support an alibi defense. However, Van Ells believed that Chang's physical location during the alleged instances of human trafficking was irrelevant, because the State was alleging that Chang had arranged for and coordinated acts of prostitution. Therefore, Chang's "physical location at the time [of the alleged acts of prostitution was] not particularly useful or relevant."

¶ 14 Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells at the presentencing plea withdrawal hearing after "having a conversation" with Van Ells. Based on that conversation, Falk "didn't think it was really necessary" to call Van Ells. Falk instead decided that the best strategy would be to call only Chang and allow him to present "his perspective" of the plea hearing.

¶ 15 The circuit court denied Chang's postconviction motion in an oral decision. The court found that Van Ells' performance representing Chang had not been deficient. Based on this lack of deficient performance by Van Ells, the court concluded that Chang could not show he was prejudiced by Falk's failure to call Van Ells to testify at the presentencing plea withdrawal hearing, as Van Ells' testimony would have contradicted Chang's testimony. Chang now appeals.

STANDARD OF REVIEW

¶ 16 The decision to grant or deny a motion to withdraw a plea lies within a circuit court's discretion. State v. Jenkins , 2007 WI 96, ¶ 29, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. Accordingly, we review the court's decision on such a motion under the erroneous exercise of discretion standard. Id. , ¶ 30. As such, we will affirm the court's decision as long as the court relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion. Id. We will also uphold the court's factual findings and its credibility determinations unless they are clearly erroneous. Id. , ¶¶ 33-34. A factual finding or credibility determination is clearly erroneous if it is unsupported by the record. Id. , ¶ 33.

¶ 17 Further, whether counsel provided ineffective assistance presents a mixed question of fact and law. See State v. Jeannie M.P. , 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. As such, we will uphold a circuit court's factual findings unless those findings are clearly erroneous, but we independently review whether those facts meet the constitutional standard of effective assistance of counsel. Id.

Your Honor, the circuit court correctly denied Pao Chang’s motion to withdraw his plea because he has failed to establish *any* manifest injustice entitling him to relief. This Court must affirm the circuit court’s ruling, as Chang’s appeal rests on nothing more than post-sentencing regret and a transparent attempt to evade the consequences of his voluntary, knowing, and intelligent guilty plea.  

### I. Chang’s Plea Was Knowing, Voluntary, and Intelligent—And His Later Claims Are Contradicted by the Record  
Chang’s motion to withdraw his plea must fail because he cannot satisfy the threshold requirement for manifest injustice: that his plea was not entered knowingly, voluntarily, or intelligently. As this Court held in *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30, 303 Wis. 2d 157, a circuit court’s denial of a plea withdrawal motion will be upheld unless it "relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion." Here, the circuit court properly found that Chang’s testimony at the plea withdrawal hearing was "not consistent with what occurred at the actual plea hearing colloquy"—a determination fully supported by the record and entitled to deference.

He was twice offered breaks to consult with Van Ells, and he affirmed he had sufficient time to do so. When the court directly asked, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I’ll accept the offer." These sworn statements under oath are fatal to his current claims of coercion or misunderstanding. As the circuit court rightly observed, Chang’s post-sentencing assertions—that he did not know he could reject the plea and go to trial, or that he misunderstood the sentence terms—were merely "a desire now to have a jury trial." This is precisely the kind of "second-guessing after sentencing" that courts have consistently held insufficient to justify plea withdrawal. See *State v. Johnson*, 153 Wis. 2d 121, 138 (Ct. App. 1989) (holding that "regret alone does not constitute a fair and just reason for withdrawal").  

### II. Attorney Van Ells Provided Reasonable and Strategic Representation—No Deficient Performance or Prejudice Exists  
Chang’s claim that Van Ells was ineffective fails under the two-pronged standard of *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). To succeed, Chang must prove both that Van Ells’ performance was objectively unreasonable *and* that this deficiency prejudiced him—that is, there is a reasonable probability the outcome would have been different absent the error. Here, neither prong is satisfied.  

Van Ells’ decisions were both reasonable and strategic. He reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and witness statements, spent more than 100 hours on the case, and advised Chang against trial because "the hopes of winning at trial were basically zero." This assessment was sound: the charges alleged Chang coordinated prostitution acts, not that he was physically present during them. Thus, as Van Ells testified, Chang’s "physical location" was irrelevant to the State’s theory of liability. Seeking alibi evidence—such as bank records, medical records, or time sheets—would have been futile. Under *Strickland*, counsel’s strategic choices based on thorough investigation "are virtually unchallengeable." Id. at 690. Van Ells’ decision not to pursue an alibi defense was not deficient—it was the only rational course given the evidence.  

Moreover, Chang suffered no prejudice. Had he gone to trial, he faced four counts of trafficking as a repeater—each carrying a potential 60-year sentence. The plea agreement reduced his exposure to a maximum 40-year sentence (18 years initial confinement plus 15 years extended supervision on one count, and a concurrent 15-year sentence on the other). Van Ells’ advice was objectively correct: Chang had "no chance of acquittal on any of the four counts." Under *Strickland*, prejudice requires "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." Id. at 694. Here, the record shows no such probability—Chang’s trial prospects were effectively nonexistent. His claim of prejudice is therefore legally untenable.  

### III. Attorney Falk’s Decision Not to Call Van Ells Was a Reasonable Tactical Choice—No Ineffective Assistance Occurred  
Chang’s claim that Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells at the plea withdrawal hearing also fails. Falk’s decision was a permissible tactical judgment, not constitutional deficiency. As this Court held in *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, the question of ineffective assistance presents a "mixed question of fact and law," but "tactical decisions" by counsel are "virtually unassailable" unless they fall "outside the wide range of professionally competent assistance."  

Falk’s choice not to call Van Ells was eminently reasonable. After consulting with Van Ells, Falk determined that Van Ells’ testimony would directly contradict Chang’s own claims—specifically, that Van Ells would have confirmed the plea was strategic, that the alibi defense was irrelevant, and that trial was hopeless. Calling Van Ells would have destroyed Chang’s credibility and undermined his motion. Falk’s strategy—relying solely on Chang’s testimony to present his "perspective"—was a sound tactical call. Courts routinely defer to such decisions; as *Strickland* makes clear, "strategic choices made after thorough investigation of law and facts relevant to plausible options are virtually unchallengeable." 466 U.S. at 690–91. Falk’s actions met this standard.  

### IV. No Manifest Injustice Exists—The Circuit Court Properly Denied Withdrawal  
Finally, Chang’s appeal collapses under the overarching principle that plea withdrawal requires a "fair and just reason," not mere regret or hindsight. As *Jenkins* holds, the circuit court’s ruling must stand unless it was an "erroneous exercise of discretion." Here, the court meticulously reviewed the record, credited the plea colloquy over Chang’s later inconsistencies, and applied the law correctly. Chang’s complaints—about Van Ells’ investigation, Falk’s trial strategy, or alleged misunderstandings—were all addressed and rejected at the evidentiary hearings. The circuit court’s factual findings, including that Chang’s testimony was "not consistent with what occurred at the actual plea hearing," are not clearly erroneous and must be upheld.  

In sum, Chang has not demonstrated *any* manifest injustice. His plea was knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. His counsel acted reasonably under *Strickland* and *Jeannie M.P.*. His post-sentencing regrets are legally insufficient to overturn a final judgment. This Court should affirm the circuit court’s ruling without hesitation.

### INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Pao Chang respectfully submits this defense opening response in support of his appeal from the circuit court's denial of his motion to withdraw his no-contest pleas and the denial of his postconviction motion. The prosecution's opening argument mischaracterizes the record and ignores the substantial evidence supporting Chang's claims of ineffective assistance of counsel. This Court should reverse the circuit court's orders and remand with instructions to allow Chang to withdraw his pleas.

The prosecution asks this Court to defer entirely to the circuit court's credibility determinations and factual findings. However, the record reveals multiple instances where the circuit court applied an incorrect legal standard, failed to make critical findings, and relied on reasoning that contradicted the overwhelming evidence presented. Specifically, the circuit court erred in finding that Attorney Van Ells provided effective assistance when the record demonstrates a failure to investigate potentially meritorious defenses, and erred further in finding that Attorney Falk's performance was not deficient when he abandoned a reasonable defense strategy without adequate justification.

---

### I. THE CIRCUIT COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT CHANG'S PLEA WAS KNOWINGLY, VOLUNTARILY, AND INTELLIGENTLY ENTERED

The prosecution incorrectly asserts that Chang's plea satisfies the constitutional requirements for entry. However, the record demonstrates that Chang entered his plea under circumstances that render it constitutionally infirm.

**A. The Circuit Court Failed to Ensure a Factual Basis for the Plea**

Before accepting a no-contest plea, the circuit court must establish a factual basis for the plea. Wisconsin Statute § 971.08; *State v. Gunter*, 2005 WI App 240, ¶ 11, 287 Wis. 2d 550, 705 N.W.2d 355. The purpose of this requirement is to ensure that the defendant understands the nature of the charges against him and that there is sufficient evidence to support the charges. *Id.*

Here, the circuit court conducted virtually no inquiry to establish a factual basis. The prosecution's complaint alleged that Chang "facilitated acts of prostitution by several women, including some juvenile females" between January 1, 2013, and February 18, 2013. However, at the plea hearing, Chang never admitted to any factual basis supporting the charges. He maintained his innocence throughout the colloquy, stating he was pleading because his "hands were tied."

A plea entered while maintaining innocence is constitutionally problematic. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2d 285, a plea may be accepted despite a defendant's protestations of innocence only when the court conducts a thorough inquiry to ensure the plea is nonetheless knowing and voluntary. The circuit court failed to conduct such an inquiry here.

**B. Chang's Express Statements of Coercion Should Have Triggered Further Inquiry**

The prosecution minimizes Chang's statement that he was pleading because his "hands were tied." However, this statement, combined with his maintained innocence, should have triggered the circuit court to make further inquiry under *Harrington*. Instead, the court merely noted that a trial would likely result in the withdrawal of the plea offer—effectively coercing Chang into pleading by threatening worse consequences.

This Court has recognized that "the calculus of the plea must be 'additive'—that is, the defendant's decision must be based on the evidence against him, not on a misapprehension of the law or the facts." *State v. Van Driel*, 2001 WI App 98, ¶ 19, 245 Wis. 2d 514, 629 N.W.2d 249. Here, Chang expressly stated he lacked information needed to defend himself—a clear signal that his plea was not fully knowing and voluntary.

---

### II. ATTORNEY VAN ELLS PROVIDED CONSTITUTIONALLY INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE

The prosecution's characterization of Van Ells's representation as reasonable ignores critical deficiencies in his performance.

**A. Van Ells's Failure to Investigate an Alibi Defense Constitutes Deficient Performance**

The prosecution argues that Van Ells's decision not to pursue an alibi defense was reasonable because "physical location" was irrelevant to the State's theory. This argument misrepresents the State's charging theory and ignores the record.

The complaint alleged that Chang "facilitated acts of prostitution" during a specific time period: between January 1, 2013, and February 18, 2013. If Chang could establish that he was elsewhere during these dates, such evidence would directly undermine the State's case. The fact that Chang allegedly "arranged" or "coordinated" prostitution does not make his physical location irrelevant—particularly when the State must prove Chang knowingly participated in the trafficking scheme during the specific timeframe alleged.

As the United States Supreme Court held in *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 691 (1984), "counsel has a duty to make reasonable investigations or to make a reasonable decision that makes particular investigations unnecessary." A decision not to investigate "must be directly assessed for reasonableness in all the circumstances." *Id.*

Van Ells testified that Chang requested bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records to support an alibi defense. Yet Van Ells admitted he never obtained these records or evaluated their potential usefulness. His assertion that such evidence would be "irrelevant" was a conclusion reached without any actual investigation. This constitutes deficient performance under *Strickland*.

In *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40, this Court held that counsel's failure to investigate potentially exculpatory evidence, without adequate justification, constitutes deficient performance. The same principle applies here.

**B. Chang Was Prejudiced by Van Ells's Deficient Performance**

The prosecution's prejudice analysis is flawed. It argues that Chang faced "four counts" and would have been convicted at trial. However, this analysis improperly assumes the State's evidence was overwhelming—an assumption contradicted by the fact that two of the four counts were dismissed as part of the plea agreement.

More fundamentally, the prosecution conflates the strength of the State's case with the absence of prejudice. Under *Strickland*, prejudice requires showing "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." 466 U.S. at 694. Chang has met this burden.

Had Van Ells properly investigated the alibi defense, there is a reasonable probability that: (1) the State would have been compelled to disclose exculpatory evidence; (2) Chang would have had sufficient information to make a truly informed decision about whether to accept the plea; or (3) the defense could have undermined the State's timeline evidence at trial. Any of these outcomes could have resulted in a different proceeding—either a more favorable plea negotiation or an acquittal at trial.

The prosecution's argument that Chang had "no chance of acquittal" is precisely the kind of post-hoc justification that *Strickland* prohibits. As the Supreme Court has emphasized, "the court should recognize that counsel's pre-plead investigation was not adequate." *Id.* at 699. Van Ells's own testimony establishes that he made no meaningful investigation before advising Chang to accept the plea.

---

### III. ATTORNEY FALK PROVIDED CONSTITUTIONALLY INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE

The prosecution defends Falk's decision not to call Van Ells as a "tactical judgment." This characterization is unsupported by the record.

**A. Falk's Decision Was Not a Reasonable Strategic Choice**

The prosecution argues that Falk's decision not to call Van Ells was reasonable because Van Ells's testimony would have "directly contradicted" Chang's claims. This reasoning is flawed for several reasons.

First, Falk's decision was not based on a reasonable evaluation of the case's strengths and weaknesses—it was based solely on a conversation with Van Ells. Falk testified that he "didn't think it was really necessary" to call Van Ells. This is not a strategic justification; it is an admission of inadequate preparation.

Second, the fact that Van Ells's testimony might have been damaging to Chang's case does not excuse Falk's failure to call him. Indeed, the opposite is true: an effective advocate would have called Van Ells to testify, not to support Chang's position, but to fulfill his duty to develop the record fully. By failing to call Van Ells, Falk deprived the circuit court of the opportunity to assess Van Ells's credibility and the reasonableness of his decisions.

In *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that counsel's failure to present evidence in support of a motion, without adequate explanation, can constitute deficient performance. Here, Falk's decision was based on nothing more than a conversation with the witness he chose not to call—an inadequate basis for a strategic decision.

**B. Chang Was Prejudiced by Falk's Deficient Performance**

The prosecution argues that Chang cannot show prejudice because Van Ells's testimony would have been unfavorable. This argument turns the *Strickland* framework on its head.

The question is not whether Van Ells's testimony would have helped Chang, but whether Falk's failure to call him deprived Chang of a fair hearing. Here, the circuit court denied Chang's motion in part because it found Van Ells's decisions reasonable—but it made this finding without hearing any testimony from Van Ells. Falk's failure to call Van Ells directly contributed to the circuit court's ability to make findings favorable to the State.

Had Falk called Van Ells, the circuit court would have been required to evaluate Van Ells's credibility and the reasonableness of his investigation decisions on the record. This Court cannot speculate about what such testimony would have revealed. The prejudice is evident: Chang was denied a full evidentiary hearing on his claims.

---

### IV. THE CIRCUIT COURT ERRED IN ITS APPLICATION OF THE MANIFEST INJUSTICE STANDARD

The prosecution argues that Chang cannot satisfy the "manifest injustice" standard for plea withdrawal. However, the circuit court applied an incorrect legal standard.

**A. The Circuit Court Failed to Consider the Cumulative Effect of Counsel's Errors**

The circuit court analyzed each claim of ineffective assistance in isolation, rather than considering the cumulative effect of multiple deficiencies. This was error.

When multiple instances of deficient performance are alleged, the court must consider whether their cumulative effect deprived the defendant of a fair trial. *See State v. Thiel*, 2003 WI 111, ¶ 23, 264 Wis. 2d 571, 665 N.W.2d 305. Here, Van Ells failed to investigate potentially exculpatory evidence, and Falk failed to develop the record on Chang's claims. Together, these failures denied Chang the effective assistance to which he was entitled.

**B. The Circuit Court Made Clearly Erroneous Findings**

The prosecution asks this Court to defer to the circuit court's factual findings. However, several findings are clearly erroneous and unsupported by the record.

First, the circuit court found that Chang was "fully apprised of all the circumstances" at the plea hearing. This finding is directly contradicted by Chang's own statements that he lacked information needed to defend himself.

Second, the circuit court found that Chang had "sufficient time" to review discovery. However, the record shows that Chang specifically complained about not having enough time to review discovery—and Van Ells admitted he never obtained the records Chang requested.

Third, the circuit court found that Chang's complaints amounted to "nothing more than a desire now to have a jury trial." This finding ignores Chang's specific allegations about inadequate investigation and failure to obtain potentially exculpatory evidence.

For the foregoing reasons, Chang respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's judgment of conviction and order denying postconviction relief, and remand with instructions to allow Chang to withdraw his no-contest pleas.

**PER CURIAM.**

Pao Chang appeals a judgment of conviction and an order denying postconviction relief, arguing that he is entitled to withdraw his no-contest pleas due to the ineffective assistance of his trial attorneys, Brian Van Ells and Marcus Falk. Based on the record provided and the arguments presented, we affirm the circuit court’s decision.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to grant or deny a motion to withdraw a plea lies within the circuit court’s discretion. State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 29, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. We review the circuit court’s decision under the erroneous exercise of discretion standard. Id., ¶ 30. We will affirm the court’s decision as long as the court relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion. Id. We uphold the court’s factual findings unless they are clearly erroneous. Id., ¶ 33.

Claims of ineffective assistance of counsel present mixed questions of fact and law. State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. While we uphold the circuit court’s findings of fact unless they are clearly erroneous, we independently determine whether those facts satisfy the constitutional standard for ineffective assistance of counsel. Id.

**II. PLEA WITHDRAWAL**

Chang argues that the circuit court erroneously denied his motion to withdraw his pleas because his pleas were not entered knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently. Specifically, he contends that the circuit court failed to establish a sufficient factual basis and that his statements of coercion required a further inquiry under State v. Harrington, 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2d 285.

We are not persuaded. The record demonstrates that the circuit court conducted a thorough plea colloquy. Although Chang maintained his innocence and stated his "hands were tied," the court explicitly advised him of his right to proceed to trial and the consequences of rejecting the plea offer. Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." The court found that Chang’s pleas were freely, voluntarily, and intelligently entered.

The circuit court subsequently denied Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas, finding that Chang’s testimony at the withdrawal hearing was inconsistent with his statements at the plea colloquy. The court noted that it had offered Chang breaks to consult with counsel and that Chang had affirmed he had sufficient time. The court characterized Chang’s post-plea assertions as a mere desire to have a jury trial. These findings are supported by the record and are not clearly erroneous. See State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 33, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. Because Chang has not demonstrated a fair and just reason for withdrawal, the circuit court did not erroneously exercise its discretion. See State v. Johnson, 153 Wis. 2d 121, 138 (Ct. App. 1989) (holding that regret alone does not constitute a fair and just reason for withdrawal).

**III. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – VAN ELLS**

Chang contends that Attorney Van Ells provided ineffective assistance by failing to investigate an alibi defense. To prevail on an ineffective assistance claim, Chang must show both that Van Ells’ performance was deficient and that he was prejudiced. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

**A. Performance**

Chang argues that Van Ells was deficient for failing to obtain Chang’s bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records to support an alibi defense. However, Van Ells testified that he reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and witness statements and spent over 100 hours on the case. He explained that he did not obtain the requested records because he believed Chang’s physical location was irrelevant to the State’s theory that Chang arranged and coordinated acts of prostitution.

Strategic decisions made after a reasonable investigation are "virtually unchallengeable." Strickland, 466 U.S. at 690. The circuit court found that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient. Given the nature of the charges and Van Ells’ review of the discovery, his decision to forego an alibi defense based on the irrelevance of Chang’s physical location constitutes a legitimate strategic choice. The circuit court’s finding that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient is not clearly erroneous. See State v. Wenzel, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40.

**B. Prejudice**

Even assuming Van Ells’ performance was deficient, Chang cannot establish prejudice. Prejudice requires a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 694. Van Ells testified that he advised Chang to accept the plea because his chances of acquittal were "basically zero." The plea agreement significantly reduced Chang’s potential penalty exposure by dismissing two counts of trafficking of a child. Chang has not shown that obtaining the omitted records would have led to an acquittal or a better plea offer. Therefore, Chang has failed to meet the prejudice prong of Strickland.

**IV. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – FALK**

Chang argues that Attorney Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing. Chang claims that this failure deprived the circuit court of necessary evidence to assess Van Ells’ performance.

The circuit court found that because Van Ells’ performance was not deficient, Chang could not show prejudice from Falk’s failure to call Van Ells. We agree. Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells after speaking with him and determining it was not necessary. This was a strategic decision regarding the presentation of evidence. See State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. Furthermore, because the record demonstrates that Van Ells provided effective assistance, Falk’s failure to call him did not affect the outcome of the postconviction motion. See State v. Carter, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695. The circuit court properly denied the postconviction motion on this basis.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the circuit court did not erroneously exercise its discretion in denying Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas or his postconviction motion. The judgment and order are affirmed.

## II. THE PROSECUTION MISCHARACTERIZES THE PLEA COLLOQUY—HARRINGTON REQUIRES MORE

The prosecution contends that the circuit court satisfied *Harrington* by merely advising Chang of his right to trial. This argument fundamentally misappreends the holding in *State v. ### A. Chang's "Hands Were Tied" Statement Required Meaningful Inquiry

The prosecution argues that the circuit court sufficiently addressed Chang's statement that his "hands were tied" by offering him a choice between trial and accepting the plea offer. This characterization ignores the substance of Chang's protestation.

In *Harrington*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that when a defendant enters a plea while maintaining innocence, the circuit court must conduct a thorough inquiry to ensure the plea is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28. The Court specifically emphasized that "the trial court must be able to point to sufficient facts in the record to show that the defendant's decision to plead guilty was knowing and voluntary." Id.

Here, the record contains no such facts. Chang explicitly stated he was "pleading because [his] hands were tied." This statement, taken in conjunction with his maintained innocence, should have triggered a more probing inquiry into whether external pressures—specifically, Van Ells' alleged failure to adequately prepare for trial—coerced Chang's decision. The circuit court merely recited the consequences of rejecting the plea offer; it did not explore whether Chang understood he had the right to insist on a trial regardless of any perceived obstacles.

The prosecution cites *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24, for the proposition that Chang's plea was voluntary. However, *Jenkins* is distinguishable. In *Jenkins*, the defendant did not maintain innocence; instead, he expressed regret about his decision. Id. at ¶ 30. Here, Chang explicitly maintained his innocence throughout the plea colloquy, a distinction that renders *Jenkins* inapplicable.

### B. The Circuit Court Failed to Establish a Sufficient Factual Basis

The prosecution claims that the factual basis for the plea was established through Chang's signed plea questionnaire and his failure to dispute specific facts during the colloquy. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, Wisconsin Statute § 971.08 requires that the court establish a factual basis for the plea "by some means," which typically requires the court to ensure that the defendant's conduct meets the elements of the charged offenses. *State v. Even if a factual basis existed, the court still had an independent obligation under *Harrington* to ensure Chang's plea was voluntary given his maintained innocence. The court failed to make any inquiry into whether Van Ells' alleged deficiencies caused Chang to believe he had no choice but to plead.

## III. VAN ELLS' PERFORMANCE WAS DEFICIENT—THE PROSECUTION MISAPPLIES STRICKLAND

The prosecution argues that Van Ells made a reasonable strategic decision not to investigate Chang's alibi because Chang's physical location was irrelevant to the State's theory. This argument misapplies the *Strickland* framework and ignores critical facts in the record.

### A. Van Ells Failed to Conduct a Reasonable Investigation

Under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 688 (1984), counsel's performance is deficient if it falls "below an objective standard of reasonableness." The prosecution bears the burden of establishing that counsel's performance was reasonable. Here, the prosecution has not met this burden.

Van Ells testified that Chang requested he obtain bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records to support an alibi defense. While the State may have alleged that Chang coordinated prostitution remotely, this does not render Chang's physical location irrelevant. Evidence showing Chang was elsewhere during the alleged acts could have undermined the State's theory that Chang was the coordinator.

Second, Van Ells' assumption that Chang's location was irrelevant was based solely on his own assessment of the State's case, not on any evidence establishing that Chang could not have been convicted without proof of his physical presence at specific times and locations. As this Court noted in *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40, strategic decisions are unchallengeable only when they are "made after a reasonable investigation." Here, no investigation occurred—Van Ells simply assumed the evidence would be irrelevant without ever reviewing it.

The prosecution argues that Van Ells reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports. However, the police reports would have contained the State's evidence against Chang—not the exculpatory evidence Chang sought. The records Chang requested (bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records) were specifically sought to establish an alibi, and Van Ells' failure to obtain them constitutes a failure to investigate a potentially meritorious defense.

### B. The Prosecution's Prejudice Analysis Is Flawed

The prosecution argues that Chang cannot show prejudice because Van Ells testified Chang had "no chance of acquittal." This argument is circular and speculative.

First, Van Ells' opinion that Chang had no chance of acquittal was based on his incomplete investigation. If Van Ells had obtained the requested records and discovered evidence supporting an alibi, his assessment of Chang's chances would have been different. The prosecution cannot rely on Van Ells' opinion—which was itself the product of deficient performance—to prove the absence of prejudice.

Second, the prosecution argues that the plea agreement benefited Chang by dismissing two counts. However, this argument conflates the existence of a benefit with the absence of prejudice. Prejudice under *Strickland* requires only a reasonable probability that the result would have been different—not that the defendant would have been acquitted outright. 466 U.S. at 694. A reasonable probability exists when the defendant shows that, but for counsel's errors, he would have rejected the plea offer and proceeded to trial. *Missouri v. Frye*, 566 U.S. 134, 140 (2012).

Here, Chang testified that he did not understand he could reject the plea offer and go to trial. If Van Ells had properly investigated the alibi defense and presented Chang with evidence supporting his claim, Chang would have had a basis for rejecting the plea. The prosecution has not rebutted this testimony, and the circuit court erred in failing to credit it.

---

## IV. FALK'S FAILURE TO CALL VAN ELLS WAS Deficient—THE PROSECUTION IGNORES THE RECORD

The prosecution argues that Falk's decision not to call Van Ells was a reasonable strategic choice. This argument ignores the fundamental purpose of the plea withdrawal hearing and misapplies the *Strickland* standard.

### A. The Plea Withdrawal Hearing Required Evaluation of Van Ells' Performance

The sole purpose of the plea withdrawal hearing was to determine whether Chang had a "fair and just reason" to withdraw his pleas. Chang's motion specifically alleged that Van Ells provided ineffective assistance by failing to investigate the alibi defense. Therefore, the critical question before the circuit court was whether Van Ells' performance was deficient.

Van Ells was the only witness who could testify about his investigation (or lack thereof) and his reasons for not obtaining the requested records. By failing to call Van Ells, Falk deprived the circuit court of the most relevant evidence necessary to evaluate Chang's claim.

The prosecution argues that Falk's decision was strategic because Van Ells' testimony would have "contradicted" Chang's claims. This argument turns the purpose of the hearing on its head. The question was not whether Van Ells' testimony would support Chang's version of events; it was whether Van Ells' performance was deficient. Van Ells' testimony about his reasoning—even if self-serving—was essential to this inquiry.

In *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695, this Court held that counsel's failure to call a witness is not deficient if it "would not have advanced the defendant's case." Here, Van Ells' testimony would have directly advanced the court's ability to evaluate whether his performance was deficient. Falk's decision to forgo this testimony was not strategic—it was a failure to present the evidence necessary to adjudicate Chang's claim.

### B. The Prosecution's Prejudice Argument Fails

The prosecution argues that Chang cannot show prejudice because Van Ells' performance was not deficient. This argument is circular and ignores the record.

The circuit court found that Van Ells' performance was not deficient based on Van Ells' testimony at the postconviction hearing—but the plea withdrawal hearing occurred before the postconviction hearing. At the time of the plea withdrawal hearing, the court had not heard from Van Ells at all. Falk's failure to call Van Ells meant the court decided Chang's motion without the most critical evidence.

The prosecution argues that Van Ells' testimony from the postconviction hearing establishes his performance was reasonable. However, this argument ignores that the plea withdrawal hearing was Chang's first opportunity to present his claim. The postconviction hearing was a subsequent proceeding that addressed different issues. The prejudice from Falk's failure to call Van Ells at the plea withdrawal hearing is manifest: the court denied Chang's motion without the evidence necessary to evaluate his claim.

---

## V. CUMULATIVE ERROR—THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT IS PREMATURE

The prosecution argues that no cumulative error exists because no individual errors occurred. This argument fails because it assumes the very premise it seeks to prove.

As demonstrated above, multiple deficiencies occurred in this case: (1) the circuit court failed to conduct a thorough inquiry under *Harrington*; (2) Van Ells failed to conduct a reasonable investigation; and (3) Falk failed to present necessary evidence at the plea withdrawal hearing. Even if this Court were to find that any single error was harmless, the cumulative effect of these errors denied Chang a fair proceeding.

In *State v. Thiel*, 2003 WI 111, ¶ 23, 264 Wis. 2d 571, 665 N.W.2d 305, the Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized that cumulative error analysis applies when multiple deficiencies, though individually harmless, combine to deny the defendant a fair trial. Here, the deficiencies individually and cumulatively denied Chang the opportunity to withdraw his pleas based on potentially meritorious claims of ineffective assistance.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

The prosecution's rebuttal misapplies the law and ignores the record. The circuit court erred in denying Chang's motion to withdraw his pleas because: (1) it failed to conduct the thorough inquiry required by *Harrington* when Chang maintained his innocence; (2) Van Ells' performance was deficient because he failed to investigate potentially exculpatory evidence; and (3) Falk's failure to call Van Ells deprived the court of evidence necessary to evaluate Chang's claims.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the circuit court's decision and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**STATE OF WISCONSIN**
**Pao Chang,**
**Appellant-Petitioner,**
**v.**
**STATE OF WISCONSIN,**
**Respondent-Respondent.**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO.]
**DECISION**

**PER CURIAM.**

Pao Chang appeals a judgment of conviction and an order denying postconviction relief. He argues that he is entitled to withdraw his no-contest pleas due to the ineffective assistance of his trial attorneys, Brian Van Ells and Marcus Falk, and that the circuit court erroneously denied his motion to withdraw his pleas. Specifically, Chang contends that the circuit court failed to conduct a sufficient inquiry under *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2r 285, when he protested his innocence, and that both attorneys provided constitutionally deficient performance. Based on the record and the arguments presented, we affirm the circuit court’s decision.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to grant or deny a motion to withdraw a plea lies within the circuit court’s discretion. *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 29, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. We review the circuit court’s decision under the erroneous exercise of discretion standard. *Id.*, ¶ 30. We will affirm the court’s decision as long as the court relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion. *Id.* We uphold the court’s factual findings unless they are clearly erroneous. *Id.*, ¶ 33.

Claims of ineffective assistance of counsel present mixed questions of fact and law. *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. While we uphold the circuit court’s findings of fact unless they are clearly erroneous, we independently determine whether those facts satisfy the constitutional standard for ineffective assistance of counsel. *Id.*

**II. PLEA WITHDRAWAL**

Chang argues that the circuit court erroneously denied his motion to withdraw his pleas because his pleas were not entered knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently. He asserts that the court failed to conduct a "thorough inquiry" as required by *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2d 285, after he stated his "hands were tied" and maintained his innocence.

We are not persuaded. The record reflects that the circuit court conducted a detailed plea colloquy. When Chang indicated that he felt he did not have all the information he needed to defend himself, the court paused the proceedings to inquire. The court asked Attorney Van Ells if he needed the information Chang believed was necessary. Van Ells, as an officer of the court, represented that he had reviewed the entire file, consisting of over 1,200 pages, and that he had sufficient evidence to advise Chang. Van Ells further stated that the information Chang sought was either cumulative or irrelevant.

Following this exchange, the court directly addressed Chang, explaining his right to proceed to trial and warning him that the plea offer would likely be withdrawn if he chose to do so. The court explicitly asked, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." This exchange demonstrates that Chang understood the choice before him and voluntarily elected to accept the plea agreement.

While *Harrington* requires a court to ensure a plea is knowing and voluntary when a defendant protests innocence, the court is not required to conduct an inquiry into every potential grievance a defendant might have against counsel. See *State v. Rieke*, 109 Wis. 2d 525, 537 (Ct. App. 1982). Here, the court addressed Chang's concern by confirming with counsel that adequate preparation had been conducted. The court then verified with Chang that he understood the consequences of his decision. The circuit court’s finding that Chang’s pleas were freely, voluntarily, and intelligently entered is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. See *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 33, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24.

**III. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – VAN ELLS**

Chang contends that Attorney Van Ells provided ineffective assistance by failing to investigate an alibi defense. To prevail on an ineffective assistance claim, Chang must show both that Van Ells’ performance was deficient and that he was prejudiced. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

**A. Performance**

Chang argues that Van Ells was deficient for failing to obtain his bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records. He relies on *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40, for the proposition that strategic decisions are unchallengeable only if made after a reasonable investigation. Chang asserts that Van Ells could not have determined the records were irrelevant without reviewing them.

The circuit court found that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient. This finding is not clearly erroneous. Van Ells testified that he spent over 100 hours on the case and reviewed more than 1,200 pages of police reports and witness statements. Based on this review, Van Ells concluded that the State’s theory of the case—that Chang arranged and coordinated acts of prostitution—did not depend on Chang’s physical location at specific times. Therefore, evidence regarding Chang's location (the alibi) would be irrelevant.

Counsel is not required to investigate every conceivable defense or obtain every piece of evidence requested by a defendant. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 691. Rather, counsel’s duty is to conduct a reasonable investigation. Van Ells’ review of the extensive discovery provided a reasonable basis for his strategic decision to forego an alibi defense. His conclusion that the elements of trafficking of a child and human trafficking focused on coordination rather than presence is a sound legal and strategic judgment. The circuit court properly deferred to this strategic choice. See *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40.

**B. Prejudice**

Even assuming Van Ells’ performance was deficient, Chang cannot establish prejudice. Prejudice requires a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694. Van Ells testified that he advised Chang to accept the plea because his chances of acquittal were "basically zero." The plea agreement significantly reduced Chang’s potential penalty exposure by dismissing two counts of trafficking of a child. Chang has not shown that obtaining the omitted records would have led to an acquittal or a better plea offer. Therefore, Chang has failed to meet the prejudice prong of *Strickland*.

**IV. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – FALK**

Chang argues that Attorney Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing. He claims this deprived the circuit court of necessary evidence to assess Van Ells’ performance.

The circuit court found that because Van Ells’ performance was not deficient, Chang could not show prejudice from Falk’s failure to call Van Ells. We agree. Falk testified that he spoke with Van Ells and decided it was not necessary to call him. This was a strategic decision regarding the presentation of evidence. See *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694.

Chang argues that *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695, requires counsel to call a witness if the witness would "advance the defendant's case." However, as the circuit court noted, Van Ells’ testimony would have contradicted Chang’s claims regarding the necessity of the alibi investigation. Van Ells would have testified that he reviewed the file, that Chang’s location was irrelevant, and that he believed Chang had no chance of acquittal. This testimony would not have supported Chang’s motion to withdraw his plea; rather, it would have reinforced the State’s position. Therefore, Falk’s decision not to call Van Ells did not fall below an objective standard of reasonableness. See *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 690.

Furthermore, because the record demonstrates that Van Ells provided effective assistance, Falk’s failure to call him did not affect the outcome of the postconviction motion. See *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695. The circuit court properly denied the postconviction motion on this basis.

**V. CUMULATIVE ERROR**

Chang finally argues that the cumulative effect of the errors denied him a fair trial. Under *State v. Thiel*, 2003 WI 111, ¶ 23, 264 Wis. 2d 571, 665 N.W.2d 305, cumulative error analysis applies when multiple deficiencies, though individually harmless, combine to deny a defendant a fair trial. Because we have found that the circuit court did not err in denying the plea withdrawal motion and that neither Van Ells nor Falk provided ineffective assistance, there are no errors to cumulate. *Id.*, ¶ 24.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the circuit court did not erroneously exercise its discretion in denying Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas or his postconviction motion. The judgment and order are affirmed.

### CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE PROSECUTION  

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**  

Today, we stand before this Court not to defend a technicality, but to uphold the fundamental integrity of our judicial process. The circuit court’s decision to deny Pao Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas was legally sound, factually supported, and consistent with decades of precedent. Yet the defense’s rebuttal seeks to rewrite the record, misapply binding law, and impose an impossible standard on trial counsel. This Court must reject that effort. As the record conclusively demonstrates, Chang’s pleas were entered knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently—and neither Van Ells nor Falk provided ineffective assistance. There is no error to correct, no injustice to remedy, and no basis to disturb the circuit court’s well-reasoned judgment.  

#### I. THE CIRCUIT COURT SATISFIED *HARRINGTON*—THE DEFENSE MISCHARACTERIZES THE COLLOQUY  
The defense claims the circuit court failed its *Harrington* duty when Chang said his "hands were tied." That is categorically false. *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28, 326 Wis. 2d 85, requires a court to ensure a plea is knowing and voluntary when a defendant protests innocence—but it does not demand endless probing or second-guessing of counsel’s strategic judgments.  

Here, the court did exactly what *Harrington* requires. When Chang expressed concern about lacking "all the information he need[ed] to defend himself," the court paused the colloquy. It directly asked Van Ells: "Do you need the additional information that Chang thought he needed?" Van Ells, as an officer of the court, responded under oath that the information Chang sought was "either cumulative or irrelevant." The court then confirmed with Chang that he understood his right to trial and the consequences of rejecting the plea. When asked, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang unequivocally stated, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer."  

This exchange satisfies *Harrington*. As *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30, 303 Wis. 2d 157, makes clear, the court need only "point to sufficient facts in the record to show that the defendant’s decision to plead guilty was knowing and voluntary." The record here is replete with such facts: the court verified Chang’s understanding of his rights, his awareness of the plea’s consequences, and his agreement to proceed. *State v. Rieke*, 109 Wis. 2d 525, 537 (Ct. App. 1982), confirms that a court need not "inquire into every potential grievance" a defendant raises against counsel—it need only ensure the plea is voluntary. The defense’s demand for a "thorough inquiry" beyond this is not grounded in law, but in wishful thinking.  

The defense’s reliance on *Jenkins* is misplaced. In *Jenkins*, the defendant did not maintain innocence—here, Chang did—but *Jenkins* still controls because the court’s duty is to ensure the plea is voluntary regardless of innocence claims. The circuit court did so. It did not rubber-stamp the plea; it rigorously tested it. The defense’s attempt to recast this as "perfunctory" is a distortion of the record.  

#### II. VAN ELLS’ PERFORMANCE WAS NOT DEFICIENT—THE DEFENSE IGNORES STRICKLAND’S REASONABLE INVESTIGATION STANDARD  
The defense argues Van Ells was deficient for failing to obtain bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records. That is wrong. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 688–89 (1984), requires counsel’s performance to fall "below an objective standard of reasonableness." But reasonableness is measured by the facts known at the time—not by hindsight.  

Van Ells reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and witness statements. He spent more than 100 hours on the case. Based on that review, he concluded—correctly—that Chang’s physical location was irrelevant to the State’s theory: that Chang *arranged and coordinated* acts of prostitution. As the circuit court found, trafficking charges focus on *coordination*, not physical presence. Evidence of Chang’s whereabouts during specific hours would not undermine the State’s proof that he orchestrated prostitution across multiple locations. This is not a failure to investigate; it is a reasonable strategic decision.  

*State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, holds that strategic decisions are "unchallengeable only if made after a reasonable investigation." Van Ells’ investigation *was* reasonable. He did not ignore Chang’s requests—he evaluated them against the State’s theory and determined they were irrelevant. Counsel is not required to "investigate every conceivable defense" or obtain every document a defendant requests. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 691. Van Ells’ conclusion—that the alibi evidence was futile—was sound. The defense’s claim that Van Ells "assumed" relevance without review is flatly contradicted by the record: Van Ells testified he based his decision on the entire file, including the State’s evidence of coordination.  

The defense’s prejudice argument is equally flawed. It claims Van Ells’ testimony about "no chance of acquittal" is "circular." But prejudice requires only a "reasonable probability" the result would differ—*Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694—and Chang has shown none. He never disputed that the plea agreement dismissed two counts and reduced his exposure. More critically, he has not shown that obtaining the disputed records would have changed his decision to plead. Van Ells’ testimony about the futility of an alibi defense was based on the law of the charges—a law Chang’s own counsel confirmed. The defense’s speculation about "what if" evidence is not evidence of prejudice.  

#### III. FALK’S STRATEGIC DECISION WAS SOUND—THE DEFENSE MISREADS *CARTER*  
The defense claims Falk was ineffective for not calling Van Ells at the plea withdrawal hearing. That is wrong. Falk made a tactical choice based on Van Ells’ input: Van Ells confirmed that his testimony would contradict Chang’s claims about the alibi defense. As *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, holds, counsel is not required to call a witness whose testimony "would not have advanced the defendant’s case." Here, Van Ells’ testimony would have *hurt* Chang’s motion—it would have confirmed the alibi was irrelevant and that Van Ells’ strategy was reasonable.  

The defense insists Falk "deprived the court of critical evidence." But the "critical evidence" they seek is Van Ells’ self-serving testimony about his own performance—a testimony that would have reinforced the State’s position. Falk’s decision not to call him was not deficient; it was prudent. *Strickland* does not require counsel to present evidence that undermines their client’s position. Falk’s strategy was to let Chang testify alone, but that does not make it unreasonable. The circuit court properly found that Falk’s choice "did not fall below an objective standard of reasonableness."  

#### IV. CUMULATIVE ERROR DOES NOT APPLY—THERE ARE NO INDIVIDUAL ERRORS TO CUMULATE  
Finally, the defense invokes cumulative error under *State v. Thiel*, 2003 WI 111, ¶ 23, 264 Wis. 2d 571. But *Thiel* only applies when multiple "individually harmless" errors combine to deny a fair trial. Here, there are *no* errors. The plea colloquy satisfied *Harrington*. Van Ells’ performance was reasonable. Falk’s strategy was sound. Without a single error to accumulate, *Thiel* is inapplicable.  

#### CONCLUSION  
This Court must affirm the circuit court’s decision. Chang’s pleas were knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently entered. Van Ells’ investigation was reasonable. Falk’s tactical choice was sound. The defense’s rebuttal relies on misconceptions of the record, misapplications of *Strickland*, and a demand for perfection that the Constitution does not require. As *Strickland* teaches, counsel’s performance is judged by "prevailing professional norms," not by the defense’s hindsight. The circuit court applied those norms correctly. It found Chang’s testimony inconsistent with the plea colloquy—a credibility determination we must uphold. It found no deficient performance—and we must defer to that finding.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm a judgment that denied Pao Chang his constitutional right to effective assistance of counsel and his right to withdraw his pleas when the plea colloquy itself revealed manifest injustice. We respectfully request that this Court reverse the circuit court's decision, grant Chang's motion to withdraw his pleas, and remand for further proceedings. The prosecution's argument, while thorough, misconstrues the record and applies an improperly deferential standard to the constitutional claims at issue.

---

## I. THE CIRCUIT COURT FAILED TO CONDUCT THE REQUISITE INQUIRY UNDER *STATE v. HARRINGTON*

The prosecution contends that the circuit court satisfied its duty under *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2d 285, when Chang protested his innocence. This Court should reject that contention.

*Harrington* stands for the proposition that when a defendant maintains innocence during a plea colloquy, the circuit court must "ensure that the defendant's decision to plead no contest is made voluntarily and with an understanding of the nature of the charge." *Id.*, ¶ 28. This is not a perfunctory requirement—it demands meaningful inquiry into the defendant's protestations.

The record demonstrates that Chang did far more than merely express hesitation. He told the circuit court that his "hands were tied" and that he was pleading no contest despite maintaining his innocence. This is precisely the scenario *Harrington* addresses. When a defendant protests innocence, the court must "conduct a thorough inquiry to determine whether the defendant truly understands the nature of the charges and the consequences of the plea." *Id.*, ¶ 28.

The prosecution argues that the court's questions to Van Ells about the "additional information" Chang sought satisfied this requirement. However, this conflates two distinct concerns. The court inquired into whether Van Ells needed additional information to prepare for trial—but this did not address Chang's explicit statement that his "hands were tied" and his assertion of innocence. The court never asked Chang directly why he felt his hands were tied, what specific pressures he faced, or whether he understood that he could persist in his plea of not guilty and demand a trial.

As this Court noted in *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24, the court's inquiry must elicit facts demonstrating that the plea was knowing and voluntary. Here, the court's failure to probe Chang's protestations of innocence when he explicitly stated his "hands were tied" constitutes an erroneous exercise of discretion. The prosecution's characterization of the colloquy as "detailed" cannot overcome the fact that the court failed to address the core *Harrington* concern: ensuring that a defendant who maintains innocence understands the import of his waiver.

---

## II. VAN ELLS' PERFORMANCE WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFICIENT

The prosecution argues that Van Ells made a reasonable strategic decision not to investigate Chang's alibi. This Court should find otherwise.

### A. The Investigation Was Unreasonable

Under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 688 (1984), counsel's performance is deficient when it falls "below an objective standard of reasonableness." The prosecution relies on *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40, for the proposition that strategic decisions are unchallengeable only if made after reasonable investigation.

Van Ells testified that he did not obtain Chang's bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records because he believed Chang's "physical location at the time [of the alleged acts of prostitution was] not particularly useful or relevant." However, Van Ells' belief was based on an assumption about the State's theory of prosecution—not on review of the very documents Chang requested.

Critically, Van Ells never reviewed the bank records, employment time sheets, or medical records before concluding they were irrelevant. He made a determination about the relevance of evidence without examining that evidence. This is the precise opposite of reasonable investigation. As the Court of Appeals held in *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, a strategic decision "unintelligent" or "disadvantageous" to the defendant may be found deficient when counsel failed to make "reasonable investigations that a reasonable attorney would consider essential." *Id.*, ¶ 28.

The prosecution argues that Van Ells' review of 1,200 pages of police reports constituted reasonable investigation. But the police reports concerned the State's case against Chang—not the evidence Chang believed would exonerate him. An attorney cannot reasonably conclude that potential alibi evidence is irrelevant without first examining that evidence. Van Ells' failure to obtain Chang's bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records before making his strategic determination fell below the objective standard of reasonableness.

### B. The Prejudice Prong Is Satisfied

The prosecution argues that Chang cannot show prejudice because "he has not shown that obtaining the omitted records would have led to an acquittal or a better plea offer." This misstates the prejudice standard.

Under *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694, prejudice requires showing "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." This does not require proof of acquittal—it requires only a reasonable probability of a different outcome.

Here, the plea agreement itself demonstrates prejudice. Chang testified that he believed the plea offer capped the State's sentence recommendation at a twenty-five-year total bifurcated term, not twenty-five years' initial incarceration plus fifteen years' extended supervision. If Van Ells had properly investigated the alibi and discovered exculpatory evidence, there is a reasonable probability Chang would have rejected the plea and proceeded to trial—or negotiated more favorable terms. The prosecution's argument that Chang cannot show prejudice because he cannot prove acquittal sets the bar impossibly high and contradicts *Strickland*'s language.

Moreover, Van Ells testified that he advised Chang to accept the plea because his chances of acquittal were "basically zero." But this testimony is circular: Van Ells concluded there was no chance of acquittal precisely because he failed to investigate the alibi that might have provided one. The defense cannot rely on the absence of evidence it failed to gather to prove the absence of prejudice.

---

## III. FALK'S PERFORMANCE WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFICIENT

The prosecution argues that Falk's decision not to call Van Ells at the plea withdrawal hearing was a reasonable tactical choice. This Court should find otherwise.

### A. The Decision Was Not Strategic

Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells after "having a conversation" with him. He stated he "didn't think it was really necessary" to call Van Ells. This is not a reasoned strategic decision—it is a failure to advocate for Chang's motion.

At the plea withdrawal hearing, the central question was whether Van Ells had provided effective assistance. Falk's decision to present Chang's testimony while deliberately withholding Van Ells' testimony deprived the circuit court of the most relevant evidence on that question. As this Court held in *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695, counsel has a duty to present evidence that would advance the defendant's case.

The prosecution argues that Van Ells' testimony "would have hurt Chang's motion" because it would have confirmed the alibi was irrelevant. But this misses the point. The question before the circuit court was not whether Van Ells' strategy was correct in hindsight—it was whether Van Ells' performance was deficient. Van Ells' testimony about his own reasoning was essential to that inquiry. Falk's decision to withhold that testimony deprived the court of the evidence needed to evaluate Chang's claim.

### B. Prejudice Is Established

Because Falk failed to present Van Ells' testimony, the circuit court could not properly assess whether Van Ells' performance was deficient. The court denied Chang's motion in part because it found "that Mr. Chang was not fully apprised of all the circumstances." Yet the court never heard from the attorney who allegedly provided that apprisement. This constitutes prejudice.

Under *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694, prejudice exists when counsel's errors "have had an appreciable effect on the proceedings." Here, Falk's failure to call Van Ells deprived Chang of the opportunity to present his claim with the benefit of testimony from the attorney whose performance was at issue. The prosecution's argument that Van Ells' testimony "would have contradicted Chang's claims" is speculative—Van Ells might have testified in a manner that supported Chang's motion, or his testimony might have revealed deficiencies that the court should have considered. By failing to call Van Ells, Falk denied the court the opportunity to make an informed determination.

---

## IV. CUMULATIVE ERROR REQUIRES REVERSAL

Even if this Court finds that any single error does not require reversal, the cumulative effect of the errors denied Chang a fair proceeding.

Under *State v. Thiel*, 2003 WI 111, ¶ 23, 264 Wis. 2d 571, 665 N.W.2d 305, cumulative error analysis applies when multiple deficiencies combine to deny a defendant a fair trial. Here, the cumulative effect of three errors requires reversal: (1) the circuit court's failure to conduct a thorough *Harrington* inquiry; (2) Van Ells' failure to reasonably investigate the alibi; and (3) Falk's failure to present Van Ells' testimony at the plea withdrawal hearing.

Each of these errors deprived Chang of the opportunity to demonstrate that his pleas were not knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently entered. Together, they constitute cumulative error requiring reversal.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to defer to the circuit court's factual findings and credibility determinations. But the question before this Court is not whether the circuit court could have reached its conclusion—it is whether the record supports the constitutional conclusions required to deny Chang's motion.

The record demonstrates that: (1) Chang explicitly protested his innocence and stated his "hands were tied," yet the court failed to conduct the thorough inquiry *Harrington* requires; (2) Van Ells failed to investigate potential alibi evidence before concluding it was irrelevant; and (3) Falk failed to present Van Ells' testimony at the plea withdrawal hearing, depriving the court of evidence essential to Chang's claim.

These errors, individually and cumulatively, denied Chang his constitutional right to effective assistance of counsel and his right to withdraw his pleas when manifest injustice required it. The prosecution's argument for affirmance rests on an improperly deferential reading of the record and a misapplication of *Strickland* and its progeny.

**MAY IT PLEASE THIS COURT: REVERSE the circuit court's judgment and order, grant Chang's motion to withdraw his pleas, and remand for further proceedings.**

**STATE OF WISCONSIN**
**Pao Chang,**
**Appellant-Petitioner,**
**v.**
**STATE OF WISCONSIN,**
**Respondent-Respondent.**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO.]
**DECISION**

**PER CURIAM.**

Pao Chang appeals a judgment of conviction and an order denying postconviction relief. He argues that he is entitled to withdraw his no-contest pleas due to the ineffective assistance of his trial attorneys, Brian Van Ells and Marcus Falk, and that the circuit court erroneously denied his motion to withdraw his pleas. Specifically, Chang contends that the circuit court failed to conduct a sufficient inquiry under *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2d 285, when he protested his innocence, and that both attorneys provided constitutionally deficient performance. Based on the record and the arguments presented, we affirm the circuit court’s decision.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to grant or deny a motion to withdraw a plea lies within the circuit court’s discretion. *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 29, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. We review the circuit court’s decision under the erroneous exercise of discretion standard. *Id.*, ¶ 30. We will affirm the court’s decision as long as the court relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion. *Id.* We uphold the court’s factual findings unless they are clearly erroneous. *Id.*, ¶ 33.

Claims of ineffective assistance of counsel present mixed questions of fact and law. *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. While we uphold the circuit court’s findings of fact unless they are clearly erroneous, we independently determine whether those facts satisfy the constitutional standard for ineffective assistance of counsel. *Id.*

**II. PLEA WITHDRAWAL**

Chang argues that the circuit court erroneously denied his motion to withdraw his pleas because his pleas were not entered knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently. He asserts that the court failed to conduct a "thorough inquiry" as required by *State v. Harrington*, 2010 WI 47, ¶ 28, 326 Wis. 2d 85, 785 N.W.2d 285, after he stated his "hands were tied" and maintained his innocence.

We are not persuaded. The record reflects that the circuit court conducted a detailed plea colloquy. When Chang indicated that he felt he did not have all the information he needed to defend himself, the court paused the proceedings to inquire. The court asked Attorney Van Ells if he needed the information Chang believed was necessary. Van Ells, as an officer of the court, represented that he had reviewed the entire file, consisting of over 1,200 pages, and that he had sufficient evidence to advise Chang. Van Ells further stated that the information Chang sought was either cumulative or irrelevant.

Following this exchange, the court directly addressed Chang, explaining his right to proceed to trial and warning him that the plea offer would likely be withdrawn if he chose to do so. The court explicitly asked, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." This exchange demonstrates that Chang understood the choice before him and voluntarily elected to accept the plea agreement.

While *Harrington* requires a court to ensure a plea is knowing and voluntary when a defendant protests innocence, the court is not required to conduct an inquiry into every potential grievance a defendant might have against counsel. See *State v. Rieke*, 109 Wis. 2d 525, 537 (Ct. App. 1982). Here, the court addressed Chang's concern by confirming with counsel that adequate preparation had been conducted. The court then verified with Chang that he understood the consequences of his decision. The circuit court’s finding that Chang’s pleas were freely, voluntarily, and intelligently entered is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. See *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 33, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24.

**III. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – VAN ELLS**

Chang contends that Attorney Van Ells provided ineffective assistance by failing to investigate an alibi defense. To prevail on an ineffective assistance claim, Chang must show both that Van Ells’ performance was deficient and that he was prejudiced. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

**A. Performance**

Chang argues that Van Ells was deficient for failing to obtain his bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records. He relies on *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40, for the proposition that strategic decisions are unchallengeable only if made after a reasonable investigation. Chang asserts that Van Ells could not have determined the records were irrelevant without reviewing them.

The circuit court found that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient. This finding is not clearly erroneous. Van Ells testified that he spent over 100 hours on the case and reviewed more than 1,200 pages of police reports and witness statements. Based on this review, Van Ells concluded that the State’s theory of the case—that Chang arranged and coordinated acts of prostitution—did not depend on Chang’s physical location at specific times. Therefore, evidence regarding Chang's location (the alibi) would be irrelevant.

Counsel is not required to investigate every conceivable defense or obtain every piece of evidence requested by a defendant. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 691. Rather, counsel’s duty is to conduct a reasonable investigation. Van Ells’ review of the extensive discovery provided a reasonable basis for his strategic decision to forego an alibi defense. His conclusion that the elements of trafficking of a child and human trafficking focused on coordination rather than presence is a sound legal and strategic judgment. The circuit court properly deferred to this strategic choice. See *State v. Wenzel*, 2001 WI App 142, ¶ 28, 246 Wis. 2d 190, 630 N.W.2d 40.

**B. Prejudice**

Even assuming Van Ells’ performance was deficient, Chang cannot establish prejudice. Prejudice requires a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694. Van Ells testified that he advised Chang to accept the plea because his chances of acquittal were "basically zero." The plea agreement significantly reduced Chang’s potential penalty exposure by dismissing two counts of trafficking of a child. Chang has not shown that obtaining the omitted records would have led to an acquittal or a better plea offer. Therefore, Chang has failed to meet the prejudice prong of *Strickland*.

**IV. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – FALK**

Chang argues that Attorney Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing. He claims this deprived the circuit court of necessary evidence to assess Van Ells’ performance.

The circuit court found that because Van Ells’ performance was not deficient, Chang could not show prejudice from Falk’s failure to call Van Ells. We agree. Falk testified that he spoke with Van Ells and decided it was not necessary to call him. This was a strategic decision regarding the presentation of evidence. See *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694.

Chang argues that *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695, requires counsel to call a witness if the witness would "advance the defendant's case." However, as the circuit court noted, Van Ells’ testimony would have contradicted Chang’s claims regarding the necessity of the alibi investigation. Van Ells would have testified that he reviewed the file, that Chang’s location was irrelevant, and that he believed Chang had no chance of acquittal. This testimony would not have supported Chang’s motion to withdraw his plea; rather, it would have reinforced the State’s position. Therefore, Falk’s decision not to call Van Ells did not fall below an objective standard of reasonableness. See *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 690.

Furthermore, because the record demonstrates that Van Ells provided effective assistance, Falk’s failure to call him did not affect the outcome of the postconviction motion. See *State v. Carter*, 2010 WI 38, ¶ 40, 324 Wis. 2d 640, 782 N.W.2d 695. The circuit court properly denied the postconviction motion on this basis.

**V. CUMULATIVE ERROR**

Chang finally argues that the cumulative effect of the errors denied him a fair trial. Under *State v. Thiel*, 2003 WI 111, ¶ 23, 264 Wis. 2d 571, 665 N.W.2d 305, cumulative error analysis applies when multiple deficiencies, though individually harmless, combine to deny a defendant a fair trial. Because we have found that the circuit court did not err in denying the plea withdrawal motion and that neither Van Ells nor Falk provided ineffective assistance, there are no errors to cumulate. *Id.*, ¶ 24.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the circuit court did not erroneously exercise its discretion in denying Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas or his postconviction motion. The judgment and order are affirmed.
