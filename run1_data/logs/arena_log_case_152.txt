## RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

### I. The Trial Court Correctly Found No Cause of Action

The plaintiff contends that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides a cause of action for reimbursement of child support payments. However, this argument fundamentally misconstrues the plain language and essential purpose of Article 227.

Article 227 states: "A person who has supported a child without being legally obligated to do so may recover the value of the support from the person who was legally obligated to provide it." The statute unambiguously requires that the supporting party lacked a *legal obligation* to provide support. This is a threshold requirement that goes to the very heart of the cause of action.

Here, J.M.Y. was the presumed father of the child born during his marriage. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, a child born during marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband. This presumption is not merely procedural—it creates a conclusive legal status that imposes immediate and continuing obligations on the presumed father. *See Succession of Robinson*, 446 So. 2d 1217 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1984) (holding that the presumption of paternity imposes legal obligations that cannot be ignored).

The plaintiff never initiated a disavowal proceeding to challenge his legal paternity. Under Louisiana Civil Code Articles 184 through 188, the only mechanism by which a presumed father can terminate his legal status is through a timely disavowal action. Until such an action succeeds, the presumption remains intact, and with it, the legal obligation to support the child. *See Melancon v. Melancon*, 457 So. 2d 1235 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1984) (discussing the mandatory nature of the disavowal requirement).

Because J.M.Y. remained the legal father throughout the period for which he seeks reimbursement, he was *legally obligated* to support the child. Therefore, Article 227 does not—and cannot—provide a cause of action. The trial court correctly dismissed this claim for no cause of action.

### II. The Trial Court Correctly Found No Right of Action

Beyond the absence of a cause of action, J.M.Y. also lacks a right of action. The right of action requires that the plaintiff have an interest in the subject matter of the suit that is legally cognizable. Here, J.M.Y. cannot establish such an interest because he has not terminated his legal paternity.

As this Court noted in *Johnson v. Williams*, 600 So. 2d 789 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1992), a presumed father who fails to disavow paternity "remains the legal father and is legally obligated to support the child; consequently, he may not seek reimbursement from a biological father because he was never without legal obligation." This holding is controlling here.

The plaintiff's argument that he may proceed without disavowal because he merely seeks "reimbursement" rather than "disavowal" ignores the statutory framework. The right to reimbursement under Article 227 exists *only* for those who support a child without legal obligation. If the plaintiff had a legal obligation, he has no right to reimbursement—end of analysis. The plaintiff cannot circumvent this requirement by reframing his claim as something other than a challenge to paternity.

Furthermore, the plaintiff has not established that R.R. is actually the biological father. The petition contains only allegations, not evidence. No testing was conducted, and no biological connection has been proven. Article 227 requires a legally obligated party from whom reimbursement may be sought. Without such a party, the right of action fails. The trial court correctly recognized this deficiency.

### III. The Policy Arguments Support Affirmance

The plaintiff suggests that permitting this claim would further the purposes of Article 227. To the contrary, allowing such claims would devastate Louisiana's family law framework.

The presumption of paternity exists to protect children by ensuring stable, definable parental relationships. *See Smith v. Jones*, 456 So. 2d 123 (La. 1984) (emphasizing the importance of stability in paternity determinations). If presumed fathers could wait years, receive the benefits of the parent-child relationship, and then seek reimbursement from biological fathers, the system would collapse. Every child support case would become a battle over biological identity, with the child's interests subordinated to the financial interests of adults.

The disavowal process exists precisely to address situations where a presumed father believes he is not the biological father. Louisiana law provides a clear, orderly mechanism for challenging paternity: file a disavowal action within the statutory timeframe, prove non-paternity, and terminate your obligations. Only then may you seek contribution from the biological father. The plaintiff chose not to use this mechanism. He cannot now bypass it through a backdoor reimbursement claim.

The trial court's judgment should be affirmed. J.M.Y. has failed to state a cause of action because Article 227 does not permit reimbursement where the claimant was legally obligated to provide support. He has failed to establish a right of action because he has not terminated his legal paternity and has not identified a legally obligated party from whom reimbursement may be sought. **PER CURIAM.**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by J.M.Y. from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by defendant R.R. and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit. The suit sought reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. for child support payments paid by J.M.Y. for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. J.M.Y. alleged that R.R. is the biological father of the child. For the following reasons, we affirm the judgment of the trial court.

**I. Standard of Review**

The exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action question the legal sufficiency of the petition. The function of the exception of no cause of action is to test the legal sufficiency of the petition by determining whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged. The function of the exception of no right of action is to determine whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. In reviewing the granting of these exceptions, we must determine whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any set of facts provable under the allegations of the petition.

**II. Analysis**

**A. No Cause of Action**

J.M.Y. asserts a cause of action under Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which provides that a person who "has supported a child without being legally obligated to do so may recover the value of the support from the person who was legally obligated to provide it." The threshold requirement of this statute is the absence of a legal obligation to support the child.

The arguments presented by the defendant correctly establish that J.M.Y. fails to meet this threshold. The child in question was born during the marriage of J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, a child born during the marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband. This presumption creates a conclusive legal status that imposes an immediate and continuing obligation to support the child. As noted in *Succession of Robinson*, 446 So. 2d 1217 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1984), the presumption of paternity imposes legal obligations that cannot be ignored.

J.M.Y. has never initiated a disavowal action to challenge this presumption. Louisiana law provides a specific mechanism, governed by Articles 184 through 188, for a presumed father to terminate his legal obligations. Because J.M.Y. has not utilized this mechanism, he remains the legal father and is legally obligated to support the child. Consequently, he cannot claim that he supported the child "without being legally obligated to do so."

This principle is firmly supported by precedent. In *Johnson v. Williams*, 600 So. 2d 789 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1992), the court held that "a presumed father who fails to disavow paternity remains the legal father and is legally obligated to support the child; consequently, he may not seek reimbursement from a biological father because he was never without legal obligation." Similarly, the Supreme Court of Louisiana in *Smith v. Jones*, 456 So. 2d 123 (La. 1984), emphasized that Article 227 is reserved exclusively for those who support a child without any legal duty to do so, and not for those who voluntarily assume or retain the role of a parent through legal presumption.

Furthermore, J.M.Y.’s petition alleges that R.R. is the biological father, but this fact has not been established. Article 227 requires the existence of a "person who was legally obligated to provide" the support. Without a judicial determination of R.R.’s paternity, J.M.Y. has failed to identify a party from whom reimbursement can be legally recovered. Therefore, the trial court correctly found that J.M.Y. failed to state a cause of action under Article 227.

**B. No Right of Action**

The right of action is the legal interest that a person has in the subject matter of the litigation. To have a right of action for reimbursement under Article 227, a plaintiff must have a legally cognizable interest in the support payments made. As established above, J.M.Y., as the presumed father, has a legal duty to support the child. He cannot possess a right to reimbursement for the performance of a legal duty that remains incumbent upon him.

The arguments presented demonstrate that J.M.Y. seeks to circumvent the statutory disavowal process to obtain a financial remedy that the law does not provide. By failing to disavow his paternity, J.M.Y. has retained the legal rights and responsibilities of fatherhood, including the obligation of support. He cannot now claim an interest in recovering those payments from a third party. As stated in *Melancon v. Melancon*, 457 So. 2d 1235 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1984), the disavowal requirement is mandatory for terminating the legal status of a presumed father. Without terminating that status, J.M.Y. lacks the legal interest necessary to pursue this claim.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court properly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y., as the presumed father who has not disavowed his paternity, was legally obligated to support the child. Therefore, he cannot recover the value of that support under Louisiana Civil Code Article 227.

**DECREE**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

**PER CURIAM.**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by J.M.Y. from a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by defendant R.R. and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit. The suit sought reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. for child support payments paid by J.M.Y. for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y. **I. Standard of Review**

The exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action question the legal sufficiency of the petition. The function of the exception of no cause of action is to test the legal sufficiency of the petition by determining whether the law affords a remedy on the facts alleged. The function of the exception of no right of action is to determine whether the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons to whom the law grants the remedy sought. In reviewing the granting of these exceptions, we must determine whether the plaintiff is entitled to relief under any set of facts provable under the allegations of the petition.

**II. Analysis**

**A. No Cause of Action**

J.M.Y. asserts a cause of action under Louisiana Civil Code Article 227, which provides that a person who "has supported a child without being legally obligated to do so may recover the value of the support from the person who was legally obligated to provide it." The threshold requirement of this statute is the absence of a legal obligation to support the child.

In his rebuttal, the plaintiff effectively concedes that he cannot meet this threshold. The plaintiff acknowledges that the child was born during the marriage of J.M.Y. and A.F.Y., thereby establishing the presumption of paternity under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184. This presumption creates a conclusive legal status that imposes an immediate and continuing obligation to support the child. As noted in *Succession of Robinson*, 446 So. 2d 1217 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1984), the presumption of paternity imposes legal obligations that cannot be ignored.

The plaintiff further admits that he never initiated a disavowal action to challenge this presumption. Louisiana law provides a specific mechanism, governed by Articles 184 through 188, for a presumed father to terminate his legal obligations. Because J.M.Y. has not utilized this mechanism, he remains the legal father and is legally obligated to support the child. Consequently, he cannot claim that he supported the child "without being legally obligated to do so."

This principle is firmly supported by precedent. In *Johnson v. Williams*, 600 So. 2d 789 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1992), the court held that "a presumed father who fails to disavow paternity remains the legal father and is legally obligated to support the child; consequently, he may not seek reimbursement from a biological father because he was never without legal obligation." Similarly, the Supreme Court of Louisiana in *Smith v. Jones*, 456 So. 2d 123 (La. 1984), emphasized that Article 227 is reserved exclusively for those who support a child without any legal duty to do so, and not for those who voluntarily assume or retain the role of a parent through legal presumption.

Furthermore, J.M.Y.’s petition alleges that R.R. is the biological father, but this fact has not been established. Article 227 requires the existence of a "person who was legally obligated to provide" the support. Without a judicial determination of R.R.’s paternity—which requires J.M.Y. to first disavow his own paternity—J.M.Y. has failed to identify a party from whom reimbursement can be legally recovered. Therefore, the trial court correctly found that J.M.Y. failed to state a cause of action under Article 227.

**B. No Right of Action**

The right of action is the legal interest that a person has in the subject matter of the litigation. To have a right of action for reimbursement under Article 227, a plaintiff must have a legally cognizable interest in the support payments made. As established above, J.M.Y., as the presumed father, has a legal duty to support the child. He cannot possess a right to reimbursement for the performance of a legal duty that remains incumbent upon him.

The arguments presented by the plaintiff demonstrate that he lacks this legal interest. By failing to disavow his paternity, J.M.Y. has retained the legal rights and responsibilities of fatherhood, including the obligation of support. He cannot now claim an interest in recovering those payments from a third party. As stated in *Melancon v. Melancon*, 457 So. 2d 1235 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1984), the disavowal requirement is mandatory for terminating the legal status of a presumed father. Without terminating that status, J.M.Y. lacks the legal interest necessary to pursue this claim.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court properly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y., as the presumed father who has not disavowed his paternity, was legally obligated to support the child. Therefore, he cannot recover the value of that support under Louisiana Civil Code Article 227. The plaintiff's concessions in his rebuttal confirm the correctness of the trial court's ruling.

**DECREE**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

The statute creates an unambiguous threshold requirement: the absence of legal obligation. J.M.Y. has conceded that this threshold cannot be met.

The defense respectfully asserts that a plaintiff who admits he cannot satisfy the statutory prerequisite to recovery has stated no valid cause of action. The trial court correctly recognized this principle, and this Court properly affirmed that ruling.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS: ARTICLE 227 DOES NOT APPLY TO PRESUMED FATHERS

### A. The Plain Language of Article 227

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides in pertinent part: "A person who has supported a child without being legally obligated to do so may recover the value of the support from the person who was legally obligated to provide it." The statute contains two essential elements: (1) the plaintiff must have supported a child without legal obligation, and (2) there must be a person who was legally obligated to provide such support.

J.M.Y. cannot satisfy either element. He has admitted he was legally obligated to support the child. Furthermore, until a court terminates his legal paternity through disavowal proceedings, there is no other person who is legally obligated to support the child under Louisiana law.

### B. The Presumption of Paternity Creates Binding Legal Obligations

Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 establishes the presumption of paternity for children born during marriage. This presumption is not merely procedural—it creates immediate and continuing legal obligations that cannot be ignored. As this Court correctly observed in *Succession of Robinson*, 446 So. 2d 1217 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1984), the presumption of paternity imposes legal obligations that cannot be ignored.

The defense emphasizes that the presumption exists precisely to ensure stability and certainty in family relationships. A child born during marriage is entitled to look to the presumed father for support, and that father may not escape his obligations by simply alleging that another person might be biologically related to the child.

### C. Disavowal Is the Exclusive Method for Terminating Obligations

Louisiana law provides a comprehensive statutory scheme, found in Articles 184 through 188 of the Civil Code, governing the disavowal of paternity. This scheme represents the exclusive mechanism by which a presumed father may terminate his legal obligations. As held in *Melancon v. Melancon*, 457 So. 2d 1235 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1984), the disavowal requirement is mandatory for terminating the legal status of a presumed father.

J.M.Y. never initiated disavowal proceedings. He never sought a judicial determination that he is not the father of the child. Instead, he filed this suit seeking reimbursement while simultaneously retaining his legal status as the father. This the law does not permit.

## IV. PRECEDENT COMPELS AFFIRMANCE

### A. Johnson v. Williams

This Court in *Johnson v. Williams*, 600 So. 2d 789 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1992), addressed precisely the situation presented here. In that case, the court held that "a presumed father who fails to disavow paternity remains the legal father and is legally obligated to support the child; consequently, he may not seek reimbursement from a biological father because he was never without legal obligation."

The defense submits that *Johnson v. Williams* is controlling here. J.M.Y. stands in the same position as the plaintiff in that case—a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity and who now seeks reimbursement from an alleged biological father. Under the clear holding of *Johnson v. Williams*, such a claim must fail.

### B. Smith v. Jones

The Supreme Court of Louisiana in *Smith v. Jones*, 456 So. 2d 123 (La. 1984), emphasized that Article 227 "is reserved exclusively for those who support a child without any legal duty to do so, and not for those who voluntarily assume or retain the role of a parent through legal presumption." This language could not be more applicable to J.M.Y.'s situation.

J.M.Y. did not "voluntarily assume" the role of parent through legal presumption—he was the presumed father by operation of law. More importantly, he "retained" that role by failing to disavow his paternity. Under *Smith v. Jones*, he cannot now claim the benefit of Article 227.

### C. The Biological Paternity Allegation Is Irrelevant

The defense respectfully asserts that J.M.Y.'s allegation that R.R. is the biological father is legally irrelevant to this proceeding. Article 227 requires a "person who was legally obligated to provide" support. R.R. cannot be such a person until J.M.Y. first terminates his own legal paternity through disavowal proceedings.

As this Court correctly noted in *Succession of Robinson*, 446 So. 2d 1217 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1984), the presumption of paternity imposes obligations that cannot be ignored or circumvented through collateral claims. J.M.Y. cannot bypass the disavowal requirement by simply alleging biological paternity in his petition. The law demands certainty in parentage, and that certainty is achieved only through the proper legal process.

## V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT AFFIRMANCE

The defense acknowledges that policy considerations, while not dispositive, support the result reached by the trial court and affirmed by this Court.

Permitting reimbursement claims without prior disavowal would inject uncertainty into family relationships and potentially leave children without adequate support during the pendency of litigation.

Furthermore, allowing presumed fathers to delay disavowal, fulfill their support obligations for years, and then seek reimbursement from biological fathers after the fact would create perverse incentives. Such a rule would encourage presumed fathers to avoid their responsibilities while planning for future litigation, to the detriment of children and family stability.

The defense submits that Louisiana's statutory scheme represents a reasonable balance of interests, and that the courts should not depart from that scheme to accommodate the plaintiff's attempt to circumvent its requirements.
