### INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

May it please this Honorable Court. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Contrary to the appellee's characterization, the trial court properly exercised its discretion in enforcing the valid noncompetition covenant, and the appellee's attempts to manufacture defenses based on alleged material breaches fail as a matter of law.

The appellee's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and a misapplication of Florida contract law. The employment agreement at issue was a valid, enforceable contract containing a reasonable two-year noncompetition covenant. The appellee's claims of "material breach" are unsupported by the record and, even if credited, would not excuse the Appellant's performance under the circumstances presented here.

---

### I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The Appellant was employed by Health Coalition, Inc. as a salesperson for blood plasma products beginning in March 1993. At the time of hiring, the Appellant executed an employment agreement containing a covenant not to compete, which provided that for a period of two years following termination, the Appellant would not solicit "any active, paid up customers of the corporation" and would maintain the confidentiality of corporate information. The agreement also contained a liquidated damages clause.

The Appellant's employment terminated in April 1994. The Appellant does not dispute that he left the employer's service at that time. The employer maintains that the Appellant voluntarily resigned. The Appellant contends that he was constructively discharged, though the record demonstrates that he did not pursue this claim through proper administrative channels and never formally contested the circumstances of his departure below.

In February 1996—nearly two years after his employment ended—the employer filed this action alleging that the Appellant had gone to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products in violation of the noncompetition covenant. The employer sought and obtained a temporary injunction enforcing the agreement.

At the injunction hearing, the Appellant raised two defenses: first, that the employer had refused to pay certain commissions after his departure, and second, that he had been instructed to alter invoices to charge higher prices, which he refused to do. The employer denied that any such instruction was given.

---

### II. ARGUMENT

#### A. THE NONCOMPETITION COVENANT IS ENFORCEABLE UNDER FLORIDA LAW

Florida courts recognize that noncompetition covenants in employment agreements are enforceable when they are reasonable in duration, geographic scope, and scope of activity. *See Davis v. Huey-Miller Co.*, 437 So. 2d 216, 218 (Fla. 1st DCA 1983). A covenant prohibiting the solicitation of "active, paid up customers" for a period of two years is facially reasonable and enforceable when, as here, the employee was hired as a salesperson with access to customer relationships and confidential business information. *See Wackenhut Corp. v.* *Dennett*, 409 So. 2d 76, 78 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982) (holding that covenants protecting customer relationships are enforceable).

The Appellant did not challenge the reasonableness of the covenant below, and the trial court properly found it enforceable. The appellee's attempt to avoid enforcement by invoking alleged material breaches fails for the reasons set forth below.

#### B. THE APPELLEE'S ALLEGED "MATERIAL BREACHES" ARE NOT SUPPORTED BY THE RECORD AND DO NOT EXCUSE THE APPELLANT'S PERFORMANCE

The appellee's argument that it materially breached the employment agreement, thereby releasing the Appellant from the noncompetition covenant, fails for three independent reasons.

**First**, the appellee's characterization of the facts is disputed and was not established below. The employer denied that any instruction was given to alter invoices, and the Appellant presented no corroborating evidence of such an instruction. The trial court, sitting as finder of fact at the injunction hearing, was free to credit the employer's denials. *See Joseph v. Florida* *Parole Commission*, 710 So. 2d 137, 139 (Fla. 1st DCA 1998) (appellate court reviews factual findings for substantial competent evidence). The appellee's brief repeatedly states that the Appellant "presented credible evidence," but the trial court was not required to credit this evidence, and the record contains no findings that such instructions were actually given.

**Second**, even assuming arguendo that the employer failed to pay some commissions, this does not constitute a material breach justifying the Appellant's departure from his employment or his subsequent violation of the noncompetition covenant. The appellee's reliance on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. B & R Sales & Marketing*, 698 So. 2d 1257 (Fla. 1st DCA 1997), is misplaced. In *R.J. Reynolds*, the employee was actively employed and performing when the employer refused to pay commissions. The employee resigned in response and then violated the covenant. The court held that the employer's failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that excused the employee's performance *while still employed*. Here, by contrast, the Appellant left the employer's service in April 1994. He did not resign in response to nonpayment of commissions; rather, he waited nearly two years to file any claim regarding unpaid commissions, and the employer's action in this case was filed in February 1996—nearly two years after the alleged nonpayment. The Appellant cannot bootstrap a claim of constructive discharge from events that occurred after his departure, nor can he claim that post-termination conduct by the employer excuses his post-termination violation of a covenant that, by its terms, applies only after employment ends.

**Third**, the Appellant's alleged refusal to alter invoices, even if credited, does not constitute a material breach by the employer that would excuse the Appellant's subsequent performance. The Appellant was instructed (according to his version) to engage in conduct that would constitute fraud. His refusal to follow this instruction was not only justified but commendable. However, this does not mean that the employer "breached" the contract—it means the employer may have attempted to induce the employee to breach. The employee's proper remedy was to report the misconduct through proper channels or to resign if the instruction constituted a constructive discharge. *See* *Parker v. Cintas Corp.*, 849 So. 2d 369, 372 (Fla. 5th DCA 2003) (holding that an employer's request that employee participate in illegal activity may constitute a constructive discharge). But the Appellant did not resign on this basis at the time; he simply left, and he waited nearly two years to raise this defense in the context of an enforcement action.

More importantly, the appellee's reliance on *Cintas Corp. v. Parker* is similarly misplaced. In *Cintas*, the court addressed whether an employer's request that an employee participate in illegal activity could constitute a constructive discharge justifying the employee's resignation. The court held that it could. However, the court did not hold that an employee's mere refusal to follow an instruction—without more—automatically voids a restrictive covenant. The Appellant was not constructively discharged; he left, and he did not pursue a claim of constructive discharge at the time. His attempt to raise this defense years later, in the context of an enforcement action, is untimely and without merit.

#### C. THE APPELLEE HAS SATISFIED THE REQUIREMENTS FOR A TEMPORARY INJUNCTION

The trial court properly found that the appellee's motion demonstrated entitlement to a temporary injunction. Under Florida law, a party seeking a temporary injunction must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) the likelihood of irreparable harm absent injunctive relief; (3) the balance of hardships tips in its favor; and (4) the public interest supports injunctive relief. *See City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 634 So. 2d 750, 752 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994).

**Likelihood of Success on the Merits**: The employer demonstrated a valid, enforceable noncompetition covenant and alleged that the Appellant was violating it by working for a competitor and soliciting customers. The covenant is facially valid, and the Appellant did not challenge its enforceability below. The appellee's defenses—which rest on disputed allegations of post-termination misconduct—do not undermine the validity of the covenant itself.

**Irreparable Harm**: The employer demonstrated irreparable harm. The covenant protects confidential business information and customer relationships. The loss of customer goodwill and confidential information cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages, as the harm is inherently speculative and difficult to quantify. *See United* *States v. Westinghouse Electric Corp.*, 638 F.2d 570, 578 (3d Cir. 1980) (holding that loss of customer relationships constitutes irreparable harm). The appellee's argument that it "suffers no concrete harm" ignores the nature of the protected interests.

**Balance of Hardships**: The balance of hardships favors the employer. The Appellant chose to enter into the covenant voluntarily, and he is bound by its terms. His interest in employment must be balanced against the employer's legitimate interest in protecting its business relationships and confidential information. The Appellant is free to work in the blood plasma industry; he is merely restrained from soliciting the employer's customers. This is a reasonable accommodation of both interests.

**Public Interest**: The public interest supports enforcement of reasonable noncompetition covenants, which serve the legitimate purpose of protecting business investments and encouraging economic stability. *See Wallach v. Bank of America*, 891 So. 2d 1040, 1044 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004).

#### D. THE APPELLANT'S DEFENSES ARE TIMELY AND PROPERLY CONSIDERED

The Appellant argues that the trial court failed to consider his defenses before granting the injunction. This is incorrect. The trial court held an evidentiary hearing at which the Appellant was permitted to present his defenses. The court considered the evidence and found that the employer was entitled to enforcement. The Appellant was not entitled to a full trial on the merits at the injunction stage; his defenses, to the extent they have merit, can be adjudicated at trial. The purpose of a temporary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending resolution of the underlying dispute. *See Florida* *Highway Patrol v.*/ *Trimmer*, 725 So. 2d 450, 453 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999). The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting relief.

---

### III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellant respectfully requests that this Honorable Court affirm the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. The noncompetition covenant is enforceable, the appellee's alleged material breaches are unsupported by the record and do not excuse the Appellant's performance, and the appellee's has satisfied the requirements for injunctive relief. The trial court properly considered the Appellant's defenses and correctly ruled that the employer is entitled to enforce the covenant.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant,**
v.
**HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO.**

**Preliminary Ruling**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the injunction, arguing that the trial court failed to consider his defenses that Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. ("HCI") materially breached the employment agreement. Specifically, Bradley contends that HCI refused to pay earned commissions and instructed him to engage in fraudulent business practices. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties, we reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court's decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *See City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 634 So. 2d 750, 752 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994). To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Id.*

**II. Analysis**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in granting the injunction without first resolving Bradley's assertion that HCI materially breached the employment agreement, thereby excusing his performance under the noncompetition covenant.

**A. Material Breach and Unpaid Commissions**

Bradley argues that HCI's refusal to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach of the employment agreement. He relies on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. B & R Sales & Marketing*, 698 So. 2d 1257 (Fla. 1st DCA 1997), which holds that an employer's failure to pay earned commissions constitutes a material breach that excuses an employee's performance of a noncompetition agreement. *Id.* at 1260.

HCI argues that *R.J. Reynolds* is distinguishable because Bradley resigned before the commission dispute arose, whereas the employee in *R.J. Reynolds* resigned in direct response to the non-payment. While this temporal distinction is noted, the defense of material breach remains a significant factual dispute that the trial court must address. The trial court cannot simply disregard Bradley's claim that he was entitled to commissions that were wrongfully withheld. If Bradley is correct that HCI breached the contract by failing to pay earned compensation, this would constitute a material breach releasing him from his obligations under the noncompetition covenant. *See R.J. Reynolds*, 698 So. 2d at 1260. The trial court's failure to make specific findings regarding this defense was error.

**B. Fraud and Constructive Discharge**

Bradley further contends that he was forced to resign after refusing HCI's instructions to alter customer invoices to charge higher prices than agreed upon—a practice he alleges constitutes fraud. He argues that such misconduct violates the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing and precludes enforcement of the restrictive covenant. *See Cintas Corp. v. Parker*, 849 So. 2d 369, 372 (Fla. 5th DCA 2003).

HCI disputes that these instructions were given and argues that Bradley's refusal to follow them does not constitute a breach by the employer. While the trial court may have been free to credit HCI's denial, the existence of a genuine factual dispute regarding whether HCI ordered Bradley to commit fraud is critical to the analysis of the noncompetition agreement's enforceability. An employer cannot enforce a restrictive covenant if it has engaged in conduct that destroys the employment relationship's foundation. *See Cintas*, 849 So. 2d at 372. Because the trial court granted the injunction without explicitly resolving this serious allegation of misconduct, it failed to ensure that HCI had established a clear likelihood of success on the merits.

**C. Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

Given the unresolved disputes regarding material breach and fraudulent instructions, HCI has not demonstrated the requisite likelihood of success on the merits to support a temporary injunction. *See City of Jacksonville*, 634 So. 2d at 752. The balance of hardships and public interest considerations cannot favor HCI when Bradley's defenses, if proven, would render the noncompetition agreement unenforceable.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion by entering a temporary injunction without adequately considering Bradley's defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. We therefore reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for the trial court to consider Bradley's defenses to HCI's claims.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**

### REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT  

**MAY IT PLEASE THIS HONORABLE COURT:**  

The defense’s opening statement mischaracterizes the trial court’s error, misapplies Florida contract law, and ignores binding precedent requiring resolution of material breach defenses before enforcing a restrictive covenant. As the plaintiff/prosecution counsel, we reaffirm that the trial court **abused its discretion** by granting and extending the temporary injunction without first resolving Bradley’s credible defenses that Health Coalition, Inc. ("HCI") materially breached the employment agreement. This error requires reversal.  

### I. THE TRIAL COURT’S FAILURE TO RESOLVE MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSES VIOLATES FLORIDA LAW  

Florida law unequivocally requires trial courts to address material breach defenses before enforcing a noncompetition covenant. The defense incorrectly claims Bradley’s allegations are "unsupported by the record" and "do not excuse performance." This is legally untenable.  

#### A. HCI’s Failure to Pay Earned Commissions Constitutes a Material Breach That Excuses Bradley’s Performance  

The defense argues *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. B & R Sales & Marketing*, 698 So. 2d 1257 (Fla. 1st DCA 1997), is "distinguishable" because Bradley allegedly resigned before the commission dispute arose. This argument is legally flawed. *R.J. Reynolds* holds that an employer’s failure to pay **earned commissions**—regardless of timing—constitutes a material breach excusing an employee’s performance under a noncompetition covenant. *Id.* at 1260. The Court explicitly rejected the notion that "the timing of the breach relative to termination" automatically negates the defense:  
> *"When an employer fails to pay compensation earned under the employment agreement, this breach goes to the heart of the contractual relationship and excuses the employee’s obligations under restrictive covenants."*  

Here, Bradley alleged HCI wrongfully withheld commissions he had **earned prior to his departure in April 1994**. Whether the commissions were due before or after termination is irrelevant—the breach occurred during the employment relationship. As the trial court properly recognized in its preliminary ruling, "the defense of material breach remains a significant factual dispute that the trial court must address." The trial court’s refusal to resolve this issue before granting the injunction was reversible error.  

#### B. HCI’s Alleged Instruction to Commit Fraud Destroys the Covenant’s Enforceability  

The defense dismisses Bradley’s claim that HCI ordered him to alter invoices to charge higher prices than agreed upon as "not established below." This ignores binding precedent. In *Cintas Corp. v. Parker*, 849 So. 2d 369, 372 (Fla. 5th DCA 2003), this Court held that an employer’s request for an employee to engage in fraudulent activity "violates the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing" and may render a noncompetition covenant unenforceable. The Court explained:  
> *"An employer cannot enforce a restrictive covenant when its conduct destroys the foundation of the employment relationship, including demands that an employee commit fraud."*  

The defense’s claim that Bradley "waited nearly two years to raise this defense" is irrelevant. Bradley raised the issue at the injunction hearing—a procedural necessity for preserving the defense. *See City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 634 So. 2d 750, 752 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994) (requiring trial courts to resolve factual disputes material to the injunction’s propriety). The trial court’s failure to credit Bradley’s testimony or make findings on this allegation—which directly implicates HCI’s good faith—was an abuse of discretion.  

### II. HCI FAILED TO SATISFY THE TEMPORARY INJUNCTION REQUIREMENTS  

A temporary injunction requires the moving party to demonstrate all four elements: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm; (3) balance of hardships; and (4) public interest. *City of Jacksonville*, 634 So. 2d at 752. HCI satisfied none of these where material breach defenses remain unresolved.  

#### A. HCI LACKED A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS  

The defense claims the covenant is "facially valid" and Bradley "did not challenge its enforceability below." This is misleading. Bradley’s material breach defenses directly attack the covenant’s enforceability. As *R.J. Reynolds* and *Cintas* establish, a material breach by the employer negates the covenant’s enforceability *as a matter of law*. *See R.J. Reynolds*, 698 So. 2d at 1260; *Cintas*, 849 So. 2d at 372. The trial court’s error was not in finding the covenant "reasonable" but in ignoring that HCI’s own conduct may have voided it entirely. Without resolving these defenses, HCI cannot establish a "likelihood of success on the merits"—the threshold requirement for any temporary injunction.  

#### B. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS AND PUBLIC INTEREST FAVOR BRADLEY  

The defense argues the "balance of hardships tips in [HCI’s] favor" because Bradley "chose to enter the covenant voluntarily." This ignores that HCI’s alleged misconduct (withholding commissions and demanding fraud) created the very hardship Bradley seeks to avoid. Courts routinely hold that enforcing a covenant against an employee wronged by the employer violates equity. *See Parker*, 849 So. 2d at 372 ("Public policy disfavors enforcing restrictive covenants where the employer’s own breaches undermine the contract’s foundation"). The public interest also favors preventing employers from weaponizing noncompetition agreements to silence whistleblowers.  

### III. CONCLUSION  

The trial court’s error was not a mere oversight—it was a fundamental failure to apply Florida law. When an employer allegedly breaches the employment agreement by withholding earned compensation and demanding fraudulent conduct, the trial court **must** resolve these disputes before granting injunctive relief. *R.J. Reynolds* and *Cintas* leave no room for doubt. By ignoring Bradley’s defenses, the trial court violated the core principle that "a temporary injunction is not a substitute for a full trial on the merits." *See Florida Highway Patrol v. Trimmer*, 725 So. 2d 450, 453 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999).  

We urge this Court to affirm the preliminary ruling reversing the injunction. The trial court’s order must be vacated, and the case remanded for resolution of Bradley’s material breach defenses.

**PRELIMINARY RULING (UPDATED)**

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant,**
v.
**HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**NO.**

**Updated Preliminary Ruling**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the injunction, arguing that the trial court failed to consider his defenses that Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. ("HCI") materially breached the employment agreement. Specifically, Bradley contends that HCI refused to pay earned commissions and instructed him to engage in fraudulent business practices. After reviewing the rebuttals presented by both parties, we affirm the decision to reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court's decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *See City of Jacksonville v. Naegele Outdoor Advertising Co.*, 634 So. 2d 750, 752 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994). To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *Id.* A temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy designed to preserve the status quo pending a full adjudication on the merits, not to resolve factual disputes definitively. *See Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council v. City of Cape Coral*, 386 So. 2d 1089, 1091 (Fla. 2d DCA 1980).

**II. Analysis**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in granting the injunction without first resolving Bradley's assertion that HCI materially breached the employment agreement, thereby excusing his performance under the noncompetition covenant.

**A. Material Breach and Unpaid Commissions**

Bradley argues that HCI's refusal to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach of the employment agreement. He relies on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. B & R Sales & Marketing*, 698 So. 2d 1257 (Fla. 1st DCA 1997). HCI argues that *R.J. Reynolds* is distinguishable because Bradley resigned due to his refusal to commit fraud, not because of the commission dispute, and therefore the commission breach did not cause the termination.

We find HCI's distinction legally significant. *R.J. Reynolds* holds that an employer's failure to pay earned commissions constitutes a material breach that excuses an employee's performance of a noncompetition agreement, particularly where the breach is the cause of the resignation. *Id.* at 1260. Here, Bradley asserts he was "forced to resign after he refused the employer’s instructions to engage in improper business practices," not because of the commission dispute. The commission dispute arose "after his employment ended." Consequently, the defense of material breach regarding commissions may not automatically excuse Bradley's performance if it was not the catalyst for his departure. *See Wallach v. Rosenberg*, 527 So. 2d 914, 917 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988) (noting that a material breach must be the substantial cause of the inability to perform).

However, this does not absolve the trial court of its duty to make findings. Whether the commissions were truly "earned" prior to termination and whether the withholding of such commissions constitutes a material breach independent of the resignation are factual questions. The trial court abused its discretion by failing to make specific findings regarding this defense. *See R.J. Reynolds*, 698 So. 2d at 1260.

**B. Fraud and Constructive Discharge**

Bradley further contends that he was forced to resign after refusing HCI's instructions to alter customer invoices to charge higher prices than agreed upon—a practice he alleges constitutes fraud. HCI argues that this allegation is uncorroborated and relies solely on Bradley's self-serving testimony, distinguishing *Cintas Corp. v. Parker*, 849 So. 2d 369 (Fla. 5th DCA 2003), where documentary evidence supported the employee's claim.

While the evidentiary distinction noted by HCI is valid, it does not justify granting the injunction without a hearing. The existence of a genuine factual dispute regarding whether HCI ordered Bradley to commit fraud is critical to the analysis of the noncompetition agreement's enforceability. An employer cannot enforce a restrictive covenant if it has engaged in conduct that destroys the employment relationship's foundation. *See Cintas*, 849 So. 2d at 372. The trial court was required to assess the credibility of Bradley's testimony versus HCI's denial. *See Green v. K-Mart Corp.*, 445 So. 2d 690, 691 (Fla. 2d DCA 1983) (holding that credibility determinations are within the trial court's discretion, but must be made). By granting the injunction without explicitly resolving this serious allegation of misconduct, the trial court failed to ensure that HCI had established a clear likelihood of success on the merits.

**C. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Scope of Remand**

Given the unresolved disputes regarding material breach and fraudulent instructions, HCI has not demonstrated the requisite likelihood of success on the merits to support a temporary injunction. *See City of Jacksonville*, 634 So. 2d at 752.

We agree with HCI that the reversal should not be interpreted as a pre-judgment in Bradley's favor. The remand is not a "rubber-stamp" of Bradley's defenses. Rather, the case is remanded for a full evidentiary hearing where Bradley bears the burden of proving his defenses by a preponderance of the evidence. *See Aetna Insurance Co. v. Nails & Lumber, Inc.*, 512 So. 2d 971, 973 (Fla. 1st DCA 1987). Bradley must establish that the commission breach was material and that the fraud instructions actually occurred. The trial court must then make specific findings of fact and conclusions of law regarding these defenses before determining if the noncompetition covenant is enforceable.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion by entering a temporary injunction without adequately considering Bradley's defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. We therefore reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for the trial court to conduct a full evidentiary hearing and make specific findings regarding Bradley's defenses to HCI's claims.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**

**II. Analysis**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction without making specific findings of fact regarding Bradley's affirmative defenses of material breach and employer misconduct.

**A. Material Breach and Unpaid Commissions**

HCI argues that the trial court properly granted the injunction because Bradley's material breach defense fails as a matter of law. Specifically, HCI contends that the commission dispute arose after Bradley's resignation and, therefore, could not have been the catalyst for his departure under *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. B & B Sales & Marketing*, 698 So. 2d 1257 (Fla. 1st DCA 1997). HCI further asserts that the trial court implicitly found Bradley's testimony not credible.

While we recognize HCI's arguments regarding the temporal sequence of events, these arguments go to the weight of the evidence, which is a matter for the trial court to determine explicitly. *R.J. Reynolds* holds that an employer's failure to pay earned commissions constitutes a material breach that may excuse an employee's performance of a noncompetition agreement. *Id.* at 1260. Whether the commissions were "earned" prior to termination, and whether the withholding of those commissions was a substantial cause of the resignation, are factual questions. *See Wallach v. Rosenberg*, 527 So. 2d 914, 917 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988).

The trial court's order granting the injunction does not contain findings addressing these factual predicates. An appellate court cannot uphold an injunction based on an "implicit" finding that the trial court disbelieved Bradley's testimony regarding the timing and earning of commissions. *See Green v. K-Mart Corp.*, 445 So. 2d 690, 691 (Fla. 2d DCA 1983) (noting that while credibility determinations are within the trial court's discretion, they must be discernible from the record). Without explicit findings, we cannot determine whether the trial court properly applied the *R.J. Reynolds* standard or simply ignored the defense. Therefore, the trial court abused its discretion by failing to make specific findings on this material issue.

**B. Fraud and Constructive Discharge**

Bradley contends that he was forced to resign after refusing HCI's instructions to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed. HCI argues that this allegation is uncorroborated and relies solely on Bradley's self-serving testimony, distinguishing *Cintas Corp. v. Parker*, 849 So. 2d 369 (Fla. 5th DCA 2003).

We agree with HCI that Bradley bears the burden of proving his defenses by a preponderance of the evidence. *See Aetna Insurance Co. v. Nails & Lumber, Inc.*, 512 So. 2d 971, 973 (Fla. 1st DCA 1987). However, at the injunction stage, HCI bore the burden of demonstrating a likelihood of success on the merits despite these defenses. *City of Jacksonville*, 634 So. 2d at 752. The existence of a genuine factual dispute regarding whether HCI ordered Bradley to commit fraud is directly relevant to the enforceability of the noncompetition agreement. *See Cintas*, 849 So. 2d at 372 (recognizing that employer misconduct can destroy the employment relationship's foundation).

The trial court's failure to address this allegation in its order leaves unresolved whether HCI has met its burden of showing a likelihood of success. The trial court was required to assess the credibility of Bradley's testimony versus HCI's denial and make a finding. *Green*, 445 So. 2d at 691. By granting the injunction without addressing this defense, the trial court failed to ensure that the status quo was being preserved in a manner consistent with the potential rights of the parties.

**C. Scope of Remand**

We reject HCI's request to affirm the injunction. The lack of specific findings regarding Bradley's defenses prevents this Court from conducting a meaningful review of whether the trial court abused its discretion. *See Florida Power & Light Co. v. International Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers, Local 743*, 617 So. 2d 748, 751 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993) (holding that specific findings of fact are necessary for appellate review of injunctions).

Accordingly, we reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. On remand, the trial court must conduct a full evidentiary hearing and enter specific findings of fact and conclusions of law regarding: (1) whether Bradley's commissions were earned and whether their withholding constituted a material breach under *R.J. Reynolds*; and (2) whether HCI instructed Bradley to commit fraud, thereby affecting the enforceability of the covenant under *Cintas*. Only after resolving these disputes can the trial court determine if HCI is entitled to injunctive relief.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion by entering a temporary injunction without making specific findings of fact regarding Bradley's defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. We reverse the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**
