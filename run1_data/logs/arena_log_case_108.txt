FACTS:
The State of Hawaii appeals a circuit court order granting a motion to suppress evidence filed by defendant David A. Haili before trial. Defendant was charged with possession of prohibited firearm in violation of Hawaii Revised Statutes § 134-8 (1976).

At approximately 10:30 p.m. on May 5, 1979, a security guard posted at the Kapaa Quarry  immediately reported the incident to the police . The guard furnished no other information.

A police bulletin of the incident was broadcasted. Within minutes, Officer Charles Davis was at the designated portion of the freeway and stood with his rifle at the chain link fence which serves to barricade the uncompleted end of the freeway. A few minutes later, Officer Davis observed a car travelling at a reasonable speed on the uncompleted freeway approaching with headlights on. He stopped the car. From a distance of sixty feet, he ordered the four occupants out of the car. Each immediately complied except Defendant Haili, who was leaning over and watching the officer. The officer raised his rifle and repeated his command. This time defendant obeyed, after causing a delay of five seconds.

At this point, additional officers arrived and they conducted a “pat down” of the four occupants. The four then sat on the ground and were guarded by one officer. The other officers scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight and spotted a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. They also saw a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat. Upon pushing the front seat forward, the officers saw a ukulele case, unlatched although the cover was down. Officer Davis opened the case and found a modified rifle and plastic boxes containing .22-caliber rifle ammunition.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Intermediate Court of Appeals of the State of Hawaii

### State of Hawaii, Plaintiff-Appellant v. David A. Haili, Defendant-Appellee

---

**May it please the Court:**

The defense respectfully opposes the State's appeal and submits that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the officers' conduct fell within constitutional boundaries, and the evidence obtained should remain suppressed. The defense addresses each phase of the officers' conduct below.

---

## I. THE INITIAL STOP OF THE VEHICLE WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION

The Fourth Amendment requires that an investigative stop of a vehicle be supported by reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). While the prosecution characterizes the circumstances as suspicious, the facts presented establish only the most minimal basis for investigation.

The officer relied upon a police bulletin broadcasted following a security guard's report of "an incident" at the Kapaa Quarry. Critically, the guard "furnished no other information." The bulletin provided no description of suspects, vehicles, or the nature of the criminal activity allegedly occurring. The prosecution argues that a vehicle traveling on an uncompleted freeway at night was "inconsistent with normal traffic patterns," but this fact alone—without more—does not establish reasonable suspicion. Members of the public may lawfully travel on uncompleted freeways for various reasons, and the mere presence of a vehicle in an unusual location does not create a fair inference of criminal conduct. The prosecution conflates mere curiosity with reasonable suspicion, a distinction this Court should maintain.

---

## II. THE PAT DOWN OF THE OCCUPANTS WAS UNLAWFUL

The prosecution does not argue, nor can the facts support, that the officers had reasonable suspicion that any of the four occupants were armed and dangerous. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a protective frisk requires "specific articulable facts" suggesting the person is "armed and dangerous." The officers conducted a "pat down" of all four occupants without any articulated basis for believing they posed a threat. This search exceeded constitutional authority and any evidence obtained from the occupants should be suppressed.

---

## III. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN OF THE VEHICLE INTERIOR EXCEEDED CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS

The prosecution relies heavily upon *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), but misapplies its holding. In *Brown*, the Court addressed whether simply using a flashlight to enhance vision during a lawful停车 (stop) constituted a search. The officer in that case was lawfully present at a lawful checkpoint and observed through the vehicle window objects in plain view. The Court held that illumination merely enhances natural vision and does not transform a lawful observation into a search.

The critical distinction this Court must recognize is that *Brown* involved observation through a vehicle window from a lawful position—not a warrantless scanning of the vehicle's entire interior. Here, Officer Davis did not merely observe what was naturally visible; he actively scanned the interior of the vehicle with a flashlight, illuminating areas that would not otherwise be visible to an observer. This distinction is constitutionally significant. The prosecution's argument would permit officers to conduct warrantless searches of any vehicle interior by simply using a flashlight, eviscerating the Fourth Amendment's protections. The defense submits that scanning the vehicle interior with a flashlight, beyond what is naturally observable from outside the vehicle, constitutes a search requiring probable cause.

---

## IV. THERE WAS NO PROBABLE CAUSE TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE

Even assuming *arguendo* that the initial stop was lawful, the prosecution has failed to establish probable cause justifying the search of the vehicle and its contents.

### A. The Totality of Circumstances Did Not Establish Probable Cause

The prosecution invokes *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), but fails to apply its standard. Probable cause requires "a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found" based on the totality of circumstances. The prosecution's factors, considered together, fall far short of this standard:

1. **The security guard's report**: The guard provided no information regarding the nature of the incident, suspects, or vehicles involved. An anonymous or near-anonymous tip lacking corroboration does not establish probable cause. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000).

2. **Vehicle on uncompleted freeway**: As discussed, this is insufficient to create reasonable suspicion, let alone probable cause.

3. **Five-second delay in compliance**: The defendant "was leaning over and watching the officer" before complying. This momentary hesitation does not create a fair inference of criminal conduct. Nervousness or confusion during a police encounter is not probable cause. *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1989).

The prosecution's attempt to bundle these weak factors together does not create probable cause. Individually and collectively, these circumstances would not warrant a person of reasonable caution in believing that evidence of a crime would be found in this vehicle.

### B. The Rifle Casing Did Not Provide Probable Cause for Further Search

The prosecution argues that the rifle casing discovered during the flashlight scan "directly corroborated" criminal activity. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the flashlight scan itself was likely unlawful for the reasons stated above. Second, a single rifle casing—without more—does not establish that a crime was committed or that additional evidence would be found in the vehicle. The casing could have been placed there innocently, and its presence alone does not create a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime exists in the vehicle.

Third, even assuming the casing provided probable cause to search the vehicle's interior for weapons, this did not authorize opening the ukulele case. Under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), the scope of a vehicle search is limited to areas where the object of the search may be found. A ukulele case is not a typical container for weapons, and its appearance as a musical instrument case did not create probable cause to open it.

---

## V. THE SEARCH OF THE UKULELE CASE EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF ANY AUTHORIZED SEARCH

The officers found a "black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat." They then "pushed the front seat forward" and saw "a ukulele case, unlatched although the cover was down." Officer Davis "opened the case and found a modified rifle and plastic boxes containing .22-caliber rifle ammunition."

Even accepting the prosecution's theory that probable cause existed to search the vehicle, the search of the ukulele case exceeded constitutional limits. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), held that a vehicle search may extend to "every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search." However, this authority does not extend to opening a container that does not appear related to the object of the search. A ukulele case, by its very nature, is a musical instrument case—not a weapon case. The officers had no probable cause to believe that a modified rifle would be found inside a ukulele case. The search of the case was a general exploratory search prohibited by the Fourth Amendment. *Maranda v. United States*, 389 U.S. 285, 297 (1967).

---

## CONCLUSION

The circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion for the initial stop and the pat down. The flashlight scan of the vehicle interior exceeded constitutional limits. Even assuming lawful presence, the totality of circumstances did not establish probable cause to search the vehicle, and the search of the ukulele case certainly exceeded any authorized scope of search.

The prosecution has not demonstrated error in the circuit court's ruling. The suppression order should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**Before the Court is the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence.** The State argues that the warrantless search of the vehicle was justified under the automobile exception and that the officers’ conduct did not violate the Fourth Amendment. After reviewing the record and the arguments presented by both parties, this Court finds that the circuit court properly granted the motion to suppress. While the State’s arguments regarding the scope of the search and the use of a flashlight are supported by precedent, the initial stop of the vehicle was not supported by reasonable suspicion, rendering the subsequent evidence inadmissible as fruit of the poisonous tree.

**I. The Initial Stop Lacked Reasonable Suspicion**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable seizures, and an investigative stop of a vehicle constitutes a seizure under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). To justify such a stop, an officer must have specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity is afoot. The State contends that the combination of the security guard’s report and the vehicle’s location on an uncompleted freeway provided reasonable suspicion. This Court disagrees.

The record indicates that the security guard reported "an incident" at the Kapaa Quarry but "furnished no other information." The bulletin broadcast to officers contained no description of a suspect, a vehicle, or the nature of the crime. As argued by the defense, an uncorroborated, vague report of an "incident" provides no basis for detaining a specific vehicle. See *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000) (holding that an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability does not justify a stop).

The State argues that the vehicle’s presence on the "uncompleted end of the freeway" at night was "inconsistent with normal traffic patterns." However, the facts provided do not indicate that the freeway was closed to the public or that entering the uncompleted portion constituted a crime. While the location may be unusual, mere presence in an area not typically used for traffic, without more, does not amount to reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. The connection between a vague "incident" at a nearby quarry and a vehicle driving on a nearby road is too tenuous to justify a seizure. Because Officer Davis lacked reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle, the detention was unconstitutional.

**II. The Pat Down Was Unlawful**

The State does not provide a specific justification for the "pat down" of the four occupants, other than the general context of the investigation. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), a frisk for weapons is permitted only if the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The facts show that the occupants complied with orders to exit the vehicle, with the exception of Defendant Haili, who hesitated for five seconds while leaning over. A momentary delay in exiting a vehicle, especially when faced with an officer holding a rifle, does not provide the specific articulable facts required to believe a person is armed and dangerous. Consequently, the pat down exceeded the scope of the unlawful stop and violated the Fourth Amendment.

**III. The Flashlight Scan and Search of the Ukulele Case**

Even if the stop had been lawful, the State’s arguments regarding the search of the vehicle are largely persuasive, though ultimately moot given the illegality of the stop.

Regarding the flashlight scan, the State correctly cites *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740-741 (1983), for the proposition that using a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle interior does not constitute a search if it merely enhances the officer’s natural vision. The defense attempts to distinguish *Brown* by arguing that the officer here "actively scanned" the interior rather than observing from a lawful position. However, the facts indicate the officers were lawfully present at the vehicle following the stop, and observing the interior with a flashlight falls squarely within the *Brown* rule. The discovery of the .22-caliber rifle casing was therefore in plain view.

Furthermore, the State’s argument that the discovery of the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the entire vehicle, including containers, is supported by *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982). Under *Ross*, if probable cause justifies the search of a vehicle, it justifies the search of every part of the vehicle that could conceal the object of the search. The defense argues that a ukulele case is not a typical container for a weapon. However, *Ross* explicitly rejects the notion that probable cause to search a vehicle depends on the specific contents of containers within it; rather, if the vehicle as a whole may contain contraband, any container within it that could hold that contraband is subject to search. A rifle can fit inside a ukulele case, and thus the search of the case was within the scope of the automobile exception. See *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565, 580 (1991).

**IV. Conclusion**

While the State has demonstrated that the search methodology—specifically the use of a flashlight and the opening of the ukulele case—was constitutionally sound under *Texas v. Brown* and *United States v. Ross*, these procedures cannot salvage the investigation from its invalid inception. The initial stop was based on a vague report and innocuous circumstances insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion under *Terry v. Ohio*. Because the stop was unlawful, the evidence obtained as a result—the rifle casing and the modified rifle—must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963).

Accordingly, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **AFFIRMED**.

Judge
Appellate Court of the State of Hawaii

May it please the Court, the State respectfully submits that the defense's arguments misapply controlling precedent and ignore the totality of circumstances establishing both reasonable suspicion for the initial stop and probable cause for the subsequent search. The circuit court erred in suppressing the evidence, as Officer Davis' conduct was fully constitutional under the Fourth Amendment. The defense's claims regarding the stop, flashlight scan, and ukulele case search are legally unsound and require reversal.

### I. The Initial Stop Was Supported by Reasonable Suspicion  
The defense incorrectly contends that the security guard's report and vehicle location failed to establish reasonable suspicion. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), an investigative stop requires only "specific and articulable facts" suggesting criminal activity, not probable cause. Here, the security guard reported an "incident" at Kapaa Quarry, and Officer Davis observed a vehicle traveling on the uncompleted freeway at night within minutes of the report. The uncompleted freeway is a restricted construction site where public vehicle access is prohibited, making its use at night inherently suspicious. As this Court emphasized in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-418 (1981), reasonable suspicion is assessed through a "totality of the circumstances" analysis, and the proximity of the vehicle to the reported incident site—combined with its presence in a restricted area—provides the minimal objective justification required. The defense's focus on "mere presence" ignores that the vehicle was not merely in an "unusual location" but on a non-public roadway where lawful traffic is impermissible, directly linking it to the recent incident report. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000), is inapposite because that case involved an uncorroborated anonymous tip with no officer-observed facts; here, Officer Davis independently observed the vehicle in a context directly related to the incident report.  

### II. The Flashlight Scan Constituted No Search Under *Texas v. Brown*  
The defense's claim that the flashlight scan "exceeded constitutional limits" is directly contradicted by *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740-741 (1983). In *Brown*, the Supreme Court unequivocally held that "the use of a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle interior during a lawful stop does not constitute a search." The Court reasoned that such illumination merely "enhances the officer's natural vision" and does not transform a lawful observation into a Fourth Amendment violation. Here, Officer Davis was lawfully present after the stop, and his use of a flashlight to detect the .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor was precisely the type of "enhancement of natural vision" permitted by *Brown*. The defense's characterization of this as "active scanning" is irrelevant; *Brown* explicitly rejects any distinction between "passive" and "active" illumination when the officer is lawfully positioned. This precedent forecloses the defense's argument that the scan required probable cause.  

### III. Probable Cause Justified the Entire Vehicle Search, Including the Ukulele Case  
The discovery of the rifle casing during the flashlight scan provided probable cause to search the vehicle. Under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause exists when "the facts available to the officer would warrant a person of reasonable caution in believing that contraband or evidence of a crime was in the vehicle." A spent rifle casing is direct evidence of firearm activity, and in the context of the incident report at Kapaa Quarry and the vehicle's presence on a restricted roadway, it creates a fair probability that additional firearm-related evidence would be found. This is not a "single casing" issue divorced from context; the casing's presence alone—coupled with the suspicious circumstances—satisfies *Gates*' totality standard.  

Under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), once probable cause justifies a vehicle search, police may examine "every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search." A ukulele case is capable of holding a rifle, as evidenced by the modified rifle recovered inside it. The case was "unlatched although the cover was down," indicating it was a container where a weapon could reasonably be concealed. *California v. ### B. The Security Guard's Report Was Insufficient to Establish Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution attempts to bolster the reasonable suspicion analysis by emphasizing the security guard's report of "an incident" at Kapaa Quarry. **OPINION OF THE COURT**

**Before the Court is the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence.** The State contends that the warrantless search of the vehicle was justified under the automobile exception and that the officers’ conduct did not violate the Fourth Amendment. After careful consideration of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court adheres to its preliminary finding that the circuit court properly granted the motion to suppress. While the prosecution correctly identifies the legal standards governing vehicle searches, it fails to demonstrate that the initial seizure of the vehicle was lawful. Because the stop was unsupported by reasonable suspicion, the evidence obtained as a result is inadmissible.

**I. The Initial Stop Lacked Reasonable Suspicion**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable seizures, and an investigative stop of a vehicle constitutes a seizure under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). To justify such a stop, an officer must have specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity is afoot. The prosecution argues that the "totality of the circumstances"—specifically, the security guard's report of an "incident" and the vehicle's presence on an "uncompleted freeway"—provided reasonable suspicion. The Court remains unpersuaded.

First, the prosecution asks this Court to accept the premise that the uncompleted freeway was a "restricted construction site where public vehicle access is prohibited." However, the factual record provided does not support this characterization. The facts state only that the freeway was "uncompleted" and barricaded by a chain link fence. There is no indication that the area was posted with signs prohibiting entry, nor is there any evidence that entering the uncompleted portion constituted a crime. While the location may be unusual, mere presence in an area not typically used for traffic, without evidence of restriction, does not amount to reasonable suspicion of criminal activity.

Second, the prosecution relies heavily on the security guard's report to corroborate the suspicious nature of the vehicle's location. As the defense correctly notes, the guard "furnished no other information" regarding the nature of the "incident," a description of a suspect, or a vehicle. The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000), by arguing that the officer here independently observed the vehicle. However, *J.L.* stands for the proposition that an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability does not justify a stop, and the corroboration of innocent activity does not transform a vague tip into reasonable suspicion. Here, the officer observed a car driving with headlights on at a reasonable speed. This is entirely innocent activity. Without a description of a vehicle or suspect from the guard, the officer could not possibly link this specific car to the unspecified "incident" at the quarry. The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-418 (1981), is misplaced because the "totality of the circumstances" in this case amounts to a vague report of an unknown crime combined with the presence of a vehicle in an area that the record does not establish was off-limits. This is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion.

**II. The Pat Down Was Unlawful**

The prosecution’s rebuttal does not address the lawfulness of the pat-down search of the four occupants. Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), a frisk for weapons is permitted only if the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The facts show that the occupants complied with orders to exit the vehicle, with the exception of Defendant Haili, who hesitated for five seconds while leaning over. A momentary delay in exiting a vehicle, particularly when faced with an officer holding a rifle, does not provide the specific articulable facts required to believe a person is armed and dangerous. Consequently, the pat down exceeded the scope of the unlawful stop and violated the Fourth Amendment.

**III. The Flashlight Scan and Search of the Ukulele Case**

The prosecution argues persuasively that the use of a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle interior does not constitute a search under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740-741 (1983), and that the discovery of a rifle casing provided probable cause to search containers within the vehicle under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982). The Court agrees with the prosecution’s interpretation of these precedents. Had the stop been lawful, the officers’ conduct in scanning the interior with a flashlight and opening the ukulele case would likely have been constitutional.

However, the prosecution’s analysis regarding the search methodology is moot because the initial stop was unconstitutional. The defense correctly invokes the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine. Under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), evidence obtained as a result of an unlawful seizure must be suppressed. The rifle casing was discovered only after the unlawful stop and detention. Therefore, the casing itself is fruit of the poisonous tree. The prosecution cannot use the tainted evidence of the casing to establish probable cause for the search of the ukulele case. To allow the State to bootstrap probable cause from evidence obtained during an unlawful seizure would eviscerate the Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches and seizures.

**IV. Conclusion**

The prosecution’s arguments regarding the legality of the search techniques are sound as a matter of law, but they cannot cure the constitutional defect of the initial stop. The stop was based on a vague report and innocuous circumstances that failed to establish reasonable suspicion under *Terry v. Ohio*. Because the stop was unlawful, the evidence obtained as a result—the rifle casing and the modified rifle—must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963).

Accordingly, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **AFFIRMED**.

## I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution urges this Court to accept the proposition that the uncompleted freeway was a "restricted construction site where public vehicle access is prohibited." This characterization finds no support in the factual record. The facts establish only that the freeway was "uncompleted" and barricaded by a chain link fence. A chain link fence barricading an uncompleted roadway is ambiguous—it may indicate construction in progress, future development, or simply an unfinished infrastructure project. Without evidence of posted restrictions or legal prohibition, the Defense maintains that the prosecution cannot transform mere location into reasonable suspicion.

The prosecution relies heavily on *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-418 (1981), for its totality-of-the-circumstances analysis. However, the circumstances here bear no resemblance to those in *Cortez*. In *Cortez*, the Supreme Court upheld an investigative stop based on a specific, articulable basis: officers observed a vehicle making an unusual border-crossing pattern, the driver acted nervously, and the vehicle was subsequently followed to a boarding house where passengers were dropped off and picked up in a manner suggestive of illegal activity. The Court in *Cortez* emphasized that reasonable suspicion requires "a particularized suspicion," not merely a "nebulous hunch." Here, by contrast, the prosecution offers only a vague report of an "incident" at a quarry and the presence of a vehicle on an uncompleted freeway. This falls far short of the particularized suspicion required under *Cortez*.

## II. THE PAT-DOWN SEARCH EXCEEDED CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS

The prosecution's closing statement fails to address the lawfulness of the pat-down search entirely. This omission is telling, as the facts demonstrate that the frisk was unconstitutional under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968).

Under the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine, as articulated in *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), evidence obtained as a result of an unlawful seizure must be suppressed. The rifle casing was discovered only after the unlawful stop and detention of the vehicle. Therefore, the casing itself is fruit of the poisonous tree. The prosecution argues that the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the ukulele case under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), and *California v. The prosecution's reliance on *Ross* and *Acevedo* is misplaced because those cases presuppose a lawful initial stop with valid probable cause. The prosecution's entire argument rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. It asks this Court to affirm the search by pointing to the evidence discovered during the search itself. This reasoning is constitutionally impermissible.

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**Before the Court is the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence.** The State contends that the warrantless search of the vehicle was justified under the automobile exception and that the officers’ conduct did not violate the Fourth Amendment. **I. The Initial Stop Lacked Reasonable Suspicion**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable seizures, and an investigative stop of a vehicle constitutes a seizure under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). To justify such a stop, an officer must have specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity is afoot. The prosecution argues that the "totality of the circumstances"—specifically, the security guard's report of an "incident" and the vehicle's presence on an "uncompleted freeway"—provided reasonable suspicion. The Court remains unpersuaded.

The prosecution asks this Court to accept the premise that the uncompleted freeway was a "restricted construction site where public vehicle access is prohibited" based solely on the presence of a chain link fence. There is no indication that the area was posted with signs prohibiting entry, nor is there any evidence that entering the uncompleted portion constituted a crime. Here, the officer observed a car driving with headlights on at a reasonable speed. This is entirely innocent activity. The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-418 (1981), is misplaced because the "totality of the circumstances" in this case amounts to a vague report of an unknown crime combined with the presence of a vehicle in an area that the record does not establish was off-limits. The prosecution’s rebuttal does not address the lawfulness of the pat-down search of the four occupants. Under *Terry v. The facts show that the occupants complied with orders to exit the vehicle, with the exception of Defendant Haili, who hesitated for five seconds while leaning over. A momentary delay in exiting a vehicle, particularly when faced with an officer holding a rifle, does not provide the specific articulable facts required to believe a person is armed and dangerous.
