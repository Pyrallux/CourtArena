FACTS:
Defendant, Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, *462administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, appeals the orders dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013. This opinion is filed pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928.

FACTS

Tina Marie Plotts died on February 27, 2004, as a result of a fire which started in the kitchen of the premises where she was a tenant.  According to the landlords and owners of the premises, defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, defendant did maintenance for them in exchange for the payment of rent to reside there. On the evening of the fire, defendant had fallen asleep on a couch in the first floor living room after consuming a six-pack of beer. Ms. Plotts was in her designated second floor bedroom when a fire originated in the kitchen of the first floor of the residence. The cause and/or ignition source was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove.

Defendant escaped the burning residence, but Ms. Plotts was trapped inside the dwelling and could not flee; her bedroom windows were sealed shut and the stairs were impassible. She was found dead by fire personnel.

Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, filed a motion pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 against defendant’s estate for defendant’s failure to obey two court orders requiring his attendance at a videotape discovery deposition along with related discovery violations which impacted plaintiff’s *463ability to proceed with the lawsuit. In this motion, plaintiff outlines a long imcontro verted history of defendant’s noncompliance with scheduled depositions.

Plaintiff’s counsel first requested deposition dates for defendant by letter to defendant’s counsel dated June 17, 2007. Defendant was then scheduled for a deposition on August 13,2007, at 12:30 p.m. which was mutually agreeable to the parties. Defendant did not appear for the deposition, and his counsel stated that he could not locate him.

On August 27, 2008, defendant’s present counsel entered his appearance for defendant. Plaintiff again requested deposition dates for defendant. Eventually, defendant’s counsel provided several deposition dates; plaintiff selected October 15,2008, with no objection from defendant, for a videotaped deposition. Again, defendant failed to appear. A new date of November 8, 2008, was selected for defendant’s deposition. On October 27, 2008, defendant’s counsel contacted plaintiff’s counsel and stated that he was unable to locate defendant about the rescheduled deposition. OnNovember 7,2008, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that because his office was unable to locate defendant, defendant’s scheduled deposition of November 8, 2008, was cancelled.

On January 7, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel wrote to defendant’s counsel to request defendant’s last known address and Social Security number so he could hire a private investigator to find defendant. After receiving the requested information, plaintiff hired a private investigator who found defendant. Plaintiff then served defendant with a subpoena to take his deposition on April 27, 2009. On April 23,2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to confirm that defendant would appear for his deposition. By letter dated April 23, 2009, *464plaintiff’s counsel issued an amended notice of videotaped deposition in accordance with Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1. By letter dated April 26, 2009, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped and would therefore not appear for his deposition.

On April 28, 2009, April 29, 2009, and April 30, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to reschedule defendant’s deposition. Ultimately, defendant’s counsel informed plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped during his deposition.

On May 18,2009, plaintiff filed a motion for contempt and for sanctions. On July 8,2009, the Honorable Scott E. Lash, on behalf of Senior Judge Albert A. Stallone, entered an order requiring defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition.

Plaintiff filed a second motion for contempt and for sanctions on May 10, 2010. On August 25, 2010, Judge Lash on behalf of Senior Judge Stallone entered an order for defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition and further ordered that the videotape would be retained by counsel for only their own use and the court’s use and would not be published to third persons. Defendant was also entitled to receive a copy of the videotape.

The parties mutually agreed on the date of November 10, 2010, for a videotaped deposition. By letter dated November 9,2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for the deposition on November 10, 2010.

The parties subsequently agreed on December 2, 2010, for defendant’s deposition. By letter dated November 30, 2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for this deposition because he *465was out of town until January 1, 2011.

Defendant’s deposition was then scheduled for January 31, 2011. By letter dated January 28, 2011, defendant’s counsel cancelled this deposition. Defendant had told his counsel that “there is no way I’m getting involved.” Defendant’s counsel further stated in the letter, “obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled.”

The only statement defendant ever gave concerning the fire was an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire. At that time he denied that he was the cause of the fire. Defendant died in January 2012.

After argument and a consideration of defendant’s dismal record of non-compliance, this court entered the orders which are the subjects of the instant appeal. This court found that the most appropriate sanction in this case was to grant plaintiff’s request for judgment in his favor and against defendant. Defendant filed an interlocutory appeal concerning this sanction.

ISSUES

Defendant raises the following issues in his concise statement of errors complained of on appeal.

1. This court erred and manifestly abused its discretion in entering judgment against defendant, pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019, as a result of defendant’s failure to appear for his videotaped deposition, since there were less severe sanctions which would cure any alleged prejudice.

2. The record demonstrates that plaintiff bears some responsibility for the alleged prejudice which has accrued as a result of defendant’s death. Moreover, the alleged prejudice is unfounded. None of the evidence produced *466during the seven years of litigation in any way suggested that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire.

3. Given the lack of evidence indicating that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire, combined with plaintiff’s failure to diligently pursue defendant’s deposition prior to his death, the entry of judgment against him is erroneous, a manifest abuse of discretion and manifestly unjust to defendant.

4. Instead of the severe sanction of a judgment against defendant, this court could have entered less severe and more appropriate sanctions.

As this Court held in *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546, 549 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), “a pattern of deliberate noncompliance with discovery obligations justifies the imposition of the most severe sanction where less severe measures have proven ineffective.” Here, the trial court correctly recognized that the defendant’s conduct was not merely negligent but a sustained, calculated refusal to participate in discovery, warranting the most severe remedy.

He did not merely miss a deposition; he affirmatively refused to participate even after the court specifically ordered him to do so on two occasions. The trial court correctly determined that this prejudice could not be cured, as “once a witness dies, the opportunity to depose them is permanently lost, and no lesser sanction can restore that lost evidence.”  

Finally, the trial court considered less severe sanctions but rightly rejected them as inadequate. Plaintiff sought and obtained multiple court orders compelling the defendant’s deposition, yet the defendant ignored them all. Under *Miller*, “when a party repeatedly defies court orders and demonstrates no willingness to comply, sanctions such as monetary penalties or adverse inference instructions are insufficient to address the obstruction.” The defendant’s steadfast refusal—evidenced by his counsel’s statements that he would not participate “nor any other time”—made it clear that lesser sanctions would not induce compliance. As the Superior Court held in *Rasko*, “the trial court’s discretion to impose default judgment is particularly appropriate where the record shows that lesser sanctions would be futile in the face of willful noncompliance.” Here, the court did not act precipitously; it weighed all factors and concluded that only default judgment would uphold the rule of law and protect plaintiff’s right to a fair trial.  

Your Honor, the trial court’s decision was a reasoned exercise of discretion grounded in precedent and the record. The defendant’s conduct was willful, prejudicial, and irreparable, leaving no alternative but to impose default judgment. We respectfully submit that the appeal should be dismissed, and the trial court’s order affirmed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPERIOR COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

---

**Defendant-Appellant, Michael J. D'Aniello, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, v. Joseph Michael Angelichio, Plaintiff-Appellee**

---

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court committed a manifest abuse of discretion in entering default judgment against the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer pursuant to Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019. While the defendant acknowledges that the deceased, Tyrone Diefenderfer, did not appear for scheduled depositions, the record does not support the conclusion that his conduct warranted the extreme sanction of default judgment. The trial court failed to properly balance the relevant factors, ignored critical evidence regarding the absence of negligence, and overlooked the plaintiff's own failure to diligently pursue discovery before the defendant's death. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's orders and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN FINDING THAT DEFAULT JUDGMENT WAS PROPORTIONATE TO THE ALLEGED DISCOVERY VIOLATIONS

The defense does not dispute that the defendant, Tyrone Diefenderfer, failed to appear for multiple scheduled depositions. However, the mere existence of discovery violations does not automatically justify the most severe sanction available under Pennsylvania law. As the Superior Court held in *Fischer v. Pittsburgh Nat'l Bank*, 376 A.2d 652, 656 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1977), "the choice of a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, but that discretion must be exercised in accordance with the guidelines established by our rules and case law." The court must balance the nature and severity of the violation, the willfulness of the noncompliance, the prejudice to the opposing party, and the availability of lesser sanctions.

The record establishes that the trial court failed to properly consider whether lesser sanctions would have been adequate. Throughout the seven-year period of noncompliance, the court entered only two orders compelling the defendant's deposition. The court never imposed monetary sanctions, never held the defendant in contempt with incarceration, and never considered adverse inference instructions as an intermediate remedy. In *West v. West*, 383 A.2d 546, 549 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1978), this Court emphasized that "the trial court should employ the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired result" and that "default judgment is appropriate only where less drastic remedies have proven ineffective."

Here, the trial court skipped directly to the most severe sanction without demonstrating that lesser remedies would have been futile. The defendant's noncompliance, while regrettable, consisted of failures to appear—not active destruction of evidence, not lying under oath, and not refusing to produce documents in his possession. The court had multiple opportunities to impose intermediate sanctions such as monetary penalties, adverse inference instructions, or continued orders with conditional provisions. The failure to do so constitutes an abuse of discretion.

---

## II. THE RECORD CONTAINS NO EVIDENCE ESTABLISHING THAT MR. DIEFENDERFER WAS NEGLIGENT OR THAT HIS CONDUCT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN CAUSING THE FIRE

A critical flaw in the trial court's analysis is its complete failure to address the fundamental question of liability. Before imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment, the court should have considered whether the plaintiff actually possessed evidence establishing that the defendant was negligent. The record before this Court contains no such evidence.

According to the facts provided, the fire originated from an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The rapid progression of the fire was caused by kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove. The plaintiff has never produced any expert testimony, physical evidence, or eyewitness account establishing that Mr. Diefenderfer placed the pot on the stove, turned on the burner, or negligently stored the kerosene container. The only statement Mr. Diefenderfer ever made was an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire in which he denied being the cause of the fire.

As this Court held in *Byrd v. Martin*, 403 A.2d 85, 88 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1979), "a default judgment is a drastic remedy that should be reserved for cases where the plaintiff's claim is clearly established by the record, not for cases where substantial questions of fact and law remain unresolved." Here, the plaintiff cannot establish even a prima facie case of negligence without speculation and conjecture. The fire began in the kitchen while Mr. Diefenderfer was asleep in the living room. The ignition source was an unattended pot—something that could have been placed on the stove by anyone, including the tenant, Ms. Plotts herself, or another occupant. The kerosene container that caused the rapid fire progression was stored near the stove, but there is no evidence establishing who placed it there or that it was defective due to any action or inaction by Mr. Diefenderfer.

The trial court's decision to enter default judgment effectively presumed negligence without any evidentiary basis. This Court should not allow a discovery sanction to serve as a substitute for proof of liability.

---

## III. THE PLAINTIFF BEARS SIGNIFICANT RESPONSIBILITY FOR THE ALLEGED PREJUDICE AND FAILED TO EXERCISE DUE DILIGENCE IN PURSUING DISCOVERY

The plaintiff's claim of irreparable prejudice rings hollow in light of his own conduct throughout the discovery process. The defense submits that the plaintiff bears substantial responsibility for the inability to obtain Mr. Diefenderfer's deposition and cannot now shift that burden entirely to the defendant.

The record reveals that from June 2007 to April 2009—a period of nearly two years—the plaintiff made no effort to locate Mr. Diefenderfer after his initial counsel stated he could not find him. The plaintiff waited until January 7, 2009, to request Mr. Diefenderfer's last known address and Social Security number so he could hire a private investigator. In *Jennings v. Rodriguez*, 518 A.2d 1243, 1247 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1986), this Court held that "a party seeking discovery must exercise due diligence in pursuing that discovery and cannot passively wait for the opposing party to comply while taking no affirmative steps to obtain the desired information."

Once the private investigator located Mr. Diefenderfer in April 2009, the plaintiff scheduled depositions but insisted on videotaped depositions despite the defendant's objection. The defendant refused to be videotaped, and rather than proceeding with a non-videotaped deposition, the plaintiff chose to engage in years of litigation over the videotaping issue. The plaintiff could have obtained the defendant's testimony through a non-videotaped deposition at any time between April 2009 and January 2012 but chose not to do so.

The plaintiff also waited until May 18, 2009, to file his first motion for contempt—nearly two years after the initial deposition request and only after the defendant failed to appear for the April 2009 deposition. The plaintiff could have sought earlier enforcement of discovery obligations but failed to do so. As the Superior Court noted in *Kellerman v. MP Corp.*, 392 A.2d 376, 380 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1978), "the court should consider whether the moving party contributed to the delay or prejudice by failing to pursue discovery in a timely manner."

The plaintiff's own delay and strategic choices contributed significantly to the alleged prejudice. He cannot now profit from his own inaction by seeking the extreme remedy of default judgment.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO PROPERLY APPLY THE FACTORS FOR DETERMINING APPROPRIATE SANCTIONS

Pennsylvania courts have consistently held that the trial court must consider multiple factors before imposing discovery sanctions, particularly the severe sanction of default judgment. In *Di Salvatore v. County of Lawrence*, 438 A.2d 1303, 1307 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1981), the Court enumerated the relevant considerations: (1) the nature and severity of the discovery violation; (2) the willfulness of the noncompliance; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded.

The trial court failed to properly apply these factors. Regarding the nature and severity of the violations, while the defendant did fail to appear for multiple depositions, these failures must be understood in context. The defendant's counsel consistently reported an inability to locate the defendant during certain periods. While this does not excuse the conduct, it distinguishes it from cases involving intentional destruction of evidence or deliberate defiance without justification. The defendant never refused to produce documents, never refused to submit to a non-videotaped deposition, and never engaged in any conduct that would have prevented the plaintiff from obtaining his testimony through alternative means.

Regarding willfulness, the record shows that the defendant was difficult to locate and ultimately refused to participate in videotaped depositions. However, the defendant's refusal to be videotaped, while possibly unwise, does not rise to the level of willfulness contemplated by the rules. In *Rasko v. McQuaid*, 634 A.2d 1209, 1212 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1993), the Court defined willfulness as requiring "a knowing and intentional disregard of discovery obligations." The defendant's primary objection was to the format of the deposition, not to the deposition itself. He never refused to provide testimony through other means.

Regarding the possibility of curing prejudice, the trial court failed to consider that the plaintiff could have obtained the defendant's testimony through a non-videotaped deposition at any time after April 2009. The plaintiff chose not to pursue this option, preferring instead to litigate the videotaping issue. This choice should not now justify default judgment.

---

## V. THE SANCTION OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT IS DISPROPORTIONATE TO THE ALLEGED OFFENSE AND VIOLATES DUE PROCESS

The defense submits that the trial court's imposition of default judgment violates basic principles of proportionality and due process. Default judgment is the most severe sanction available to a trial court, effectively terminating the litigation without any determination of the merits. As this Court warned in *Fischer v. Pittsburgh Nat'l Bank*, 376 A.2d at 657, "the drastic remedy of default judgment should be employed only in extreme circumstances and where lesser sanctions have proven inadequate."

The circumstances here do not justify such a drastic remedy. The defendant never denied his identity, never refused to produce documents, and never refused to provide testimony in any format—he only refused to be videotaped. The plaintiff could have obtained the defendant's testimony at any time by agreeing to a non-videotaped deposition, but chose not to do so. The defendant's estate now faces liability for the full value of the plaintiff's claim without any opportunity to defend on the merits, simply because the defendant refused to submit to a particular format of deposition.

This result is particularly unjust given the complete absence of evidence linking the defendant to the cause of the fire. The plaintiff has never established that Mr. Diefenderfer was negligent. He has never established that Mr. Diefenderfer's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. He has never established that Mr. Diefenderfer bore any responsibility for the tragic death of Ms. Plotts. Yet the trial court entered judgment against the defendant's estate based solely on discovery violations, effectively presuming liability without proof.

---

## VI. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO CONSIDER ALTERNATIVE SANCTIONS

The trial court had numerous alternatives to default judgment that would have adequately addressed the discovery violations while preserving the defendant's right to defend on the merits. These alternatives include:

**Monetary Sanctions**: The court could have imposed financial penalties to compel compliance. In *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546, 549 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), the Court noted that monetary sanctions "may serve as an effective deterrent without completely foreclosing the defendant's day in court."

**Adverse Inference Instructions**: The court could have instructed the jury that they may infer that the defendant's testimony would have been unfavorable to his case. This sanction addresses the prejudice to the plaintiff without terminating the litigation.

**Continued Orders with Contempt Provisions**: The court could have continued to order the defendant's deposition while specifying that continued noncompliance would result in incarceration for contempt.

**Dismissal Without Prejudice**: If the plaintiff's claims could not proceed without the defendant's testimony, the court could have dismissed the case without prejudice, allowing the plaintiff to refile if and when the defendant became available.

The trial court failed to consider or explain why these lesser sanctions would have been inadequate. This failure constitutes an abuse of discretion.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's orders dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, and remand this matter for further proceedings. The trial court abused its discretion by imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment without properly considering lesser alternatives, without any evidence establishing the defendant's negligence, and while ignoring the plaintiff's own contribution to the alleged prejudice. The defendant estate is entitled to defend this action on the merits, and the plaintiff should be required to prove his case through evidence rather than through discovery sanctions that effectively presume liability.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

The matter before the Court concerns the appeal of Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, from orders entering default judgment against the estate as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. After reviewing the record, the facts provided, and the arguments of counsel, the Court is tasked with determining whether the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the severe sanction of default judgment pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The authority to impose sanctions for discovery violations lies within the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court will not reverse the decision of a trial court absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. As stated in *Fischer v. Pittsburgh Nat'l Bank*, 376 A.2d 652, 656 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1977), "the choice of a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, but that discretion must be exercised in accordance with the guidelines established by our rules and case law." In determining the appropriate sanction, Pennsylvania courts consider the factors outlined in *Di Salvatore v. County of Lawrence*, 438 A.2d 1303, 1307 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1981): (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness of the noncompliance; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded.

**II. NATURE AND SEVERITY OF THE VIOLATION AND WILLFULNESS**

The record demonstrates a pattern of noncompliance spanning approximately seven years. The defense argues that the violations were not severe because they primarily involved a refusal to be videotaped rather than a refusal to testify, and that the defendant was difficult to locate. However, the record contradicts the assertion that the refusal was limited to the format of the deposition. The facts indicate that by January 28, 2011, defendant’s counsel communicated to plaintiff’s counsel that the defendant stated, "there is no way I’m getting involved," and further asserted, "obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled."

This statement constitutes a categorical refusal to comply with discovery obligations, not merely an objection to videotaping. As this Court held in *Rasko v. McQuaid*, 634 A.2d 1209, 1212 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1993), "willfulness is established when a party knowingly and intentionally disregards discovery obligations." The defendant’s explicit declaration that he would not appear "nor any other time" establishes a knowing and intentional disregard of the court’s authority. Furthermore, the defendant ignored two separate court orders dated July 8, 2009, and August 25, 2010, which specifically compelled his appearance for a videotaped deposition. The severity of the violation is magnified by the defendant’s defiance of these direct court orders.

**III. PREJUDICE TO THE OPPOSING PARTY AND POSSIBILITY OF CURING**

The defense contends that the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, noting a delay from 2007 to 2009 in locating the defendant. While the record supports the fact that the plaintiff did not immediately hire a private investigator, this delay does not absolve the defendant of his obligation to comply with court orders issued in 2009 and 2010. The prejudice to the plaintiff is undeniable. The defendant was the only other person present in the residence at the time of the fire. His death in January 2012 has permanently foreclosed the possibility of obtaining his testimony.

As noted in *In re Estate of Scafidi*, 582 A.2d 601, 604 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990), "when a party’s death renders discovery impossible, prejudice to the opposing party is inherent and irreparable, justifying severe sanctions." The defense argues that the plaintiff could have obtained a non-videotaped deposition, but this ignores the fact that the court had ordered a videotaped deposition. A party cannot unilaterally choose to ignore a court order by offering to comply in a different manner. The defendant’s refusal to comply with the specific orders issued by the court, followed by his death, has created a situation where the prejudice cannot be cured.

**IV. IMPORTANCE OF EVIDENCE AND PROPRIETY OF SANCTION**

The defense argues that the trial court erred because there is no evidence establishing that the defendant was negligent, citing the cause of the fire as an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container. They argue that default judgment is improper where the plaintiff's claim is not clearly established by the record, relying on *Byrd v. Martin*, 403 A.2d 85, 88 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1979). While the plaintiff bears the burden of proof, the defendant’s conduct in refusing to provide testimony—testimony that could have explained his actions regarding the pot or the kerosene—has effectively prevented the plaintiff from meeting that burden or the defendant from rebutting it.

The trial court must balance the need to enforce discovery rules against the severity of the sanction. In *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546, 549 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), the Court held that "a pattern of deliberate noncompliance with discovery obligations justifies the imposition of the most severe sanction where less severe measures have proven ineffective." Here, the trial court attempted to resolve the matter through court orders, which were defied. The defendant’s statement that he would not appear "nor any other time" signaled that lesser sanctions, such as monetary penalties or further continuances, would have been futile. The defendant’s conduct effectively closed the door on the plaintiff’s ability to proceed, leaving the trial court with little recourse but to enter judgment to preserve the integrity of the judicial process.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the *Di Salvatore* factors and the history of the litigation. The defendant’s willful noncompliance, spanning years and culminating in explicit defiance of court orders, combined with the permanent loss of critical evidence due to his death, justifies the sanction imposed. While the defense correctly identifies that default judgment is a drastic remedy, the defendant’s actions left the trial court with no viable alternative to address the irreparable prejudice caused to the plaintiff.

Accordingly, the preliminary ruling of this Court is that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the defendant. The orders of the trial court are affirmed.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENT REGARDING THE AVAILABILITY OF LESSER SANCTIONS

The plaintiff argues that lesser sanctions were unavailable because the defendant expressed an unwillingness to appear for any deposition. This argument, while superficially appealing, ignores fundamental principles of Pennsylvania discovery law and the actual record before this Court.

Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019(c) provides a graduated scale of sanctions available to trial courts:

> "(c) Motion for Sanctions. The court may, on motion, make an order imposing any of the sanctions listed in subdivision (a) or (b) or both unless the failure to comply with the rule or the order was reasonable, or unless the moving party has failed to exercise due diligence to obtain the discovery sought."

The list of sanctions in Rule 4019(a) includes, in ascending order of severity: (1) an order that the matters to which the discovery relates shall be taken to be established for purposes of the action; (2) an order prohibiting the party from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses; (3) an order striking out pleadings or parts thereof; (4) an order staying further proceedings until compliance is achieved; (5) an order dismissing the action; and (6) an order entering default judgment against the disobedient party. The plaintiff cites *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), for the proposition that severe sanctions are justified when lesser measures have proven ineffective. Here, the record does not establish that the trial court ever held Mr. Diefenderfer in contempt, nor does it establish that the court ever imposed monetary sanctions or other intermediate remedies before resorting to the extreme measure of default judgment.

The defense further notes that the trial court could have entered an order, pursuant to Rule 4019(a)(1), that certain facts be deemed established for purposes of the action. For example, the court could have ordered that Mr. Diefenderfer was present in the residence on the night of the fire, without entering default judgment. This would have given the plaintiff the benefit of that inference while still requiring the plaintiff to prove the essential elements of his negligence claim.

The plaintiff's argument that lesser sanctions were "futile" because Mr. Diefenderfer expressed unwillingness to appear assumes that the defendant had the unilateral authority to dictate the terms of compliance with court orders. This assumption is incorrect. A trial court possesses inherent authority to enforce its orders through contempt proceedings, and the failure to utilize this authority constitutes an abuse of discretion. *See Commonwealth v. Baker*, 432 A.2d 558 (Pa. 1981) (discussing the court's inherent contempt powers).

The defense submits that the trial court erred in failing to consider and implement intermediate sanctions before resorting to default judgment, and that this Court should reverse on that basis.

## V. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENT REGARDING THE PROPRIETY OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The plaintiff argues that default judgment was appropriate because Mr. Diefenderfer's conduct prevented the plaintiff from proving his case. The defense submits that this argument conflates the purpose of discovery sanctions with the merits of the underlying claim.

Default judgment as a discovery sanction is a drastic remedy that should be imposed only in extreme cases. *See Fischer v. Pittsburgh Nat'l Bank*, 376 A.2d 652, 657 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1977). The purpose of such sanctions is to enforce compliance with discovery rules, not to adjudicate the merits of the underlying controversy. *See Byers v. McCune*, 533 A.2d 22 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1987) (holding that discovery sanctions are remedial, not punitive).

Here, the trial court entered default judgment against Mr. Diefenderfer without any hearing on the merits of the plaintiff's negligence claim. The record contains no evidence establishing that Mr. Diefenderfer was negligent, that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire, or that he bears any responsibility for the death of Tina Marie Plotts. The fire was caused by an unattended pot on an electric stove and was exacerbated by a defective kerosene container—conditions over which Mr. Diefenderfer exercised no control.

The plaintiff argues that default judgment is appropriate because Mr. Diefenderfer "deprived the plaintiff of the means to prove liability." This argument improperly shifts the burden of proof. Under Pennsylvania law, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving each element of his negligence claim by a preponderance of the evidence. *See Wagner v. Pittsburgh-Corning Corp.*, 728 A.2d 1134 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1999). The plaintiff cannot satisfy this burden by demonstrating that the defendant failed to provide evidence; rather, the plaintiff must affirmatively establish each element of his claim through admissible evidence.

The defense notes that the plaintiff has not explained what testimony Mr. Diefenderfer could have provided that would have established his negligence. The plaintiff speculates that Mr. Diefenderfer could have explained "his actions regarding the pot or the kerosene," but there is no evidence in the record establishing that Mr. Diefenderfer had any involvement with either the pot or the kerosene. To the contrary, the record indicates that Mr. Diefenderfer was asleep on the couch at the time the fire started—a fact that would have been favorable to his defense.

The plaintiff's argument also ignores the availability of other evidence. The landlords owned the premises and were responsible for maintaining the stove and the kerosene container. The plaintiff could have deposed these witnesses, obtained maintenance records, and retained experts to inspect the equipment. The plaintiff's failure to pursue these alternative discovery avenues does not constitute prejudice attributable to Mr. Diefenderfer.

The defense further notes that the trial court's entry of default judgment effectively adjudicates liability without any evidence. This result is fundamentally unfair and contrary to the principles underlying our justice system. A defendant is entitled to contest the merits of a claim against him, and default judgment should not be used to deprive him of this right absent clear and convincing evidence of a discovery violation that has caused irreparable prejudice. *See Di Salvatore v. County of Lawrence*, 438 A.2d 1303 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1981).

2. The plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in pursuing discovery and cannot therefore claim irreparable prejudice;

3. Mr. Diefenderfer's conduct, while regrettable, did not rise to the level of culpability warranting the extreme sanction of default judgment;

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

The matter before the Court is the appeal of Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, from orders entering default judgment against the estate as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. Having reviewed the rebuttal arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court must determine whether the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the severe sanction of default judgment pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The authority to impose sanctions for discovery violations lies within the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court will not reverse the decision of a trial court absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. As stated in *Fischer v. Pittsburgh Nat'l Bank*, 376 A.2d 652, 656 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1977), "the choice of a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, but that discretion must be exercised in accordance with the guidelines established by our rules and case law." In determining the appropriate sanction, Pennsylvania courts consider the factors outlined in *Di Salvatore v. County of Lawrence*, 438 A.2d 1303, 1307 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1981): (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness of the noncompliance; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded.

**II. NATURE AND SEVERITY OF THE VIOLATION AND WILLFULNESS**

The defense argues that the trial court erred in characterizing the defendant’s conduct as a "deliberate obstruction," suggesting instead that the refusal was limited to the method of recording (videotaping) and stemmed from frustration rather than culpability. The defense relies on *In re Contempt of McBride*, 560 A.2d 733 (Pa. 1989), to argue that civil contempt, rather than default judgment, was the appropriate remedy for such disobedience.

The Court is not persuaded by this characterization. While the defendant initially objected to being videotaped, the record clearly establishes that this objection evolved into a categorical refusal to comply with any discovery obligation. By letter dated January 28, 2011, defendant’s counsel communicated to plaintiff’s counsel that the defendant stated, "there is no way I’m getting involved," and further asserted, "obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled." This statement constitutes a total repudiation of the court's authority, not merely a dispute over procedural format.

As this Court held in *Rasko v. McQuaid*, 634 A.2d 1209, 1212 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1993), "willfulness is established when a party knowingly and intentionally disregards discovery obligations." The defendant’s explicit declaration that he would not appear "nor any other time" establishes a knowing and intentional disregard. Furthermore, the defendant ignored two separate court orders dated July 8, 2009, and August 25, 2010. The defense’s argument that the defendant was merely a frustrated layperson does not excuse a willful violation of court orders spanning several years.

**III. PREJUDICE TO THE OPPOSING PARTY AND PLAINTIFF’S DILIGENCE**

The defense contends that the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, citing an eighteen-month delay between the initial request in 2007 and the hiring of a private investigator in 2009. The defense relies on *Jennings v. Rodriguez*, 518 A.2d 1243, 1247 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1986), to argue that this lack of diligence bars the extreme sanction of default judgment. Additionally, the defense argues that the prejudice is speculative because the defendant was asleep at the time of the fire and because other witnesses, such as the landlords, were available for deposition.

While the plaintiff’s delay in 2007 is noted, it does not absolve the defendant of his obligation to comply with court orders issued in 2009 and 2010. Once the defendant was located and served with a subpoena, his refusal to appear became the sole cause of the discovery impasse. The prejudice to the plaintiff is undeniable. The defendant was the only other person present in the residence at the time of the fire. His death in January 2012 has permanently foreclosed the possibility of obtaining his testimony.

The defense’s suggestion that the plaintiff could have relied on the landlords’ testimony is unpersuasive. The landlords were not present during the fire; only the defendant could speak to his actions, his location, and the condition of the premises immediately prior to the fire’s ignition. As noted in *In re Estate of Scafidi*, 582 A.2d 601, 604 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990), "when a party’s death renders discovery impossible, prejudice to the opposing party is inherent and irreparable, justifying severe sanctions." The defendant’s refusal to comply, followed by his death, has created a situation where the prejudice cannot be cured.

**IV. POSSIBILITY OF CURING PREJUDICE THROUGH LESSER SANCTIONS**

The defense argues that the trial court abused its discretion by skipping intermediate sanctions, such as monetary fines or contempt proceedings, and by failing to consider an order precluding the defendant from offering evidence. The defense cites *Commonwealth v. Baker*, 432 A.2d 558 (Pa. 1981), regarding the court's inherent contempt powers, and *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), to argue that severe sanctions are only justified when lesser measures have proven ineffective.

The Court finds that the record supports the conclusion that lesser sanctions would have been futile. The trial court did issue orders compelling the defendant’s appearance, which were defied. The defendant’s statement that he would not appear "nor any other time" signaled that no amount of coercion, including monetary penalties or the threat of contempt, would induce his compliance. As held in *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546, 549 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), "a pattern of deliberate noncompliance with discovery obligations justifies the imposition of the most severe sanction where less severe measures have proven ineffective." Here, the defendant’s categorical refusal rendered intermediate sanctions ineffective. The trial court was not required to engage in a ritualistic ascent through the ladder of sanctions when the defendant had already made clear that he would not participate under any circumstances.

**V. IMPORTANCE OF EVIDENCE AND PROPRIETY OF SANCTION**

The defense argues that the trial court erred because there is no evidence establishing that the defendant was negligent, relying on *Byrd v. Martin*, 403 A.2d 85, 88 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1979), for the proposition that default judgment requires the moving party's case to be clearly established by the record. The defense contends that the fire was caused by an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container, conditions over which the defendant had no control.

The Court acknowledges that default judgment is a drastic remedy. However, the defendant’s conduct in refusing to provide testimony—testimony that could have explained his actions regarding the pot or the kerosene—has effectively prevented the plaintiff from meeting the burden of proof. In *Miller v. Borden*, the Court recognized that where a defendant’s conduct prevents the plaintiff from proving his case, the sanction of default judgment is appropriate to address the inequity caused by that conduct. The trial court’s sanction was not a substitute for proof of negligence on the merits, but a response to the defendant’s refusal to provide the only evidence that could establish or rebut liability.

The defense’s reliance on *Byrd v. Martin* is misplaced in this context. *Byrd* generally requires that the moving party's right to relief is clear before entering default judgment for failure to plead or defend. However, where the default judgment is entered as a sanction for discovery abuse that has prejudiced the plaintiff's ability to prove his case, the focus shifts to the defendant's misconduct. The defendant cannot benefit from his own wrongdoing by arguing that the plaintiff failed to prove a case that the defendant made impossible to prove.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the *Di Salvatore* factors and the history of the litigation. The defendant’s willful noncompliance, spanning years and culminating in explicit defiance of court orders, combined with the permanent loss of critical evidence due to his death, justifies the sanction imposed. The defense’s arguments regarding the availability of lesser sanctions and the plaintiff’s diligence, while well-founded in principle, are insufficient to overcome the record of categorical refusal and irreparable prejudice. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the defendant.

Accordingly, the ruling of this Court is that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the defendant. The orders of the trial court are affirmed.

/s/ The Judge

The record establishes that plaintiff's counsel first requested deposition dates by letter dated June 17, 2007, but did not hire a private investigator to locate the defendant until January 7, 2009—an eighteen-month delay during which the defendant remained essentially unlocatable according to defense counsel. Ct. 1986), this Court recognized that the plaintiff's diligence in pursuing discovery is a relevant factor in determining whether severe sanctions are appropriate. The plaintiff's failure to diligently pursue the defendant's location during this period contributed directly to the situation wherein the defendant's deposition could not be completed before his death.

The defense further notes that even after the defendant was located in 2009, the plaintiff insisted on videotaping the deposition despite the defendant's categorical objection to this method. While the plaintiff was entitled to seek a court order compelling the defendant's attendance, and while the defendant was ultimately required to submit to a videotaped deposition pursuant to court orders, the plaintiff's insistence on this particular method contributed to the litigation delay. The defendant's objection to videotaping, while ultimately overruled, was not frivolous or made in bad faith. The plaintiff could have proceeded with a non-videotaped deposition, which the defendant might have attended, thereby preserving his testimony for subsequent proceedings. The plaintiff's strategic choice to insist on videotaping, in the face of the defendant's objection, contributed to the breakdown in the discovery process.

The plaintiff cannot now be heard to complain of prejudice when the defendant's death rendered discovery impossible, when the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in locating the defendant and when the plaintiff's own litigation decisions contributed to the delay in obtaining his testimony. The defense submits that the *Di Salvatore* factor regarding the plaintiff's diligence weighs heavily in the defense's favor and demonstrates that the extreme sanction of default judgment was unwarranted.

## III. THE PREJUDICE TO THE PLAINTIFF IS SPECULATIVE AND NOT SUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The defense submits that the plaintiff has failed to demonstrate the kind of concrete, irreparable prejudice necessary to justify the severe sanction of default judgment. The plaintiff argues that the defendant was the only other person present in the residence at the time of the fire and that his testimony was essential to proving the underlying negligence claim. However, the record does not support the conclusion that the defendant possessed information that would have established the plaintiff's case or that the absence of his testimony has prevented the plaintiff from meeting its burden of proof.

The cause and origin of the fire were determined by fire officials to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove, with rapid progression and expansion caused by kerosene leaking from a defective container stored near the stove. These conditions—specifically, the presence of an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container—were not within the defendant's control or knowledge. The defendant was asleep on the first floor couch at the time the fire originated in the kitchen. He had consumed alcohol and was unaware of the fire's inception. His testimony, even if preserved, would likely have established that he was asleep, that he did not place the pot on the stove, that he did not know of the defective kerosene container, and that he was not negligent in any manner that caused the fire to start or spread.

The plaintiff has produced no expert testimony, no physical evidence, and no eyewitness accounts establishing that the defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The landlords, who owned the premises, were aware of or in control of the condition of the stove and the storage of the kerosene container. The plaintiff has not established that the defendant had any responsibility for the maintenance of the stove or the storage of hazardous materials. Under these circumstances, the prejudice claimed by the plaintiff is speculative at best. The defendant's testimony would not have supplied the missing elements of the plaintiff's case because the defendant likely possessed no knowledge that would have established liability.

In *Byrd v. Martin*, 403 A.2d 85, 88 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1979), this Court held that default judgment requires the moving party's right to relief to be "clearly established by the record." The plaintiff has not established—and cannot establish without speculation—that the defendant was negligent or that his conduct caused the fire. The trial court improperly adjudicated the merits of the underlying negligence claim by entering default judgment, effectively finding the defendant liable without any evidence of negligence having been presented. This result violates fundamental principles of due process and exceeds the authority granted by Pennsylvania's discovery rules.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN ADJUDICATING THE MERITS OF THE NEGLIGENCE CLAIM THROUGH THE SANCTION OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The defense submits that the trial court improperly used the discovery sanction of default judgment as a substitute for a trial on the merits of the underlying negligence claim. Default judgment pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019 is a procedural sanction designed to compel compliance with discovery obligations, not a mechanism for adjudicating the substantive merits of a claim. The trial court's reasoning—that the defendant's refusal to provide testimony prevented the plaintiff from proving his case—improperly conflates the discovery sanction with an adjudication of liability.

The plaintiff bears the burden of proving each element of negligence, including duty, breach, causation, and damages, by a preponderance of the evidence. The defendant was not required to prove his innocence; the plaintiff was required to prove the defendant's negligence. The cause and origin investigation established that the fire was caused by an unattended pot on an electric stove and by a defective kerosene container. Neither of these conditions was shown to be the result of the defendant's conduct. The plaintiff has never established that the defendant placed the pot on the stove, that the defendant was responsible for the defective kerosene container, or that the defendant had any duty regarding these conditions.

The trial court's entry of default judgment effectively relieved the plaintiff of its burden of proof and adjudicated the negligence claim in the plaintiff's favor without any evidence of negligence having been presented. This result is contrary to the nature and purpose of discovery sanctions and violates the fundamental principle that liability must be proven, not presumed. The defense submits that the proper remedy for the defendant's discovery violations, if any, was a sanction that compelled compliance or compensated the plaintiff for the loss of the defendant's testimony—not an award of judgment on the merits of the underlying claim.

---

## V. THE DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT, WHILE CULPABLE, DID NOT RISE TO THE LEVEL OF WILLFUL OBSTRUCTION WARRANTING DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The defense acknowledges that the defendant's conduct in failing to appear for depositions was improper and that his categorical statement that he would not appear "nor any other time" demonstrated a willful disregard for the trial court's authority. However, the defense submits that this conduct, while deserving of sanction, did not rise to the level of willfulness contemplated by Pennsylvania law for the imposition of default judgment.

The defendant was a layperson without legal training who was thrust into the role of defendant in a wrongful death lawsuit arising from a tragic fire. He was not a sophisticated litigant engaging in strategic obstruction; he was a frustrated individual who believed that the plaintiff's demands were excessive and unnecessary. His statement that he would not appear for depositions was made in frustration and without appreciation for the legal consequences of his position. While this does not excuse his noncompliance, it distinguishes his conduct from cases where parties have deliberately and strategically refused to comply with discovery obligations in order to gain a litigation advantage.

The trial court's characterization of the defendant's conduct as a "total repudiation of the court's authority" is hyperbolic and unsupported by the record. The defendant did not seek to undermine the judicial process; he sought to avoid what he perceived as an intrusive and unnecessary requirement. His conduct, while improper, was the product of frustration and misunderstanding rather than deliberate obstruction. The trial court abused its discretion in failing to consider this distinction when imposing the severe sanction of default judgment.

---

## VI. THE DEFENSE'S KEY POINTS AND CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court consider the following key points in determining whether the trial court abused its discretion:

**First**, the trial court failed to meaningfully consider or attempt lesser sanctions before imposing default judgment. Monetary sanctions, evidentiary preclusions, or contempt proceedings were available and may have been effective in compelling the defendant's compliance or in addressing any prejudice suffered by the plaintiff.

**Second**, the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence in locating the defendant for deposition, contributing to the circumstances that led to the permanent loss of his testimony. The plaintiff's eighteen-month delay in hiring a private investigator, and its insistence on videotaping despite the defendant's objection, contributed to the discovery impasse.

**Third**, the prejudice to the plaintiff is speculative and not sufficient to support default judgment. The plaintiff has not established that the defendant possessed information that would have established liability, and the cause and origin investigation does not suggest that the defendant was negligent.

**Fourth**, the trial court improperly adjudicated the merits of the underlying negligence claim through the sanction of default judgment, effectively relieving the plaintiff of its burden of proof without any evidence of negligence having been presented.

**Fifth**, the defendant's conduct, while improper, did not rise to the level of willful obstruction contemplated by Pennsylvania law for the imposition of the most severe sanction available.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court and remand this matter for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. The defense submits that the appropriate remedy for the defendant's discovery violations is a sanction less severe than default judgment, such as monetary sanctions or evidentiary preclusions, rather than an adjudication of liability on the merits of the underlying negligence claim.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

The matter before the Court is the appeal of Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, from orders entering default judgment against the estate as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court must determine whether the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the severe sanction of default judgment pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The authority to impose sanctions for discovery violations lies within the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court will not reverse the decision of a trial court absent an abuse of discretion or an error of law. As stated in *Fischer v. Pittsburgh Nat'l Bank*, 376 A.2d 652, 656 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1977), "the choice of a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, but that discretion must be exercised in accordance with the guidelines established by our rules and case law." In determining the appropriate sanction, Pennsylvania courts consider the factors outlined in *Di Salvatore v. County of Lawrence*, 438 A.2d 1303, 1307 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1981): (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness of the noncompliance; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing the prejudice through lesser sanctions; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought to be precluded.

**II. NATURE AND SEVERITY OF THE VIOLATION AND WILLFULNESS**

The defense argues that the trial court erred in characterizing the defendant’s conduct as a "deliberate obstruction," suggesting instead that the refusal was limited to the method of recording (videotaping) and stemmed from frustration rather than culpability. The defense relies on *In re Contempt of McBride*, 560 A.2d 733 (Pa. 1989), to argue that civil contempt, rather than default judgment, was the appropriate remedy for such disobedience.

The Court is not persuaded by this characterization. While the defendant initially objected to being videotaped, the record clearly establishes that this objection evolved into a categorical refusal to comply with any discovery obligation. By letter dated January 28, 2011, defendant’s counsel communicated to plaintiff’s counsel that the defendant stated, "there is no way I’m getting involved," and further asserted, "obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled." This statement constitutes a total repudiation of the court's authority, not merely a dispute over procedural format.

As this Court held in *Rasko v. McQuaid*, 634 A.2d 1209, 1212 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1993), "willfulness is established when a party knowingly and intentionally disregards discovery obligations." The defendant’s explicit declaration that he would not appear "nor any other time" establishes a knowing and intentional disregard. Furthermore, the defendant ignored two separate court orders dated July 8, 2009, and August 25, 2010. The defense’s argument that the defendant was merely a frustrated layperson does not excuse a willful violation of court orders spanning several years.

**III. PREJUDICE TO THE OPPOSING PARTY AND PLAINTIFF’S DILIGENCE**

The defense contends that the plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, citing an eighteen-month delay between the initial request in 2007 and the hiring of a private investigator in 2009. The defense relies on *Jennings v. Rodriguez*, 518 A.2d 1243, 1247 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1986), to argue that this lack of diligence bars the extreme sanction of default judgment. Additionally, the defense argues that the prejudice is speculative because the defendant was asleep at the time of the fire and because other witnesses, such as the landlords, were available for deposition.

While the plaintiff’s delay in 2007 is noted, it does not absolve the defendant of his obligation to comply with court orders issued in 2009 and 2010. The record indicates that defense counsel represented that they could not locate the defendant during the initial period, which mitigates the plaintiff's delay. Once the defendant was located and served with a subpoena, his refusal to appear became the sole cause of the discovery impasse. The prejudice to the plaintiff is undeniable. The defendant was the only other person present in the residence at the time of the fire. His death in January 2012 has permanently foreclosed the possibility of obtaining his testimony.

The defense’s suggestion that the plaintiff could have relied on the landlords’ testimony is unpersuasive. The landlords were not present during the fire; only the defendant could speak to his actions, his location, and the condition of the premises immediately prior to the fire’s ignition. As noted in *In re Estate of Scafidi*, 582 A.2d 601, 604 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1990), "when a party’s death renders discovery impossible, prejudice to the opposing party is inherent and irreparable, justifying severe sanctions." The defendant’s refusal to comply, followed by his death, has created a situation where the prejudice cannot be cured. The defense's argument that the defendant's testimony would have been exculpatory because he was asleep is speculative; the plaintiff was entitled to discover the extent of the defendant's knowledge regarding the premises and the fire's origin.

**IV. POSSIBILITY OF CURING PREJUDICE THROUGH LESSER SANCTIONS**

The defense argues that the trial court abused its discretion by skipping intermediate sanctions, such as monetary fines or contempt proceedings, and by failing to consider an order precluding the defendant from offering evidence. The defense cites *Commonwealth v. Baker*, 432 A.2d 558 (Pa. 1981), regarding the court's inherent contempt powers, and *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), to argue that severe sanctions are only justified when lesser measures have proven ineffective.

The Court finds that the record supports the conclusion that lesser sanctions would have been futile. The trial court did issue orders compelling the defendant’s appearance, which were defied. The defendant’s statement that he would not appear "nor any other time" signaled that no amount of coercion, including monetary penalties or the threat of contempt, would induce his compliance. As held in *Miller v. Borden*, 588 A.2d 546, 549 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), "a pattern of deliberate noncompliance with discovery obligations justifies the imposition of the most severe sanction where less severe measures have proven ineffective." Here, the defendant’s categorical refusal rendered intermediate sanctions ineffective. The trial court was not required to engage in a ritualistic ascent through the ladder of sanctions when the defendant had already made clear that he would not participate under any circumstances.

**V. IMPORTANCE OF EVIDENCE AND PROPRIETY OF SANCTION**

The defense argues that the trial court erred because there is no evidence establishing that the defendant was negligent, relying on *Byrd v. Martin*, 403 A.2d 85, 88 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1979), for the proposition that default judgment requires the moving party's case to be clearly established by the record. The defense contends that the fire was caused by an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container, conditions over which the defendant had no control.

The Court acknowledges that default judgment is a drastic remedy. However, the defendant’s conduct in refusing to provide testimony—testimony that could have explained his actions regarding the pot or the kerosene—has effectively prevented the plaintiff from meeting the burden of proof. In *Miller v. Borden*, the Court recognized that where a defendant’s conduct prevents the plaintiff from proving his case, the sanction of default judgment is appropriate to address the inequity caused by that conduct. The trial court’s sanction was not a substitute for proof of negligence on the merits, but a response to the defendant’s refusal to provide the only evidence that could establish or rebut liability.

The defense’s reliance on *Byrd v. Martin* is misplaced in this context. *Byrd* generally requires that the moving party's right to relief is clear before entering default judgment for failure to plead or defend. However, where the default judgment is entered as a sanction for discovery abuse that has prejudiced the plaintiff's ability to prove his case, the focus shifts to the defendant's misconduct. The defendant cannot benefit from his own wrongdoing by arguing that the plaintiff failed to prove a case that the defendant made impossible to prove.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the *Di Salvatore* factors and the history of the litigation. The defendant’s willful noncompliance, spanning years and culminating in explicit defiance of court orders, combined with the permanent loss of critical evidence due to his death, justifies the sanction imposed. The defense’s arguments regarding the availability of lesser sanctions and the plaintiff’s diligence, while well-founded in principle, are insufficient to overcome the record of categorical refusal and irreparable prejudice. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the defendant.

Accordingly, the ruling of this Court is that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the defendant. The orders of the trial court are affirmed.
