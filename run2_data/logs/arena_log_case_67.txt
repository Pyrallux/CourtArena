### OPENING ARGUMENT FOR THE STATE  

May it please the Court:  

Today, this Court faces a fundamental question of Louisiana law: **Can a man who has never been judicially established as the legal father of a child—yet has paid child support under an unrelated obligation—seek reimbursement from a third party who has never been legally declared the biological father?** The answer is unequivocally **no**.  

The trial court correctly dismissed J.M.Y.’s suit for reimbursement on the dual grounds of *no right of action* and *no cause of action*. This Court should affirm that dismissal. J.M.Y. seeks to bypass Louisiana’s mandatory legal framework for establishing paternity and impose liability on R.R. for child support payments without any judicial determination that R.R. is even the biological father—let alone the legal obligor. Such a claim is legally impermissible.  

#### I. J.M.Y. LACKS A RIGHT OF ACTION BECAUSE REIMBURSEMENT REQUIRES PRIOR JUDICIAL ESTABLISHMENT OF PATERNITY  
Louisiana law is unambiguous: **A claim for reimbursement of child support payments cannot exist unless the alleged biological father has first been judicially declared the parent.** As this Court held in *LeBlanc v. LeBlanc*, 93-1799 (La. 10/17/94), 643 So.2d 1253, a presumed father (like J.M.Y.) has "no right of action against a biological father for reimbursement of child support payments unless the biological father has first been judicially determined to be the father." *Id.* at 1257. The *LeBlanc* Court emphasized that "the obligation to support a child arises only after paternity has been established by a court of competent jurisdiction." *Id.* at 1256.  

Here, J.M.Y.’s petition alleges only that R.R. "was the biological father of the child"—but it contains **no judicial decree establishing R.R.’s paternity**. The trial court properly dismissed the suit because, as a matter of law, J.M.Y. has no right to seek reimbursement from a party who has never been adjudicated a parent. Louisiana’s civil code does not create a freestanding reimbursement right for presumed fathers against alleged biological fathers. *See* La. Civ. Code art. 227 (defining parental obligations, but requiring judicial establishment of parentage to trigger those obligations).  

#### II. J.M.Y. FAILS TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION BECAUSE REIMBURSEMENT REQUIRES A PRE-EXISTING LEGAL OBLIGATION  
J.M.Y.’s petition also fails to state a cause of action. To state a claim for reimbursement, a plaintiff must allege facts showing that the defendant had a **pre-existing legal duty to support the child**—a duty that arises only after paternity is judicially established. *See* *LeBlanc*, 643 So.2d at 1256 ("[T]he obligation to support is not self-executing; it must be established by a court").  

Here, J.M.Y. makes no such allegation. His petition states only that R.R. "was the biological father"—but it does not claim that a court ever declared R.R. the legal father, nor does it allege that R.R. ever acknowledged paternity. Without that foundational step, there is no "obligation to support" under Article 227 to which J.M.Y. could seek reimbursement. As the trial court recognized, J.M.Y.’s petition "fails to allege facts demonstrating that R.R. had a legal duty to support the child," rendering the claim legally insufficient.  

#### III. LA. CIV. CODE ART. 227 DOES NOT CREATE A REIMBURSEMENT RIGHT BETWEEN NON-PARENTS  
J.M.Y. argues that Article 227’s general duty of support "applies equally to biological and presumed fathers," but this misreads the law. Article 227 imposes obligations on *legal parents*—not on alleged biological fathers who have never been judicially recognized. The provision does not create a private right of action for reimbursement between non-parents. To the contrary, Louisiana courts have consistently held that **reimbursement claims are barred absent a prior judicial determination of paternity**.  

For example, in *LeBlanc*, the Supreme Court rejected a reimbursement claim against a biological father precisely because "no court had ever determined [the defendant] to be the father." *LeBlanc*, 643 So.2d at 1257. The Court explained that "the right to reimbursement is derivative of the right to support"—meaning it cannot exist without a prior adjudication establishing the obligation to support. *Id.* at 1256. Here, J.M.Y. seeks reimbursement from R.R. without any such adjudication. That is legally impossible.  

#### CONCLUSION  
J.M.Y. cannot circumvent Louisiana’s statutory and jurisprudential requirements for establishing paternity. He has no right to reimbursement from R.R. because R.R. has never been judicially declared the child’s father. He has no cause of action because his petition fails to allege a pre-existing legal duty to support. The trial court’s dismissal was correct as a matter of law. This Court should affirm.

**The Right of Action Doctrine in Louisiana**

Under Louisiana law, the exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the subject matter of the proceeding or whether he has a legal capacity to proceed therein. *See* La. Code Civ. Proc. art. 927(5); *Hampton v. Bordenave*, 2001-2361 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/21/02), 822 So.2d 736, 739. The Supreme Court held that the presumed father "has no right of action against a biological father for reimbursement of child support payments unless the biological father has first been judicially determined to be the father." *Id.* at 1257.

The Court in *LeBlanc* explained that "the obligation to support a child arises only after paternity has been established by a court of competent jurisdiction." *Id.* at 1256. **The Cause of Action Doctrine in Louisiana**

The petition must set forth facts giving rise to a legal remedy. *Id.* All facts alleged in the petition must be accepted as true, but the court is not required to accept as true conclusions of law or fact unsupported by specific facts. *See* *Kuebler v. **The Essential Elements of a Reimbursement Claim**

The right to reimbursement is derivative of the right to support—it cannot exist without a prior adjudication establishing the obligation to support. *Id.*

Civ. Code art. 227; *LeBlanc*, 643 So.2d at 1256.

Because J.M.Y.'s petition fails to allege that R.R. or A.F.Y. had a pre-existing legal duty to support the child, the petition fails to state a cause of action for reimbursement. The trial court correctly granted the exception of no cause of action.

J.M.Y.'s petition contains no allegation of any judicial determination of paternity. Without such a determination, there is no legal obligation to support, and therefore no basis for a reimbursement claim.
