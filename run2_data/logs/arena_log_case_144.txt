Discussion

[14] The issue is whether the trial court erred or abused its discretion in permitting Casey's statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. Generally, we review the trial court's ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State , 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State , 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We may affirm a trial court's decision regarding the admission of evidence if it is sustainable on any basis in the record. Barker v. State , 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998), reh'g denied . A trial court "has the duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted" before it, Garcia v. State , 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), and "is given wide latitude of discretion in carrying out [its] duties." Pitman v. State , 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

[15] Casey cites Ind. Evidence Rule 403 and asserts that the unsolicited presentation of the duplicitous and cumulative evidence deprived him of his right to a fair trial. He argues that Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 outlines strict circumstances when a jury may be entitled to receive duplicitous or cumulative evidence and none of the circumstances were present in his case.

[16] The State argues the trial court properly exercised its discretion by replaying Casey's statement to police in response to jury questions. It asserts that the trial court reasonably exercised its discretion to replay the evidence based on the multiple jury questions asking about the content of the already played video and other concerns regarding audio problems and redaction. The State also contends that Casey has not shown substantial prejudice.

[17] Initially, we note that Casey did not specifically mention Ind. Evidence Rule 403 in his objection to the replaying of the video at trial. Ind. Evidence Rule 403 provides: "The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." To the extent the objection by Casey's counsel that the replaying was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over," falls under Rule 403, we will address the issue.3 Transcript Volume II at 45.

[18] The trial court replayed Casey's recorded statement due to multiple questions from the jury and a juror's inability to hear. We have previously held that, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State , 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied . We cannot say that Casey has demonstrated that replaying his statement to police would not aid the jury.  Under these circumstances, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion. See Chambers v. State , 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981) (holding that, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it); see also Harris v. State , 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995) (holding that the trial court did not abuse its discretion by sending exhibits and a tape player to the jury room when the jury began its deliberations where the audio taped confession served to aid the jury in its attempt to understand to what extent defendant participated in the charged crimes and how the crimes were committed, and there was little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court).

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Standard of Review

The defense does not dispute the standard of review. This Court reviews the trial court's ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied. A trial court's decision constitutes an abuse of discretion only when it is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied.

### B. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Replaying Casey's Statement

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court's decision to replay Casey's recorded statement was an abuse of discretion under the specific facts and circumstances of this case. While the prosecution correctly notes that trial courts have broad discretion in managing proceedings, that discretion must be exercised in a manner consistent with the defendant's constitutional right to a fair trial and the rules of evidence.

#### 1. The Replay Was Unnecessarily Cumulative Evidence

Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence "may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence."

The defense objected specifically on the ground that the replay was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Transcript Volume II at 45. This objection, though not citing Rule 403 by number, was sufficient to preserve the issue for appellate review. The prosecution's own brief acknowledges that the statement had already been played once during Detective Leitze's testimony. The replay therefore presented the same evidence a second time—a textbook example of cumulative evidence.

The prosecution attempts to distinguish this case by arguing that the replay was necessary to address juror questions about audibility. However, the record does not support the conclusion that a full replay was necessary. The trial court had several less prejudicial alternatives available: it could have directed the witness to answer the specific questions posed by jurors, it could have permitted the juror who claimed inability to hear to specify which portions she missed, or it could have played only the specific segments at issue.

Instead, the trial court ordered the "entire video" replayed. Transcript Volume II at 54. This was the most cumulative and prejudicial option available. The prosecution's reliance on Lewis v. State is misplaced. In Lewis, the Court noted that "it is essential that jurors be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied. However, the Lewis Court was addressing a situation where testimony had not been heard at all—not a situation where testimony had already been played in full and the jury simply had questions about its content.

#### 2. The Trial Court's Discretion Was Exercised in a Manner That Prejudiced Casey

The defense further asserts that even if the trial court had discretion to address juror concerns about audibility, it abused that discretion by choosing the most prejudicial method available.

The prosecution argues that Casey cannot show "substantial prejudice." This burden, however, shifts the inquiry improperly. Under Evidence Rule 403, the question is whether the probative value is "substantially outweighed" by the danger of cumulative evidence—not whether the defendant can demonstrate prejudice sufficient to satisfy an elevated standard. Here, the probative value of replaying a statement already heard was minimal, while the danger of cumulative effect was substantial.

The prosecution cites Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), for the proposition that replaying a tape "at most" constitutes cumulative evidence. This argument actually supports the defense. The Chambers Court found no error only after noting the specific circumstances of that case—noting, importantly, that the replay was of limited duration and served to ensure all jurors could hear the testimony. Here, by contrast, the trial court replayed the "entire video" despite the fact that only one juror claimed difficulty hearing—and her concern appeared to relate to a specific answer about whose bedroom the shotgun was in, not the entire statement.

#### 3. Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 Does Not Foreclose Review

The prosecution contends that Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is inapplicable because the replay occurred during trial testimony rather than during jury deliberations. The defense agrees that the statute, which governs read-back requests during deliberations, does not directly apply. However, this does not mean the trial court was free to replay evidence without constraint. The rules of evidence—particularly Evidence Rule 403—apply throughout trial, and the trial court's authority to manage proceedings does not override those rules.

The prosecution's argument effectively contends that any time a trial court decides to replay evidence in response to juror questions, no rule of evidence can constrain that decision. Such a reading renders Evidence Rule 403 meaningless during the presentation of evidence—precisely when its protections are most needed.

### C. The Error Was Not Harmless

Having established that the trial court abused its discretion, the defense must demonstrate that this error was not harmless. The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the error did not affect the substantial rights of the defendant. Here, the prosecution cannot carry this burden.

Casey's entire defense rested on his claim that his incriminating statements in the recorded interview were false—he testified that he lied because he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Transcript Volume II at 56. He further explained that he had recently smoked spice, which affected his judgment. The replay of his recorded statement—containing those very incriminating statements—occurred after the defense had presented its theory of the case and served to reinforce the prosecution's version of events in the minds of jurors who had already heard the recording once.

The cumulative effect of this replay cannot be considered harmless. The prosecution's case depended entirely on establishing that Casey possessed the shotgun and that his statement to police was credible. By replaying the statement, the trial court gave the prosecution an additional opportunity to present its most damaging evidence, effectively allowing the State to bolster its case through repetition—a tactic expressly disfavored by Evidence Rule 403's prohibition on "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence."

## IV. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument fails for several reasons:

First, the prosecution conflates the trial court's general authority to manage proceedings with the specific question of whether replaying evidence already admitted was appropriate under Evidence Rule 403. The cases cited by the prosecution—Pitman v. State, 436 N.E.2d 74 (Ind. 1982), and Garcia v. State, 517 N.E.2d 402 (Ind. 1988)—establish only that trial courts have broad discretion in managing trials. They do not stand for the proposition that this discretion is unlimited or that it permits the repeated presentation of evidence over defense objection when Rule 403 is implicated.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522 (Ind. 1995), is misplaced. In Harris, the Court upheld the sending of exhibits to the jury room during deliberations—not the replay of evidence during trial. Moreover, the Harris Court emphasized that there was "little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court." Harris, 659 N.E.2d at 527. Here, by contrast, the replay occurred during trial, before deliberations, and was specifically requested in response to juror questions—creating precisely the risk of giving undue weight to the evidence that the Harris Court deemed minimal in the different context of post-deliberation review.

Third, the prosecution's argument that the replay was necessary because a juror "couldn't hear" ignores the record. The juror's question related to "whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid"—a specific detail. Transcript Volume II at 44. The trial court nonetheless replayed the entire video rather than addressing the specific concern. This was not a reasonable exercise of discretion; it was an abuse of discretion.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in permitting Casey’s recorded statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. We review the trial court’s ruling on the admission of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). We will reverse only if the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). A trial court is given wide latitude in managing and controlling the proceedings before it. Pitman v. State, 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

Casey argues that the trial court erred by replaying the video in its entirety, asserting that the presentation was "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence" under Indiana Evidence Rule 403. While we agree that the replay of evidence constitutes cumulative evidence, the mere fact that evidence is cumulative does not automatically render its admission erroneous. As held in Chambers v. State, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981).

The record indicates that the trial court’s decision to replay the video was prompted by specific inquiries from the jury. The court noted multiple questions from jurors regarding the content of the video, including questions about the confrontation, the timing of the tip, the warrant process, and the defendant's unlawful possession. Furthermore, the court identified a specific juror who was unable to hear portions of the testimony. It is essential for jurors to be able to hear testimony in order to properly perform their duty. Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). Under these circumstances, the trial court acted within its discretion to ensure the jury comprehended the evidence.

The defense contends that the trial court should have utilized less prejudicial alternatives, such as answering specific questions or playing only specific segments. However, the trial court is in the best position to determine the most effective method to address juror confusion and audibility issues. # DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Court of Appeals of Indiana

---

### INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW

Your Honors, the prosecution's closing argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the abuse-of-discretion standard and the protections afforded to defendants under Indiana Evidence Rule 403. While this Court has wide latitude to affirm on any basis in the record, the defense respectfully maintains that the trial court's decision to replay Casey's entire recorded statement constituted an abuse of discretion that substantially prejudiced the defendant's right to a fair trial.

The standard of review is clear: this Court must reverse only when the trial court's decision is "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Joyner v. State, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). However, the mere existence of discretion does not immunize every decision from review. The question before this Court is not whether the trial court had discretion, but whether it exercised that discretion in a manner consistent with the safeguards established by the Indiana Rules of Evidence.

---

### I. THE REPLAY CONSTITUTED NEEDLESSLY CUMULATIVE EVIDENCE UNDER INDIANA EVIDENCE RULE 403

The prosecution erroneously conflates the authority to replay evidence with the unlimited discretion to do so repeatedly. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 explicitly authorizes exclusion of relevant evidence when its "probative value is substantially outweighed by... needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." The defense objected on this precise ground, stating that replaying the video was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Transcript Volume II at 45.

The prosecution cites Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981) for the proposition that replaying a tape is permissible. However, the defense distinguishes Chambers on critical grounds. In Chambers, the Indiana Supreme Court noted that the repeated playing was "cumulative evidence" but upheld the replay because the original tape had been inaudible to the jury. Here, the record demonstrates that the original playing was audible to the entire jury—the problem was not systemic inaudibility but a single juror's claim that she "couldn't hear" certain portions. This distinction is legally significant.

The prosecution's argument that any inability to hear justifies unlimited replay finds no support in Chambers. That case addressed a situation where the jury as a whole could not comprehend the evidence—not a scenario where one juror claimed difficulty. To hold otherwise would permit replaying entire witness examinations whenever any single juror expresses a hearing difficulty, a result that would transform the courtroom into an interactive replay booth rather than a forum for the orderly presentation of evidence.

---

### II. THE PROSECUTION MISREADS LEWIS v. STATE

The prosecution's reliance on Lewis v. State, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000) is equally misplaced. While the Lewis Court stated that "it is essential that [jurors] be able to hear the testimony," this principle was articulated in the context of systemic audibility problems affecting the jury's ability to fulfill its collective duty. The Court did not hold, as the prosecution suggests, that a single juror's complaint about hearing justifies replaying the entire evidence presentation.

The defense's position is supported by the specific facts here. The trial court noted that one juror had difficulty hearing "a specific answer regarding whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid." Transcript Volume II at 44. This was not a claim that the testimony was inaudible to the jury as a whole, but rather a complaint about missing a single answer to a single question. The appropriate remedy for such a limited deficiency would be a targeted response—such as having the witness answer the specific question again, or playing only the relevant portion of the recording—rather than replaying the entire statement.

The prosecution argues that requiring "systemic" audibility problems would "undermine the foundational requirement that all jurors must hear testimony." This argument is a straw man. The defense does not suggest that a single juror's hearing difficulty is irrelevant; rather, the defense contends that the remedy must be proportionate to the identified problem. Replaying the entire video to address one missed answer is akin to repeating an entire witness examination because one juror did not hear one question—an approach that transforms the exception into the rule.

---

### III. THE TIMING OF THE REPLAY CAUSED SUBSTANTIAL UNFAIR PREJUDICE

The prosecution dismisses the defense's timing argument as "unfortunate" but legally irrelevant. This Court should not be so cavalier about the sequence of evidence presentation. The replay occurred before Casey testified and before the defense had any opportunity to explain the circumstances under which the statement was made. The prosecution then relied on the statement during closing arguments, asking the jury to convict Casey based on his alleged admission.

Casey testified that he made the statement because he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Transcript Volume II at 56. He further explained that he had just smoked "Katie," which is "blunts... spice." Id. at 56-57. This testimony was critical to the defense's theory—that Casey's statement was not a true admission of guilt but a misguided attempt to protect his girlfriend.

When the trial court replayed the statement before Casey could testify, it allowed the prosecution to present Casey's alleged confession to the jury twice: once before the defense could offer any explanation, and again after the defense had explained the circumstances. This sequencing created an unfair advantage for the prosecution that Rule 403 was designed to prevent.

The prosecution argues that the jury was instructed to consider all evidence and that the defense had opportunity to cross-examine Detective Leitze. However, the damage was already done. The human mind does not easily erase first impressions. By the time Casey explained his statement, the jury had already heard it twice and had the prosecution's framing of it in their minds. The instruction to consider all evidence is a palliative, not a cure, for this type of prejudice.

---

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO BALANCE PROBATIVE VALUE AGAINST PREJUDICE

The prosecution claims the trial court properly balanced the factors under Rule 403. The record does not support this assertion. The trial court's discussion focused on whether to replay all or part of the video, not on whether replay was necessary given the limited nature of the identified deficiency. The court stated: "I don't know whether it's fair to leave her wondering when everyone else heard it." Transcript Volume II at 44.

This statement reveals the trial court's concern was with juror satisfaction, not with the proportionality of the remedy to the identified problem. A trial court may not sacrifice the defendant's right to a fair trial simply to assuage a juror's curiosity. The question under Rule 403 is not whether a juror might prefer to hear the evidence again, but whether the probative value of the replay substantially outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice or the needlessly cumulative presentation of evidence.

Here, the probative value of replaying the entire statement was minimal—the juror missed one answer about whose bedroom it was. The danger of unfair prejudice was substantial—the replay allowed the prosecution to present Casey's alleged confession twice before the defense could explain it. The needlessly cumulative nature of the evidence was apparent—the statement had already been played once in its entirety. On these facts, the trial court's decision to replay was "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Joyner, 678 N.E.2d at 390.

---

### V. THE DEFENSE HAS NOT SHOWN SUBSTANTIAL PREJUDICE—BUT THE PROSECUTION HAS THE BURDEN WRONG

The prosecution argues that Casey has not shown "substantial prejudice." This argument misstates the burden. Under Rule 403, the party seeking to exclude evidence—here, the defense—bears the burden of showing that the probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. However, once the defense has made this showing, the burden shifts to the prosecution to demonstrate that the evidence should be admitted despite this imbalance.

The defense has demonstrated that the replay was needlessly cumulative—the statement had already been played once. The defense has demonstrated unfair prejudice—the replay occurred before Casey could explain his statement, allowing the prosecution to establish its narrative before the defense could respond. Under these circumstances, the prosecution has not shown that the trial court's decision was justified.

---

### VI. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON HARRIS v. STATE IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites Harris v. State, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-27 (Ind. 1995) for the proposition that replaying evidence already in the record does not create undue prejudice. However, Harris is distinguishable on a critical basis: the replay in Harris occurred during jury deliberations, after both parties had rested. The Harris Court specifically noted that "the audio tapes had already been played in open court" and that there was "little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits." Id.

Here, the replay did not occur during deliberations. It occurred during the presentation of evidence, before the defense had rested, and before Casey had testified. The risk of prejudice was therefore substantially greater than in Harris. The prosecution's attempt to extend Harris to evidence replayed during trial stretches the precedent beyond its holding.

---

### VII. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and remand for a new trial. The trial court's decision to replay Casey's entire recorded statement, in response to one juror's claim that she missed a single answer, constituted an abuse of discretion under Indiana Evidence Rule 403. The replay was needlessly cumulative, occurred at a time that substantially prejudiced the defense, and was not justified by the limited nature of the identified audibility problem.

The prosecution has not demonstrated that the probative value of the replay substantially outweighed the danger of unfair prejudice. The trial court's discretion was not unlimited, and its decision was "clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances." Joyner, 678 N.E.2d at 390.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for a new trial.

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in permitting Casey’s recorded statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. Casey argues that the trial court abused its discretion by replaying the video in its entirety, asserting that the presentation was "needlessly presenting cumulative evidence" under Indiana Evidence Rule 403. The defense asserts that Lewis applies only to systemic audibility problems affecting the jury as a whole, not to individual juror complaints. However, the text of the Lewis holding provided in the record states broadly that "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Id. The trial court observed a specific juror who "clearly couldn't hear" and had made a note that she missed Casey's answer regarding the bedroom. Transcript Volume II at 44. The trial court was in the best position to assess the juror's ability to comprehend the proceedings. Ensuring that a juror can hear the testimony is fundamental to that juror's ability to perform their duty. We find no error in the trial court's determination that the principles of Lewis applied to the specific audibility issue identified during the trial.

Next, we consider the defense's distinction of Chambers v. State, 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981). The defense argues that Chambers is distinguishable because the tape there was inaudible to the jury, whereas here the original playing was audible to everyone but one juror. The record, however, reflects that the trial court found the juror had missed a specific answer and could not hear portions of the testimony. The trial court's decision to replay the statement was a direct response to this identified deficiency in the juror's reception of the evidence. As held in Chambers, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. Id. The fact that the deficiency affected one juror, rather than the entire panel, does not transform the trial court's remedial action into an abuse of discretion.
