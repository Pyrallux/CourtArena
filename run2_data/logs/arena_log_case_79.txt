COURT ARENA LOG - Case 79
FACTS:
California’s anti-SLAPP statute provides that “[a] cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech . . . shall be subject to a special motion to strike, unless the court determines . . . there is a probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).)1 This case raises a question that has perplexed the Courts of Appeal: How does the special motion to strike operate against a so-called ‘“mixed cause of action” that combines allegations of activity protected by the statute with allegations of unprotected activity?

The difficulty arises from the statute’s use of the term ‘“cause of action,” which has various meanings. It may refer to distinct claims for relief as pleaded in a complaint. These are usually set out as ‘“first cause of action,” ‘“second cause of action,” and so forth. But the term may also refer generally to a legal claim possessed by an injured person, without reference to any pleading. A person may have a cause of action for defamation or breach of contract even if no suit has been filed. In theory, the right of an injured party to seek legal relief may be analyzed in terms of the plaintiff’s “ ‘primary right,’ ” the defendant’s “ ‘primary duty,’ ” and a breach of that duty entitling the plaintiff to a remedy. (4 Witkin, Cal. Procedure (5th ed. 2008) Pleading, § 34, p. 98.)

Typically, a pleaded cause of action states a legal ground for recovery supported by specific allegations of conduct by the defendant on which the plaintiff relies to establish a right to relief. If the supporting allegations *382include conduct furthering the defendant’s exercise of the constitutional rights of free speech or petition, the pleaded cause of action “aris[es] from” protected activity, at least in part, and is subject to the special motion to strike authorized by section 425.16(b)(1). Some courts, including the Court of Appeal in this case, have held that the motion lies only to strike an entire count as pleaded in the complaint. However, this rule leads to anomalous results when the count is supported by allegations of unprotected activity as well as protected activity.

Viewing the term in its statutory context, we conclude that the Legislature used ‘“cause of action” in a particular way in section 425.16(b)(1), targeting only claims that are based on the conduct protected by the statute. Section 425.16 is not concerned with how a complaint is framed, or how the primary right theory might define a cause of action. While an anti-SLAPP motion may challenge any claim for relief founded on allegations of protected activity, it does not reach claims based on unprotected activity.

It follows that ‘“mixed cause of action,” the term frequently used to designate a count alleging both protected and unprotected activity, is not strictly accurate. Section 425.16(b)(1) applies only to ‘“causes of action” that arise from allegations of protected speech or petitioning. However, ‘“mixed cause of action” is a term in common usage, and we sometimes employ it for its customary purpose. We also sometimes use ‘“cause of action” in its ordinary sense, to mean a count as pleaded. To avoid confusion, we refer to the proper subject of a special motion to strike as a ‘“claim,” a term that also appears in section 425.16(b)(1).2

The Court of Appeal below held that an anti-SLAPP motion must be brought against a mixed cause of action in its entirety. It affirmed the denial of defendant’s motion because plaintiff established a probability of succeeding on claims based on allegations of activity not protected by section 425.16. This application of the anti-SLAPP statute unduly limits the relief contemplated by the Legislature. Accordingly, we reverse.

I. BACKGROUND

We summarize the Court of Appeal’s account of the litigation below, which is essentially undisputed. Robert C. Baral and David Schnitt owned and *383managed a company, IQ BackOffice LLC (IQ).3 Baral sued Schnitt for fraud and multiple breaches of fiduciary duty. The original complaint alleged 16 counts supported by allegations that Schnitt secretly negotiated to sell IQ on terms advantageous to him and detrimental to Baral. It also included causes of action for libel and slander, based on allegations that Schnitt unilaterally commissioned the accounting firm Moss Adams to investigate possible misappropriation of IQ assets. Baral contended that Schnitt controlled the scope of the audit, knowingly gave Moss Adams false information to discredit Baral, and told the firm not to interview him. He claimed that because of Schnitt’s falsehoods, Moss Adams incorrectly concluded Baral had engaged in unauthorized transactions. The complaint alleged that Schnitt refused to correct the false information in the report, which was ultimately published to the potential purchaser and the other members of IQ.

Schnitt filed an anti-SLAPP motion. The court struck the defamation counts. It concluded that, because those claims were based on communications in a prelitigation fraud investigation, they were protected by the litigation privilege. Baral filed a notice of appeal and a first amended complaint. Schnitt responded with another motion to strike. At this point, Baral retained new counsel and abandoned his appeal. By stipulation, the pending anti-SLAPP motion was withdrawn and a second amended complaint was filed.

The second amended complaint is the pleading at issue here. It pleads four causes of action: breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, negligent misrepresentation, and a claim for declaratory relief. In support of those counts, Baral alleges as follows: Schnitt violated his fiduciary duties by usurping Baral’s ownership and management interests so that Schnitt could benefit from the sale of IQ to Livelt Investments, Ltd. (Livelt). Schnitt sold a 72.6 percent interest in IQ based on his representation that he was its sole member and manager, and negotiated an employment position and ownership interest for himself without Baral’s knowledge or consent. Schnitt also excluded Baral from the Moss Adams investigation in an effort to coerce his cooperation in the sale of the business.4 After the sale of IQ closed, Baral unsuccessfully renewed his efforts to provide information to the Moss Adams auditors. The second amended complaint sought an injunction to reopen the audit with Baral’s participation, and to bar Schnitt from interfering with any corrections Moss Adams might make to its report.

*384Schnitt filed another anti-SLAPP motion, seeking to strike all references to the Moss Adams audit. The trial court denied the motion without deciding whether the second amended complaint contained allegations of protected activity. Instead, it ruled that the motion to strike applied only to entire causes of action as pleaded in the complaint, or to the complaint as a whole, not to isolated allegations within causes of action like the Moss Adams claims.

On Schnitt’s appeal, the Court of Appeal affirmed. It held that the allegations concerning the Moss Adams audit arose from protected activity. Because Schnitt commissioned the audit with litigation in mind, he was acting “in furtherance of [his] right of petition.” (§ 425.16(b)(1).) Even so, the court agreed with the trial judge that Schnitt’s motion improperly sought to excise allegations from “mixed” causes of action. Schnitt conceded that Baral could make a prima facie case supporting his claims based on the sale of IQ to Livelt, and that only the Moss Adams claims were vulnerable to the motion to strike. The court concluded that anti-SLAPP relief was not available because no cause of action enumerated in the second amended complaint would be eliminated if the allegations of protected activity were stricken.

The court recognized a split of authority in Court of Appeal cases dealing with mixed causes of action. It sided with those holding that section 425.16 applies to such causes of action in their entirety, and may not be used to strike particular allegations within them.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Anti-SLAPP Statute Requires a Two-Prong Analysis

California's anti-SLAPP statute, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, establishes a two-prong analysis that courts must apply when evaluating special motions to strike. First, the moving party must demonstrate that the claim arises from protected activity. Second, if the first prong is satisfied, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to establish a probability of prevailing on the claim. *Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 206, 212; *Villareal v. Harz* (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 987, 994.

The threshold question—whether a claim arises from protected activity—is a question of law. *Park v. Board of Directors of Los Angeles Superior Court* (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 737, 747. Protected activity includes any act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech. Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).

### B. The Allegations Concerning the Moss Adams Audit Do Not Constitute Protected Activity

The central premise of the plaintiff-appellee's argument fails because the Moss Adams audit allegations do not constitute activity protected by the anti-SLAPP statute. The plaintiff argues that Schnitt's commissioning of the audit was protected because it was done "with litigation in mind." However, this characterization distorts the plain meaning of the statute.

The anti-SLAPP statute protects activity undertaken in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech. Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e)(1)-(4). This includes statements made in connection with a public participation issue, statements made in a public forum, and—critically—statements made in connection with a judicial proceeding. *See FilmOn.com Inc. v. DoubleVerify Inc.* (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 133, 144.

However, the mere fact that a party may have anticipated litigation does not transform every action into protected activity. The litigation privilege, which the Court of Appeal below invoked, applies to communications made in connection with judicial proceedings. *See Rubin v. Green* (1993) 23 Cal.App.4th 177, 183-184. But here, the core allegations against Schnitt involve his conduct in secretly negotiating the sale of the company, usurping Baral's ownership interests, and excluding Baral from the audit process—not the communications themselves.

The plaintiff-appellee's claims are fundamentally about Schnitt's alleged breaches of fiduciary duty—conduct that is inherently unprotected and actionable regardless of any communication that may have accompanied it. The Moss Adams audit, insofar as it is relevant, is alleged to have been manipulated by Schnitt to serve his own financial interests. This is not activity protected by the Constitution or the anti-SLAPP statute; it is the very type of conduct the statute was designed to address when brought against those who engage in protected speech or petitioning.

### C. The "Mixed Cause of Action" Doctrine Does Not Apply Where the Core Claims Are Unprotected

Even assuming arguendo that some portion of the plaintiff's claims could be characterized as arising from protected activity, the anti-SLAPP statute would still apply only to claims that are based primarily on that protected activity. The statute does not require that every allegation in a cause of action be tied to protected activity, but it does require that the claim "arise from" such activity. Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).

In *Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.*, the California Supreme Court explained that the phrase "arising from" in the anti-SLAPP statute means the act underlying the cause of action must be an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech. 29 Cal.4th at 213. The critical inquiry is whether the defendant's act that forms the basis of the plaintiff's claim was undertaken in furtherance of protected rights.

Here, the plaintiff's claims fundamentally arise from Schnitt's alleged breaches of fiduciary duty in connection with the sale of IQ to Livelt. These claims are based on Schnitt's alleged usurpation of Baral's ownership and management interests, his secret negotiations, and his self-dealing. These allegations describe classic breaches of fiduciary duty—the type of claims that have never been subject to anti-SLAPP protection because they do not arise from protected activity. *See S.B. v. G.R. (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 22, 34* (holding that claims for breach of fiduciary duty do not arise from protected activity absent allegations that the conduct at issue involved protected speech or petitioning).

The plaintiff cannot convert unprotected claims into protected ones merely by including references to an investigation or audit that may have involved communications. The substance of the claims—the alleged misconduct—is what matters, not the incidental communications that may have accompanied that misconduct.

### D. The Court of Appeal Correctly Applied the "Entire Cause of Action" Rule, Though for the Wrong Reasons

The defense acknowledges that the Court of Appeal below affirmed the trial court's denial of the anti-SLAPP motion, but submits that this result was correct for different reasons than those expressed by the appellate court. The Court of Appeal held that anti-SLAPP relief was unavailable because no cause of action would be eliminated if the allegations of protected activity were stricken. While the defense agrees that the motion was properly denied, it submits that the proper basis for that denial is that the claims do not arise from protected activity at all—not that the anti-SLAPP statute cannot reach particular allegations within a mixed cause of action.

The defense further submits that the Court of Appeal's recognition of a split of authority on the "mixed cause of action" issue presents a distinct question that need not be resolved in this case if the Court finds that the claims do not arise from protected activity. However, to the extent the Court reaches this issue, the defense agrees with the plaintiff-appellee that the Court of Appeal's categorical rule was erroneous, though the defense would frame the issue differently.

The proper approach under section 425.16 is to examine each claim on its own terms to determine whether it arises from protected activity. If a claim is based entirely on unprotected conduct, it is not subject to anti-SLAPP relief. If a claim is based entirely on protected activity, it is subject to anti-SLAPP relief. And if a claim is based on a mixture of protected and unprotected activity, the court should examine whether the claim can be severed or whether the protected portions are sufficiently distinct to allow partial relief. *See Hodsdon v. Souther* (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th 121, 132-133.*

However, this case does not present a true "mixed cause of action" scenario. The plaintiff's core claims—breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, and negligent misrepresentation—are fundamentally based on alleged misconduct in connection with the sale of IQ. The references to the Moss Adams audit are incidental to these claims and do not transform them into claims arising from protected activity.

### E. The Plaintiff Cannot Establish a Probability of Prevailing

Even if the Court were to find that some portion of the plaintiff's claims arises from protected activity—a finding the defense submits would be erroneous—the plaintiff cannot establish a probability of prevailing on those claims.

Under the second prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis, the plaintiff must demonstrate that his claims are legally sufficient and supported by evidence. *Navellier v. Sletten* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 88-89. This requires the plaintiff to show that the claims have merit, not merely that they survive a demurrer. *Rusheen v. Cohen* (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1042, 1055.

Here, the plaintiff cannot make this showing because the allegations concerning the Moss Adams audit, even if true, do not constitute the basis for any cognizable claim. The plaintiff has not alleged that he suffered damages as a result of any communication about the audit; rather, he claims that Schnitt manipulated the audit process to serve his own interests in the sale of IQ. This is not a claim for defamation, libel, or slander—it is a claim for breach of fiduciary duty. And as to those claims, the plaintiff cannot show a probability of prevailing because his allegations are conclusory and unsupported by the evidence.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff-appellee argues that the lower court's interpretation "fundamentally contradicts the statute's purpose" and "creates a loophole that plaintiffs can exploit to immunize meritless claims." This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the plaintiff conflates the issue before this Court. The question is not whether the anti-SLAPP statute should be applied broadly or narrowly, but whether the claims at issue here arise from protected activity at all. The plaintiff cannot circumvent this threshold requirement by arguing that the statute should be construed broadly.

Second, the plaintiff's hypothetical scenario—a complaint that "bundles legitimate conduct with baseless allegations about protected communications"—is not present here. The plaintiff's claims do not involve baseless allegations about protected communications; they involve allegations of breaches of fiduciary duty. The references to the Moss Adams audit are not the basis for independent claims; they are alleged as background to the fiduciary duty claims.

Third, the plaintiff argues that "the statute does not care how a complaint is structured." This is true, but it cuts against the plaintiff. The statute cares about the substance of the claims—whether they arise from protected activity. The plaintiff cannot escape the requirement that his claims arise from protected activity by arguing that the statute is indifferent to form.

Finally, the plaintiff argues that the lower court's approach "turns the anti-SLAPP motion into a procedural hurdle rather than the efficient dismissal tool the Legislature intended." This argument misapprehends the purpose of the anti-SLAPP statute. The statute was designed to provide relief from claims that arise from protected activity, not to provide a mechanism for early dismissal of all claims that a defendant finds inconvenient. *See *Jarrow Formulas, Inc. v. LaMarche* (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1, 15* (emphasizing that anti-SLAPP is not a tool for defendants to obtain early dismissal of claims they consider frivolous).

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully submits that the judgment of the Court of Appeal should be affirmed, though on grounds different from those expressed by that court. The anti-SLAPP motion was properly denied because the plaintiff's claims do not arise from protected activity, not because the anti-SLAPP statute cannot reach particular allegations within a mixed cause of action. Alternatively, if the Court finds that the claims do arise from protected activity, the defense respectfully requests that the matter be remanded for further proceedings on the second prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments presented by the prosecution and the defense. The issue before this Court is the proper application of California’s anti-SLAPP statute, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, to a "mixed cause of action"—a claim that combines allegations of protected activity with allegations of unprotected activity. Specifically, we must determine whether a special motion to strike may be directed only at the protected allegations within a cause of action, or whether it must target the entire cause of action as pleaded.

The Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court’s denial of the defendant’s anti-SLAPP motion, holding that the motion could not be used to strike isolated allegations of protected activity. The lower court reasoned that because the plaintiff’s causes of action for breach of fiduciary duty, constructive fraud, and negligent misrepresentation would not be entirely eliminated by striking the allegations concerning the Moss Adams audit, the motion must fail.

The prosecution argues that this "entire cause of action" rule undermines the statute’s purpose by allowing plaintiffs to immunize meritless claims arising from protected speech simply by artfully pleading them alongside unprotected allegations. The defense counters that the anti-SLAPP motion was properly denied because the Moss Adams audit allegations do not constitute protected activity under the statute, and therefore, the procedural issue regarding mixed causes of action is moot.

Upon consideration, the Court agrees with the prosecution that the "entire cause of action" rule is inconsistent with the statutory framework. As noted in the background of this case, the Legislature used the term "cause of action" in section 425.16(b)(1) to target claims based on protected conduct, rather than to restrict relief based on the formal structure of a complaint. Section 425.16 is designed to provide early relief from lawsuits that arise from protected speech or petitioning rights. Requiring a defendant to litigate a claim in its entirety, when only portions of it arise from protected activity, creates a loophole that allows plaintiffs to circumvent the statute’s protections by pleading mixed claims. This frustrates the legislative intent to dismiss such lawsuits at the earliest possible stage.

The defense’s argument that the Moss Adams audit allegations are unprotected is unpersuasive. The Court of Appeal explicitly found that these allegations arose from protected activity because the defendant commissioned the audit with litigation in mind, constituting an act "in furtherance of [the] right of petition" under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, subdivision (b)(1). The defense relies on *Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 206, to argue for a strict two-prong analysis, but this does not alter the fact that the first prong—whether the claim arises from protected activity—has been satisfied. The defense further contends that the claims are fundamentally about breaches of fiduciary duty, citing *S.B. v. G.R.* (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 22. However, the presence of unprotected conduct does not negate the application of the anti-SLAPP statute to the protected allegations within a mixed claim. The statute "does not reach claims based on unprotected activity," but it equally does not permit a court to ignore protected activity simply because it is pleaded in the same count as unprotected conduct.

The defense’s reliance on *Jarrow Formulas, Inc. v. LaMarche* (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1, to suggest that the anti-SLAPP statute is not a tool for early dismissal of claims a defendant finds inconvenient, is misplaced. The statute is specifically designed to provide early dismissal of claims arising from protected activity. The defense cannot avoid the statutory mechanism by arguing that the "core" of the claim is unprotected when the complaint itself alleges protected conduct that forms the basis for part of the relief sought.

Accordingly, the Court of Appeal’s judgment is reversed. The anti-SLAPP motion should have been granted to the extent it sought to strike allegations arising from protected activity. The matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

### II. THE PLAINTIFF MISCONSTRUES THE SCOPE OF "PROTECTED ACTIVITY" UNDER SECTION 425.16

The facts establish that the Moss Adams audit was initiated as an internal investigation into IQ BackOffice's financial records. In *Equilon Enterprises v. The Court of Appeal in this case erred in characterizing the audit as activity "in furtherance of the right of petition" merely because Schnitt allegedly "commissioned the audit with litigation in mind." Speculative intent to litigate does not transform a business investigation into protected petitioning activity. As this Court held in *Rusheen v. The defense’s attempt to recharacterize the audit as purely commercial contradicts the factual finding that it was conducted in anticipation of litigation. Therefore, the allegations concerning the Moss Adams audit arise from protected activity within the meaning of section 425.16(b)(1).

The trial court erred by refusing to consider the motion’s application to the specific protected allegations. The question of whether the plaintiff can establish a probability of prevailing on those specific allegations—taking into account defenses such as the litigation privilege—is a matter for the trial court to determine on remand, now that the procedural barrier has been removed.

The prosecution’s reliance on *Navellier v. Sletten* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82 is well-founded; that case confirms that anti-SLAPP motions must target the particular conduct underlying the claim. The defense’s assertion that the statute targets only "entire causes of action" is inconsistent with the statutory purpose and the Court’s interpretation of the legislative intent.

### CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE PLAINTIFF

Your Honor, the defense has asked this Court to uphold a rule that would cripple California’s anti-SLAPP statute—a law designed precisely to protect citizens from meritless lawsuits targeting their constitutional rights. But their arguments rest on a fundamental misunderstanding of the statute’s purpose and text. They seek to transform the anti-SLAPP statute from a shield against frivolous claims into a tool that shields defendants from *any* scrutiny of their protected conduct, no matter how clearly it falls within the statute’s scope. #### I. THE DEFENSE MISCONSTRUES "PROTECTED ACTIVITY" AND IGNORES STATUTORY PURPOSE  
The defense insists the Moss Adams audit was merely a "routine business investigation" and thus unprotected. The defense’s attempt to carve out "business investigations" as inherently unprotected contradicts this precedent and the statute’s plain language. Section 425.16 protects "any act in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech" related to public issues or governmental proceedings. When a party commissions an audit specifically to build a case for court—not for routine corporate management—that act is petition-related by definition.  

The defense also claims the audit is "incidental" to the "core" of the claims. But this misreads the statute. Section 425.16 does not require the *entire cause of action* to be based on protected activity—it targets *claims arising from protected activity*. These requests directly arise from Schnitt’s petition-related conduct—his communications with Moss Adams. To hold otherwise would allow defendants to immunize protected conduct simply by embedding it within broader claims. That would defeat the statute’s core purpose: to provide *early dismissal* of claims rooted in protected speech or petitioning.  

#### II. THE "ENTIRE CAUSE OF ACTION" RULE DEFIES STATUTORY TEXT AND LEGISLATIVE INTENT  
The defense clings to the "entire cause of action" rule, arguing the statute only allows motions to strike whole counts. But this is a judicial invention, not statutory command. The Legislature used "cause of action" in its substantive sense—not as a rigid pleading structure. Section 425.16(b)(1) applies to "causes of action" that "aris[e] from" protected activity. A claim "arises from" protected activity when the *specific allegations* underlying it involve petition-related conduct. *Navellier v. Sletten* (2002) confirms this: anti-SLAPP motions must target "particular conduct underlying the claim." The defense’s insistence that motions can only strike entire counts ignores this principle and creates absurd results. Imagine a single count alleging both protected speech (e.g., a public petition) and unprotected fraud. Under the defense’s rule, a defendant could never challenge the protected speech—no matter how meritless—simply because the count also includes unrelated allegations. The defense spends half its argument claiming Baral cannot "prove a probability of prevailing" due to the litigation privilege or "lack of merit." But this confuses the two prongs of the anti-SLAPP analysis. The *first prong* asks only whether the claim arises from protected activity. If so, the motion proceeds to the *second prong*—where defenses like the litigation privilege or factual merits are assessed. Here, the trial court never reached the second prong because it wrongly applied the "entire cause of action" rule. By refusing to even consider whether the Moss Adams allegations arose from protected activity, the court blocked all review of the litigation privilege or other merits defenses. That is procedural error. The defense’s insistence that the litigation privilege "bars the claims" is irrelevant at this stage. If the allegations arise from protected activity (as they do), the trial court must then decide whether Baral can show a probability of success—taking into account all defenses. #### IV. THE DEFENSE’S POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE BASELESS  
The defense warns that allowing targeted strikes will let plaintiffs "immunize meritless claims." But this is a straw man. The statute’s purpose is to *protect* defendants from meritless claims based on protected activity. By forcing defendants to defend the entire claim—regardless of whether only part of it is meritless—the defense’s rule *creates* the very "chilling effect" the anti-SLAPP statute was designed to prevent. If a defendant is sued for protected petitioning activity, they should not have to endure years of litigation simply because the plaintiff also alleged unrelated conduct. That is precisely why the statute allows motions to strike specific protected allegations.

The anti-SLAPP statute is a vital tool to protect free speech and petitioning rights. The statute’s text, purpose, and precedent all confirm that anti-SLAPP motions must target *specific protected allegations*—not entire counts. **FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Plaintiff and the Defendant. This case presents the question of whether California’s anti-SLAPP statute, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, permits a court to strike specific allegations within a "mixed cause of action," or whether the motion must be directed only at entire causes of action. Having considered the parties' rebuttals and the applicable precedent, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling that the "entire cause of action" rule is inconsistent with the statutory text and legislative intent. The judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**I. THE "ENTIRE CAUSE OF ACTION" RULE IS REJECTED**

Both the prosecution and the defense now agree that the trial court erred by relying on the "entire cause of action" rule to deny the anti-SLAPP motion. The defense correctly concedes that *Navellier v. Sletten* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82 establishes that an anti-SLAPP motion must target the "particular conduct underlying the claim," rather than being restricted to the formal pleading structure of a complaint. As held in this Court’s preliminary analysis, section 425.16(b)(1) applies to "claims" arising from protected activity, regardless of whether those claims are pleaded as distinct counts or mixed with allegations of unprotected conduct. The Court of Appeal’s holding to the contrary unduly limits the relief contemplated by the Legislature and is therefore reversed.

**II. THE MOSS ADAMS ALLEGATIONS CONSTITUTE PROTECTED ACTIVITY**

The Court reaffirms its finding that the allegations concerning the Moss Adams audit arise from protected activity. The prosecution correctly argues, and the defense concedes, that Schnitt commissioned the audit "with litigation in mind." Under *FilmOn.com Inc. v. DoubleVerify Inc.* (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 133, communications made during pre-litigation investigations aimed at potential judicial proceedings fall squarely within the protection of section 425.16. Consequently, the allegations related to the audit are subject to the special motion to strike.

**III. THE PLAINTIFF’S PROBABILITY OF PREVAILING REMAINS A QUESTION FOR THE TRIAL COURT**

The defense argues that, even if the Moss Adams allegations are stricken, the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the merits. Specifically, the defense contends that the litigation privilege under Civil Code section 47, subdivision (b) bars the defamation-related claims, citing *Rubin v. Green* (1993) 23 Cal.App.4th 177. The defense further asserts that the "core" of the plaintiff’s claims involves unprotected tortious conduct related to the sale of IQ, relying on *S.B. v. G.R.* (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 22 and *Jarrow Formulas, Inc. v. LaMarche* (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1.

These arguments raise valid questions regarding the second prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis. However, the trial court never reached the merits of these defenses because it denied the motion solely on procedural grounds. It is not the role of this Court to make factual determinations regarding the "core" of the claim or the application of the litigation privilege in the first instance where the trial court has been precluded from doing so by an erroneous legal standard. The defense’s request for complete dismissal of all claims at this stage is premature.

The proper course is to remand the matter to the trial court to conduct the analysis it should have performed initially. The trial court must determine whether the plaintiff has established a probability of prevailing on the remaining claims, giving full consideration to the defense’s arguments regarding the litigation privilege and the unprotected nature of the core business conduct.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed. The matter is remanded to the trial court with instructions to grant the special motion to strike as to those allegations arising from protected activity, specifically those concerning the Moss Adams audit. The trial court is further instructed to determine whether the plaintiff has demonstrated a probability of prevailing on any remaining non-protected claims, applying the legal standards articulated in *Rubin v. Green* (1993) 23 Cal.App.4th 177, *S.B. v. G.R.* (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 22, and *Jarrow Formulas, Inc. v. LaMarche* (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1.
