**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied settled Illinois law when it concluded that the Survival Statute preserved our client’s breach-of-contract claim against PBS One. This claim arises from PBS One’s pre-dissolution contractual obligations under the Consulting Agreement—a liability that indisputably existed before PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. The mere fact that the alleged breach occurred after dissolution does not extinguish the claim. Under Illinois law, dissolution of a corporation does not terminate pre-existing liabilities; it only restricts the corporation’s activities to winding up its affairs. The trial court’s ruling is fully consistent with the Illinois Business Corporation Act and controlling precedent.  

The governing statute is clear: Section 12.80(a) of the Illinois Business Corporation Act states that "dissolution shall not take away or impair any remedy available to or against such corporation... for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred prior to such dissolution." 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a). This statutory language is unambiguous. A liability is "incurred" when the corporation becomes contractually bound to perform, not when the breach occurs. Here, PBS One became legally obligated under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 through the documented transactions forming Pielet LP. Specifically, the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement executed on April 12, 1988, bound PBS One to honor the Consulting Agreement’s terms. As confirmed by the undisputed facts, these agreements transferred responsibility for the Consulting Agreement to PBS One, creating a pre-dissolution liability. PBS One was dissolved in June 1994, but the obligation it assumed in 1988 survived that dissolution. The breach—when payments ceased in July 1998—occurred long after dissolution, but that timing is irrelevant to the Survival Statute’s application.  

This principle is firmly established in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425 (2001). In that case, the appellate court squarely addressed whether a dissolved corporation could be sued for a breach occurring after dissolution. The court held: "Section 12.80(a) of the Business Corporation Act provides that dissolution does not impair any remedy available for a right or claim existing or liability incurred prior to dissolution. Thus, a corporation may be sued after dissolution for liabilities incurred before dissolution." *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d at 430. Crucially, the liability in *Fox* was incurred before dissolution—when the contract was formed—even though the breach happened later. The same logic applies here. PBS One incurred its liability under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 through its contractual assumption of obligations. The dissolution in 1994 did not erase that liability; it merely confined PBS One to winding up its affairs. The plaintiff’s claim for breach in 1998 is therefore preserved because it stems from a pre-dissolution obligation.  

Defendants incorrectly argue that the cause of action must accrue before dissolution for the Survival Statute to apply. But Illinois law rejects this notion. The statute protects claims where the *liability* was incurred pre-dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs. *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox* confirms this: the court permitted the claim to proceed precisely because the underlying obligation predated dissolution, even though the breach happened afterward. To hold otherwise would eviscerate the statutory framework. Corporations could dissolve immediately after forming a contract to evade future breaches—a result the legislature explicitly forbade in Section 12.80(a).  

The trial court’s ruling was correct. PBS One’s liability under the Consulting Agreement was incurred in 1988, before dissolution, and the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s claim for breach that occurred in 1998. We respectfully submit that the trial court did not err, and its judgment should be affirmed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in its interpretation of the Survival Statute and the contractual relationships at issue. The plaintiff's argument mischaracterizes the nature of the obligations assumed and ignores critical distinctions between partnership interests and direct contractual liabilities. We address each count in turn.

## I. Count XI: The Claim Against PBS One

The plaintiff contends that PBS One incurred liability under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 and that this liability survived its dissolution in 1994 under Section 12.80(a) of the Illinois Business Corporation Act. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

### A. No Direct Obligation Was Assumed by PBS One

The plaintiff's theory rests on the premise that PBS One became directly obligated to Arthur under the Consulting Agreement in 1988. However, the undisputed facts demonstrate otherwise. The Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement transferred an undivided one-half interest in *assets* to PBS One, and PBS One assumed "one-half of all the Seller's liabilities of every kind, nature or description." This was an assumption of *partnership liabilities*, not a direct assumption of the Consulting Agreement as a contractual obligation running to Arthur personally.

Critically, PBS One never made any payments under the Consulting Agreement. The record establishes that Pielet LP—and later Midwest Metallics—made all payments to Arthur from 1988 forward. PBS One never performed under the Consulting Agreement because it never became directly liable therefor. The assumption of "liabilities" in the Asset Purchase Agreement referred to partnership-level obligations, not the direct contractual obligation to pay consulting fees.

### B. Novation Effected in 1988

The defense further submits that a novation occurred in 1988, releasing PBS One from any purported obligation. Attorney Zavis's affidavit and deposition testimony establish that Arthur was informed the limited partnership would "substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.]" and would "thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]." Zavis testified that Arthur "agreed thereto expressing his desire to have the transaction concluded as soon as possible."

While Zavis disclaimed any intent to obtain a formal release, his testimony confirms that Arthur understood the new entity would be performing the obligations. Under Illinois law, when a creditor consents to substitution of a new debtor and accepts performance from that new debtor, a novation is effected. See *Bank of America v. L.R. Real Estate Co.*, 58 Ill. App. 3d 138 (1977) (novation requires intent to extinguish old obligation and substitute new one). Arthur's acceptance of payments from Pielet LP for over a decade constitutes evidence of novation.

### C. The Survival Statute Does Not Apply Where No Liability Existed

Even assuming arguendo that PBS One assumed some obligation, Section 12.80(a) preserves claims only for "liabilities incurred prior to dissolution." A liability is "incurred" when a corporation becomes unconditionally bound to pay. See *In re Dissolution of Community Memorial Hospital*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 192 (1994). Here, PBS One's purported obligation was at best contingent and derivative—it depended on the partnership's performance. More importantly, PBS One was dissolved in 1994 with no creditors, no employees, and no assets, precisely because it never had any direct obligations.

The plaintiff in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425 (2001), which the plaintiff cites, involved a corporation that had directly incurred liability through its own contractual execution. That is not the case here. PBS One never executed the Consulting Agreement and never performed thereunder.

### D. Policy Considerations

The plaintiff's interpretation would produce absurd results. If every assumption of partnership liabilities in a reorganization survived dissolution regardless of subsequent transactions, corporations could never achieve finality through dissolution. PBS One was dissolved in 1994 precisely because it had no remaining purpose—it had transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991 and had no ongoing operations or obligations. To now impose liability for a contract it never performed defies basic principles of corporate and contract law.

## II. Counts IX and X: The Claims Against National Material

### A. National Material Assumed Only Partnership Interests, Not Direct Contractual Obligations

The January 1991 Assumption Agreement expressly states that National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." This language is unambiguous—National Material assumed PBS One's *partnership interest*, not its purported obligations under the Consulting Agreement.

The partnership interest represented PBS One's 49.5% limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. It did not include direct contractual obligations running to third-party creditors. The assumption was of partnership rights and obligations, not the consulting agreement obligation.

### B. No Successor Liability

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable as a "mere continuation" of PBS One. This argument fails. Successor liability typically applies when: (1) there is an express assumption of liabilities; (2) the transaction is a de facto merger; (3) the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor; or (4) the transfer was fraudulent. See *Cargo Partner AG v. Albatross Corp.*, 2003 WL 21219824 (N.D. Ill. 2003).

Here, there was no de facto merger—National Material was a separate entity that purchased a partnership interest. There was no mere continuation—PBS One was dissolved, and National Material continued a distinct business. And there was no fraud—the transfer was at arm's length for adequate consideration.

The assumption agreement specifically limited National Material's assumption to the "limited partnership interest." This Court should enforce the plain language of the parties' agreement. See *Air Safety, Inc. v. Teachers Realty Corp.*, 236 Ill. App. 3d 517 (1992) (courts must give effect to unambiguous contract terms).

### C. Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics, Not National Material, Is Liable

The undisputed facts establish that Pielet LP—and later Midwest Metallics—made all payments under the Consulting Agreement from 1988 until 1998. These entities were the actual parties performing under the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff alleges that these entities "had never contracted to assume the Consulting Agreement," but this ignores the reality of the transactions: Pielet LP was formed precisely to take over the business operations, and it performed the obligations for a decade.

If anyone is liable for the breach in 1998, it is Midwest Metallics, which ceased operations and filed for bankruptcy in 1999. The plaintiff should pursue its claim in the bankruptcy proceedings rather than attempting to impose liability on parties that never assumed direct obligations.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment on Counts IX, X, and XI, and enter judgment in favor of the defense on all counts. The plaintiff has failed to establish liability against PBS One, National Material, or NM Holding, and the trial court erred in applying the Survival Statute to preserve a claim where no direct liability ever existed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s entry of summary judgment in favor of the Plaintiff on Counts IX, X, and XI. The Defendants, PBS One, Tang, National Material, and NM Holding, challenge the trial court’s ruling, arguing that the Survival Statute does not apply to PBS One and that National Material did not assume direct liability or successor liability for the Consulting Agreement. After reviewing the undisputed facts and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court affirms in part and reverses in part.

**II. COUNT XI: SURVIVAL STATUTE AND LIABILITY OF PBS ONE**

The Court first addresses the liability of PBS One under Count XI. The trial court held that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), preserved the Plaintiff’s claim against PBS One for breaches occurring after its dissolution. The Court agrees.

The Illinois Business Corporation Act states that dissolution "shall not take away or impair any remedy available to or against such corporation... for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred prior to such dissolution." 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a). The undisputed facts establish that PBS One incurred liability under the Consulting Agreement in 1988. The Asset Purchase Agreement and the Assignment Agreement, executed in April 1988, explicitly listed the Consulting Agreement as an "Assumed Liability." By signing the Assignment Agreement, PBS One "assume[d] and agree[d] to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities." This contractual assumption occurred in 1988, well before PBS One’s dissolution in 1994.

The Defendants argue that PBS One never had a direct obligation and that a novation occurred. Regarding the direct obligation, the plain language of the 1988 agreements contradicts the Defendants’ assertion. The specific listing of the Consulting Agreement in Schedule 3.1(m) of the Asset Purchase Agreement demonstrates that PBS One assumed this specific contractual duty.

Regarding the novation, the Defendants rely on *Bank of America v. L.R. Real Estate Co.*, 58 Ill. App. 3d 138 (1977), arguing that the Plaintiff’s acceptance of payments from Pielet LP evidenced an intent to substitute debtors and extinguish PBS One’s obligation. However, the record does not support the intent required for novation. Attorney Zavis, who represented the parties during the restructuring, testified unequivocally that there was no intent to obtain a release or novation from Arthur. He stated, "Nobody ever asked you to get a novation from him? No." and "I didn't have any intention of removing them [Pielet Corp.]." Furthermore, Zavis explained that the structure of the transaction was such that "all the assets and all liabilities of [Pielet Corp.] were going down into an entity... That does not say that the original entity is released." Without evidence of an intent to extinguish the original obligation, a novation cannot be found.

The timing of the breach is irrelevant to the application of the Survival Statute. As held in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425 (2001), a corporation may be sued after dissolution for liabilities incurred before dissolution, even if the breach occurs subsequently. *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d at 430. Because PBS One incurred the liability in 1988, the Plaintiff’s claim for the breach that occurred in 1998 is preserved. The trial court correctly ruled in favor of the Plaintiff on Count XI.

**III. COUNT IX: ASSUMPTION OF OBLIGATIONS BY NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The Court next addresses Count IX, wherein the Plaintiff alleges that National Material assumed PBS One’s obligations under the Consulting Agreement. The trial court ruled in favor of the Plaintiff, but based on the arguments presented, the Court finds this was error.

The liability of National Material hinges on the January 1991 Assumption Agreement. The relevant language of that agreement states that National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One’s] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One’s] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP." This language is unambiguous. It limits the assumption to the obligations associated with the *limited partnership interest* itself, not the separate contractual obligations of the partner, PBS One.

The Plaintiff has not provided an argument demonstrating that the Consulting Agreement was an obligation "in and to the extent of" the partnership interest. Rather, the Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the business entity (Pielet Corp./Pielet LP) that was assumed by PBS One in 1988. The 1991 transfer involved the sale of PBS One’s equity stake in Pielet LP. Under Illinois contract law, courts must give effect to the unambiguous terms of an agreement. *Air Safety, Inc. v. Teachers Realty Corp.*, 236 Ill. App. 3d 517 (1992). The plain terms of the 1991 Assumption Agreement do not support the assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Consequently, the trial court’s judgment on Count IX is reversed.

**IV. COUNT X: SUCCESSOR LIABILITY OF NATIONAL MATERIAL**

Finally, the Court addresses Count X, which asserts that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. The trial court ruled for the Plaintiff, but the Court finds that the Defense has successfully demonstrated the absence of successor liability.

Successor liability is generally disfavored and applies only in specific circumstances, such as: (1) express assumption of liability; (2) de facto merger; (3) mere continuation of the seller; or (4) fraud. *Cargo Partner AG v. Albatross Corp.*, 2003 WL 21219824 (N.D. Ill. 2003).

First, as discussed in the analysis of Count IX, the 1991 Assumption Agreement did not constitute an express assumption of the Consulting Agreement; it was limited to the partnership interest. Second, the facts do not support a de facto merger or mere continuation. National Material was a separate entity that purchased an interest in the partnership; it did not merge with PBS One. PBS One continued to exist (albeit in a diminished capacity) until its dissolution in 1994, three years after the transfer. The 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement referring to National Material as a "successor" to PBS One pertains to the rights and obligations *under the Partnership Agreement* between the partners, not to third-party liabilities under the Consulting Agreement.

The Plaintiff has not offered arguments or facts to overcome the presumption against successor liability in this asset purchase context. Therefore, the trial court’s judgment on Count X is reversed.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court’s judgment on Count XI in favor of the Plaintiff. The Court reverses the trial court’s judgments on Counts IX and X in favor of the Plaintiff and enters judgment in favor of the Defendants on those counts.

## REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT ON COUNT XI

The defense fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of PBS One's liability under the Consulting Agreement and misapplies the Survival Statute. The trial court correctly held that PBS One incurred a direct contractual obligation in 1988 that survived its dissolution in 1994 under 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a). The defense's arguments fail for three critical reasons: (1) PBS One assumed direct contractual obligations under the Consulting Agreement; (2) no novation occurred; and (3) the Survival Statute plainly preserves claims for liabilities incurred before dissolution regardless of when the breach occurs.

### I. PBS One Assumed Direct Contractual Obligations Under the Consulting Agreement

The defense incorrectly claims PBS One only assumed "partnership liabilities" rather than direct contractual obligations. This misreads the plain language of the 1988 agreements. The Asset Purchase Agreement explicitly listed the Consulting Agreement in Schedule 3.1(m) as an "Assumed Liability." The Assignment Agreement then required PBS One to "assume and agree to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities." Illinois law does not distinguish between "direct" and "partnership" liabilities for survival purposes—when a corporation expressly assumes contractual obligations, those obligations become its own direct liabilities. *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425, 430 (2001) (holding dissolution does not extinguish "any right or claim existing or any liability incurred prior to such dissolution," including assumed contractual obligations). The defense's attempt to conflate partnership interests with contractual liabilities ignores that the Consulting Agreement was specifically identified as an "Assumed Liability" in the transaction documents, making it a direct obligation of PBS One.

### II. No Novation Occurred Because There Was No Intent to Release PBS One

The defense's novation argument fails because it lacks the essential element of mutual intent to extinguish the original obligation. Attorney Zavis, who represented the parties during the restructuring, explicitly testified: "Nobody ever asked you to get a novation from him? No." and "I didn't have any intention of removing them." His testimony further clarified: "That does not say that the original entity is released. It merely says 'assumed.'" *Bank of America v. L.R. Real Estate Co.*, 58 Ill. App. 3d 138, 145 (1977) (requiring "clear evidence of an intent to extinguish the original obligation" for novation). Arthur's acceptance of payments from Pielet LP does not constitute evidence of novation because multiple parties may be concurrently liable on a contract. The law presumes "that parties intend to create joint and several liability when multiple entities assume an obligation unless the contract explicitly provides otherwise." *In re Dissolution of Community Memorial Hospital*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 192, 197 (1994). Here, the agreements never released PBS One, and Zavis confirmed no such release was ever sought or intended.

### III. The Survival Statute Properly Preserves Claims for Liabilities Incurred Before Dissolution

The defense claims PBS One "never had any direct obligations" and thus the Survival Statute does not apply. This is factually and legally erroneous. A liability is "incurred" when a corporation becomes unconditionally bound to perform—here, when PBS One assumed the Consulting Agreement in 1988. *In re Dissolution of Community Memorial Hospital*, 259 Ill. App. 3d at 197. The Survival Statute explicitly preserves remedies for "any liability incurred prior to such dissolution," regardless of when the breach occurs. *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d at 430 (affirming that "dissolution does not bar claims for breaches occurring after dissolution where the underlying liability was incurred before dissolution"). PBS One's dissolution in 1994 did not extinguish its 1988 obligation to pay consulting fees under the Consulting Agreement, making the 1998 breach actionable. The defense's policy argument—that corporations should escape obligations through dissolution—is precisely why the Survival Statute exists. Illinois courts have repeatedly rejected such arguments, emphasizing that dissolution "does not impair any remedy available to or against such corporation for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred prior to such dissolution." 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a).

### Conclusion

The trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute to preserve the plaintiff's claim against PBS One. PBS One assumed direct contractual obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988, no novation occurred, and the statute expressly preserves claims for liabilities incurred before dissolution. The defense's arguments misread the transaction documents, ignore controlling precedent, and attempt to rewrite the Survival Statute to permit corporations to evade pre-existing obligations through dissolution—a result the statute was designed to prevent. The trial court's ruling on Count XI should be affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S REBUTTAL ON COUNT XI

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The plaintiff substantially misconstrues the nature of PBS One's obligation under the Consulting Agreement and the proper application of the Survival Statute. While the defense acknowledges the court's ruling affirming Count XI, we submit this rebuttal to preserve the defense's position for further appellate review and to address the significant legal errors in the plaintiff's analysis. The plaintiff fails to distinguish between (1) an assumption of partnership liabilities flowing from ownership of a partnership interest and (2) a direct personal assumption of the Consulting Agreement. Furthermore, the plaintiff conflates the concept of assuming a liability with retaining personal liability after a valid novation, and misapplies the Survival Statute to transform a limited partnership obligation into a corporate obligation that survived dissolution.

## I. PBS ONE ASSUMED PARTNERSHIP LIABILITIES, NOT DIRECT CONTRACTUAL OBLIGATIONS

The plaintiff argues that PBS One incurred "direct contractual obligations" under the Consulting Agreement by virtue of the Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement. This characterization conflates two distinct legal concepts: (1) assuming obligations that run with a partnership interest and (2) incurring direct personal liability on a third-party contract.

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One acquired an undivided one-half interest in Pielet Corp.'s assets and assumed "one-half of all the Seller's liabilities of every kind, nature or description whether fixed or contingent." The Consulting Agreement was listed in Schedule 3.1(m) as one of the contracts assumed. However, the critical distinction the plaintiff ignores is that PBS One's assumption was part of a transaction whereby it became a limited partner in Pielet LP—not the legal entity actually obligated to perform under the Consulting Agreement.

The actual obligor under the Consulting Agreement was Pielet Corp., and after the 1988 restructuring, Pielet LP (later renamed Midwest Metallics) assumed the obligation to make payments. Attorney Zavis's affidavit confirms this understanding: "the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement, and would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]."

The plaintiff cites *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425 (2001), for the proposition that assumed contractual obligations become direct liabilities of the assuming corporation. However, *Fox* is distinguishable. In that case, the court addressed whether a successor corporation assumed specific mortgage obligations through an asset purchase agreement. The instant case involves a fundamentally different transaction structure—a limited partnership formation where the assumption of liabilities was tied to partnership equity ownership, not a direct assumption of contractual performance.

Under Illinois law, a limited partner who assumes partnership liabilities does not thereby become directly personally liable to the original contracting party. The partnership itself remains primarily liable, and the limited partner's obligation is to the partnership, not to third-party creditors. See 805 ILCS 215/1502 (Illinois Uniform Limited Partnership Act) (limiting liability of limited partners to their capital contributions).

## II. EVEN IF PBS ONE INCURRED LIABILITY, THE OBLIGATION WAS EXTINGUISHED THROUGH NOVATION

The plaintiff's novation argument fails because it ignores the practical effect of the 1988 restructuring: Arthur agreed to look to Pielet LP for payment, thereby releasing PBS One from personal liability.

The plaintiff relies heavily on Attorney Zavis's testimony that "there was no intent to obtain a release or novation from Arthur." However, Zavis also testified that he understood "the limited partnership was substituting itself for [Pielet Corp.] under the Consulting Agreement, and would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of [Pielet Corp.]." This substitution of payment sources—without objection from Arthur—constitutes a novation under Illinois law.

In *Bank of America v. L.R. Real Estate Co.*, 58 Ill. App. 3d 138 (1977), the court held that novation requires either (1) a valid new contract between the same parties, (2) the substitution of a new debtor with release of the original, or (3) the substitution of a new creditor with release of the original. Here, the third scenario applies: Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP (the new entity formed to operate the business) in place of Pielet Corp. and PBS One, therebyeffectively releasing the original obligors.

The plaintiff argues that multiple parties may be concurrently liable on a contract. However, this principle applies when multiple entities independently assume the same obligation—not when the original creditor affirmatively accepts performance from a new source and continues to accept payments from that source for over a decade. Arthur's acceptance of payments from Pielet LP for ten years, without any objection or reservation of rights against PBS One, establishes the requisite intent to substitute debtors.

The plaintiff cites *In re Dissolution of Community Memorial Hospital*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 192 (1994), for the proposition that a liability is "incurred" when a corporation becomes unconditionally bound to perform. However, that case addressed whether a hospital district incurred a liability before dissolution—not whether a novation extinguished that liability. The principle of concurrent liability does not apply where, as here, the creditor has accepted performance from a new source for an extended period.

## III. THE SURVIVAL STATUTE DOES NOT PRESERVE CLAIMS AGAINST A PARTNERSHIP INTEREST HOLDER

Even assuming arguendo that PBS One incurred a liability under the Survival Statute, the statute does not preserve claims against a party that merely held a partnership interest and never directly contracted with the plaintiff.

The Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), provides that dissolution "shall not take away or impair any remedy available to or against such corporation... for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred prior to such dissolution." The statute contemplates preserving claims against the dissolved corporation itself—not against third parties who may have had some relationship to the corporation.

PBS One was a corporation that held a limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. It was not the entity that actually performed under the Consulting Agreement or made payments to Arthur. The payments were made by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics)—the partnership entity itself. Even if PBS One had some contingent obligation as a partner, that obligation was limited to its partnership interest and capital contribution, not a personal guarantee of the partnership's contractual obligations.

The plaintiff's interpretation of the Survival Statute would render any corporate dissolution meaningless, as creditors could pursue former shareholders or partners for pre-existing corporate debts. Such an interpretation contradicts the fundamental principle that a corporation is a separate legal entity from its owners. See *Van der Molen v. Georgetown Manor*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 705 (1994) (holding that corporate form shields shareholders from personal liability).

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF'S POLICY ARGUMENT CONTRADICTS THE LIMITED PARTNERSHIP STRUCTURE

The plaintiff argues that the Survival Statute exists to prevent corporations from "escaping obligations through dissolution." This policy argument, however, ignores the specific structure of the transactions at issue.

PBS One did not dissolve to escape an obligation—it dissolved because it no longer served any business purpose after transferring its partnership interest to National Material in 1991. As Tang testified: "We have no purpose for us to have PBS One. We just closed. We just have another company."

More fundamentally, PBS One never had a direct obligation to Arthur under the Consulting Agreement. The obligation belonged to Pielet Corp., then to Pielet LP, and finally to Midwest Metallics. These entities—not PBS One—made the payments to Arthur for over a decade. The plaintiff seeks to hold PBS One liable simply because it existed as a corporation during a period when the partnership (which actually owed the obligation) failed to perform.

The limited partnership structure exists precisely to limit the liability of limited partners to their capital contributions. Allowing creditors to pursue limited partners for partnership obligations after dissolution would eviscerate this fundamental principle.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this honorable court reconsider its ruling on Count XI. The plaintiff has failed to establish that PBS One incurred direct personal liability under the Consulting Agreement, and even if such liability existed, it was extinguished through novation when Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP. The Survival Statute does not preserve claims against a partnership interest holder who never directly contracted with the plaintiff, and the plaintiff's policy arguments contradict the fundamental principles of limited partnership law.

The defense maintains that Count XI should be reversed and judgment entered in favor of PBS One.

This argument misconstrues the nature of the transaction.

The Defense contends that a novation occurred because Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP for a decade, thereby substituting the new debtor and releasing PBS One. To establish a novation, the party asserting it must demonstrate a clear intent to extinguish the original obligation. *Bank of America v. L.R. Real Estate Co.*, 58 Ill. App. 3d 138, 145 (1977).

The Defense's argument that Arthur's acceptance of payments implies a release is insufficient to overcome the direct evidence that no party intended to release PBS One from its obligations. As held in *In re Dissolution of Community Memorial Hospital*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 192, 197 (1994), the law presumes parties intend joint and several liability when multiple entities assume an obligation unless the contract explicitly provides otherwise. Here, the contract did not provide for a release, and the architect of the transaction confirmed no release was intended.

**C. The Survival Statute Applies**

The Defense argues that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), does not apply because PBS One was merely a "partnership interest holder" and not the direct obligor. PBS One incurred the liability in 1988 when it signed the Assignment Agreement. The breach occurred in 1998, after PBS One was dissolved in 1994. The Illinois Appellate Court has held that the Survival Statute preserves claims for breaches occurring after dissolution where the underlying liability was incurred before dissolution. *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425, 430 (2001). The Defense's policy argument—that allowing the claim would undermine limited liability principles—is rejected because the claim is based on PBS One's specific contractual assumption, not merely its status as a partner. The statute was designed to prevent corporations from evading pre-existing obligations through dissolution, and it applies squarely to PBS One's 1988 liability.

The parties did not submit any rebuttal arguments addressing Counts IX (Assumption of Obligations by National Material) or X (Successor Liability of National Material). Consequently, the Court relies on its preliminary analysis of these counts.

As previously ruled, the 1991 Assumption Agreement unambiguously limited National Material's assumption to the obligations associated with PBS One's limited partnership interest in Pielet LP, not the separate contractual obligations of PBS One, such as the Consulting Agreement. Furthermore, the facts do not support a finding of successor liability, as National Material was a separate entity that purchased an equity interest and did not merge with or continue PBS One. Therefore, the trial court's judgments on Counts IX and X in favor of the Plaintiff are reversed.

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's judgment on Count XI in favor of the Plaintiff. The Court reverses the trial court's judgments on Counts IX and X in favor of the Plaintiff and enters judgment in favor of the Defendants on those counts. ## I. COUNTS IX AND X: NATIONAL MATERIAL DID NOT ASSUME THE CONSULTING AGREEMENT

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by the Plaintiff and the Defense. The Court has reviewed the undisputed facts, the trial court's statement of undisputed facts, the preliminary ruling, and the final arguments presented by counsel. The issues on appeal concern the liability of PBS One under Count XI (Survival Statute) and the liability of National Material and NM Holding under Counts IX and X (Assumption of Obligations and Successor Liability). For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the trial court's judgment in favor of the Plaintiff on Count XI and affirms the reversal of the trial court's judgments in favor of the Plaintiff on Counts IX and X.

**II. COUNT XI: LIABILITY OF PBS ONE**

The Court affirms the trial court's entry of summary judgment in favor of the Plaintiff on Count XI. The Defense's arguments regarding the nature of PBS One's liability, the occurrence of a novation, and the application of the Survival Statute are unpersuasive in light of the undisputed facts and controlling Illinois law.

**A. Direct Contractual Liability**

The Defense contends that PBS One's liability was limited to its status as a limited partner and therefore did not constitute direct personal liability. This argument ignores the specific contractual language of the 1988 Assignment Agreement. In that agreement, PBS One, as "Assignee," explicitly agreed to "assume and agree to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was listed as an "Assumed Liability" in Schedule 3.1(m) of the Asset Purchase Agreement.

By executing this agreement, PBS One voluntarily undertook a direct contractual obligation. The fact that PBS One subsequently contributed assets to a limited partnership does not negate the personal liability it incurred through the Assignment Agreement. As held in *Van der Molen v. Georgetown Manor*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 705, 711 (1994), a corporation cannot hide behind its corporate form or subsequent business restructuring to avoid direct contractual liabilities it has expressly assumed. The Defense's reliance on 805 ILCS 215/1502 is misplaced because that statute governs liability arising from the status of being a partner, not liability voluntarily assumed via a separate contract.

**B. Absence of Novation**

The Defense argues that a novation occurred, releasing PBS One from its obligations because Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP for ten years. Under Illinois law, a novation requires "clear intent to extinguish the original obligation." *Bank of America v. L.R. Real Estate Co.*, 58 Ill. App. 3d 138, 145 (1977).

The undisputed testimony of Attorney Michael Zavis, who represented the parties during the restructuring, refutes the existence of such intent. Zavis testified unequivocally that "Nobody ever asked you to get a novation from him? No." and "Nobody ever asked you to get a release of his rights against [Pielet Corp.]? No." He further stated that Arthur "never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor." This testimony establishes that the parties' objective intent was to add an obligor (the limited partnership) rather than to substitute or release the existing obligors. As held in *In re Dissolution of Community Memorial Hospital*, 259 Ill. App. 3d 192, 197 (1994), the law presumes joint and several liability when multiple entities assume an obligation unless the contract explicitly provides otherwise. Here, the contract contained no release language, and the architect of the transaction confirmed no release was intended. Therefore, no novation occurred.

**C. Application of the Survival Statute**

The Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(a), provides that dissolution "shall not take away or impair any remedy available to or against such corporation... for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred prior to such dissolution." PBS One incurred the liability under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 when it executed the Assignment Agreement. Although PBS One was dissolved in 1994 and the breach occurred in 1998, the statute preserves the remedy because the liability was incurred prior to dissolution.

The Defense's argument that the breach was "too remote" is rejected. In *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Fox*, 321 Ill. App. 3d 425, 430 (2001), the court held that the Survival Statute preserves claims for breaches occurring after dissolution where the underlying liability was incurred before dissolution. The purpose of the statute is to prevent corporations from evading pre-existing obligations through dissolution. PBS One cannot utilize dissolution to escape the contractual liability it expressly undertook six years prior.

**III. COUNTS IX AND X: LIABILITY OF NATIONAL MATERIAL**

The Court affirms the reversal of the trial court's judgments in favor of the Plaintiff on Counts IX and X. National Material did not assume the obligations under the Consulting Agreement, nor is it liable as a successor to PBS One.

**A. Scope of the Assumption Agreement**

Count IX alleges that National Material assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement. The 1991 Assumption Agreement, however, explicitly limits National Material's assumption. It states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]."

This language unambiguably restricts the assumption to obligations associated with the limited partnership interest itself. It does not encompass separate contractual obligations that PBS One may have incurred outside of its capacity as a limited partner, such as the direct assumption of the Consulting Agreement in 1988. The Plaintiff has not pointed to any language in the 1991 agreement where National Material expressly assumed the specific debts of PBS One unrelated to the partnership interest. Therefore, Count IX fails.

**B. Successor Liability**

Count X alleges that National Material is liable as the successor to PBS One. Under Illinois law, successor liability requires either: (1) an express or implied assumption of the predecessor's liabilities; (2) a merger or de facto merger; (3) continuation of the predecessor's business and identity; or (4) fraud or inequitable conduct. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 179 Ill. 2d 338, 344-45 (1997).

None of these elements are present. National Material purchased PBS One's equity interest in Pielet LP; it did not purchase PBS One's assets, nor did it merge with PBS One. PBS One was a passive investor with no ongoing business operations to continue. The 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement referring to National Material as a "successor" pertains to rights and obligations within the partnership structure, not general corporate successor liability for all pre-existing debts. Without evidence of a merger, continuation, or fraud, successor liability cannot attach.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's judgment on Count XI in favor of the Plaintiff. The Court affirms the reversal of the trial court's judgments on Counts IX and X, and judgment is entered in favor of the Defendants on those counts.
