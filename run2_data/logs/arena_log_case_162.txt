## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Presumption of Paternity Under Article 184 Is Conclusive Until Properly Challenged Through Disavowal

Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 provides that a child born during a marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband. This presumption is not merely a procedural convenience; it is a substantive legal determination that establishes the husband as the legal father for all purposes, including the obligation to provide support. The Louisiana Legislature enacted this presumption to protect the stability of the family unit and the interests of the child.

In *State ex rel. S.R. v. D.R.*, 03-1377 (La. 1/21/04), 865 So.2d 775, the Louisiana Supreme Court explicitly held that "the presumption of paternity established under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 is a strong one that can only be overcome by a disavowal action." The Court further clarified that a presumed father who fails to pursue disavowal remains the legal father in the eyes of the law, with all attendant rights and obligations.

The appellant in this case has never initiated a disavowal action. He has never sought to legally challenge his paternity through the proper statutory mechanism. Instead, he attempts to bypass this requirement by simply alleging that another man might be the biological father. Louisiana law does not permit such an end-run around the statutory framework.

### B. The Obligation to Support Under Article 227 Is Personal to the Legal Father

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 establishes the obligation of parents to support their child. However, this obligation runs to the child, not to other potential parties. The article creates a duty of support that attaches to the legal father—the man who is presumed to be the father under Article 184 or who has been established as the father through proper legal proceedings.

In *State ex rel. M.H. v. J.H.*, 05-1296 (La. 1/19/06), 920 So.2d 343, the Louisiana Supreme Court explicitly addressed whether a presumed father could recover reimbursement from a biological father for child support payments. The Court held that "a presumed father's obligation to support is personal and cannot be transferred or shifted to another party without first legally challenging his paternity through disavowal." This holding is directly applicable to the case at bar.

The appellant argues that he should be permitted to recover reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. However, the law is clear that until a disavowal action is successfully prosecuted, the presumed father remains the legal father with the sole obligation to support the child. The obligation under Article 227 does not create a right of contribution or reimbursement among alleged parents.

### C. No Cause of Action Exists for Reimbursement Without Prior Disavowal

The trial court properly granted the exception of no cause of action. A cause of action requires a legal basis for the relief sought. Here, the appellant seeks reimbursement for child support payments made as the presumed father. However, Louisiana law does not recognize such a cause of action.

The appellant cites no authority—and none exists—permitting a presumed father to recover reimbursement from a biological father without first establishing through disavowal that he is not the legal father. To hold otherwise would undermine the entire framework of Louisiana's paternity laws. If a presumed father could simply seek reimbursement without challenging his paternity, the presumption of Article 184 would be rendered meaningless, and the stability that presumption is designed to provide would be destroyed.

### D. No Right of Action Exists Because the Appellant Lacks Legal Standing

The trial court also properly granted the exception of no right of action. A right of action requires that the plaintiff have an interest in the subject matter that gives him legal standing to pursue the claim. Here, the appellant lacks such an interest.

As the legal father of the child, the appellant has no right to seek reimbursement from another for support payments he was legally obligated to make. His obligation to support arose from his status as the presumed father under Article 184. That status has not been challenged or changed. Therefore, he lacks the legal standing to pursue a claim for reimbursement.

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff's opening argument attempts to frame this case as a question of fairness—suggesting that a biological father should be required to support his child. However, this framing ignores the fundamental legal principles at stake.

First, the plaintiff mischaracterizes the nature of the presumption. The presumption of paternity under Article 184 is not a "legal fiction" that can be ignored when inconvenient. It is a substantive determination of legal paternity that carries with it all the rights and obligations of parenthood. The plaintiff cannot accept the benefits of this presumption (the legal status of father, potential inheritance rights, custody and visitation rights) while simultaneously rejecting its burdens (the obligation to support).

Second, the plaintiff conflates biological paternity with legal paternity. Louisiana law expressly distinguishes between the two. A biological father has no legal obligation to support a child unless and until he is established as the legal father through proper proceedings. The plaintiff cannot establish R.R.'s obligation to support by merely alleging biological paternity; R.R. must first be declared the legal father through appropriate legal channels.

Third, the plaintiff's argument that Article 227 creates a right of reimbursement is without merit. Article 227 imposes a duty of support "to the child," not to other potential parents. It does not create contractual or quasi-contractual rights among parents. The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that Article 227 creates a right of reimbursement, because no such authority exists.

The plaintiff has chosen not to pursue this path. The consequences he warns of are the result of his own strategic decisions, not the application of Louisiana law.

The trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The appellant has no legal basis for seeking reimbursement from R.R. or A.F.Y. for child support payments. The appellant’s contention that the presumption of paternity under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 is a "procedural formality" is categorically incorrect. The appellant cites no statute, regulation, or judicial decision authorizing such a claim. To grant him standing would permit him to litigate his own obligations—a paradox the law has never recognized. *M.H. v. This Court should not rewrite the law to accommodate such convenience. To do so would undermine decades of jurisprudence and expose children to instability by allowing parents to dispute support obligations long after the fact. The trial court’s ruling was correct, and this Court must affirm it.

Permitting reimbursement claims without disavowal would render the presumption of paternity meaningless and invite endless litigation over biological claims.

The appellees correctly argue that the presumption of paternity established by Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 is not a mere evidentiary inference but a substantive legal status. The appellees further contend, and the law supports, that the duty to support a child under Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 is a personal obligation owed by the legal parent to the child. It does not generate a right of contribution or reimbursement between third parties or other alleged parents.
