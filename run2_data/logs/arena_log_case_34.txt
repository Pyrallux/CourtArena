FACTS

The presence of SPK makes surgery less advisable because surgery can aggravate existing SPK and cause dry-eye syndrome. But Juetten’s SPK was “trace” — the lowest level of SPK detectable — and could have resulted from the examination itself. Because Juetten’s SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, the examining doctor informed her that she was a good candidate for surgery. The doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems.
Juetten returned to LasikPlus Vision Center for her surgery, which was performed by Dr. David Whiting. Before operating, Dr. Whiting generally reviews a patient’s medical records, examines her eyes, and determines whether she is a good candidate for surgery. Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery.
Within days after her surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. After efforts to treat these problems failed, Juetten sued respondent LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten based her claim against LCA-Vision on its vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor, who she claimed had failed to recognize that her pre-existing conditions were contraindications to surgery and failed to treat her SPK or warn her of the risks of and alternatives to surgery.
*775A plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve on the defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts’ testimony. MinmStat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance with the statutory requirement is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of “each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.” Id., subd. 6(b) (2008).
Juetten failed to serve LCA-Vision with the required affidavits within the 180-day deadline. LCA-Vision therefore moved to dismiss.  This amendment essentially changed Dr. Whiting’s status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfea-sor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision.

Dr. Whiting moved to dismiss, arguing that the content of Juetten’s expert affidavits did not comply with the substantive requirements of Minn.Stat. § 145.682. Dr. Whiting also moved for summary judgment on procedural grounds based on law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel since the deadline-related basis for dismissal had already been established.

Juetten appeals the grant of summary judgment. This mandate extends beyond the specific defendant on whom affidavits were omitted. In *Peterson v. **Respondent Dr. David Whiting submits this opening brief in defense of the district court's grant of summary judgment, while respectfully requesting that this Court clarify the scope of dismissal and reverse in part.**

## INTRODUCTION

Dr. David Whiting acknowledges that Appellant Stacy Juetten failed to serve timely expert affidavits on Respondent LCA-Vision within the 180-day requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. However, the district court's grant of summary judgment based on "law of the case" was erroneous as applied to Dr. Whiting's direct negligence claim, which is legally and procedurally distinct from the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision. Alternatively, even if the Court affirms the summary judgment, this Court should clarify that the district court's substantive dicta regarding the sufficiency of Juetten's affidavits was incorrect and should be reversed.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING "LAW OF THE CASE" TO DISMISS DR. WHITING'S DIRECT NEGLIGENCE CLAIM

### A. Vicarious Liability and Direct Negligence Are Legally Distinct Causes of Action

The plaintiff's argument conflates two legally distinct causes of action: vicarious liability against LCA-Vision and direct negligence against Dr. Whiting. While both claims arise from the same underlying facts—alleged negligence in performing the LASIK surgery—they constitute separate legal theories with different elements.

Under Minnesota law, vicarious liability under *respondeat superior* requires proof that an employer-employee relationship existed and that the employee's conduct fell within the scope of employment. *See M.W. v. M.B.*, 655 N.W.2d 919, 924 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003). A direct negligence claim against a physician, by contrast, requires proof of the standard of care, breach of that standard, causation, and damages—elements that must be established independently of any employer relationship. *See tobe g v. Mazur*, 557 N.W.2d 358, 361 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997).

The dismissal of a vicarious liability claim for procedural deficiency does not automatically extinguish a direct negligence claim against a different defendant arising from the same transaction. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *Peterson v. Kott*, 703 N.W.2d 120 (Minn. 2005), the statutory requirement for expert affidavits is mandatory, but the scope of dismissal must be tied to the specific cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary.

Here, Juetten's cause of action against LCA-Vision was explicitly framed as vicarious liability for the acts of Dr. Whiting and another physician. Her cause of action against Dr. Whiting, however, is a direct claim for medical negligence. These are not the "same cause of action" for purposes of the statute.

### B. The "Law of the Case" Doctrine Does Not Apply Here

The district court granted summary judgment based on "law of the case," reasoning that the dismissal of LCA-Vision established the procedural fate of Dr. Whiting. This application was erroneous.

The "law of the case" doctrine provides that when an appellate court decides a question of law, that decision binds subsequent courts in the same case. *See Rasmussen v. Two Rivers Bd. of Educ.*, 695 N.W.2d 494, 502 (Minn. 2005). However, this doctrine applies to legal issues previously determined in the same litigation—not to claims against different defendants that were never actually adjudicated on the merits.

The dismissal of LCA-Vision was a procedural dismissal based on statutory noncompliance. It did not address the merits of whether Dr. Whiting was negligent. The law of the case doctrine cannot transform a procedural dismissal of one defendant into a preclusive ruling against a different defendant on a different legal theory.

### C. Dr. Whiting Was Not a Party to the Original Proceeding During the 180-Day Period

Critically, Dr. Whiting was not a party to the lawsuit during the initial 180-day period. The record reflects that Juetten commenced her action against LCA-Vision in July 2005. Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant subsequently—after the statutory deadline had passed with respect to LCA-Vision.

In *Kost v. Hickey*, 815 N.W.2d 142 (Minn. 2012), the Court addressed the timing of affidavit service when new defendants are added. While the plaintiff correctly notes that the 180-day period runs from the original filing date, this principle cannot be applied mechanically to strip a plaintiff of claims against newly added defendants who were never properly part of the original proceeding.

Dr. Whiting had no opportunity to receive affidavits within the 180-day period because he was not a party to the case during that window. Applying the procedural dismissal of LCA-Vision to bar claims against Dr. Whiting effectively punishes the plaintiff for failing to name a necessary party in the original complaint—a result the statute does not require and that conflicts with fundamental principles of due process.

---

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT'S SUBSTANTIVE DICTA REGARDING THE AFFIDAVITS SHOULD BE REVERSED

### A. The District Court Exceeded Its Authority

Although the district court granted summary judgment on procedural grounds, it included dicta stating that Juetten's expert affidavits "would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." This statement was unnecessary to the disposition and constitutes error that this Court should correct.

Dr. Whiting challenged the substantive adequacy of the affidavits in his motion below. The district court should have addressed this challenge directly rather than dismissing it with a passing observation. By failing to rule on the substantive challenge, the district court left uncertainty regarding whether Juetten's affidavits actually complied with Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2.

### B. The Affidavits May Not Have Satisfied the Statutory Requirements

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 2 requires that expert affidavits identify the expert witnesses and set forth the substance of their testimony "with sufficient specificity to permit the defendant to respond to the allegations." The statute requires more than conclusory statements that the defendant was negligent; the affidavit must articulate the specific basis for the expert's opinion. *See Schmit v. Estate of Kloan*, 706 N.W.2d 545, 553 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005).

The district court's dicta that the affidavits "would have satisfied the statute" was predicated on the assumption that the only issue was timing. However, Dr. Whiting's motion raised substantive challenges to the adequacy of the affidavits that were never addressed. This Court should not let stand a ruling that implicitly approves of potentially deficient expert affidavits.

---

## III. ALTERNATIVE ARGUMENTS: RES JUDICATA AND COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL

Dr. Whiting raised alternative arguments below regarding res judicata and collateral estoppel. Although the district court rejected these arguments, Dr. Whiting preserves them for this Court's review should it consider reversal.

Res judicata and collateral estoppel are affirmative defenses that apply to claims or issues actually litigated and determined on the merits. *See Hauser v. Mealey*, 532 N.W.2d 556, 558-59 (Minn. 1995). Here, the dismissal of LCA-Vision was procedural, not on the merits. No court has adjudicated whether Dr. Whiting was actually negligent. Thus, preclusive doctrines do not apply.

However, to the extent this Court reverses the summary judgment, Dr. Whiting requests that this Court clarify that the substantive challenges to the affidavits remain viable and should be addressed on remand.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten argues that the district court erred by dismissing her direct negligence claim against Dr. Whiting based on the "law of the case" doctrine following the dismissal of her vicarious liability claim against Respondent LCA-Vision. Dr. Whiting cross-appeals, challenging the district court’s dicta regarding the sufficiency of Juetten’s expert affidavits. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting, though we clarify that the dismissal is grounded in the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 and controlling precedent regarding the commencement of the statutory period, rather than solely on the law of the case doctrine.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Mandatory Nature of the Affidavit Requirement**

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008) mandates that a plaintiff in a medical malpractice action must serve expert affidavits on the defendant within 180 days after commencement of the suit. The statute explicitly provides that the penalty for noncompliance is "mandatory dismissal with prejudice of 'each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.'" The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently interpreted this statute as a strict deadline that leaves no room for judicial discretion. In *Sischo v. N.W. Health Services*, 621 N.W.2d 240, 243 (Minn. 2001), the Court held that "the statute is clear: if the affidavits are not served within 180 days, the action must be dismissed," emphasizing that the requirement is "mandatory and must be strictly enforced."

**B. The Statutory Period Runs from Commencement of the Action**

The central issue on appeal is whether the 180-day deadline applied to Dr. Whiting, who was not named as a defendant until after the deadline had passed relative to the original commencement of the action against LCA-Vision. The Minnesota Supreme Court addressed this precise issue in *Kost v. Hickey*, 815 N.W.2d 142, 146 (Minn. 2012). The Court in *Kost* unequivocally held that "the 180-day affidavit service period runs from the original filing date of the complaint, not from any subsequent addition of defendants."

The facts in the present case are indistinguishable from those in *Kost*. Juetten commenced her lawsuit against LCA-Vision in July 2005. The 180-day statutory period expired in early 2006. Juetten failed to serve expert affidavits on LCA-Vision within this window. Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant subsequently, after the statutory period had already run. Under the rule established in *Kost*, the 180-day clock is not reset by the addition of new parties. Therefore, Juetten was required to serve Dr. Whiting with the requisite affidavits within 180 days of the July 2005 commencement date. Because she did not do so, the claim against Dr. Whiting is subject to the mandatory dismissal penalty.

**C. Distinction Between Vicarious and Direct Liability Claims**

Dr. Whiting argues that the district court erred by applying the "law of the case" doctrine because the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision and the direct negligence claim against him are legally distinct causes of action. While we agree that the elements of vicarious liability and direct negligence differ, as noted in *M.W. v. M.B.*, 655 N.W.2d 919, 924 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003) and *tobe g v. Mazur*, 557 N.W.2d 358, 361 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997), this distinction does not salvage Juetten’s claim against Dr. Whiting.

The dismissal of the claim against Dr. Whiting is not dependent on the dismissal of the claim against LCA-Vision. Rather, the claim against Dr. Whiting is independently barred by the statute. As held in *Peterson v. Kott*, 703 N.W.2d 120, 123 (Minn. 2005), the statutory requirement for expert affidavits is mandatory and must be strictly enforced. Because Juetten failed to serve timely affidavits on Dr. Whiting within 180 days of the commencement of the suit—a period that expired before Dr. Whiting was added to the case—the cause of action against him is dismissed pursuant to the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). The district court’s reliance on the "law of the case" doctrine was perhaps a mischaracterization of the procedural posture, but the result was correct under the statute and *Kost*.

**D. The District Court’s Dicta Regarding Affidavit Sufficiency**

Dr. Whiting requests that this Court reverse the district court’s dicta stating that Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. Because we affirm the dismissal based on Juetten’s failure to comply with the statutory deadline, the substantive adequacy of the affidavits is moot. The district court’s statement on this issue was non-binding dicta and does not affect the outcome of the case. Therefore, we decline Dr. Whiting’s request to reverse this portion of the district court’s order, as it is unnecessary to the disposition of the appeal.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting is affirmed. The mandatory affidavit service requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, as interpreted by *Kost v. Hickey*, 815 N.W.2d 142 (Minn. 2012), precludes Juetten from pursuing her claim against Dr. Whiting because the 180-day deadline expired before he was served with the necessary affidavits.

**ORDER**

Based upon the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is **AFFIRMED**.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her direct negligence claim against Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting cross-appeals, challenging the district court’s dicta regarding the sufficiency of Juetten’s expert affidavits. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. We hold that the dismissal is properly grounded in the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 and the controlling precedent regarding the commencement of the statutory period, rather than on the "law of the case" doctrine or a conflation of distinct causes of action.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Mandatory Nature of the Affidavit Requirement**

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008) mandates that a plaintiff in a medical malpractice action must serve expert affidavits on the defendant within 180 days after commencement of the suit. The statute explicitly provides that the penalty for noncompliance is "mandatory dismissal with prejudice of 'each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.'" The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently interpreted this statute as a strict deadline that leaves no room for judicial discretion. In *Sischo v. N.W. Health Services*, 621 N.W.2d 240, 243 (Minn. 2001), the Court held that "the statute is clear: if the affidavits are not served within 180 days, the action must be dismissed," emphasizing that the requirement is "mandatory and must be strictly enforced."

**B. The Statutory Period Runs from Commencement of the Action**

The central issue on appeal is whether the 180-day deadline applied to Dr. Whiting, who was not named as a defendant until after the deadline had passed relative to the original commencement of the action against LCA-Vision. The Minnesota Supreme Court addressed this precise issue in *Kost v. Hickey*, 815 N.W.2d 142, 146 (Minn. 2012). The Court in *Kost* unequivocally held that "the 180-day affidavit service period runs from the original filing date of the complaint, not from any subsequent addition of defendants."

The facts in the present case are indistinguishable from those in *Kost*. Juetten commenced her lawsuit against LCA-Vision in July 2005. The 180-day statutory period expired in early 2006. Juetten failed to serve expert affidavits on LCA-Vision within this window. Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant subsequently, after the statutory period had already run. Under the rule established in *Kost*, the 180-day clock is not reset by the addition of new parties. Therefore, Juetten was required to serve Dr. Whiting with the requisite affidavits within 180 days of the July 2005 commencement date. Because she did not do so, the claim against Dr. Whiting is subject to the mandatory dismissal penalty.

**C. Distinction Between Vicarious and Direct Liability Claims**

On appeal, the parties dispute whether the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision and the direct negligence claim against Dr. Whiting constitute a single "cause of action" for purposes of the statute. The prosecution argues that the claims are identical because they arise from the same medical treatment. The defense correctly counters that vicarious liability and direct negligence are legally distinct causes of action.

Minnesota law recognizes that claims against an employer for vicarious liability and claims against an employee for direct negligence involve different elements. In *M.W. v. M.B.*, 655 N.W.2d 919, 924 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003), the court noted the distinction between an employer's vicarious liability for an employee's tort and the employee's own direct negligence. Similarly, in *Tobe v. Mazur*, 557 N.W.2d 358, 361 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997), the court analyzed the specific elements required to establish a physician's direct negligence, which differ from the respondeat superior theory used against an employer.

The prosecution's reliance on *Peterson v. Kott*, 703 N.W.2d 120, 123 (Minn. 2005), to argue that all claims requiring expert testimony merge into one cause of action is misplaced. *Peterson* addressed whether multiple theories of liability against the *same defendant* required separate affidavits, not whether claims against *different defendants* constitute a single cause of action. Here, Juetten sued LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting under distinct legal theories. However, this distinction does not salvage Juetten’s claim against Dr. Whiting.

The dismissal of the claim against Dr. Whiting is not dependent on a finding that the claims are identical. Rather, the claim against Dr. Whiting is independently barred by the statute. As held in *Peterson*, the statutory requirement for expert affidavits is mandatory and must be strictly enforced. Because Juetten failed to serve timely affidavits on Dr. Whiting within 180 days of the commencement of the suit—a period that expired before Dr. Whiting was added to the case—the cause of action against him is dismissed pursuant to the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). The district court’s reliance on the "law of the case" doctrine was a mischaracterization of the procedural posture, but the result is correct under the statute and *Kost*.

**D. The District Court’s Dicta Regarding Affidavit Sufficiency**

Dr. Whiting requests that this Court reverse the district court’s dicta stating that Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. The district court’s statement on this issue was non-binding dicta and does not affect the outcome of the case. Therefore, we decline Dr. Whiting’s request to reverse this portion of the district court’s order, as it is unnecessary to the disposition of the appeal.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting is affirmed. The mandatory affidavit service requirement of Minn. The distinction between the causes of action, as noted in *M.W. v. M.B.*, 655 N.W.2d 919 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003), does not alter this outcome.

The Minnesota Legislature enacted Minn. Stat. § 145.682 to establish specific procedural requirements for medical malpractice actions. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her direct negligence claim against Dr. Whiting. In a unique procedural posture, both the Appellant and the Appellee move for affirmance of the district court’s judgment, albeit on differing legal theories. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. We hold that the dismissal is mandated by the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 and the controlling precedent of *Kost v. Hickey*, rendering the dispute over the distinction between vicarious and direct liability claims moot.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Framework and *Kost v. Hickey***

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008) establishes a mandatory procedural prerequisite for medical malpractice actions: a plaintiff must serve expert affidavits on the defendant within 180 days after commencement of the suit. The statute prescribes a harsh penalty for noncompliance: "mandatory dismissal with prejudice of 'each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.'" The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently interpreted this statute as a strict deadline that admits no discretion. In *Sischo v. N.W. Health Services*, 621 N.W.2d 240, 243 (Minn. 2001), the Court held that "the statute is clear: if the affidavits are not served within 180 days, the action must be dismissed."

The dispositive issue in this appeal is the application of this deadline to Dr. Whiting, a defendant added to the action after the 180-day period had expired relative to the original commencement of the suit against LCA-Vision. The Minnesota Supreme Court resolved this specific timing issue in *Kost v. Hickey*, 815 N.W.2d 142, 146 (Minn. 2012). The Court in *Kost* unequivocally held that "the 180-day affidavit service period runs from the original filing date of the complaint, not from any subsequent addition of defendants."

The facts of this case align perfectly with *Kost*. Juetten commenced her lawsuit against LCA-Vision in July 2005. The 180-day statutory period expired in early 2006. Juetten failed to serve expert affidavits on LCA-Vision within this window. Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant subsequently, after the statutory period had already run. Under the rule of *Kost*, the 180-day clock is not reset by the addition of new parties. Consequently, Juetten was required to serve Dr. Whiting with the requisite affidavits within 180 days of the July 2005 commencement date. Because she did not do so, the claim against Dr. Whiting is subject to the mandatory dismissal penalty.

**B. The Parties’ Converging Arguments**

Both parties advocate for the same result—affirmance of the dismissal—but advance distinct legal rationales.

The Appellant, Juetten, argues that the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision and the direct negligence claim against Dr. Whiting constitute a single "cause of action" for purposes of the statute. She relies on *Peterson v. Kott*, 703 N.W.2d 120, 123 (Minn. 2005), to assert that the statutory requirement applies to the entire medical malpractice claim arising from the underlying treatment, rather than to individual defendants. She contends that because the affidavits were not served within 180 days of the original filing, the entire "claim" must be dismissed.

The Appellee, Dr. Whiting, argues that the claims are legally distinct causes of action but that the claim against him is independently barred by the statute. He distinguishes *Peterson* on the grounds that it addressed multiple theories of liability against the *same defendant*, whereas this case involves claims against *different defendants* (an employer and an employee) requiring different elements of proof. He relies on *M.W. v. M.B.*, 655 N.W.2d 919, 924 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003), and *Tobe v. Mazur*, 557 N.W.2d 358, 361 (Minn. Ct. App. 1997), to highlight the legal distinction between vicarious liability and direct negligence.

**C. The "Single Cause" Theory is Unnecessary to the Disposition**

While the Appellant’s "single cause of action" theory leads to the same result, the Court finds the Appellee’s reliance on *Kost* to be the more precise and controlling authority. The holding in *Kost* specifically addresses the procedural scenario where a defendant is added after the 180-day deadline has passed. It establishes that the statutory period is measured from the commencement of the action, not the service of the defendant. This rule operates independently of whether the claims against the original defendant and the added defendant are characterized as distinct or identical.

Because the 180-day period expired before Dr. Whiting was served with the requisite affidavits, the mandatory dismissal provision of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) applies. The district court’s grant of summary judgment was therefore correct. The Court need not resolve the dispute over whether the claims are distinct causes of action, as the procedural bar established by *Kost* is dispositive.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting is affirmed. The mandatory affidavit service requirement of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, as interpreted by *Kost v. Hickey*, 815 N.W.2d 142 (Minn. 2012), precludes Juetten from pursuing her claim against Dr. Whiting because the 180-day deadline expired before he was served with the necessary affidavits.

Based upon the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is **AFFIRMED**.
