# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Wisconsin

### Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Appellant v. City of Appleton, Respondent

---

## INTRODUCTION

Appellant Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this defense opening response to affirm the arguments presented in its initial brief and to rebut the City's characterization of the issues in this appeal. The City of Appleton urges this Court to affirm the circuit court's denial of Auto-Owners' motion for an injunction prohibiting the raze order. However, the City's arguments overlook critical statutory language, ignore fundamental principles of administrative law, and ask this Court to sanction a procedure that fundamentally undermines the rights of property owners and insurers alike. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the circuit court's order and grant Auto-Owners' requested relief.

---

## I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE RAZING OF A BUILDING DAMAGED BY A SUDDEN FIRE

### A. Plain Language of the Statute

The City's argument that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 authorizes raze orders for fire-damaged structures ignores the plain language of the statute. Section 66.0413(1)(b)1. authorizes a raze order when a building is "out of repair." However, the statute must be read in context with its other provisions to give effect to the Legislature's intent.

Wisconsin Stat. § 66.0413(1)(b)1. provides that a raze order may issue when a building is "out of repair" such that it is "unsafe or unsanitary for human occupancy or for the purpose for which it may lawfully be used." The statute, however, was designed primarily to address buildings that have deteriorated over time due to neglect, age, or chronic neglect of maintenance. See *Town of Wilson v. City of Sheboygan*, 200 Wis. 2d 274, 282, 546 N.W.2d 192 (Ct. App. 1996) (holding that the raze order statute addresses "structures which have fallen into disrepair" and are "dangerous to the public").

The City relies heavily on *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), but that case is distinguishable. In *Hickey*, the building in question had been abandoned for an extended period and was in a state of chronic disrepair from long-term neglect. The court in *Hickey* specifically noted that the building had "been allowed to deteriorate over a period of years" and that "the City had received numerous complaints about the property." Here, by contrast, the McLartys' home was damaged by a sudden, catastrophic event—an overnight fire on August 26, 2015. The home was a well-maintained residence that became damaged in a single incident. This distinction is critical.

The statute's legislative history supports the interpretation that it was designed to address chronic conditions, not sudden events. The raze order statute is part of a broader municipal code enforcement scheme aimed at addressing blighted properties that diminish neighborhood values and pose ongoing risks. A sudden fire does not create a blighted property; it creates a damaged property that may be repaired.

### B. The City's Interpretation Produces Absurd Results

If this Court accepts the City's interpretation that any building damaged by fire is automatically subject to raze orders, absurd results follow. Under the City's theory, a minor kitchen fire that causes $10,000 in damage to a $300,000 home would authorize raze proceedings—a result the Legislature clearly never intended. The statute was designed to address buildings that are fundamentally unsafe due to their condition, not buildings that have suffered temporary damage from an insurable event.

As this Court noted in *Kenosha v. J.P. Carruth & Co.*, 251 Wis. 2d 592, 602, 642 N.W.2d 469 (Ct. App. 2002), statutes must be interpreted to avoid absurd results. The City's interpretation would convert the raze order statute into an insurance enforcement mechanism, effectively allowing municipalities to force total losses on insurers by issuing raze orders after any fire.

---

## II. THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE BECAUSE THE HOME COULD BE REPAIRED AT REASONABLE COST

### A. The Statutory Framework

Under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c), a raze order may only issue if "the cost of repairing the building would exceed 50 percent of the value of the building." This provision establishes a clear numerical threshold that must be met before a raze order can issue. The purpose of this provision is to ensure that buildings that can be reasonably repaired are not summarily destroyed.

### B. The Evidence Demonstrates Repairs Were Feasible

The undisputed facts establish that the McLartys' home could be repaired at reasonable cost. The ServPro estimate of $112,850 must be compared to the home's value. However, the record does not contain a proper appraisal establishing the home's value at the time of the fire. The circuit court appeared to assume the home was worth approximately $200,000 based on the policy limit of $287,500, but this assumption is unsupported by the record.

Critically, Auto-Owners presented evidence that the ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 in costs for smoke remediation—costs that should not be considered when calculating repair feasibility under the statute.

### C. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Must Be Excluded from Repair Cost Calculations

The City's argument that all fire-related damage must be included in the repair calculation misreads the statute. Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) refers to the "cost of repairing the building"—not the cost of remediating all damage caused by a fire.

Smoke damage remediation is fundamentally different from structural repair. Smoke remediation involves cleaning, deodorizing, and cosmetic work to remove smoke residue from surfaces. It does not involve repairing load-bearing elements or addressing conditions that make a building unsafe or unsanitary. Similarly, water damage from fire suppression efforts may cause secondary damage, but this damage is often remediate-able through standard drying and restoration processes.

In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), the Court addressed what costs must be included in the repair calculation. However, the City misreads *Stein* as standing for the proposition that all fire-related costs must be included. To the contrary, *Stein* held that "the cost of repairing a building includes all costs necessary to return the building to a condition that complies with applicable building codes." The key phrase is "necessary to return the building to a condition that complies with applicable building codes."

Smoke and water damage remediation, while perhaps necessary to make a home habitable and pleasant, is not necessary to bring a building into compliance with building codes. Building codes address structural integrity, fire safety, electrical systems, plumbing, and similar matters. They do not require removal of all smoke odors or treatment of all water stains.

If this Court accepts the City's interpretation that smoke and water damage must be included in the repair calculation, municipalities could effectively force raze orders on any home with significant smoke or water damage, regardless of whether the structural repairs are minimal. This interpretation would gut the protection that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) provides to property owners.

Excluding the $76,500 in smoke remediation costs, the actual structural repair costs were approximately $36,350—far less than half the home's value even under the most conservative valuation.

## III. THE RAZE ORDER WAS ISSUED AS A RESULT OF THE INSURED'S INQUIRY, NOT AN INDEPENDENT MUNICIPAL DETERMINATION

### A. The Procedural History

The facts establish that the raze order was not issued as a result of routine municipal enforcement. Rather, it was issued in response to an inquiry from the McLartys' attorney. After receiving the ServPro estimate, the McLartys' attorney contacted Inspector Craanen and "requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order for the building."

This procedural history is deeply troubling. The raze order statute authorizes municipalities to issue raze orders to protect public safety—not to respond to inquiries from property owners seeking to trigger insurance losses. When a property owner requests a raze order, the municipality should exercise extreme caution, as the requestor has every incentive to see the building demolished.

### B. The City Acted Arbitrarily

The circuit court found that Inspector Craanen "acted professionally and in good faith" when issuing the raze order. However, good faith does not cure arbitrary action. The facts demonstrate that Craanen:

1. Did not take any action based on the initial fire report
2. Issued the raze order only after the McLartys' attorney requested one
3. Did not independently verify the accuracy of the ServPro estimate
4. Did not personally inspect the property until after the raze order was issued

This sequence of events demonstrates that the raze order was not the product of independent municipal judgment—it was a response to a request from the property owner. This Court should hold that when a raze order is issued at the request of the property owner, heightened scrutiny is required.

---

## IV. THE BUILDING INSPECTOR FAILED TO PERSONALLY INSPECT THE PREMISES

### A. The Statutory Requirement

While Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not explicitly require a personal inspection, the statute contemplates that the building inspector will make an independent determination based on personal knowledge or investigation. A raze order is an extreme measure that deprives a property owner of valuable property. Before issuing such an order, the inspector must have a factual basis for concluding that the building meets the statutory criteria.

### B. The City v. Stein Case Does Not Support the City's Position

The City relies on *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that a personal inspection is not required. However, *Stein* is distinguishable. In *Stein*, the inspector had driven by the property and observed visible damage from the street. The Court noted that "the extent of the damage was evident from the inspector's observations and from reliable reports."

Here, by contrast, Inspector Craanen admitted that he "did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order." He issued the raze order based solely on the ServPro estimate, without any independent verification. He had no personal knowledge of the property's condition.

The distinction is critical. In *Stein*, the inspector had personally observed the property and supplemented that observation with professional reports. Here, Craanen had no personal observation whatsoever. He blindly relied on an estimate prepared by a restoration company hired by the property owner—an entity with obvious incentives to inflate repair costs.

### C. The Importance of Independent Verification

A raze order permanently deprives a property owner of valuable real estate. Before issuing such an order, the inspector must exercise independent judgment. Relying solely on an estimate provided by the property owner's contractor is insufficient. The inspector should have:

1. Visited the property to observe the damage firsthand
2. Obtained an independent estimate from a contractor of the City's choosing
3. Verified that the property actually met the statutory criteria for a raze order

The City's argument that inspectors may rely on "professional estimates" conflates reliance on professional estimates with reliance on one-sided estimates prepared by the property owner's contractor. The statute requires the inspector to exercise independent judgment, not to rubber-stamp the property owner's request.

---

## V. THE CITY'S ARGUMENTS IGNORE FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF ADMINISTRATIVE LAW

### A. The Raze Order Must Be Supported by Substantial Evidence

Under Wisconsin law, administrative agencies must base their decisions on substantial evidence. See *Hook v. State Racing Comm'n*, 88 Wis. 2d 334, 348, 276 N.W.2d 299 (1979). A raze order is an administrative action that must be supported by adequate factual findings.

Here, the record establishes that Inspector Craanen issued the raze order without conducting any independent investigation. He did not inspect the property, verify the estimate, or make any findings beyond reviewing a document prepared by the property owner's contractor. This is not substantial evidence.

### B. The Raze Order Must Be Reasonable

A raze order must be reasonable under the circumstances. See *City of Madison v. Becco*, 127 Wis. 2d 1, 8, 375 N.W.2d 227 (Ct. App. 1985). Reasonableness requires that the agency consider all relevant factors and reach a conclusion that a reasonable agency could reach—not that the Court agrees with the agency's decision.

Here, the raze order was unreasonable because:

1. It was issued without personal inspection
2. It was based on an unverified estimate from the property owner's contractor
3. It was issued in response to the property owner's request, not as part of routine enforcement
4. It included costs (smoke remediation) that are not properly considered under the statute

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for an injunction and remand with instructions to grant the requested relief. The raze order was invalid because:

1. The raze order statute does not authorize the razing of buildings damaged by sudden fires
2. The raze order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at reasonable cost
3. Smoke and water damage remediation must be excluded from the repair cost calculation
4. The building inspector failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing the raze order
5. The raze order was issued as a result of the insured's inquiry, not an independent municipal determination

The City of Appleton's arguments, if accepted, would eviscerate the protections that Wisconsin law provides to property owners and would convert the raze order statute into a tool for forcing insurance losses. This Court should reject such an interpretation and reverse the circuit court's decision.

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the order of the Circuit Court denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the Circuit Court's order.**

The City correctly cites *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. The statute's 50% repair-cost threshold serves as the safeguard against the "absurd results" Auto-Owners predicts, ensuring that minor fire damage does not trigger a raze order.

**II. CALCULATION OF REPAIR COSTS**

Auto-Owners contends that smoke and water damage remediation costs must be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c). The Court rejects this interpretation. As the City argues, *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. Therefore, Auto-Owners' arguments are rejected, and the Circuit Court's order denying the injunction is affirmed.

## I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZING A BUILDING DAMAGED BY A SUDDEN FIRE

The plaintiff contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies to sudden fire damage, citing *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996). However, the defense argues that the plaintiff's interpretation stretches the statute beyond its evident purpose and leads to absurd results that the legislature could not have intended.

In *Hickey*, the court addressed a building with ongoing code violations that had persisted over time. The court in that case stated that "the statute does not require that the condition of the building have existed for any particular length of time," but this statement must be read in context—the building at issue in *Hickey* had a history of deterioration and code violations, not sudden catastrophic damage from a one-time event. The defense does not dispute that the statute can apply to fire damage, but rather contends that the statute was designed primarily to address structures suffering from chronic neglect or long-term deterioration that poses ongoing risks to the community.

The distinction between chronic conditions and sudden events is not merely semantic—it reflects the statute's core purpose of addressing blight and dangerous conditions that develop over time and give municipalities opportunity to notice and address them. A sudden fire is fundamentally different from a building that has been allowed to deteriorate over months or years. In the fire context, the damage is catastrophic but isolated, and the homeowner has every incentive to repair or rebuild. The statute's 50% threshold was designed as a safeguard for structures that have effectively become economic liabilities to the community due to neglect, not for homes that have suffered sudden, accidental damage where the homeowner has every reason to rebuild.

The plaintiff's argument that the statute focuses on "current condition" is correct, but this argument proves too much. Every sudden disaster—fire, storm, flood—creates a "current condition" of damage. If the plaintiff's interpretation were correct, any hurricane-damaged home with repair costs exceeding 50% of value could be razed without regard to whether the homeowner wishes to rebuild. This cannot be the statute's intent. The defense submits that the statute was enacted to address the chronic problem of blighted properties that owners abandon or neglect, not to provide a mechanism for municipalities to force demolition of homes damaged in sudden, accidental events where the owner is prepared to repair.

The plaintiff argues that smoke and water damage remediation costs must be included in the "cost of repair" calculation under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c), relying on *Village of Shorewood v. In *Stein*, the court held that "cost of repairing the building" includes all costs necessary to restore the structure to a condition that complies with applicable building codes and is safe for occupancy. However, the defense argues that this interpretation must be read in conjunction with the statute's overall scheme. The purpose of the raze order statute is to address buildings that are unsafe or unsanitary—not merely buildings that require cleaning or cosmetic work.

Smoke and water damage remediation, while certainly important to making a home habitable, is fundamentally different from structural repairs necessary to make a building safe. Smoke remediation involves cleaning, deodorizing, and removing smoke residue from surfaces. Water damage remediation involves drying, mold treatment, and replacing damaged materials. These are restoration activities, not structural repairs. A building could be perfectly safe from a structural standpoint yet require smoke or water remediation to be habitable.

The defense submits that the plaintiff conflates "habitability" with "safety." The statute specifically uses the phrase "unsafe or unsanitary" in Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(b)1., not "uninhabitable" or "requiring remediation." Smoke residue and water damage, while creating unpleasant conditions and potential health concerns, do not typically render a building "unsafe" in the sense that the statute contemplates. More importantly, the defense notes that including smoke and water remediation costs in the calculation creates a perverse incentive. Under the plaintiff's interpretation, any home with significant smoke or water damage will automatically exceed the 50% threshold, regardless of whether the structural damage is minimal. **Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the order of the Circuit Court denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the Circuit Court's order.**

Auto-Owners' attempt to limit repair costs to structural repairs ignores the statutory mandate to consider all necessary work for safety and habitability. Excluding these essential remediation costs would allow a structure to remain standing despite being uninhabitable, contravening the statute's evident purpose.

**III. INSPECTION AND REASONABLENESS OF THE RAZE ORDER**

Auto-Owners argues the raze order was unreasonable because the building inspector did not personally inspect the premises and relied solely on the estimate provided by the property owner's contractor. The defense asserts that *Village of Shorewood v. Stein* requires a personal inspection and that reliance on estimates is permissible only in addition to such an inspection. The Court is not persuaded.

The City correctly cites *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), for the principle that an inspector may rely on reliable reports and professional estimates without conducting an independent on-site inspection, provided they act in good faith. Furthermore, under *Hook v. State Racing Comm'n*, 88 Wis. 2d 334, 276 N.W.2d 299 (1979), administrative decisions are valid if supported by substantial evidence. The record shows Inspector Craanen reviewed the ServPro estimate and determined the costs exceeded the statutory threshold. The Circuit Court explicitly found that Craanen acted professionally and in good faith. Auto-Owners' argument that this reliance was arbitrary or violated due process is unsupported by the precedent, which permits the use of expert assessments to determine compliance with building codes and safety standards. The defense's policy concerns regarding the procedure do not override the inspector's statutory authority and the circuit court's factual findings.

**IV. THE RAZE ORDER AND THE INSURED'S INQUIRY**

Finally, Auto-Owners argues that the raze order is invalid because it was issued at the behest of the insured rather than through municipal initiative, characterizing the order as a mechanism to obtain an insurance windfall. The City counters that the source of the inquiry does not invalidate the decision if it is based on objective property conditions.

The Court agrees with the City. In *City of Madison v. Becco*, 127 Wis. 2d 1, 375 N.W.2d 227 (Ct. App. 1985), the court held that municipalities may consider information from private citizens when making administrative decisions. The fact that the McLartys' attorney initiated the inquiry does not negate the City's duty to assess building safety. The decision to issue the raze order was based on the ServPro estimate and the objective condition of the home, not the identity of the inquirer. Auto-Owners' argument that the statute should be interpreted to prevent insurance windfalls is inconsistent with the statute's plain purpose of public safety. The statute's 50% threshold and the requirement that the building be "out of repair" are sufficient to prevent abuse, regardless of who prompts the inspection.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 and the precedent cited by the City, the raze order was properly issued. The home was "out of repair" due to fire damage, the repair costs—including necessary smoke and water remediation—exceeded the statutory threshold, and the inspector acted within his authority and in good faith. Therefore, Auto-Owners' arguments are rejected, and the Circuit Court's order denying the injunction is affirmed.
