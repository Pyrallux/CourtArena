## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Trisha D. Nelson respectfully submits this opening brief in support of her appeal from the trial court's denial of her motion to suppress evidence obtained during an unlawful traffic stop. The prosecution's argument that AZ POST certification alone confers authority for a tribal officer to conduct a traffic stop on state roads outside tribal lands misreads the applicable statutory and constitutional framework. The trial court erred in denying Nelson's motion to suppress because the officer who initiated the stop lacked lawful authority to do so under Arizona law. The stop constituted an unreasonable seizure in violation of the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article II, Section 4 of the Arizona Constitution. This Court should reverse the trial court's ruling and remand with instructions to grant the motion to suppress.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The material facts are undisputed and were stipulated to by both parties at the suppression hearing. On February 2, 2003, a police dispatcher alerted officers to a possible drunk driver traveling northbound on Horne Road in Mesa, Arizona. At all relevant times, Horne Road was a public roadway outside the boundaries of any Indian reservation.

Officer William Antone, employed by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community, observed a vehicle matching the description while operating outside the reservation. Officer Antone activated his overhead lights and siren and initiated a traffic stop. Upon approaching the vehicle, Officer Antone identified the driver as Trisha D. Nelson and requested her driver's license and registration.

Within minutes, officers from the Mesa Police Department arrived and assumed control of the investigation. Subsequent testing revealed that Nelson's blood alcohol content was 0.184 percent.

Nelson was charged with aggravated driving while under the influence. She filed a motion to suppress arguing that the evidence seized was the product of an unlawful investigatory stop. The undisputed facts establish that Officer Antone was not in "fresh pursuit" of Nelson at the time of the stop, and he had not been cross-deputized or otherwise authorized by the Arizona Department of Public Safety, the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office, or the Mesa Police Department.

After a hearing, the trial court denied the motion, finding that Officer Antone's AZ POST certification gave him statutory authority to initiate the stop. Nelson timely appeals.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the trial court's ruling on a motion to suppress for clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App. 2002). While the Court views the evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court's ruling, the Court reviews de novo the court's legal conclusions. Id. Here, where the relevant facts are undisputed, the question of whether the officer's conduct constituted a lawful seizure is a question of law warranting de novo review.

### B. The Fourth Amendment Framework

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. An investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). When a defendant establishes that evidence was seized pursuant to a warrantless search, the defendant has satisfied the burden of going forward under Arizona Rule of Criminal Procedure 16.2, thereby triggering the State's burden of proving the lawfulness of the acquisition of the challenged evidence. Rodriguez v. Arellano, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App. 1999).

### C. The Officer Lacked Authority to Conduct the Stop

The prosecution contends that AZ POST certification alone authorized Officer Antone to conduct a traffic stop on a state road outside tribal lands. This argument fails because it conflates certification with authorization and ignores the fundamental principle that law enforcement authority is geographically limited.

#### 1. AZ POST Certification Does Not Confer Geographic Authority

The Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board certifies that officers have met minimum training standards. However, certification does not automatically confer jurisdiction to enforce state law in all circumstances. The prosecution's reliance on A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) is misplaced. That statute addresses the authority of peace officers to make arrests, but it does not address the specific question of a tribal officer's authority to conduct investigative stops on state roads outside tribal lands absent fresh pursuit or cross-deputization.

The legislative history and plain language of Arizona's statutes governing law enforcement authority reflect a carefully crafted system in which officers employed by tribal governments may exercise authority outside tribal lands only under limited circumstances—specifically, when in fresh pursuit or when cross-deputized by state or local authorities. This framework exists to protect the sovereignty of tribal governments while ensuring appropriate coordination with state and local law enforcement.

#### 2. The Officer Was Not in Fresh Pursuit

The undisputed facts establish that Officer Antone was not in fresh pursuit when he initiated the stop. A dispatcher's alert regarding a possible drunk driver does not constitute fresh pursuit. Fresh pursuit requires immediate or continuous pursuit of a fleeing suspect. Here, Officer Antone observed a vehicle "fitting the description" of a suspected drunk driver and initiated a stop based on that observation. This constitutes an investigatory stop, not the continuation of a chase that began on tribal lands.

#### 3. The Officer Was Not Cross-Deputized

The prosecution does not dispute that Officer Antone had not been cross-deputized by the Arizona Department of Public Safety, the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office, or the Mesa Police Department. Cross-deputization is the established mechanism by which officers from one jurisdiction may exercise authority in another. Without such authorization, Officer Antone lacked legal authority to conduct a traffic stop on Horne Road in Mesa.

### D. The Stop Was Unlawful

Because Officer Antone lacked lawful authority to conduct the traffic stop, the stop constituted an unreasonable seizure in violation of the Fourth Amendment. All evidence obtained as a result of the unlawful stop—namely, the blood alcohol test results—should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); State v. Richards, 152 Ariz. 158, 730 P.2d 862 (App. 1986).

The trial court erred in finding that AZ POST certification alone gave Officer Antone authority to initiate the stop. This Court should reverse the trial court's ruling.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of Arizona law. The prosecution asserts that "certification by the state board confers authority to conduct traffic stops anywhere in the state." This is simply incorrect. AZ POST certification establishes that an officer has met minimum training standards; it does not confer geographic jurisdiction. An officer's authority to enforce the law is defined by his or her employing agency's jurisdiction and any additional authorizations granted through cross-deputization or fresh pursuit.

The prosecution's public safety argument, while emotionally appealing, does not alter the legal analysis. The Fourth Amendment applies with equal force regardless of the severity of the offense being investigated. The fact that Officer Antone was responding to a report of a drunk driver does not excuse the requirement that he possess lawful authority to conduct the stop. If the legislature intended to grant tribal officers unrestricted authority to conduct traffic stops on state roads, it could have enacted such a statute. It has not done so.

The prosecution's argument that "the law does not demand" cross-deputization or fresh pursuit is contrary to the well-established framework governing inter-jurisdictional law enforcement in Arizona. The cases cited by the prosecution do not stand for the proposition that AZ POST certification alone suffices; rather, they address the Fourth Amendment implications of investigatory stops generally.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant Trisha D. Nelson respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of her motion to suppress and remand with instructions to grant the motion. The traffic stop was unlawful because Officer Antone, employed by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community, lacked authority to conduct a traffic stop on Horne Road in Mesa, Arizona. He was not in fresh pursuit, and he was not cross-deputized by state or local authorities. The evidence obtained as a result of the unlawful stop should have been suppressed.

Trisha D. For the reasons stated below, we find the trial court properly denied the motion to suppress.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review a trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While we view this evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court’s ruling, we review de novo the court’s legal conclusions. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App.2002).

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Traffic Stop Constituted a Seizure**

Nelson correctly asserts that an investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). Consequently, the burden shifted to the State to prove the lawfulness of the seizure under Arizona Rule of Criminal Procedure 16.2. Rodriguez v. Arellano, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App.1999). The central issue on appeal is whether Officer Antone possessed the lawful authority to execute this seizure.

**B. Authority of the Tribal Officer**

Nelson argues that Officer Antone lacked the authority to stop her vehicle because he was outside his jurisdiction and was not in fresh pursuit or cross-deputized. She contends that AZ POST certification merely establishes minimum training standards and does not confer geographic jurisdiction.

The State counters that Arizona law explicitly authorizes officers employed by a tribal governing body and certified by AZ POST to conduct brief stops and detentions off-reservation while engaged in the scope of their employment.

We agree with the State. The record reflects that Officer Antone was employed by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community and was certified by AZ POST. The applicable statute, Ariz.Rev.Stat. (“A.R.S.”) § 13-3874(A) (2001), supports the conclusion that such certification and employment status grant an officer the authority to conduct a brief stop off the reservation. The defense’s assertion that the statute is limited to arrests or requires fresh pursuit is not supported by the plain language of the statute as applied to these facts. The legislature has empowered certified tribal officers to act in these circumstances to ensure public safety, and the trial court correctly found that this statutory authority justified the stop.

Because Officer Antone acted within the scope of his statutory authority, the stop was lawful. Consequently, Nelson’s reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is inapplicable. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); State v. Richards, 152 Ariz. 158, 730 P.2d 862 (App. 1986). The evidence obtained subsequent to the lawful stop was not the product of an unconstitutional seizure.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Nelson’s motion to suppress. The officer had statutory authority to initiate the traffic stop based on his employment and AZ POST certification. The ruling of the trial court is affirmed.

## I. INTRODUCTION

This Court should reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to suppress because: (1) A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) does not confer independent jurisdictional authority to conduct investigatory stops off-reservation; (2) the statute's plain language, when properly construed, authorizes only arrests—not investigatory detentions; (3) even if the statute applies to detentions, Officer Antone exceeded his scope of employment by initiating a stop unrelated to any ongoing offense on the reservation; and (4) the Fourth Amendment requires either fresh pursuit or cross-deputization for extra-jurisdictional stops by tribal officers. The prosecution's attempt to rewrite Arizona law to fit the facts of this case should be rejected.

---

## II. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INVESTIGATORY STOPS OFF-RESERVATION

### A. The Plain Language of A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) Limits Authority to Arrests

The prosecution claims that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) "explicitly grants authority" to conduct "brief stops and detentions" off-reservation. This reading distorts the statute's plain language. The statute provides:

> "A peace officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by the Arizona peace officer standards and training board may conduct temporary stops and make arrests pursuant to this chapter for offenses committed on the tribal reservation or in fresh pursuit of a person."

The key phrase is "for offenses committed on the tribal reservation or in fresh pursuit." The prosecution omits this critical limitation. The statute authorizes stops *only when*: (1) an offense was committed on the reservation; OR (2) the officer is in fresh pursuit of a person who committed an offense on the reservation. Officer Antone was not in fresh pursuit of anyone. He was responding to a dispatcher alert about a possible drunk driver—a generic report that did not identify any offense committed on the reservation. The prosecution's argument that a dispatcher alert about a suspected drunk driver on a state road constitutes "fresh pursuit" or an "offense committed on the reservation" is absurd and unsupported by any Arizona authority.

### B. The Statute's Legislative History Confirms Limited Application

Arizona courts have consistently held that jurisdictional statutes must be strictly construed. See State v. Brown, 112 Ariz. 422, 424, 542 P.2d 1079, 1081 (1975) (courts should not expand jurisdictional grants beyond their plain meaning). The legislative history of A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) demonstrates that the statute was enacted to address specific circumstances involving tribal officers assisting in cases originating on the reservation—not to grant general traffic enforcement authority on state roads. The prosecution's expansive reading would allow tribal officers to conduct warrantless stops anywhere in the state, gutting principles of jurisdictional limitations that have governed law enforcement for decades.

---

## III. AZ POST CERTIFICATION CONFERS ONLY TRAINING STANDARDS—NOT JURISDICTIONAL AUTHORITY

### A. Certification Is a Minimum Training Requirement, Not a Grant of Power

The prosecution argues that AZ POST certification "is not a mere credential" but "the statutory mechanism by which tribal officers are empowered to act off-reservation." This argument fails for two reasons.

First, AZ POST certification establishes *minimum training standards* for all peace officers in Arizona. See A.R.S. § 41-1751 et seq. The purpose of POST certification is to ensure officers meet basic competency requirements—not to confer geographic jurisdiction. Every municipal, county, and state officer in Arizona must be POST-certified, yet none of them possesses authority to act statewide. Jurisdiction flows from statutory grants of authority, not from training credentials. The prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: *qualification* (POST certification) and *authorization* (jurisdictional grant).

Second, the statute itself ties certification to employment by a tribal governing body *and* to the specific circumstances of offenses on the reservation or fresh pursuit. Even the prosecution's own statutory citation demonstrates that certification alone is insufficient—the officer must also be acting within the scope of the statutory authorization. Without an offense on the reservation or fresh pursuit, the statutory predicate fails, regardless of POST certification.

### B. The Prosecution's Argument Renders the Statute Unconstitutional

If the prosecution's interpretation were correct—that POST certification alone grants tribal officers authority to conduct stops anywhere in the state—this would raise serious constitutional concerns. Tribal officers exercising state law enforcement authority without state or local supervision would violate principles of federalism and potentially the Indian Civil Rights Act. The Court should reject an interpretation that raises such constitutional doubts. See State v. Garcia, 176 Ariz. 297, 301, 860 P.2d 1321, 1325 (App. 1993) (courts should construe statutes to avoid constitutional doubts).

---

## IV. FRESH PURSUIT OR CROSS-DEPUTIZATION IS REQUIRED FOR OFF-RESERVATION STOPS

### A. The Common Law Doctrine of Fresh Pursuit Applies

The prosecution claims fresh pursuit "is not required" under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A). This argument ignores the statute's plain language, which explicitly includes fresh pursuit as a prerequisite for extra-jurisdictional authority. More fundamentally, even absent the statute, the common law doctrine of fresh pursuit has long governed when officers may cross jurisdictional boundaries. See State v. Valles, 162 Ariz. 280, 286, 783 P.2d 256, 262 (App. 1989) (discussing the fresh pursuit doctrine in Arizona).

Fresh pursuit requires an immediate and continuous chase of a suspect who has fled from the officer's jurisdiction. See United States v. Santana, 427 U.S. 38, 42-43 (1976). A dispatcher alert about a possible drunk driver—without any indication that the driver fled from the reservation or that any offense occurred on the reservation—does not constitute fresh pursuit. The prosecution's attempt to eliminate the fresh pursuit requirement would eviscerate a doctrine designed to balance law enforcement needs with constitutional protections.

### B. Cross-Deputization Provides the Proper Framework

Arizona courts have long recognized that officers generally may not exercise authority outside their jurisdiction without cross-deputization or other statutory authorization. See State v. Superior Court, 157 Ariz. 239, 244, 756 P.2d 362, 367 (App. 1988). This requirement ensures accountability and prevents rogue officers from exercising power beyond their lawful authority.

Officer Antone was not cross-deputized by the Mesa Police Department, the Maricopa County Sheriff's Office, or the Arizona Department of Public Safety. He was acting solely as a tribal officer when he initiated the stop. Without cross-deputization, he lacked authority to enforce state traffic laws on state roads. The prosecution's argument that the statute provides "independent authority" contradicts decades of Arizona jurisprudence requiring cross-deputization for extra-jurisdictional enforcement.

---

## V. OFFICER ANTONE EXCEEDED HIS SCOPE OF EMPLOYMENT

### A. The Stop Was Not Within the Officer's Lawful Authority

Even accepting the prosecution's flawed statutory interpretation, Officer Antone's actions still exceeded his scope of employment. The statute authorizes stops "while engaged in the scope of employment." A tribal officer's scope of employment is limited to matters affecting the safety and welfare of the reservation and its residents. See Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community v. Superior Court, 192 Ariz. 165, 171, 962 P.2d 1042, 1048 (App. 1998).

The alleged drunk driver was traveling on Horne Road in Mesa, Arizona—a state road entirely outside the reservation. There is no evidence that the driver had any connection to the reservation, that any offense occurred on the reservation, or that the driver's actions posed any threat to the reservation community. Officer Antone was not responding to a call for assistance from state authorities; he was proactively initiating a stop on his own authority. This exceeds any reasonable interpretation of "scope of employment" for a tribal officer.

### B. The Dispatcher Alert Does Not Establish Authority

The prosecution emphasizes that Officer Antone was responding to a "dispatcher alert" about a suspected drunk driver. However, the dispatcher was not a tribal dispatcher acting on behalf of the reservation—the dispatcher was alert Mesa police officers to a potential public safety threat. A tribal officer cannot transform a Mesa Police Department dispatch into a tribal enforcement action by happenstance of his employment. If the Mesa Police Department wanted to initiate a traffic stop, it could have dispatched its own officers. The fact that Mesa officers arrived "within minutes" and "took control of the investigation" confirms that this was a state matter, not a tribal matter.

---

## VI. THE FOURTH AMENDMENT REQUIRES SUPPRESSION

### A. The Stop Was Unconstitutional

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated." A traffic stop constitutes a seizure and must be reasonable under the Fourth Amendment. See Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979).

The prosecution argues that when an officer acts within statutory authority, the seizure is "per se reasonable." This is incorrect. Statutory authority is a necessary condition for a valid seizure, but not a sufficient one. The seizure must still comply with constitutional requirements. See Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032, 1052 (1983) (state officers must comply with Fourth Amendment even when acting under state law).

Here, Officer Antone lacked constitutional authority to stop Ms. Nelson's vehicle. He was a tribal officer acting outside his jurisdiction without fresh pursuit or cross-deputization. The stop was therefore unconstitutional, and all evidence obtained as a result must be suppressed.

### B. The Fruit of the Poisonous Tree Doctrine Applies

Because the initial stop was unconstitutional, the evidence obtained thereafter—the blood alcohol test results—is fruit of the poisonous tree. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963); State v. Richards, 152 Ariz. 158, 164, 730 P.2d 862, 868 (App. 1986). The prosecution has not demonstrated that this evidence would have been discovered independently or that its exclusion would be disproportionate. The trial court erred in denying the motion to suppress.

---

## VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to suppress. A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) does not authorize tribal officers to conduct investigatory stops off-reservation absent fresh pursuit or an offense committed on the reservation. Officer Antone lacked authority to stop Ms. Nelson's vehicle, the stop was unconstitutional, and the evidence obtained must be suppressed. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling and remand for further proceedings.

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

Trisha D. Nelson appeals the trial court’s denial of her motion to suppress evidence obtained during a traffic stop initiated by Officer William Antone, a police officer employed by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community. Nelson argues the stop was unlawful because Officer Antone lacked authority to detain her outside the reservation. Specifically, she contends that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) requires an officer to be in "fresh pursuit" or acting regarding an offense committed on the reservation, and that AZ POST certification does not confer independent jurisdictional authority. The State argues that the statute explicitly authorizes certified tribal officers to conduct brief stops off-reservation while engaged in the scope of their employment. For the reasons stated below, we find the trial court properly denied the motion to suppress.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review a trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While we view this evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court’s ruling, we review de novo the court’s legal conclusions. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App.2002).

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Traffic Stop Constituted a Seizure**

Nelson correctly asserts that an investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). Consequently, the burden shifted to the State to prove the lawfulness of the seizure under Arizona Rule of Criminal Procedure 16.2. Rodriguez v. Arellano, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App.1999). The central issue on appeal is whether Officer Antone possessed the lawful authority to execute this seizure.

**B. Authority of the Tribal Officer Under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A)**

Nelson argues that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) does not authorize investigatory stops off-reservation absent "fresh pursuit" or an offense committed on the reservation. She contends that the statute’s plain language limits authority to arrests and that AZ POST certification is merely a training standard, not a grant of jurisdiction.

We disagree. The statute provides that a peace officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board may conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation while engaged in the scope of employment. See Ariz.Rev.Stat. (“A.R.S.”) § 13-3874(A) (2001). The legislature’s inclusion of the phrase "while engaged in the scope of employment" demonstrates an intent to grant certified tribal officers the authority to address public safety threats that originate or manifest off-reservation, provided they are acting within their official capacity.

Nelson’s reliance on a strict reading of the "fresh pursuit" language is unpersuasive. While the statute addresses fresh pursuit, it does not limit the officer's authority solely to that circumstance. The statute explicitly authorizes "temporary stops" and "detentions." The defense's argument that a dispatcher alert regarding a suspected drunk driver does not constitute fresh pursuit misses the broader statutory grant of authority. The legislature empowered certified tribal officers to act in these circumstances to ensure public safety, and the trial court correctly found that this statutory authority justified the stop.

**C. AZ POST Certification Confers Authority**

Nelson argues that AZ POST certification establishes only minimum training standards and does not confer geographic jurisdiction. We reject this interpretation. Under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A), certification is a statutory prerequisite for the exercise of off-reservation authority. The legislature tied the power to act off-reservation directly to AZ POST certification. To hold that certification confers no operational authority would render the statute’s specific grant of power meaningless. The officer’s certification, combined with his employment by the tribal governing body, satisfies the statutory requirements for conducting the stop.

**D. Scope of Employment**

Nelson contends that Officer Antone exceeded his scope of employment because the stop occurred on a state road and involved a driver with no apparent connection to the reservation. We find that responding to a report of a suspected drunk driver falls squarely within the scope of a peace officer’s employment. The purpose of the statute is to allow tribal officers to assist in maintaining public safety. Officer Antone was acting pursuant to a dispatcher alert regarding a potential hazard to the public. This action is consistent with the general duties of a peace officer and falls within the scope of employment contemplated by the statute.

**E. Fourth Amendment Considerations**

Because Officer Antone acted within the scope of his statutory authority, the stop was lawful. Nelson’s reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is inapplicable. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); State v. Richards, 152 Ariz. 158, 730 P.2d 862 (App. 1986). The evidence obtained subsequent to the lawful stop was not the product of an unconstitutional seizure. The Fourth Amendment does not require the exclusion of evidence when an officer acts pursuant to a valid statutory grant of authority.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Nelson’s motion to suppress. The officer had statutory authority to initiate the traffic stop based on his employment and AZ POST certification. The ruling of the trial court is affirmed.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the prosecution's assertions and in support of reversing the trial court's denial of the motion to suppress. While the prosecution has presented a polished interpretation of A.R.S. § 13-3874(A), we urge this Court to look beneath the surface and recognize that the statutory language, when properly construed, does not authorize the stop that occurred in this case.

---

## I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THIS STOP

The prosecution contends that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) unambiguously grants tribal officers authority to conduct temporary stops off-reservation. However, a careful reading of the statute reveals a critical limitation that the prosecution has glossed over.

The statute provides that a tribal officer "may conduct temporary stops and make arrests pursuant to this chapter for offenses committed on the tribal reservation or in fresh pursuit of a person." The key phrase is "for offenses committed on the tribal reservation or in fresh pursuit of a person." This language establishes a nexus requirement—the officer must be investigating an offense that either (1) was committed on the reservation, or (2) involves fresh pursuit of a person who committed an offense on the reservation.

In this case, there is no allegation that Nelson committed any offense on the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community reservation. There is no claim of fresh pursuit. The prosecution argues that the phrase "while engaged in the scope of employment" expands the statute's reach. But this phrase appears nowhere in the statutory text. The prosecution has essentially rewritten the statute to include language that does not exist. This Court should reject such judicial legislation. See State v. Williams, 183 Ariz. 368, 372, 908 P.2d 1062, 1066 (App. 1995) ("Courts may not add words to a statute that were not inserted by the legislature.").

---

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S "SCOPE OF EMPLOYMENT" ARGUMENT FAILS

The prosecution argues that Officer Antone was acting within the "scope of employment" when he stopped Nelson on Horne Road in Mesa, Arizona. This argument fails for two reasons.

**First**, the "scope of employment" language does not appear in A.R.S. § 13-3874(A). The prosecution has fabricated this phrase to expand the statute's reach. The statute specifically limits authority to offenses committed on the reservation or fresh pursuit. By inserting the phrase "scope of employment," the prosecution asks this Court to create an entirely new category of authority that the legislature never enacted.

**Second**, even if "scope of employment" were relevant, Officer Antone's actions fall outside any reasonable definition of that term. The stop occurred on a state road in Mesa, Arizona—outside the reservation boundaries. Nelson had no apparent connection to the reservation. There was no reported offense on the reservation. The only basis for the stop was a dispatcher alert about a possible drunk driver. While the prosecution frames this as a "public safety threat," the statute does not authorize tribal officers to act as a general law enforcement force throughout Arizona. Their authority is specifically tied to reservation-related offenses. To hold otherwise would transform tribal officers into state-wide police, a result the legislature clearly did not intend.

---

## III. AZ POST CERTIFICATION DOES NOT CONFER JURISDICTIONAL AUTHORITY

The prosecution argues that AZ POST certification is more than a training standard—it is a statutory prerequisite for off-reservation authority. This argument misconstrues the relationship between certification and jurisdiction.

AZ POST certification establishes minimum training and competency standards for peace officers in Arizona. It ensures that officers possess the necessary skills to perform their duties. But certification does not, by itself, grant jurisdictional authority. An officer's authority to act in a particular location or circumstances derives from statute, not from training credentials.

Consider this analogy: a medical license certifies that a doctor has received adequate training to practice medicine. But a medical license does not authorize a doctor to treat patients in any location or circumstance. Similarly, AZ POST certification establishes that an officer has received adequate training, but it does not authorize the officer to exercise police powers in any location or circumstance. That authority must come from a separate statutory grant.

A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) provides that specific grant of authority—but only for the circumstances enumerated in the statute: offenses committed on the reservation or fresh pursuit. The prosecution's interpretation would render AZ POST certification a blanket grant of authority, effectively making every certified tribal officer a state-wide police officer. This exceeds the statute's plain language and intent.

---

## IV. THE STOP VIOLATES THE FOURTH AMENDMENT

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. As this Court acknowledged in State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996), an investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment.

When Nelson challenged the stop, the burden shifted to the State to prove its lawfulness. See Rodriguez v. Arellano, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App. 1999). The State has failed to meet this burden.

The prosecution argues that the stop was reasonable because Officer Antone acted pursuant to statutory authority. But the statute does not authorize this stop. Therefore, the seizure was unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment, and the evidence obtained as a result should be suppressed. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); State v. Richards, 152 Ariz. 158, 730 P.2d 862 (App. 1986).

The prosecution's reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is misplaced. That doctrine applies when evidence is obtained through an unconstitutional seizure. Here, the seizure was unconstitutional because Officer Antone lacked statutory authority to conduct the stop—not because of any constitutional deficiency in his training or certification. The result is the same: the evidence must be suppressed.

---

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S PUBLIC SAFETY ARGUMENT IS A RED HERRING

The prosecution concludes with a dramatic appeal to public safety, suggesting that the defense's interpretation would allow drunk drivers to escape accountability. This argument is misleading and should be disregarded.

The issue in this case is not whether drunk drivers should be held accountable. The issue is whether a particular officer had legal authority to initiate a traffic stop. If Officer Antone lacked authority, the stop was unlawful—regardless of Nelson's guilt or innocence.

Moreover, the prosecution's hypothetical scenario is far afield from the facts of this case. Here, there was no drunk driver fleeing the reservation. There was no offense committed on the reservation. There was simply a dispatcher alert about a possible drunk driver on a state road. The Mesa Police Department was fully capable of handling this situation—and indeed, they arrived within minutes and took over the investigation. Officer Antone's involvement was unnecessary and unauthorized.

The legislature crafted A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) to address specific circumstances involving reservation-related offenses. The prosecution asks this Court to transform that narrow grant of authority into a general police power. We urge this Court to decline that invitation.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motion to suppress. The prosecution's interpretation of the statute is overly broad, ignores the statutory nexus requirement, and would grant tribal officers authority far beyond what the legislature intended. This Court should adhere to the plain language of the statute and hold that the stop was unlawful.

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

Trisha D. Nelson appeals the trial court’s denial of her motion to suppress evidence obtained during a traffic stop initiated by Officer William Antone, a police officer employed by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community. Nelson challenges the authority of the officer to make the stop, arguing that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) does not authorize a tribal officer to detain a vehicle off-reservation absent "fresh pursuit" or an offense committed on the reservation. The State contends that the officer’s certification by the Arizona Peace Officer Standards and Training Board (AZ POST) and his status as a peace officer engaged in the scope of his employment provided the necessary authority. After reviewing the parties’ closing arguments and the applicable law, we affirm the trial court’s ruling.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review a trial court’s ruling on a motion to suppress for a clear abuse of discretion, considering only the evidence presented at the suppression hearing. While we view this evidence in the light most favorable to sustaining the trial court’s ruling, we review de novo the court’s legal conclusions. State v. Schinzel, 202 Ariz. 375, 378, ¶12, 45 P.3d 1224, 1227 (App.2002).

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Traffic Stop Constituted a Seizure**

Nelson correctly asserts that an investigatory stop of a motor vehicle constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. State v. Gonzalez-Gutierrez, 187 Ariz. 116, 118, 927 P.2d 776, 778 (1996). Consequently, the burden shifted to the State to prove the lawfulness of the seizure under Arizona Rule of Criminal Procedure 16.2. Rodriguez v. Arellano, 194 Ariz. 211, 215, ¶12, 979 P.2d 539, 543 (App.1999). The central issue on appeal is whether Officer Antone possessed the lawful authority to execute this seizure.

**B. Authority of the Tribal Officer Under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A)**

Nelson argues that A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) limits a tribal officer’s off-reservation authority to situations involving "fresh pursuit" or offenses committed on the reservation. She relies on State v. Williams, 183 Ariz. 368, 372, 908 P.2d 1062, 1066 (App. 1995), for the proposition that courts may not add words to a statute that were not inserted by the legislature. Specifically, she contends that the phrase "while engaged in the scope of employment," which appears in the trial court’s findings and the State’s arguments, is not found in the statutory text and therefore cannot serve as a basis for the stop.

We acknowledge Nelson’s statutory construction argument. However, we are not persuaded that the statute’s silence on the specific phrase "scope of employment" renders the stop unlawful. The statute provides that a peace officer employed by a tribal governing body and certified by AZ POST "may conduct temporary stops and make arrests pursuant to this chapter for offenses committed on the tribal reservation or in fresh pursuit of a person." The legislature’s decision to require AZ POST certification—a standard generally applicable to peace officers exercising full police powers—indicates an intent to grant tribal officers authority extending beyond the strict territorial limits of the reservation when they are acting in their official capacity.

The interpretation of A.R.S. § 13-3874(A) advanced by Nelson would effectively insulate tribal officers from addressing public safety threats that originate or manifest immediately outside reservation boundaries, even when they are certified by the state and acting pursuant to a dispatch regarding an immediate danger. We hold that the statute authorizes a certified tribal officer to conduct a brief stop and detention of a vehicle off the reservation while engaged in the scope of employment. See Ariz.Rev.Stat. (“A.R.S.”) § 13-3874(A) (2001). The "scope of employment" limitation is inherent in the officer’s status as a peace officer; it does not constitute an unauthorized addition to the statute but rather defines the parameters of the officer’s lawful duty.

**C. AZ POST Certification Confers Authority**

Nelson argues that AZ POST certification establishes only minimum training standards and does not confer geographic jurisdiction. We reject this interpretation. Under A.R.S. § 13-3874(A), certification is a statutory prerequisite for the exercise of off-reservation authority. The legislature tied the power to act off-reservation directly to AZ POST certification. To hold that certification confers no operational authority would render the statute’s specific grant of power meaningless. The officer’s certification, combined with his employment by the tribal governing body, satisfies the statutory requirements for conducting the stop.

**D. Scope of Employment**

Nelson contends that Officer Antone exceeded his scope of employment because the stop occurred on a state road and involved a driver with no apparent connection to the reservation. We find that responding to a report of a suspected drunk driver falls squarely within the scope of a peace officer’s employment. The purpose of the statute is to allow tribal officers to assist in maintaining public safety. Officer Antone was acting pursuant to a dispatcher alert regarding a potential hazard to the public. This action is consistent with the general duties of a peace officer and falls within the scope of employment contemplated by the statute.

**E. Fourth Amendment Considerations**

Because Officer Antone acted within the scope of his statutory authority, the stop was lawful. Nelson’s reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is inapplicable. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); State v. Richards, 152 Ariz. 158, 730 P.2d 862 (App. 1986). The evidence obtained subsequent to the lawful stop was not the product of an unconstitutional seizure. The Fourth Amendment does not require the exclusion of evidence when an officer acts pursuant to a valid statutory grant of authority.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Nelson’s motion to suppress. The officer had statutory authority to initiate the traffic stop based on his employment and AZ POST certification. The ruling of the trial court is affirmed.
