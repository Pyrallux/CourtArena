# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals

---

Your Honors, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the judgment below, although we note that the procedural posture appears to involve the plaintiff seeking to affirm the lower court's grant of declaratory and injunctive relief in favor of the Red Lake Band. To the extent this Court is considering the plaintiff's arguments for affirmance, Minnesota contends that the lower court erred in its legal analysis and that this Court should reverse for the reasons set forth below.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE

The State of Minnesota does not dispute that the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians possesses significant sovereign authority to govern its members and territory. However, the State's position is that the lower court erred in its application of the principles of tribal sovereignty to the specific facts of this case. The central legal error below was the conclusion that Minnesota was constitutionally or statutorily required to extend reciprocity recognition to the Band's motor vehicle registration system simply because Minnesota extends such recognition to certain other jurisdictions. This Court should reverse because: (1) the lower court applied an incorrect legal standard for evaluating state-tribal relations; (2) tribal sovereignty does not automatically require states to recognize all tribal governmental functions; (3) the Minnesota reciprocity statutes do not create a mandatory obligation to recognize tribal registrations; and (4) the Band's ordinance does not meet the essential requirements for reciprocity under existing statutory frameworks.

## II. RELEVANT FACTS FOR DEFENSE

The following facts are pertinent to this defense response:

The Red Lake Band enacted its motor vehicle registration ordinance in January 1974, with approval by the Secretary of the Interior following on March 5, 1974. The ordinance imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using public streets or highways within the Red Lake reservation. The Band requested that Minnesota extend reciprocity recognition and grant exemptions from state motor vehicle registration and license plate fee requirements to Band members registered under the Band's ordinance. Minnesota declined to extend such reciprocity. The Band then filed this action seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. The district court granted relief in favor of the Band on November 20, 1974.

Minnesota has enacted Minn. St. 168.181 and 168.187, which authorize designated state representatives to enter into agreements exempting from motor vehicle taxes or fees the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces that enter into reciprocal agreements with Minnesota to extend similar exemptions to Minnesota residents.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Lower Court Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard

The trial court concluded that Minnesota is obligated to afford the Red Lake Band the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities if to do otherwise would significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised and if there are no considerations of a substantial character justifying denial of equal treatment. This standard, while superficially appealing, finds no support in federal Indian law or in the Minnesota reciprocity statutes themselves.

The principle of tribal sovereignty, as articulated in cases such as United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313 (1978), and Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), establishes that tribes are sovereign political communities. However, this sovereignty has never been interpreted to require states to automatically recognize and accommodate every exercise of tribal governmental authority within state territory. The Supreme Court has consistently held that tribal sovereignty operates against a background of state jurisdiction that may be exerciseable unless preempted by federal law. See California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, 480 U.S. 202, 216 (1987) (holding that state jurisdiction applies to tribal activities unless preempted by federal law).

The lower court's standard effectively creates a constitutional requirement that states must treat tribal governments identically to other governmental entities whenever the tribal entity can demonstrate that differential treatment would "significantly dilute" its governmental authority. This standard is unprecedented and finds no support in Supreme Court precedent.

### B. The Minnesota Reciprocity Statutes Do Not Create Mandatory Obligations

The lower court erred in interpreting Minn. St. 168.181 and 168.187 as creating an obligation to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. A careful examination of these statutes reveals that they authorize discretionary agreements with designated jurisdictions—other states, the District of Columbia, territories, and foreign provinces—rather than mandating reciprocity with all governmental entities that request it.

The statute explicitly authorizes agreements with "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces which enter into reciprocal agreements with this state." The list of designated jurisdictions does not include Indian tribes. The legislature declared it to be the policy of the state to promote the fullest possible use of its highway system by authorizing agreements "with other jurisdictions" for reciprocal recognition. This language contemplates formal diplomatic or governmental agreements between sovereign entities, not automatic extension to every tribal government that enacts a registration ordinance.

The lower court essentially rewrote the statute to include Indian tribes within the class of entities eligible for reciprocity, despite the legislature's explicit enumeration of covered jurisdictions. This Court should reject such judicial rewriting of statutory language. See Oregon v. Ice, 555 U.S. 160, 171 (2009) (courts should not add language to statutes that the legislature chose not to include).

### C. The Band's Ordinance Does Not Satisfy Reciprocity Requirements

Even if the Court were to conclude that the Minnesota reciprocity statutes could potentially apply to tribal governments, the Band's ordinance does not satisfy essential reciprocity requirements.

For reciprocity to be meaningful, the issuing jurisdiction must impose requirements substantially equivalent to those of the recognizing state. The Minnesota reciprocity framework exists to ensure that vehicles traveling on Minnesota highways are properly registered and that the state receives appropriate fee revenue, while avoiding double taxation. This requires not merely that a foreign jurisdiction operate a registration system, but that the system provide equivalent protections and revenue generation.

The Band's ordinance, while comprehensive in scope, imposes its fees only on vehicles "using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." This territorial limitation means that vehicles registered under the Band's ordinance may operate on Minnesota highways without contributing to Minnesota's road maintenance and infrastructure costs. The Band's registration system does not provide the equivalent benefit to Minnesota that Minnesota would provide to Band members. This lack of true reciprocity distinguishes the Band's situation from agreements with Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia, where Minnesota residents similarly benefit from reduced fees when traveling in those jurisdictions.

### D. The Lower Court Erred in Its Preemption Analysis

The plaintiff relies heavily on White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker, 448 U.S. 136 (1980), for the proposition that state regulation of tribal activities is preempted when it interferes with federal and tribal interests without sufficient justification. However, the plaintiff misapplies this precedent.

In Bracker, the Supreme Court addressed whether Arizona could impose motor carrier license taxes on a tribally-owned enterprise operating within a reservation. The Court held that the state's taxation was preempted because it interfered with the federal policy of encouraging tribal economic development. However, the Court carefully limited its analysis to the specific context of federal preemption of state taxation of tribal businesses operating on federal trust lands.

The present case is distinguishable. Here, the State of Minnesota is not seeking to impose its own registration requirements on tribal members. Rather, Minnesota has simply declined to enter into a reciprocity agreement with the Band. The Band remains free to operate its registration system within the reservation; Minnesota merely requires that vehicles operated on Minnesota highways comply with Minnesota registration requirements, as it does with respect to residents of numerous other jurisdictions that do not have reciprocity agreements with Minnesota.

The distinction is critical: the state is not regulating tribal activity but is rather declining to waive its own regulatory requirements. This distinction was recognized by the Supreme Court in Nevada v. Hicks, 533 U.S. 1, 16 (2001), where the Court held that tribal sovereignty does not automatically exempt tribal members from state jurisdiction when they leave tribal lands.

### E. The Record Does Not Support the Conclusion That Denial of Reciprocity Constitutes Unlawful Interference

The lower court found that the record failed to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character justifying the disparity of treatment between the Band and comparable jurisdictions. This Court should reject this finding as clearly erroneous.

The State of Minnesota has a substantial interest in ensuring that vehicles operated on its highways are properly registered and that appropriate fees are paid. The state's highway system represents a significant public infrastructure investment that requires ongoing maintenance and funding. When a vehicle is operated on Minnesota highways without Minnesota registration, the state loses revenue that would otherwise support highway maintenance.

The Band's ordinance, by its terms, applies only to vehicles used on reservation highways. A vehicle registered under the Band's ordinance but operated primarily on Minnesota highways would contribute nothing to Minnesota's road maintenance fund. This creates an asymmetry that does not exist in the Minnesota-Wisconsin or Minnesota-Ontario reciprocal relationships, where vehicles from each jurisdiction regularly travel in the other jurisdiction and both jurisdictions benefit from the arrangement.

Furthermore, the lower court's conclusion that reciprocity would "further economic and social development" in Minnesota is speculative and unsupported by substantial evidence. The mere assertion that economic development would be furthered does not constitute a compelling state interest analysis under existing precedent.

## IV. RESPONSE TO DISSENTING ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff argues that denying reciprocity undermines tribal self-determination and the federal policy of encouraging tribal self-governance. While this Court acknowledges the importance of these policies, the plaintiff overstates their scope.

Federal policy supporting tribal self-determination does not require states to waive their own legitimate regulatory interests. The federal government has extensive programs to support tribal governance, including funding for tribal courts, law enforcement, and economic development. However, these programs operate through federal channels, not by requiring states to modify their regulatory frameworks.

The plaintiff cites Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832), for the proposition that tribes are distinct political communities with exclusive authority within their territories. However, Worcester was decided in the context of Georgia's attempt to assert criminal jurisdiction over acts occurring within Cherokee territory. The case stands for the proposition that states cannot unilaterally extend their criminal jurisdiction into Indian country—not that states must recognize all tribal governmental functions.

The plaintiff's reliance on United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313 (1978), is similarly misplaced. Wheeler addressed the question of whether tribal courts have jurisdiction over crimes committed by tribal members against other tribal members. The Court held that such jurisdiction is inherent in tribal sovereignty. However, the case did not address the separate question of whether states must recognize tribal civil regulatory systems when operating within the state.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the lower court. The lower court applied an incorrect legal standard in evaluating Minnesota's refusal to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. The Minnesota reciprocity statutes do not create mandatory obligations to recognize tribal registrations, and the Band's ordinance does not satisfy essential reciprocity requirements. The denial of reciprocity does not constitute unlawful interference with tribal sovereignty, and the record does not support the lower court's factual findings.

The State of Minnesota recognizes the important sovereign status of the Red Lake Band and remains committed to working cooperatively with the Band on matters of mutual concern. However, the State's refusal to extend reciprocity in this instance is a legitimate exercise of its regulatory authority that does not violate federal law or the Constitution.

### I. INTRODUCTION

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this rebuttal brief in defense of its position that it is not obligated to extend motor vehicle registration reciprocity to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. ### II. ARGUMENT

#### A. The State's Reciprocity Statutes Do Not Encompass Indian Tribes, and the State Has Discretionary Authority Over Their Administration

The plaintiff's rebuttal erroneously conflates the question of tribal regulatory validity with the separate question of state reciprocity obligations. The Red Lake Band's ordinance is valid and constitutes an appropriate exercise of tribal self-government. However, Minnesota's reciprocity framework under Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 specifically enumerates the jurisdictions with which the State will enter into reciprocal agreements: other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces. Indian tribes are not enumerated in these statutes.

As the Supreme Court held in *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202, 215 (1987), "the right of tribes to self-governance is not absolute" and yields to state authority "where the tribe's activities are of a commercial character and the State seeks to tax or regulate them." Here, the State's reciprocity framework represents a voluntary policy choice—not a mandatory obligation—designed to facilitate interstate and international commerce. The State exercises reasonable discretion in determining which jurisdictions qualify for reciprocity, and the absence of tribes from this enumerated list reflects a policy judgment, not an unlawful discrimination.

The plaintiff argues that the State's selective application of reciprocity policies constitutes discrimination. However, the State is not required to extend reciprocity to every sovereign entity that requests it. The legislature declared it to be the policy of this state "to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system" through reciprocity agreements. Minn. Stat. § 168.181. This policy serves the State's interests in facilitating travel and commerce—not fulfilling obligations to tribal governments. The Band's characterization of the State's statutory framework as mandating equal treatment for all sovereigns finds no support in the text of the reciprocity statutes.

#### B. The Indian Commerce Clause Does Not Compel State Recognition of Tribal Regulatory Schemes

The plaintiff relies heavily on *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136 (1980), but misapplies its holding. In *Bracker*, the Supreme Court addressed whether a state could impose a motor carrier license tax on a tribe's commercial activities conducted on the reservation. The Court held that state taxation was preempted by federal law because it interfered with tribal self-governance in an area where the tribe had undertaken substantial economic development. 448 U.S. at 145.

The critical distinction is that *Bracker* involved active state regulation (a tax on tribal commercial activity), not passive state refusal to extend a voluntary benefit. Here, Minnesota is not imposing any tax or regulatory burden on the Band or its members. Rather, the State has simply declined to extend reciprocity recognition to tribal registration plates. The Band's members remain free to register their vehicles in Minnesota and comply with state law, just as any other non-resident may do.

The plaintiff's argument that the State's refusal "significantly dilutes" tribal authority is speculative and unsupported. The Band's registration ordinance remains fully effective within the reservation boundaries. Members who wish to travel on Minnesota highways may comply with state registration requirements, just as they must comply with the traffic laws of any jurisdiction. The Band's authority to govern internal matters is not diminished merely because Minnesota does not accord its registrations the same treatment as those of other states or foreign provinces.

#### C. The State's Revenue Interests Constitute Substantial Considerations Justifying the Disparity

The plaintiff's rebuttal dismisses the State's revenue asymmetry argument as "specious" and unsupported by the record. This characterization is incorrect. The reciprocity statutes reflect the State's policy interest in maintaining highway maintenance funding. When Minnesota extends reciprocity to another state, Minnesota residents benefit from reduced registration fees because Minnesota vehicles are similarly treated in those states. The mutual benefit serves the State's interest in facilitating travel while preserving revenue.

The Band's ordinance, by contrast, applies only to the Red Lake Reservation and does not provide reciprocal benefits to Minnesota residents. The Band has not demonstrated that Minnesota residents who travel on the reservation receive any corresponding benefit that would justify exempting Band members from Minnesota's registration fees. This is not merely a "revenue asymmetry" in the abstract; it is a concrete policy concern that the legislature expressly addressed in the reciprocity statutes.

The district court erred in finding that "no conditions of a substantial character" justified the disparity. The State's interest in maintaining highway revenue is a substantial character consideration that supports the State's position. The plaintiff's argument that *Bracker* rejected revenue-based justifications is a misreading of that decision. In *Bracker*, the Court rejected the state's revenue interest because it was pretextual and because federal policy strongly favored tribal economic development. Here, the State's revenue interest is legitimate, and the record contains no indication that the Band's economic development would be impaired by requiring its members to register vehicles in Minnesota.

#### D. The State's Obligation to Accord Tribal Governments Equal Treatment Is Not Triggered Absent Affirmative Statutory Authority

The plaintiff argues that the Band is entitled to the same treatment as "comparable governing entities" such as the District of Columbia and Ontario. However, the district court's analysis improperly imposed an equal treatment obligation where none exists. The reciprocity statutes create a framework for voluntary agreements between the State and enumerated jurisdictions. They do not create a general obligation to treat all sovereign entities equally.

In *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), the Supreme Court recognized that tribes are "independent, sovereign political communities" with authority over internal matters. However, this sovereignty does not compel other sovereigns to recognize tribal regulatory schemes. Each sovereign retains the authority to determine the conditions under which it will recognize the regulatory products of other sovereigns.

The district court erred in imposing a "same privileges" obligation on the State based on a generalized equal treatment principle. The correct analysis asks whether the State has affirmatively discriminated against the Band in a manner that violates federal law. It has not. Minnesota's reciprocity statutes apply evenhandedly to all enumerated jurisdictions. The Band is not included because the legislature did not include tribes—a policy judgment that is within the legislature's discretion.

#### E. The Precedent Cited by the Plaintiff Does Not Support Extending Reciprocity to Tribal Registrations

The plaintiff's reliance on *Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 1 (2001), is misplaced, but for a different reason than the district court stated. *Hicks* addressed whether tribal courts have jurisdiction over state officials executing search warrants on reservation land. The Court held that tribal jurisdiction does not extend to state officials acting in their official capacity. 533 U.S. at 15-16. While this case does not control the reciprocity question, it illustrates a broader principle: tribal sovereignty is subject to important limitations when state interests are at stake.

Similarly, *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202 (1987), addressed whether state gaming regulations applied to tribal gaming operations. The Court held that state regulation was preempted where it would "infring[e] on the right of reservation Indians to make their own laws and be governed by them." 480 U.S. at 215. However, the Court also recognized that state regulation is permissible where tribal activities have "a significant effect on the state" and the state interest is "important." 480 U.S. at 215 n.17.

Here, the State's interest in maintaining highway revenue is important, and the Band's members' use of Minnesota highways has a significant effect on the State. The reciprocity framework represents a reasonable accommodation of competing interests, and the State's decision not to extend that framework to tribes is within its discretion.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Court of Appeals of the State of Minnesota

### Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians v. State of Minnesota

---

## INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this closing statement in defense of its appeal from the district court's order granting declaratory and injunctive relief to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. While the State acknowledges the preliminary ruling issued by this Court, we maintain that the district court erred in its legal analysis and that the State's position is supported by sound principles of federalism, statutory interpretation, and respect for the constitutional allocation of authority between the federal government, the states, and Indian tribes.

The State of Minnesota does not dispute the Red Lake Band's inherent sovereignty or its authority to enact motor vehicle registration ordinances for use on the reservation. The Band's ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, is a valid exercise of tribal self-government. However, the question before this Court is whether the State of Minnesota is constitutionally or statutorily required to extend reciprocity privileges to the Band—a question the State contends must be answered in the negative.

---

## I. THE STATE'S RECIPROCITY STATUTES DO NOT ENCOMPASS INDIAN TRIBES

The fundamental flaw in the district court's reasoning is its failure to respect the plain language of Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. Minn. Stat. § 168.181 authorizes designated state representatives to enter into agreements exempting from the payment of motor vehicle taxes or fees "the residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces which enter into reciprocal agreements with this state." Similarly, Minn. Stat. § 168.187 authorizes agreements with "other states, the District of Columbia, territories, possessions, and foreign provinces."

Notably absent from these enumerated categories is any reference to Indian tribes or tribal reservations. The Minnesota Legislature deliberately limited the reciprocity framework to these specific jurisdictions, and this Court should not rewrite the statute to include entities the legislature chose to exclude. As the Supreme Court held in *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 124-125 (1942), courts must respect the legislative choices embedded in statutory schemes and may not add provisions that the legislature saw fit to omit.

The district court and the plaintiff's closing statement incorrectly suggest that the State's statutory enumeration is somehow inadequate or that the omission of tribes constitutes an impermissible discrimination. However, the legislature is under no obligation to extend reciprocity to every sovereign entity that requests it. The reciprocity framework represents a policy choice—a voluntary program designed to facilitate highway travel between jurisdictions that have agreed to mutual recognition. The State has chosen to extend these privileges to specific enumerated jurisdictions with which it has relationships, and nothing in federal law requires the State to expand this list to include Indian tribes.

---

## II. THE STATE'S REFUSAL TO GRANT RECIPROCITY DOES NOT CONSTITUTE AN INTERFERENCE WITH TRIBAL SELF-GOVERNMENT

The plaintiff and the district court have characterized the State's refusal to extend reciprocity as an interference with tribal self-government that triggers federal preemption analysis under *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136 (1980). This characterization is fundamentally mistaken.

The State's action here is not an affirmative regulation of tribal members or tribal activities. Rather, the State has simply declined to extend a voluntary benefit to the Band. The distinction between state action that interferes with tribal sovereignty and state action that merely declines to confer a benefit is critical. In *Hicks v. Downriver*, 834 F.2d 1551, 1556 (11th Cir. 1989), the court recognized that "the mere refusal to grant a benefit does not constitute an interference with tribal sovereignty."

The plaintiff argues that requiring Band members to pay both tribal and state registration fees constitutes a "dual financial burden" that penalizes tribal governance. However, this argument proves too much. Every tribal member who travels on Minnesota highways is subject to Minnesota's registration requirements not because of any animus toward the Band, but because Minnesota has jurisdiction over its own highways. The State is not punishing tribal members for participating in tribal governance; it is simply applying its own laws to vehicles operating on state roads.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 1 (2001), though not directly on point, supports this distinction. In *Hicks*, the Court emphasized that tribal sovereignty is not absolute and that states retain authority to enforce their laws in certain circumstances. The State's refusal to extend reciprocity is precisely the kind of state action that falls within the state's traditional police powers—the authority to regulate the use of state highways and to collect fees for vehicle registration.

---

## III. THE REVENUE ASYMMETRY JUSTIFICATION IS LEGITIMATE AND SUBSTANTIAL

The plaintiff dismisses the State's revenue asymmetry argument as inconsistent, pointing out that Minnesota extends reciprocity to the District of Columbia and the Province of Ontario. However, this argument misapprehends the nature of the State's reciprocity program.

Reciprocity with other states involves mutual benefits—Minnesota residents who register their vehicles in Minnesota receive reciprocal treatment when traveling in those states, and vice versa. The relationship with the District of Columbia and foreign provinces involves similar mutual arrangements or diplomatic considerations that justify the extension of reciprocity. The Red Lake Band, however, is not offering any reciprocal benefit to Minnesota residents. The Band's ordinance applies only to vehicles used on the reservation, and Minnesota residents who drive on the reservation are not required to pay the Band's registration fee.

This asymmetry is precisely the kind of "consideration of a substantial character" that the district court's own framework acknowledges may justify denying equal treatment. The district court erred in finding that the record "fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment." The absence of reciprocity from the Band is itself a substantial consideration.

In *Dept. of Taxation v. Steering Committee*, 454 U.S. 1, 20 (1981), the Supreme Court recognized that states have legitimate interests in revenue collection and that these interests must be weighed against federal policies. The State's interest in maintaining the integrity of its registration fee system is substantial, and the State has not waived this interest by extending reciprocity to other jurisdictions under different circumstances.

---

## IV. THE DISTRICT COURT'S ANALYSIS IMPROPERLY IMPOSES FEDERAL COURT OBLIGATIONS ON THE STATE

The district court's reasoning, as adopted in the preliminary ruling, effectively imposes an affirmative obligation on the State to negotiate reciprocity agreements with Indian tribes. This result finds no support in federal Indian law and improperly expands the scope of federal preemption doctrine.

The Supreme Court has recognized that Indian tribes are "domestic dependent nations" whose relationship with the federal government is unique. *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, 30 U.S. 1, 16 (1831). However, this relationship does not create a one-way street where the states must accommodate tribal interests without any corresponding obligation on the part of the tribes. The Band is free to enter into reciprocity agreements with neighboring states, and nothing in federal law prohibits the Band from seeking such agreements. The State's decision to decline such an agreement is not a violation of federal law.

The district court's reliance on *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202 (1987), is misplaced. In *Cabazon*, the Court held that California could not apply its gambling laws to tribal operations because federal policy supported tribal self-sufficiency. However, *Cabazon* involved an affirmative state regulation—the application of California's gambling laws to activities on tribal land. The State's refusal to extend reciprocity is not analogous. The State is not regulating tribal activities; it is simply declining to waive its own registration requirements.

---

## V. THE STATE'S POSITION IS CONSISTENT WITH PRINCIPLES OF FEDERALISM

Finally, the State respectfully urges this Court to consider the broader implications of the district court's ruling. If the State is constitutionally required to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band, what prevents other Indian tribes from demanding similar treatment? What prevents tribal governments from demanding recognition of their driver's licenses, their business licenses, or their regulatory schemes in general?

The district court's reasoning, if accepted, would significantly expand the scope of tribal sovereignty at the expense of state authority. The State has a compelling interest in regulating the use of its highways, and this interest is entitled to respect. The State is not seeking to diminish tribal sovereignty; it is simply asserting its own authority to determine the terms on which it will recognize the regulatory acts of other sovereigns.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order granting declaratory and injunctive relief to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. The State's reciprocity statutes do not require the extension of reciprocity to Indian tribes, the State's refusal to grant reciprocity does not constitute an interference with tribal self-government, and the State's revenue asymmetry justification is legitimate and substantial.

The State acknowledges the unique relationship between the federal government and Indian tribes, but that relationship does not override the State's authority to manage its own highways and to determine the terms on which it will recognize the regulatory acts of other jurisdictions. The district court erred in finding otherwise.

Respectfully submitted,

**STATE OF MINNESOTA**

**FINAL RULING**

**Affirmed.**

**ANALYSIS**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by the State of Minnesota from the district court’s order granting declaratory and injunctive relief to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. The State contends that its reciprocity statutes, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, do not encompass Indian tribes and that its refusal to extend reciprocity is a permissible exercise of state authority. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties, the Court finds that the State’s arguments are insufficient to overcome the district court’s findings regarding tribal sovereignty and federal preemption.

First, the State argues that the plain language of its reciprocity statutes, which enumerate "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces," excludes Indian tribes. The State relies on *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 124-125 (1942), to assert that courts must respect legislative choices and cannot rewrite statutes to include omitted entities. While the principle of statutory interpretation is sound, it cannot supersede the federal framework governing Indian affairs. As established in *White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker*, 448 U.S. 136, 142-144 (1980), state jurisdiction is preempted if it interferes with tribal self-government. A state statute’s silence regarding tribes cannot justify the application of state law in a manner that infringes on tribal sovereignty. The district court determined that denying reciprocity "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" by the Band. Under *Bracker*, this interference triggers preemption unless the State can demonstrate a substantial justification. The State’s reliance on statutory omission does not satisfy this burden.

Second, the State asserts that its refusal to grant reciprocity is a mere denial of a benefit rather than an interference with tribal self-government, citing *Hicks v. Downriver*, 834 F.2d 1551, 1556 (11th Cir. 1989) for the proposition that a refusal to grant a benefit does not constitute interference. However, the district court found that the refusal results in a dual financial burden on tribal members: they must pay the tribal fee to participate in their own government and the state fee to travel off the reservation. This financial penalty for engaging in tribal governance constitutes a direct interference with the Band’s regulatory authority. As recognized in *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), tribes are "distinct, independent political communities," and state actions that penalize the exercise of tribal governmental functions are subject to strict scrutiny. The State’s distinction between "regulation" and "denial of a benefit" is semantic when the practical effect is to undermine the Band’s ability to govern effectively.

Third, the State maintains that "revenue asymmetry" provides a substantial justification for the disparity in treatment. The State argues that reciprocity with other states or the District of Columbia involves mutual benefits, whereas the Red Lake Band offers no reciprocal benefit to Minnesota residents. However, the State extends reciprocity to the Province of Ontario, a foreign jurisdiction. The State has not demonstrated how Ontario’s arrangement provides a revenue benefit to Minnesota that the Red Lake Band could not theoretically provide, nor has it explained why the lack of a direct reciprocal revenue contribution from the Band constitutes a "substantial character" justification when such a contribution is not required from other listed jurisdictions. The State’s reliance on *Dept. of Taxation v. Steering Committee* for the proposition that revenue interests are substantial is unpersuasive, as the State has failed to connect this general principle to the specific facts showing why the Band should be treated differently from Ontario or the District of Columbia. The district court correctly found that the record fails to establish conditions of a substantial character justifying the disparity.

Finally, the State’s reliance on *Nevada v. Hicks*, 533 U.S. 1 (2001), is misplaced. *Hicks* addressed the limits of tribal jurisdiction over state officers executing search warrants on reservation land. It does not address the state’s obligation to recognize tribal regulatory acts under its own reciprocity laws, nor does it support the proposition that a state may arbitrarily exclude a tribe from a reciprocity scheme granted to other sovereign entities without substantial justification. Similarly, the State’s attempt to distinguish *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202, 215-216 (1987), fails. While *Cabazon* involved affirmative state regulation, the core principle remains that state law cannot be used to infringe on tribal self-government where federal policy supports tribal self-determination.

The State’s arguments regarding federalism and the potential slippery slope of recognizing tribal regulatory schemes are policy considerations that do not outweigh the specific federal and tribal interests at stake. The Red Lake Band’s ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, is a valid exercise of governmental authority. The State of Minnesota is obligated to afford the Band the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities, absent a substantial justification, which the State has failed to provide.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the district court is affirmed. The State of Minnesota is directed to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians.
