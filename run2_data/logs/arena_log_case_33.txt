### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are drawn from the case record and are undisputed for purposes of this appeal.

At the time of his death, Levi had designated his mother, Loretta Angell, as the sole beneficiary of two distinct funding instruments: (1) his Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy; and (2) the federal death-gratuity program available to active-duty service members under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480.

Following Levi's death, Loretta Angell received the following benefits:

1. **April 2004**: $100,000 in death-gratuity benefits from the federal government, payable to the designated survivor of Levi as a member of an armed force who died during active duty.

2. **May 2004**: $250,352 from Levi's Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy.

3. **August 2005**: $150,000 in additional death-gratuity benefits pursuant to the Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act for Defense, the Global War on Terror, and Tsunami Relief, 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-13, § 1013(b), 119 Stat. 231, 247 (2005), which authorized retroactive additional payment of death gratuity for deaths incurred in the theater of Operation Enduring Freedom or Operation Iraqi Freedom.

The total benefits received by Loretta Angell amounted to $500,352.

The classification and distribution of these funds were the sole issues in the marriage dissolution proceeding. The court also awarded Gordon Angell a cash settlement of $150,000, consisting of $100,000 from Loretta Angell's nonmarital property and $50,000 from marital property, relying on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which permits the district court to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse to prevent an unfair hardship.

### IV. ARGUMENT

#### A. The District Court Correctly Classified the Death Benefits as Loretta Angell's Nonmarital Property

The threshold question before this Court is whether the life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity benefits received by Loretta Angell constitute nonmarital property under Minnesota law. This Court should affirm the district court's classification because the benefits were acquired by Loretta Angell as the designated beneficiary of her deceased son's policies, not through marital efforts or the marital estate.

Minnesota law defines "nonmarital property" to include "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b (2008). The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently held that life insurance proceeds payable to a spouse as beneficiary constitute nonmarital property because they are acquired by bequest or inheritance. *In re Marriage of Bier*, 306 N.W.2d 429, 431 (Minn. 1981).

In *Bier*, the court addressed a situation remarkably similar to the present case. There, the husband was the sole beneficiary of a life insurance policy on the life of his father, who had died during the marriage. The court held that the life insurance proceeds were not marital property because they were "acquired by" the beneficiary spouse from a third party—the deceased insured—rather than from the marital estate. The court explicitly rejected the argument that the statutory presumption favoring marital property applied because the proceeds were not "acquired by" either spouse during the marriage; they were acquired by one spouse from a third party as beneficiary. *Bier*, 306 N.W.2d at 431.

This Court reinforced this principle in *In re Marriage of Hagen*, 410 N.W.2d 449, 451 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987), holding that "when a spouse is designated as beneficiary of a life insurance policy, the proceeds are acquired by bequest" and therefore constitute nonmarital property. The court emphasized that "the source of the property determines its character." *Hagen*, 410 N.W.2d at 451.

Here, the source of the $500,352 in benefits was Levi's Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy and the federal death-gratuity program—not the marital estate. Levi, as the insured servicemember, designated his mother Loretta as the sole beneficiary. The benefits were paid directly to Loretta upon Levi's death in active military service. These payments were not acquired through any marital effort or from the marital estate; they were acquired by Loretta individually as the designated beneficiary of her son's policies.

The fact that the parties were married at the time the benefits were received does not transform nonmarital property into marital property. As the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Bier*, the statutory presumption that property acquired during marriage is marital "applies only to property acquired during the marriage by the efforts of either spouse or by the couple's joint efforts." 306 N.W.2d at 431. The death benefits at issue here were not acquired through any such efforts; they were acquired by Loretta alone, as beneficiary, from a third party—her deceased son.

Accordingly, the district court correctly classified the life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property. This Court should affirm that classification.

#### B. The District Court Erred in Awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell's Nonmarital Property

Although the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property is correct, this Court should reverse the district court's award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell because such an award violates federal law protecting military death benefits from attachment, execution, and other legal process.

The Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act, codified at 38 U.S.C. § 1965 *et seq.*, and the federal death-gratuity statute, codified at 10 U.S.C. § 1475 *et seq.*, both contain express anti-attachment provisions that protect these benefits from state court interference. Section 1970 of the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act provides that "insurance payable under this chapter shall not be subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." 38 U.S.C. § 1970 (2000). Similarly, the death-gratuity statute provides that payments thereunder "shall not be subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." 10 U.S.C. § 1475(d) (2000 & Supp. IV 2004).

The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that these federal provisions preempt state law and prevent state courts from interfering with the designated beneficiary's right to the proceeds. In *Wissner v. Wissner*, 338 U.S. 655, 658 (1950), the Supreme Court addressed the predecessor to the current Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act and held that "Congress has spoken with force and clarity in its choice of beneficiary," and that state courts cannot override that designation. The Court explained that federal law governing servicemembers' life insurance "preempts state law" and "prevents state courts from interfering with the designated beneficiary's right to the proceeds." *Wissner*, 338 U.S. at 658.

In *Ridgway v. Ridgway*, 454 U.S. 46, 54 (1981), the Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle, holding that the anti-attachment provisions of the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act "unmistakably reflect a federal policy to protect the service member's dependents by guaranteeing a specified death benefit, regardless of the claims of the member's creditors." The Court emphasized that "the statute's language and structure" demonstrated "a congressional intent to avoid the traditional state-law limitations on the alienability of property." *Ridgway*, 454 U.S. at 54.

These precedents make clear that the district court lacked authority to order Loretta Angell to pay $150,000 from her nonmarital death benefits to Gordon Angell. The federal anti-attachment provisions explicitly prohibit exactly this kind of state court interference with military death benefits. Regardless of Minnesota's statutory framework permitting the division of nonmarital property to prevent unfair hardship, federal law preempts such state law when the property at issue consists of protected military death benefits.

The district court erred in relying on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to support its award. That provision allows the district court to apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse to prevent an unfair hardship. However, federal law protects military death benefits from precisely such apportionment. The anti-attachment provisions of 38 U.S.C. § 1970 and 10 U.S.C. § 1475(d) are express and unambiguous, and they preclude any state court action to reach these benefits, whether through direct seizure or through the indirect mechanism of a property division.

This Court should therefore reverse the district court's award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell.

#### C. Response to Gordon Angell's Notice of Review

Gordon Angell filed a notice of review challenging the district court's classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property. As argued above, the classification was correct under Minnesota law. Gordon's arguments to the contrary are without merit.

Gordon contends that the benefits should be classified as marital property because Loretta Angell did not acquire them "as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" and because she did not overcome the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property. This argument misapprehends the nature of the transaction.

The Minnesota statutes define nonmarital property to include property acquired "by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." Minn. Stat. § 518.003, subd. 3b. The question is not whether the beneficiary "acquired" the property in the abstract, but whether the property was acquired from a third party as opposed to from the marital estate. *See In re Marriage of Bier*, 306 N.W.2d at 431.

Here, Loretta Angell acquired the death benefits from her son Levi, who is a third party to the marriage. Levi designated Loretta as the beneficiary of his military life-insurance policy and death-gratuity benefits. The benefits were paid to Loretta directly by the federal government as a result of Levi's designation. This constitutes acquisition "from a third party" within the meaning of the statute.

Gordon's reliance on the presumption that property acquired during marriage is marital is similarly misplaced. As the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Bier*, this presumption "applies only to property acquired during the marriage by the efforts of either spouse or by the couple's joint efforts." 306 N.W.2d at 431. Death benefits paid to a spouse as beneficiary of a deceased third party's insurance policy are not acquired by marital effort; they are acquired by the beneficiary's status as the designated recipient of the deceased's property.

The Minnesota Court of Appeals has consistently applied this reasoning in cases involving life insurance proceeds. In *In re Marriage of Hagen*, 410 N.W.2d at 451, the court held that proceeds from a life insurance policy acquired by one spouse as beneficiary constitute nonmarital property because they are "acquired by bequest"—that is, from a third party as the result of the insured's designation. The same principle applies here.

Gordon's argument that Loretta "did not overcome the presumption" incorrectly places the burden of proof on the wrong party. When property is acquired by one spouse as beneficiary of a third party's insurance policy, the property is nonmarital *ipso facto*; the spouse claiming that it is marital bears the burden of proving that the property falls within the marital estate. *See In re Marriage of Evans*, 486 N.W.2d 174, 177 (Minn. Ct. App. 1992) (holding that the party seeking to include property in the marital estate bears the burden of proof). Gordon has not met, and cannot meet, this burden because the benefits were manifestly acquired from a third party—Levi—not from the marital estate.

Accordingly, this Court should reject Gordon Angell's challenge to the classification and affirm the district court's determination that the benefits are Loretta Angell's nonmarital property.

The source of the funds—Levi's military service and beneficiary designations—makes him a third party regardless of his familial relationship. *Bier* makes this clear: "the fact that the proceeds were received during the marriage does not make them marital property." *Bier*, 306 N.W.2d at 431. The defense's focus on Levi's status as a child ignores the statute's plain language and the precedential holding that the insured's relationship to the marital estate—not the beneficiary's relationship to the insured—controls classification.

Third, the defense claims the timing of receipt during the marriage triggers the marital presumption. This argument fails because *Bier* directly addresses and rejects it. As the Minnesota Supreme Court stated: "the fact that the proceeds were received during the marriage does not make them marital property." *Bier*, 306 N.W.2d at 431. The statutory presumption that property acquired during marriage is marital only applies if the property does not fall within one of the enumerated exceptions. Here, the benefits were acquired by bequest from a third party—Levi—which squarely fits the statutory exception. The defense's focus on timing mischaracterizes the law: Minnesota law looks to *how* property was acquired, not merely *when*. Finally, the defense argues the "source-of-funds" argument misapplies Minnesota law. This is incorrect. The defense conflates "source" with "marital efforts." The defense's claim that beneficiary designations are merely "contractual arrangements" ignores that *Bier* specifically treated such designations as bequests under the statute.

The life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits were acquired by bequest from Levi, a third party to the marriage, and thus constitute nonmarital property as a matter of law. The defense's arguments distort the statute and ignore controlling case law. Their attempt to relitigate classification is legally untenable. The district court's classification should be affirmed.

## In the Matter of the Dissolution of Marriage of Gordon and Loretta Angell

---

Your Honor, this closing statement is submitted on behalf of the defense, **Gordon Angell**, in response to the plaintiff's closing statement and in support of our position that the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits should be classified as marital property subject to equitable division.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the classification of death benefits paid upon the death of a child who was never a party to the marriage dissolution proceeding. The plaintiff, Loretta Angell, received substantial death benefits following the tragic death of our son, Levi Angell, during active military service in Iraq. The defense maintains that these benefits should be classified as marital property because: (1) Levi Angell was not a "third party" to the marriage in the sense contemplated by the statutory definition of nonmarital property; (2) beneficiary designations under insurance policies are fundamentally distinct from testamentary bequests; and (3) the policies were acquired and maintained during the marriage using marital funds.

Although we respect the Court's ruling on classification, we submit this analysis to preserve our arguments for further review and to demonstrate that the plaintiff's closing statement overstates the precedential support for its position.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Statutory Framework

Under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, nonmarital property includes "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The plaintiff bears the burden of proving that property falls within this statutory exception to the general rule that property acquired during marriage is marital. The question before this Court is whether Levi Angell, as the parties' son, qualifies as a "third party" to the marriage such that his beneficiary designations constitute a "bequest" under Minnesota law.

### B. Levi Angell Was Not a "Third Party" in the Contemplation of the Statute

The plaintiff's closing statement erroneously conflates "third party to the marriage" with any biological relative. This interpretation stretches the statutory language beyond its intended scope and ignores the fundamental distinction between transfers from strangers and transfers from one's own child.

When a spouse inherits from a parent or receives a gift from a sibling, that relative is indeed a "third party" to the marriage because the spouse has no legal obligation to share that property with the other spouse. However, when a child dies and leaves benefits to a parent, the situation is fundamentally different. The child is not a stranger to the marital estate; rather, the child is the product of the marriage itself. Both parents have an interest in the child's welfare, and the child's military service—while individually rendered—represents the culmination of a family endeavor.

The defense submits that the Minnesota Legislature did not intend to exclude from the marital estate benefits paid upon the death of a child simply because the child named one parent as beneficiary. The statutory purpose behind the nonmarital property classification is to protect property that comes from outside the marital relationship—property that has no connection to the marriage's communal nature. Benefits paid upon the death of a child of the marriage bear a direct connection to that marriage.

### C. Beneficiary Designations Are Not "Bequests" Under Minnesota Law

The plaintiff's reliance on *In re Marriage of Bier*, 306 N.W.2d 429 (Minn. 1981), is misplaced. In *Bier*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed whether life insurance proceeds paid to a spouse were marital property. The Court held that because the proceeds were paid to the named beneficiary, they constituted property "acquired by bequest" from the deceased insured. However, the defense submits that *Bier*'s reasoning should be limited to its facts—where the insured was a spouse to the marriage.

A "bequest" traditionally and ordinarily means a testamentary transfer made by will. Black's Law Dictionary defines "bequest" as "[a] testamentary gift of personal property" or "[t]he act of bequeathing property by will." A beneficiary designation under a life insurance policy is a contractual arrangement that takes effect upon the insured's death but is not a will. It does not require probate, need not comply with testamentary formalities, and is governed by contract law rather than probate law.

The Minnesota Legislature knew how to distinguish between testamentary transfers and contractual arrangements. In enacting section 518.003, subdivision 3b, the Legislature used the terms "bequest, devise, or inheritance"—all of which connote testamentary transfers—alongside "gift," which encompasses inter vivos transfers. The Legislature did not include "beneficiary designation" or similar contractual language, suggesting that the drafters intended the term "bequest" to carry its traditional meaning.

If the Minnesota Supreme Court intended beneficiary designations to constitute bequests, it effectively rewrote the statute to include a category of transfers that the Legislature did not enumerate. While we respect stare decisis, we submit that *Bier* was wrongly decided or, at minimum, should be confined to its specific factual scenario involving a spouse-insured.

### D. The Marital Presumption Applies Because the Benefits Were Acquired During the Marriage

The plaintiff argues that timing is irrelevant and that the benefits are nonmarital simply because they came from a third party. This argument ignores the fundamental principle that all property acquired during marriage is presumed marital unless it falls within a statutory exception. The plaintiff has not established that Levi Angell was a "third party" to the marriage, and without that showing, the statutory exception does not apply.

The defense submits that the better interpretation of Minnesota law is that the statutory exceptions apply only when property is acquired from persons who have no connection to the marriage. A child of the marriage is not such a person. Therefore, the benefits should be classified as marital property, and the district court should have divided them equally.

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

### A. Response to the "Bequest" Argument

The plaintiff cites *Bier* for the proposition that beneficiary designations constitute bequests. However, the plaintiff fails to acknowledge that *Bier* involved a spouse-insured, not a child-insured. The *Bier* Court reasoned that the proceeds came from the deceased insured, who was a third party to the marital estate. This reasoning does not apply when the insured is a child of the marriage because the child has a direct connection to the marital relationship.

Furthermore, the plaintiff conflates "bequest" with "transfer at death." A bequest requires a testamentary instrument. A beneficiary designation is a contract. The Minnesota Legislature, in its wisdom, did not include "contractual beneficiary designation" in the statutory list of exceptions. This omission should be given effect.

### B. Response to the "Source of the Property" Argument

The plaintiff argues that "the source of the property determines its character" and that the source here was Levi Angell's military service. This argument proves too much. If the source of the property determines its character, then property acquired through a spouse's employment would be marital (which it is), but property acquired through a child's death would also be marital because the child is part of the family unit.

The plaintiff's argument conflates "source" with "third party to the marriage." The statute says the property must come from "a third party to one but not the other spouse." This language suggests that the transferor must be a stranger to the marriage, not merely a person who transfers property at death. A child is not a stranger to the marriage; the child is the product of the marriage.

### C. Response to the Federal Preemption Argument

Although the defense disagrees with the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property, we acknowledge that federal law may restrict the division of these benefits. The Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance Act, 38 U.S.C. § 1970, provides that payments are "not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." Similarly, 10 U.S.C. § 1475(d) contains identical protections for death gratuities.

The United States Supreme Court has interpreted these provisions to preempt state community property laws. In *Ridgway v. Ridgway*, 454 U.S. 46 (1981), the Court held that the anti-attachment clause reflects a congressional intent to protect the service member's designated beneficiary from all claims. Similarly, in *Wissner v. Wissner*, 338 U.S. 655 (1950), the Court held that federal law prevents state courts from diverting benefits to anyone other than the designated beneficiary.

If this Court affirms the classification of the benefits as nonmarital property, we agree that federal law may prohibit their division. However, we submit that the classification issue should be resolved in favor of marital property, and the division issue should be addressed separately.

1. **Levi Angell was not a "third party" to the marriage.** A child of the marriage is not a stranger to the marital relationship. The statutory term "third party" should be interpreted to mean persons who have no connection to the marriage, such as strangers, distant relatives, or unrelated entities.

2. **Beneficiary designations are not bequests.** A bequest is a testamentary transfer made by will. A beneficiary designation is a contractual arrangement. The Minnesota Legislature did not include contractual designations in the statutory definition of nonmarital property.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. Classification of Property**

The Court has carefully considered the closing arguments presented by both parties regarding the classification of the life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity benefits. The defense, Gordon Angell, argues that the district court erred in classifying these funds as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. Specifically, the defense contends that (1) Levi Angell was not a "third party" to the marriage because he was the child of the parties, and (2) beneficiary designations do not constitute a "bequest" under Minnesota law, distinguishing the controlling precedent of *In re Marriage of Bier*, 306 N.W.2d 429 (Minn. 1981).

The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive and affirms the district court’s classification.

First, the defense’s argument that a child is not a "third party" to the marriage is contrary to the plain language of the statute and established case law. Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3b, defines nonmarital property as property "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The "marital estate" consists of the two spouses. Any person other than the two spouses is legally a "third party" to that estate. While Levi Angell was the son of the parties, he was a distinct legal entity and not a party to the marriage dissolution. The defense’s assertion that a child is the "product of the marriage" and therefore not a third party is a policy argument that cannot override the statutory definition. As noted in *In re Marriage of Hagen*, 410 N.W.2d 449, 451 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987), "the source of the property determines its character." Here, the source of the funds is Levi Angell, a third party relative to the marital estate of Gordon and Loretta Angell.

Second, the defense’s assertion that a beneficiary designation is not a "bequest" is foreclosed by binding precedent. In *In re Marriage of Bier*, 306 N.W.2d at 431, the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly held that life insurance proceeds paid to a named beneficiary are "acquired by bequest" within the meaning of the statute. The court rejected the argument that such proceeds are marital property simply because they were acquired during the marriage. The defense’s attempt to limit *Bier* to situations where the insured is a spouse is without merit. The statutory language applies regardless of the relationship between the insured and the beneficiary, provided the insured is a third party to the marriage. The Court is bound by the Supreme Court’s interpretation of the term "bequest" in this context. Furthermore, the defense’s argument regarding the payment of insurance premiums is not supported by the facts provided; the record is silent regarding the source of premium payments, and thus the defense cannot overcome the statutory presumption based on speculation.

Accordingly, the district court properly classified the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

**II. Division of Property and Federal Preemption**

The Court next addresses the division of these nonmarital benefits. The district court awarded Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property pursuant to Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to prevent unfair hardship.

The Court reaffirms its previous conclusion that federal law preempts the state court’s authority to divide these military death benefits. The Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance Act, 38 U.S.C. § 1970, provides that payments of insurance are "not subject to execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process." Similarly, 10 U.S.C. § 1475(d) contains identical anti-attachment protections for death gratuities.

The United States Supreme Court has consistently interpreted these provisions to preempt state community property laws. In *Ridgway v. Ridgway*, 454 U.S. 46, 54 (1981), the Court held that the anti-attachment clause of the SGLI reflects a congressional intent to protect the service member’s designated beneficiary from all claims, including those of a former spouse. The Court stated that federal law "preempts state law" to the extent it attempts to reach the SGLI proceeds. This principle was established earlier in *Wissner v. Wissner*, 338 U.S. 655, 658 (1950), where the Court held that federal law governing servicemembers' insurance prevents state courts from diverting the benefits to anyone other than the designated beneficiary.

The defense concedes in its closing statement that if the benefits are classified as nonmarital property, federal law likely prohibits their division. Because the Court has affirmed the classification as nonmarital property, the federal anti-attachment statutes govern. The district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property constituted "other legal process" prohibited by federal statute. Therefore, the district court lacked the authority to divide these funds.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court affirms the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The Court reverses the district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell, as federal law preempts the division of these benefits. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.**
