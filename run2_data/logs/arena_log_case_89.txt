COURT ARENA LOG - Case 89
FACTS:
When S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC acquired a nursing home previously operated by a different company, it decided to meet its immediate staffing needs in part by offering temporary employment to employees of the prior operator. S & F informed the employees it would consider hiring those who met S & F’s “operational needs” and it would hire them on an at-will basis, subject to a probationary period, to various drug and background checks, and to their signing an arbitration agreement. When it began operations, S & F implemented these and other new terms and conditions of employment.

The National Labor Relations Board later held that, because S & F failed to notify the employees that the terms and conditions of their employment would change, S & F was a “perfectly clear” successor within the meaning of NLRB v. Burns International Security Services, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972). As such, S & F was bound by the collective bargaining agreement (CBA) between its predecessor and the Service Employees International Union and had violated § 8(a)(1) and (5) of the National Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 158(a)(1) and (5), by unilaterally changing the terms and conditions of employment prescribed in that CBA. Consequently, the Board ordered S & F to restore the preexisting terms of employment and to make the employees whole with back pay. S & F petitions for review, and the Board cross-appeals for enforcement, of that order.

We hold the Board erred in concluding S & F was a “perfectly clear” successor and was not entitled to implement new terms and conditions of employment. We therefore grant in part the petition for review and deny in part enforcement of the *446Board’s order. We enforce the Board’s order as it relates to matters S & F does not contest.

I. Background

S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC (d/b/a Windsor Convalescent Center of North Long Beach) is affiliated with a chain of nursing and assisted living facilities managed by SnF Management and operated under the Windsor name. In 2004 S & F purchased Candlewood Care Center from Covenant Care Orange Inc. Covenant Care had collective bargaining agreements with the SEIU, Local 434B, covering two different bargaining units at Candlewood.

Prior to assuming control of the Candle-wood facility, S & F concluded it would need to increase the level of care, replace the staff, and renovate the building. Closer to taking over on July 1, 2004 S & F decided it could not replace the entire staff at once because the turnover would be too disruptive to the residents. Instead, S & F decided to hire some of Candlewood’s employees for up to 90 days while it continued to recruit new employees.

In June 2004 S & F caused applications for employment to be distributed to the existing staff. A cover sheet informed the employees that S & F “intends to implement significant operational changes” and “[c]urrent Candlewood employees interested in positions with [S & F] must submit the attached application for employment.” Further, it advised, only “[a]pplieants who meet the [Company’s] operational needs will be interviewed,” and any offer of employment “will be contingent on your passing a pre-employment physical, drug test and acceptable reference and background checks.” The job application itself required the applicant to affirm his or her understanding that successfully passing the tests and checks was a condition of employment, that any employment would be at will, and that S & F “can change benefits, policies and conditions at any time.”

At the end of June, S & F interviewed all the Candlewood employees who had submitted applications. In each interview, the applicant was informed that any possible employment would be temporary and would last no more than 90 days.

S & F’s director of human resources sent to each employee who was selected a letter dated June 30 with the subject line “OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT.” The letter explained, “As a temporary employee of Windsor, you are not eligible for company benefits.... Other terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor’s personnel policies and its employee handbook.” The letter further stated the employment was temporary because S & F had not yet been able to assess the employee’s “skills and abilities” or “the building’s ongoing operational and staffing needs.” It also noted that employment was at will and that “[n]o later than the expiration of the 90-day period, which ends on September 29th, your employment with Windsor will end, unless you are selected for regular employment.” In order to accept the offer the addressee was required to sign and date the letter. Those hired also had to sign an “Agreement to be Bound by Alternative Dispute Resolution Policy,” which provided that arbitration would be the exclusive means of resolving all disputes relating to termination of employment, unlawful discrimination, and sexual harassment.

S & F began operations on July 1 with approximately 120 employees. Most were former Candlewood employees newly employed on a temporary basis; 10 to 12 had not been employed by Candlewood. Seventeen Candlewood employees were not *447employed either because they had not applied or because they were not chosen.

At a staff meeting on July 9, S & F distributed to the employees handbooks describing Windsor’s policies and its terms and conditions of employment. The temporary employees received a handbook dated July 1, 2004 that did not include information about employer-provided benefits. The “regular” employees (i.e., those who had not worked for Candlewood) received a handbook dated January 1, 2004, reflecting the most recent revision of Windsor’s system-wide policies; this version did include information about employer-provided benefits.

S & F continued over the next three months to replace former Candlewood employees with new employees. By October 1 a majority of the company’s employees had been hired from outside the Candle-wood staff; some 30-40 of the Candlewood employees hired initially as temporary employees had been hired for regular employment and given the January 1 version of the handbook.

Also following the takeover, S & F invested about $500,000 to renovate the facility. Among other changes, S & F repainted the employee lounge and the hallway where Candlewood had hung a bulletin board for the use of the Union, at which time S & F removed the bulletin board.

Meanwhile, on July 1 the Union requested bargaining with S & F. The Company responded on July 7 that it had not yet hired a representative complement of employees, so the Union’s demand for bargaining was premature. The Union filed unfair labor practice charges, and the Board General Counsel issued a complaint alleging that when S & F began operating the facility oh July 1, 2004, it became “a successor to Candlewood” but refused to bargain with the Union, in violation of § 8(a)(1) and (5) of the Act. The complaint further alleged that since July 1, 2004 S & F had made unilateral changes at the former Candlewood facility — removing union-related materials from a bulletin board, prohibiting the posting of union materials, and implementing new employment policies — again in violation of § 8(a)(1) and (5), and had violated § 8(a)(3) in several ways unrelated to the issue of successorship.

Following a hearing, an Administrative Law Judge found S & F was a successor to Candlewood. According to the ALJ, the “temporary” employees were actually akin to probationary employees because their work was to be evaluated during the period of temporary employment and they did not have a definite anticipated termination date. Therefore the temporary employees could be counted in determining whether S & F had a substantial and representative complement of employees, and whether a majority of that complement were from Candlewood, when S & F began operations on July 1. Having answered both questions in the affirmative, the ALJ concluded S & F was obligated to recognize and to bargain with the Union as of July 1.

At the same time, the ALJ found S & F, although a successor to Candlewood, was not a “perfectly clear” successor because S & F’s pre-employment communications to the Candlewood employees put them on notice that the terms and conditions of their employment would change. Accordingly, although S & F was obliged to recognize and to bargain with the Union, it was entitled first to establish the initial terms and conditions of employment.

The Board affirmed the ALJ’s finding that S & F was a successor to Candlewood but went on, with Member Schaumber dissenting, to hold (1) as a matter of law, S & F was a “perfectly clear” successor and, (2) as a matter of fact, had “failed to clearly announce its intent to establish a *448new set of conditions prior to inviting former [Candlewood] employees to accept employment.” Windsor Convalescent Ctr. of N. Long Beach, 351 N.L.R.B. 975, 980 (2007). The Board concluded, therefore, that S & F did not have the right unilaterally to set the initial terms and conditions of employment. S & F petitions for review solely of the legal and factual underpinnings of this determination.

The Board also held, and S & F does not contest, that S & F refused to hire four union stewards, suspended or terminated certain employees for engaging in protected activity, and informed employees the facility would be nonunion, all in violation of § 8(a)(1) and (3) of the Act; and, although a successor employer, S & F failed to recognize and to bargain with the Union, in violation of § 8(a)(5) of the Act. In keeping with this court’s “longstanding rule,” Carpenters & Millwrights, Local Union 2471 v. NLRB, 481 F.3d 804, 808 (2007), “[t]he Board is entitled to summary enforcement of the uncontested portions of its order.” Flying Food Group Inc. v. NLRB, 471 F.3d 178, 181 (2006).

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Governing Legal Standard

The Supreme Court's decision in **NLRB v. Burns International Security Services**, 406 U.S. 272 (1972), established the framework for determining when a successor employer is bound to recognize and bargain with a union representing employees of the predecessor. The Court held that a successor employer is generally required to recognize and bargain with the union if: (1) the successor employs a majority of the predecessor's employees; and (2) the successor is a "substantial continuity" of the predecessor's business. **Id. at 281-82.**

However, the Court further recognized an important exception: a successor employer may establish its own initial terms and conditions of employment if it makes it "perfectly clear" to prospective employees that it will not be bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. **Id. at 282-83.** As this Court has explained, the purpose of this exception is to allow successors to "make known to the employees their intention to establish a new set of conditions prior to inviting them to accept employment." **Id.**

The Board correctly found that S & F was a successor employer obligated to recognize and bargain with the Union. S & F does not contest this determination. However, the Board erred in holding that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor that could not establish initial terms without first bargaining.

### B. S & F Made It Perfectly Clear That It Would Establish New Terms

The record establishes that S & F provided more than sufficient notice to prospective employees that their employment would be governed by entirely new terms and conditions. The Board's contrary finding is unsupported by the record and reflects a misapplication of the Burns standard.

#### 1. The Cover Sheet and Job Application

The cover sheet distributed to existing Candlewood employees explicitly stated that S & F "intends to implement significant operational changes." This unambiguous language put employees on notice that their terms of employment would change. The job application further required applicants to acknowledge that "benefits, policies and conditions" could be "change[d] at any time." These documents alone provided clear notice that S & F did not intend to be bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement.

The Board characterized these communications as insufficient to establish that S & F made its intent "perfectly clear." This characterization is untenable. The cover sheet's explicit statement of intent to implement "significant operational changes" is precisely the type of communication the Burns Court envisioned. The job application's acknowledgment that benefits and policies could be changed at any time further reinforced this message.

#### 2. The Offer of Temporary Employment

The June 30, 2004 offer letters provided additional, unambiguous notice. The subject line itself—"OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT"—signaled that this was a new employment relationship, not a continuation of the old one. The letters explicitly stated that temporary employees would "not be eligible for company benefits" and that "other terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor's personnel policies and its employee handbook." This language could not be clearer: employment would be governed by Windsor's policies, not the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement.

The offer letters also explained that the temporary nature of the employment was because S & F "had not yet been able to assess the employee's skills and abilities or the building's ongoing operational and staffing needs." This explanation reinforced that S & F was establishing a new employment relationship, not continuing the old one.

Most significantly, the offer letters stated that employment "will end" after ninety days "unless you are selected for regular employment." This made explicit that continued employment was not guaranteed and that the terms of any continued employment would be determined anew. Prospective employees knew they were not being hired under the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement—they were being offered temporary positions that might lead to regular employment under entirely new terms.

#### 3. The Employee Handbooks

On July 9, 2004, S & F distributed employee handbooks describing its policies and terms of employment. Temporary employees received a handbook dated July 1, 2004, that did not include information about employer-provided benefits. This was consistent with the offer letters' statement that temporary employees would not be eligible for benefits.

The distribution of these handbooks provided yet another layer of notice. Employees received written documentation of Windsor's policies—policies that differed from those under the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. This further reinforced the message that employment would be governed by Windsor's terms, not the old CBA.

### C. The Board's Factual Finding Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence

The Board found that S & F "failed to clearly announce its intent to establish a new set of conditions prior to inviting former [Candlewood] employees to accept employment." This finding is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The standard for "perfectly clear" successor status does not require that the successor engage in a particular form of notification. Rather, the question is whether the totality of the successor's communications made it unmistakably clear that the old terms would not apply. **See Burns**, 406 U.S. at 282-83. Here, the totality of S & F's communications—the cover sheet, job application, offer letters, and handbooks—collectively provided unmistakable notice that S & F would establish new terms.

The Board's finding that S & F failed to clearly announce its intent is contradicted by the record. The cover sheet explicitly stated S & F's intent to implement "significant operational changes." The offer letters explicitly stated that temporary employees would not be eligible for benefits and that employment would be governed by Windsor's policies. These are not ambiguous or subtle hints—they are clear, unambiguous statements of intent.

The Board appears to have focused on the "temporary" nature of the employment, suggesting that this created ambiguity. However, the record demonstrates that S & F was entirely transparent about the temporary nature of the employment. The offer letters explicitly stated that employment would last no more than ninety days and would end unless the employee was selected for regular employment. This transparency does not undermine the clarity of S & F's announcement—it reinforces it.

### D. The Board's Legal Conclusion Is Erroneous

The Board held that, "as a matter of law," S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor. This holding reflects a misapplication of the Burns standard.

The Burns Court explicitly recognized that a successor may establish initial terms if it "make[s] known to the employees their intention to establish a new set of conditions prior to inviting them to accept employment." **406 U.S. at 282-83.** This standard is satisfied when the successor's communications, taken as a whole, make it unmistakably clear that the old terms will not apply.

The Board appears to have imposed a requirement that the successor explicitly reference the collective bargaining agreement and specifically announce that it will not be bound by its terms. This goes beyond what Burns requires. The standard is satisfied when prospective employees understand, based on the successor's communications, that they will not be working under the old agreement—that is, that the successor will establish new terms.

Here, S & F's communications made this unmistakably clear. The cover sheet announced "significant operational changes." The offer letters stated that benefits would not be available and that employment would be governed by Windsor's policies. The handbooks set forth Windsor's terms, which differed from the predecessor's CBA. Any reasonable prospective employee would understand from these communications that they were not being hired under the old collective bargaining agreement.

The Board's contrary conclusion reflects an unduly restrictive interpretation of the Burns standard—an interpretation that would effectively require successors to use specific magic words referencing the predecessor's CBA. This is not required by Burns and should be rejected by this Court.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff's opening argument mischaracterizes the record and misapplies the governing legal standard.

### A. The Plaintiff's Characterization of the Record Is Inaccurate

The plaintiff argues that S & F "failed to clearly announce its intent to establish a new set of conditions." This is simply incorrect. The record demonstrates that S & F clearly announced its intent through multiple communications:

- The cover sheet announced S & F's intent to implement "significant operational changes."
- The job application required acknowledgment that benefits and policies could be "change[d] at any time."
- The offer letters explicitly stated that temporary employees would "not be eligible for company benefits" and that employment would be governed by Windsor's policies.
- The handbooks set forth Windsor's terms, which differed from the predecessor's CBA.

The plaintiff attempts to downplay these communications by characterizing them as merely describing the "temporary" nature of employment. However, the communications went far beyond describing the temporary nature—they explicitly announced that employment would be governed by Windsor's policies, not the predecessor's agreement.

### B. The Plaintiff Misapplies the Burns Standard

The plaintiff argues that the Burns standard requires "perfection" and that S & F failed to meet this standard. This misreads Burns. The standard is not perfection—it is clear communication of intent to establish new terms. The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that a successor must use particular language or follow a particular format. The standard is satisfied when the successor's communications, taken as a whole, make it unmistakably clear that the old terms will not apply.

Here, S & F's communications satisfied this standard. The plaintiff's argument to the contrary should be rejected.

### C. The Plaintiff's Policy Arguments Are Misguided

The plaintiff suggests that the Board's ruling is necessary to protect employees from being misled. However, the record demonstrates that S & F was entirely transparent with its employees. It told them explicitly that it would implement significant operational changes, that benefits and policies could be changed, that temporary employees would not be eligible for benefits, and that employment would be governed by Windsor's policies.

If anything, the Board's ruling penalizes transparency. Under the Board's reasoning, an employer who clearly communicates its intent to establish new terms is held to have failed to make its intent "perfectly clear." This creates a perverse incentive for successors to be less than transparent—to conceal their intent rather than communicate it clearly.

The Board's ruling should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of a decision by the National Labor Relations Board (the "Board"), and the Board's cross-application for enforcement. The central issue on review is whether the Board erred in determining that S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC ("S & F") was a "perfectly clear" successor employer bound by the collective bargaining agreement of its predecessor, Candlewood Care Center, or whether S & F provided sufficient notice to establish its own initial terms and conditions of employment. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the parties and the factual record provided.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

The legal standard for determining whether a successor employer may set initial terms of employment without bargaining was established in *NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972). Under *Burns*, a successor employer is generally required to bargain with the incumbent union, but this obligation does not arise if the successor makes it "perfectly clear" to the employees prior to hiring them that it does not intend to be bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. *Burns*, 406 U.S. at 282-83. The question of whether a successor has met this standard is a mixed question of law and fact. *Windsor Convalescent Ctr. of N. Long Beach*, 351 N.L.R.B. 975, 980 (2007).

**B. The Board’s Finding Regarding "Perfectly Clear" Notice**

The Board found that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor and, consequently, that S & F violated the National Labor Relations Act by unilaterally changing the terms and conditions of employment. The Board specifically concluded that S & F "failed to clearly announce its intent to establish a new set of conditions prior to inviting former [Candlewood] employees to accept employment." *Windsor Convalescent Ctr. of N. Long Beach*, 351 N.L.R.B. at 980.

**C. Evaluation of S & F’s Arguments**

S & F argues that the Board’s finding is contradicted by the record and constitutes a misapplication of the *Burns* standard. The Court finds S & F’s arguments persuasive on this point.

S & F directs the Court to several pre-employment communications that explicitly notified prospective employees of the new terms. First, the cover sheet distributed with the applications stated that S & F "intends to implement significant operational changes." Second, the job application required applicants to acknowledge that employment was at-will and that S & F "can change benefits, policies and conditions at any time."

Most significantly, S & F points to the June 30, 2004 offer letters, which were explicitly titled "OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT." These letters informed employees that, as temporary employees, they were "not eligible for company benefits" and that "[o]ther terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor’s personnel policies and its employee handbook." The letters further clarified that employment would end after 90 days unless the employee was selected for regular employment. By signing these letters, employees acknowledged and accepted these terms.

The Board’s determination that these communications failed to clearly announce an intent to establish new conditions appears to disregard the plain language of the offer letters. The statement that terms would be governed by Windsor’s policies, rather than the predecessor’s agreement, constitutes clear notice that the prior collective bargaining agreement would not control. The *Burns* standard requires that the successor’s intent be unmistakably communicated; it does not require the employer to use specific "magic words" denouncing the union agreement. See *NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. at 282-83.

Furthermore, the distribution of the employee handbooks on July 9, 2004, which outlined the specific policies governing employment, reinforced the message communicated in the offer letters. The temporary employees received a handbook that explicitly excluded employer-provided benefits, consistent with the offer letters.

The Board’s reliance on the "temporary" nature of the employment to find ambiguity is unpersuasive. The fact that S & F hired employees on a temporary basis to assess their skills and the facility's needs does not negate the clarity of the notice that, during and potentially after this period, employment would be governed by S & F’s new terms. To the contrary, the explicit distinction between temporary status (no benefits, governed by Windsor policies) and potential regular status supports the conclusion that S & F was establishing a new regime.

**D. Conclusion on Successorship**

Based on the arguments presented and the record, the Court finds that S & F provided clear, unambiguous, and repeated notice to prospective employees—prior to their acceptance of employment—that their terms and conditions would differ from those under the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. Therefore, S & F was entitled to establish its initial terms and conditions of employment without first bargaining with the Union. The Board’s conclusion to the contrary is not supported by substantial evidence and represents an error of law.

**III. RULING**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court grants in part the petition for review and denies in part the Board’s cross-application for enforcement.

1.  The petition for review is **GRANTED** regarding the Board’s determination that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor bound by the predecessor’s collective bargaining agreement. The Board’s order is **VACATED** insofar as it requires S & F to restore the preexisting terms of employment and make employees whole for changes to those terms.
2.  The Board’s order is **ENFORCED** as to the portions not contested by S & F, specifically the findings that S & F refused to hire union stewards, suspended or terminated employees for engaging in protected activity, informed employees the facility would be nonunion, and failed to recognize and bargain with the Union regarding mandatory subjects other than initial terms. See *Carpenters & Millwrights, Local Union 2471 v. NLRB*, 481 F.3d 804, 808 (2007); *Flying Food Group Inc. v. NLRB*, 471 F.3d 178, 181 (2006).

The Clerk of the Court is directed to enter judgment accordingly.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by the parties. The National Labor Relations Board (the "Board") found that S & F Market Street Healthcare LLC ("S & F") was a "perfectly clear" successor employer bound by the collective bargaining agreement of its predecessor, Candlewood Care Center. S & F petitions for review of this determination. The Court has previously issued a preliminary ruling holding that the Board erred in concluding S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor. Having reviewed the closing arguments provided by the parties, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Legal Standard**

The legal standard governing successor employers is established in *NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. 272, 92 S.Ct. 1571, 32 L.Ed.2d 61 (1972). Under *Burns*, a successor employer is generally required to bargain with the incumbent union. However, this obligation does not arise if the successor makes it "perfectly clear" to the employees prior to hiring them that it does not intend to be bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. *Burns*, 406 U.S. at 282-83. The question of whether a successor has met this standard is a mixed question of law and fact, and the Board's decision must be supported by substantial evidence. *Windsor Convalescent Ctr. of N. Long Beach*, 351 N.L.R.B. 975, 980 (2007).

**B. Evaluation of the Arguments**

The parties have submitted extensive closing arguments addressing the applicability of the *Burns* standard to the facts at hand. Both arguments presented to the Court advocate for the position that the Board erred in its determination.

**1. The "Magic Words" Requirement**

The arguments persuasively contend that the Board misapplied the *Burns* standard by effectively requiring "magic words"—an explicit reference to the collective bargaining agreement—to establish clear notice. As noted in the arguments, the Supreme Court in *Burns* held that the successor need only make its intent "perfectly clear" such that "the employees would understand" that the predecessor's agreement would not apply. *NLRB v. Burns International Security Services*, 406 U.S. at 282-83.

The Board's insistence on specific language rejecting the union contract constitutes a legal error. The statement in S & F’s offer letters that terms would be governed by Windsor’s policies—policies which explicitly excluded benefits for temporary employees—constituted clear notice of a change in terms. The Court agrees with the assertion that imposing a "magic words" requirement would chill legitimate business decisions and is inconsistent with the flexible standard established by the Supreme Court.

**2. Totality of Communications**

The arguments correctly highlight that the Board failed to consider the totality of communications provided to prospective employees. The Board’s finding that S & F "failed to clearly announce its intent to establish a new set of conditions" ignores the cumulative effect of the cover sheet, job application, offer letters, and employee handbooks.

As detailed in the arguments, the cover sheet distributed with applications explicitly stated that the company "intends to implement significant operational changes." The job application required applicants to acknowledge that "benefits, policies and conditions can be changed at any time." Most significantly, the June 30, 2004 offer letters, explicitly titled "OFFER OF TEMPORARY EMPLOYMENT," informed employees that they were "not eligible for company benefits" and that "[o]ther terms and conditions of your employment will be set forth in Windsor’s personnel policies and its employee handbook."

Viewed together, these communications left no reasonable doubt that the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement would not govern the employment relationship. The Board’s conclusion that these statements were ambiguous is not supported by substantial evidence.

**3. The Significance of the "Temporary" Designation**

The arguments effectively rebut the Board’s reliance on the "temporary" nature of the employment to find ambiguity. The temporary status was not a mere formality; it was a clear indicator that the employment relationship was fundamentally different from the permanent status contemplated by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. The offer letters made clear that employment would end after 90 days unless the employee was selected for regular employment, at which point they would be subject to Windsor's system-wide policies. This distinction reinforces the conclusion that S & F had established a new regime.

**C. Substantial Evidence Review**

Upon review of the record, the Court finds that the Board's determination that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor is not supported by substantial evidence. The record contains abundant evidence that S & F provided clear notice of its intent to establish new terms. The standard under *Burns* is not whether the successor used specific magic words, but whether the communications were sufficient to put employees on notice. Here, the evidence overwhelmingly establishes that S & F met this standard.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court adheres to and affirms its preliminary ruling. S & F provided clear, unambiguous notice to prospective employees—through multiple pre-employment communications—that it intended to establish new terms and conditions of employment and would not be bound by the predecessor's collective bargaining agreement. The Board’s determination that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor was based on an erroneous application of the *Burns* standard and is not supported by substantial evidence.

**IV. RULING**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling.

1.  The petition for review is **GRANTED** regarding the Board’s determination that S & F was a "perfectly clear" successor bound by the predecessor’s collective bargaining agreement. The Board’s order is **VACATED** insofar as it requires S & F to restore the preexisting terms of employment and make employees whole for changes to those terms.
2.  The Board’s order is **ENFORCED** as to the portions not contested by S & F, specifically the findings that S & F refused to hire union stewards, suspended or terminated employees for engaging in protected activity, informed employees the facility would be nonunion, and failed to recognize and bargain with the Union regarding mandatory subjects other than initial terms. See *Carpenters & Millwrights, Local Union 2471 v. NLRB*, 481 F.3d 804, 808 (2007); *Flying Food Group Inc. v. NLRB*, 471 F.3d 178, 181 (2006).

The Clerk of the Court is directed to enter judgment accordingly.

SO ORDERED.
