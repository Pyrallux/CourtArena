### I. The Death Benefits Should Have Been Classified as Marital Property

#### A. The Presumption of Marital Property Applies and Was Not Rebutted

Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 1, creates a presumption that "all property acquired during the marriage" is marital property. This presumption is powerful and can only be overcome by clear and convincing evidence that the property was acquired by one of the means specified in subdivision 5. *See In re Marriage of Evans*, 253 N.W.2d 916, 919 (Minn. 1977) (holding that the party claiming property as nonmarital bears the burden of proving by clear and convincing evidence that it falls within an exclusion).

The defense does not dispute that Loretta Angell received the benefits at issue following the death of her son, Levi Angell. However, the mere fact that benefits were received upon the death of a family member does not automatically transform them into nonmarital property under Minnesota law. The question is not merely who died, but whether the benefits were acquired by Loretta through the means specified in the statute: gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance *from a third party*.

The defense submits that Loretta failed to meet her burden of proving by clear and convincing evidence that these benefits were acquired by inheritance. While *In re Marriage of Smith*, 477 N.W.2d 634 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), and *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 579 N.W.2d 421 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), hold that certain death benefits may constitute nonmarital property, those cases are distinguishable on their facts. In both *Smith* and *Johnson*, the courts carefully examined the specific statutory and contractual provisions governing the benefits to determine whether they constituted inheritance. Here, the defense argues that the district court failed to conduct the requisite individualized analysis.

#### B. The Benefits Were Not Acquired by Inheritance Within the Meaning of Minnesota Law

The defense contends that the life-insurance proceeds and death-gratuity benefits were not acquired by "inheritance" as that term is used in Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5. The statutory definition of nonmarital property encompasses property "acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse."

The critical distinction lies in the nature of the benefits received. The Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance (SGLI) policy is a government-provided life insurance program for service members. The death-gratuity benefits are provided under 10 U.S.C. sections 1475-80 and the Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act. Neither of these programs constitutes an "inheritance" in the traditional sense of property passing through a decedent's estate pursuant to a will or intestacy laws.

As this Court noted in *In re Marriage of Lohr*, 304 N.W.2d 768 (Minn. 1981), the characterization of benefits depends upon the source and nature of the benefit. In *Lohr*, the Minnesota Supreme Court recognized that benefits received by a survivor upon the death of another are acquired by inheritance *when they derive from the decedent's estate*. However, when the benefits derive from a contract or statute that provides for payment to a designated beneficiary regardless of estate law, the characterization is less clear.

The defense submits that the SGLI proceeds and death-gratuity benefits more closely resemble contractual benefits—paid pursuant to a government program to a designated beneficiary—than traditional inheritances. The fact that Levi designated his mother as the beneficiary does not transform the benefit into an inheritance from a third party in the marital property context. The benefit arose from Levi's military service and his participation in the SGLI program, not from any testamentary disposition of his estate.

#### C. The Presumption Was Not Overcome

Even if this Court finds that the benefits could theoretically qualify as nonmarital property, the defense submits that Loretta failed to overcome the presumption of marital property. The presumption under Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 1, applies with full force to property acquired during the marriage, regardless of the source. *See Laws v. People*, 435 N.W.2d 493, 498 (Minn. 1989) (discussing the strong presumption in favor of marital property classification).

The fact that the funds were deposited into a separate account does not, standing alone, rebut the presumption. Traceability is relevant to the *division* of property, but it does not determine the initial classification. The question is whether the property was acquired by one of the means specified in section 518.54, subdivision 5. The defense submits that Loretta failed to demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that the benefits were acquired by inheritance from Levi as a third party, rather than as a result of the marital relationship.

### II. The District Court's Reliance on Section 518.58 Was Improper

Even assuming *arguendo* that the benefits were properly classified as nonmarital property, the defense submits that the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta's nonmarital property under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2.

#### A. Section 518.58 Does Not Authorize the Division of Federally Protected Benefits

Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, provides that the district court may, "upon request of a spouse," apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse if the court finds that "the spouse's nonmarital property is so inadequate that the spouse cannot provide for reasonable needs" or "enforcement of the support or property division will cause undue hardship."

However, the defense submits that this statute cannot be applied to divide military death benefits that are protected from attachment, assignment, or garnishment under federal law. The federal anti-attachment statutes, including 10 U.S.C. section 1475-80 and the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance provisions, create a comprehensive scheme protecting these benefits from creditors and, by extension, from division in dissolution proceedings.

In *Cuppett v. Cuppett*, 269 S.E.2d 408 (Ga. 1980), the Georgia Supreme Court held that military retirement pay could not be divided in a dissolution proceeding despite state law authorizing such division, because federal law prohibited the assignment of military pay. Similarly, in *In re Marriage of Wilson*, 530 P.2d 1374 (Or. 1975), the Oregon Supreme Court held that state courts could not divide military retirement benefits because federal law preempted state law in that area.

The defense submits that the same principle applies here. The death-gratuity benefits and SGLI proceeds are protected by federal law from attachment, assignment, or garnishment. *See 38 U.S.C. § 1970* (protecting Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance from "the claims of creditors" and from "levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process"). #### B. The District Court's Award Violates Federal Policy

The federal government provides death-gratuity benefits and SGLI coverage to service members and their families as a matter of national policy, to provide financial security to the families of those who die in service. The purpose of these benefits is to support the surviving family members during a difficult period. If these benefits could be seized by a former spouse in a dissolution proceeding, the federal policy would be undermined.

The defense submits that the district court erred in relying on section 518.58 to award Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta's nonmarital property. The district court's reliance on state law to divide these benefits violates the Supremacy Clause of the United States Constitution.

### I. THE CLASSIFICATION OF BENEFITS AS NONMARITAL PROPERTY IS CONSISTENT WITH MINNESOTA LAW AND FEDERAL PRECEDENT

#### A. The Statutory Framework

Minnesota Statutes § 518.54, subdivision 5, defines nonmarital property as "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The critical inquiry is whether the benefits at issue were acquired from a "third party" to the marriage upon the death of that third party.

Here, Levi Angell was the son of both Gordon and Loretta Angell, but he was unquestionably a third party to the marital relationship. #### B. The Preemption Analysis

The defense submits that the reasoning of *Cuppett* and *Wilson* applies with equal force to the military death benefits at issue here.
