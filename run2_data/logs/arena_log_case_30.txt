## DEFENSE RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S OPENING ARGUMENT

**Your Honor, the defense respectfully opposes the State's appeal and contends that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence. ## I. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN CONSTITUTED A SEARCH UNDER THE FOURTH AMENDMENT

The State contends that Officer Davis's use of a flashlight to scan the vehicle's interior was not a Fourth Amendment "search." This argument misapplies the precedent cited and ignores critical distinctions between those cases and the present facts.

In *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), the Court held that a flashlight illuminating the interior of a vehicle did not constitute a search **because the officer was lawfully in a position to observe the interior**—he had lawfully stopped the defendant's vehicle at a traffic checkpoint and the balloon containing heroin was in plain view on the floor of the vehicle. The Court emphasized that the officer was "in a place where he had a right to be" when he made the observation. Here, Officer Davis had not yet lawfully stopped the vehicle when he scanned its interior with a flashlight. He observed the car from sixty feet away, shining a flashlight into the vehicle's interior before any lawful basis for the stop had been established. This distinction is constitutionally significant.

Similarly, in *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986), the Court held that a police officer may look through a car window without a warrant **during a lawful traffic stop** when the officer observes a piece of paper on the floorboard that turns out to be a vehicle identification number. The officer was already legitimately engaged in a lawful activity—conducting a traffic stop—before making the observation. The Court explicitly noted that the officer's observation occurred "in the course of a lawful traffic stop."

In contrast, Officer Davis was not engaged in any lawful activity vis-à-vis the vehicle at the time he scanned its interior with a flashlight. He had positioned himself at a chain link fence barricading an uncompleted freeway and was observing traffic from a distance of sixty feet. The use of a flashlight to peer into the darkened interior of a vehicle from such a distance constitutes exploration beyond what is plain view from a lawful vantage point. As the Supreme Court held in *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), the Fourth Amendment protects people against unreasonable searches and seizures, and this protection extends to reasonable expectations of privacy. A person maintains a reasonable expectation of privacy in the interior of their vehicle, and the government may not enhance its ability to observe that interior through artificial means such as flashlights without a warrant or applicable exception.

The defense respectfully submits that the circuit court correctly found that the flashlight scan constituted a search requiring either a warrant or satisfaction of a recognized exception to the warrant requirement.

---

## II. THE FACTS KNOWN TO OFFICER DAVIS DID NOT ESTABLISH PROBABLE CAUSE

Assuming arguendo that the flashlight scan constituted a search, the State contends that the facts known to Officer Davis established probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. The defense respectfully disagrees.

### A. The Informant's Tip Did Not Provide Probable Cause

The State relies heavily on a tip from a security guard who reported hearing gunshots. However, the reliability of this informant tip must be assessed under the framework established in *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), and *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990).

In *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), the Supreme Court held that an anonymous tip that a person was carrying a gun was insufficient to justify a Terry stop, even when the tip was corroborated by police observation of the person's location. The Court emphasized that an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability does not establish probable cause, even if it is partially corroborated.

Here, the security guard's tip suffers from significant reliability concerns. The guard did not identify himself by name or provide any basis for assessing his credibility. He did not witness who fired the shots—he only heard them. He could not describe any suspects or vehicles. The guard merely reported hearing 6-7 gunshots and feeling that they came from the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway. This is precisely the type of uncorroborated, conclusory tip that *J.L.* found insufficient to establish probable cause.

The State argues that the guard's tip was reliable because he was a "security guard." However, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the identity of an informant alone does not establish reliability. As the Court noted in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the informant's veracity and basis of knowledge must be assessed under the totality of circumstances. A security guard is not equivalent to a citizen informant with firsthand observation of criminal activity. The guard here had no direct knowledge of who fired the shots or whether any crime had been committed.

### B. The Vehicle's Presence on the Unfinished Freeway Did Not Create Probable Cause

The State contends that the vehicle's presence on the unfinished H-3 freeway at 10:30 p.m. created probable cause. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the fact that a vehicle is on an unfinished freeway does not, standing alone, create probable cause to believe that the vehicle contains evidence of a crime. An unfinished freeway is a physical location that may be accessible for various lawful purposes, including legitimate access by construction workers, surveyors, or others with authorization.

Second, the vehicle was traveling at a "reasonable speed" with its headlights on—behavior entirely consistent with lawful operation. As the Supreme Court has held, probable cause requires more than mere suspicion or mere opportunity to commit a crime. See *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40 (1968).

Third, the temporal proximity to the reported gunshots does not establish probable cause. The Supreme Court in *Florida v. J.L.* rejected the argument that mere temporal and spatial proximity to reported criminal activity establishes probable cause. The State has not established that the vehicle was the source of the gunshots or that it contained evidence related to any crime.

The totality of circumstances here did not warrant a person of reasonable caution in believing that the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. The combination of an uncorroborated tip from an unidentified informant and the vehicle's presence on an unfinished roadway at night is insufficient under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983).

---

## III. THE VEHICLE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The State invokes the vehicle exception to the warrant requirement, citing *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), and *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991). However, the vehicle exception does not authorize the search conducted here.

The vehicle exception permits warrantless searches of vehicles when there is probable cause to believe the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991), the exception requires probable cause **before** the search. Here, for the reasons stated above, there was no probable cause at the time Officer Davis scanned the vehicle's interior with a flashlight.

Moreover, the vehicle exception requires that the vehicle itself provides the probable cause. The facts must establish a connection between the vehicle and the criminal activity. Mere presence at the scene of reported criminal activity, without more, does not establish the requisite probable cause. See *Yarborough v. Alvarado*, 541 U.S. 652 (2004) (probable cause requires facts sufficient for a reasonable person to believe that the defendant had committed or was committing a crime).

The State has not established that Officer Davis had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime before he scanned its interior. The vehicle exception therefore does not apply, and the search was unconstitutional.

---

## IV. EVEN IF PROBABLE CAUSE EXISTED, THE SCOPE OF THE SEARCH EXCEEDED CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS

Even assuming arguendo that probable cause existed, the scope of the search exceeded constitutional limits. After the flashlight scan revealed a .22-caliber rifle casing and a black case, the officers pushed the front seat forward to reveal a ukulele case, unlatched the case, and searched its contents. This went far beyond what was necessary to confirm or refute probable cause.

The Fourth Amendment requires that searches be reasonably limited in scope to the purpose of the search. As the Supreme Court held in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and *Terry v. Ohio*'s progeny, a search must be "reasonably related in scope to the circumstances which justified the interference in the first place." Here, once the officers observed the rifle casing, they had sufficient evidence to seize the vehicle and seek a warrant. The further intrusion into the ukulele case was not reasonably justified by any legitimate law enforcement need.

---

## CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully submits that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence. The flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior constituted a search under the Fourth Amendment. The facts known to Officer Davis did not establish probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. The vehicle exception does not apply, and even if it did, the scope of the search exceeded constitutional limits.

The State has not demonstrated that the search was constitutional. The evidence obtained from the search must remain suppressed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee.**

**NO. **

**APPEAL FROM THE CIRCUIT COURT OF THE FIRST CIRCUIT **

**ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO SUPPRESS **

**PREAMBLE**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State argues that the warrantless search of Defendant’s vehicle was justified under the Fourth Amendment. After reviewing the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable precedent, this Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a trial court’s decision on a motion to suppress evidence, the appellate court must examine the facts presented to the trial court and determine whether the ruling was correct as a matter of law. *State v. Kealoha*, 79 Hawaii 109, 900 P.2d 130 (1995). The application of the Fourth Amendment to the facts is a question of law which is reviewed de novo. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690 (1996).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Flashlight Scan Did Not Constitute a Search**

The defense contends that the flashlight scan of the vehicle’s interior constituted a search under the Fourth Amendment because Officer Davis lacked a lawful basis for the stop at the time the scan occurred. This argument mischaracterizes the sequence of events provided in the facts.

The facts clearly state that Officer Davis stopped the car, ordered the four occupants out, and conducted a pat down before "the other officers scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight." Because the scan occurred after the vehicle was lawfully stopped, the officers were legitimately present at the vehicle. Under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle does not constitute a search if the officer is lawfully present. The Supreme Court held that "the officer’s use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of the vehicle did not constitute a 'search' within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment" because it merely revealed what was already visible from a lawful vantage point. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983). Similarly, in *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986), the Court confirmed that police may look through a car window without a warrant during a lawful encounter. Consequently, the flashlight scan was lawful and did not violate the Defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights.

**B. Probable Cause Existed to Search the Vehicle**

The defense argues that the security guard’s tip was unreliable and that the vehicle’s presence on the unfinished freeway was insufficient to establish probable cause. The Court disagrees.

The "totality of the circumstances" test set forth in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), requires the court to evaluate all available information to determine if there is a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place. Here, the officers possessed the following facts: (1) a security guard reported hearing 6-7 gunshots and a bullet striking a building; (2) the guard specifically identified the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway as the source of the shots; (3) Officer Davis responded to that exact location within minutes; and (4) he observed a vehicle traveling on the uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m.

The defense relies on *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), to argue that an anonymous tip regarding a gun is insufficient. However, *J.L.* involved an anonymous tip regarding a person standing at a bus stop, with no predictive information or indication of criminal activity beyond the possession of a weapon. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000). In contrast, the security guard here reported a specific, completed crime—discharging a firearm—at a specific location. The corroboration of the location of the vehicle—the very place the guard said the shots came from—provided the necessary indicia of reliability. See *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990) (holding that a tip is corroborated if police observe the suspect engaging in conduct predicted by the informant). The vehicle’s presence on a closed, unfinished freeway at night was not merely "consistent with lawful operation" as the defense suggests; it was unusual behavior that, combined with the report of gunshots, created a fair probability that the vehicle contained the weapon used in the shooting.

**C. The Scope of the Search Was Constitutional**

The defense argues that even if probable cause existed, the search of the ukulele case exceeded the constitutional limits of the vehicle exception. The defense relies on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), to argue that the search must be strictly limited to the justification for the interference. However, *Terry* governs investigatory stops and frisks for weapons, not full searches of vehicles based on probable cause.

Once probable cause exists to believe a vehicle contains evidence of a crime, the vehicle exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement permits a thorough search of the vehicle and any containers within it that might conceal the object of the search. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991). The Supreme Court in *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991), explicitly held that "when the police have probable cause to believe that a vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime, they may search the vehicle without a warrant," including containers within the vehicle. Because the officers had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained a firearm related to the reported shooting, they were authorized to search the ukulele case, a container capable of holding a rifle. The discovery of the .22-caliber casing and the black case further justified this search.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The flashlight scan was not a search under the Fourth Amendment, and the officers had probable cause to search the vehicle under the vehicle exception. The scope of the search, including the opening of the ukulele case, was justified by the probable cause that the vehicle contained the weapon used in the reported shooting.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the State of Hawaii’s appeal is hereby GRANTED. The circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is REVERSED, and the case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

It identified no specific vehicle, no description of occupants, and no direction of travel. A tip that shots were fired from a general area does not provide reasonable suspicion to stop every vehicle subsequently found in that area. *See Florida v. ### II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN CONSTITUTED AN UNLAWFUL SEARCH

Even assuming *arguendo* that the stop was lawful, the flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior exceeded the scope of any lawful investigative detention and constituted a search requiring either consent, a warrant, or probable cause.

The State relies on *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), for the proposition that a flashlight scan is not a search. However, the defense respectfully submits that the State misapplies this precedent. In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court addressed whether the use of a flashlight during a lawful traffic stop constituted a search. The Court held that it did not, reasoning that the officer was lawfully present and the flashlight merely enhanced his ability to see what was already in plain view. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. at 735.

Critically, however, the *Brown* Court emphasized that the officer must be lawfully present at the location from which he views the interior of the vehicle. Here, the officers had no lawful right to be scanning the interior of the vehicle absent probable cause or consent. The vehicle had been stopped on an unsubstantiated suspicion. The officers were not conducting a lawful traffic stop for a violation. They were not investigating a specific vehicle matching a description. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971). Here, the officers were not looking at items that were obviously contraband. They were systematically scanning the interior of the vehicle—looking under seats, behind objects, and into concealed areas. This goes beyond what the flashlight doctrine permits.

The State's reliance on *New York v. Class*, 475 U.S. 106 (1986), is similarly misplaced. In *Class*, the Court held that an officer could look through a car window during a lawful traffic stop to verify vehicle identification numbers. The Court emphasized that the officer was engaged in a legitimate traffic enforcement purpose. Here, there was no traffic violation, no regulatory purpose, and no basis for the officers to be examining the interior of the vehicle.

The circuit court correctly held that the flashlight scan was unlawful.

There was no description of a vehicle, no direction of travel, no timeline, and no information about what the vehicle would be doing. The only "corroboration" the State can point to is that a vehicle happened to be in the general area—a fact that is entirely consistent with innocent activity.

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee.**

**NO. **

**ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO SUPPRESS **

**PREAMBLE**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal of the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State argues that the warrantless search of Defendant’s vehicle was justified under the Fourth Amendment. After reviewing the record, the rebuttals of counsel, and the applicable precedent, this Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a trial court’s decision on a motion to suppress evidence, the appellate court must examine the facts presented to the trial court and determine whether the ruling was correct as a matter of law. *State v. Kealoha*, 79 Hawaii 109, 900 P.2d 130 (1995). The application of the Fourth Amendment to the facts is a question of law which is reviewed de novo. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690 (1996).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Stop of the Vehicle Was Unlawful**

The State contends that the stop of the vehicle was justified by the "totality of the circumstances," specifically the security guard’s report of gunshots and the vehicle’s presence on the unfinished freeway. The Court disagrees.

The Fourth Amendment requires that a police officer have reasonable suspicion—specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity—before stopping a vehicle. *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 663 (1979); *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). Reasonable suspicion must be based on "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

Here, the security guard reported hearing gunshots and "felt" they were coming from the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway. The facts explicitly state that the guard "furnished no other information." Critically, the guard provided no description of a vehicle, no description of any suspects, and no information regarding the direction of travel or the number of individuals involved. The tip was therefore a generalized report of a crime in a specific area, lacking any detail that would link a specific vehicle to that crime.

The State argues that the vehicle’s presence on the unfinished freeway corroborated the tip and provided reasonable suspicion. However, the corroboration of the mere location of a crime does not establish reasonable suspicion to stop any vehicle found in that location. In *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000), the Supreme Court held that an anonymous tip indicating a person has a gun is insufficient to justify a stop without some indicia of reliability. While the guard here was not anonymous, the lack of any predictive information or description of the perpetrator renders the tip insufficient to distinguish the Defendant’s vehicle from any other vehicle that might have been in the area.

The State relies on *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990), to argue that the location corroboration was sufficient. In *Alabama v. White*, the informant predicted the suspect’s future behavior, stating the suspect would leave a specific apartment at a specific time and travel in a specific car with cocaine. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325, 327 (1990). The police corroborated these predictive details. Here, by contrast, the guard made no predictions about the vehicle or its occupants. The officer simply found a car in the general area where shots were reported. This is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on, conduct consistent with innocent activity, even on an unfinished roadway. Without a description connecting the vehicle to the reported gunshots, the stop was an impermissible seizure based on unparticularized suspicion. *See Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 91 (1979) (noting that the Fourth Amendment requires individualized suspicion).

Because the stop was unlawful, the subsequent detention and search of the vehicle are tainted.

**B. The Flashlight Scan Constituted an Unlawful Search**

The State argues that the flashlight scan was lawful under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), because the officer was lawfully present. However, as established above, the officer was not lawfully present at the vehicle because the stop itself violated the Fourth Amendment.

The "plain view" doctrine and the holding of *Texas v. Brown* presuppose that the officer is lawfully present in the place from which the observation is made. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 739-40 (1983). Since the initial stop was unlawful, the officers had no right to be at the vehicle scanning its interior. Consequently, the discovery of the .22-caliber casing and the black case was the result of an unconstitutional search.

**C. The Search of the Ukulele Case Violated the Fourth Amendment**

The State argues that the vehicle exception to the warrant requirement justified the search of the ukulele case. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991). However, the vehicle exception only applies if the police have probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565, 577 (1991).

The probable cause to search the vehicle was derived entirely from the unlawful stop and the subsequent unlawful flashlight scan. The officers had no independent information suggesting the vehicle contained a weapon prior to the stop. The guard’s tip did not describe a vehicle. Therefore, the probable cause is "fruit of the poisonous tree." *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963). The evidence discovered during the flashlight scan (the casing and the black case) cannot be used to bootstrap probable cause for a full vehicle search.

Furthermore, even if probable cause had existed independently, the search of the ukulele case exceeded the scope of the vehicle exception under the facts known to the officers at the time. The officers had no information suggesting a weapon would be concealed in a musical instrument case. While *California v. Acevedo* allows the search of containers if there is probable cause to search the vehicle, the probable cause here was entirely dependent on the unlawfully obtained evidence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress. The security guard’s tip lacked the specificity and predictive detail necessary to provide reasonable suspicion for the stop. The stop was therefore unlawful, rendering the subsequent flashlight scan and the discovery of the evidence unconstitutional violations of the Fourth Amendment. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine requires suppression of all evidence derived from these violations.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the State of Hawaii’s appeal is hereby DENIED. The circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is AFFIRMED.

Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Here, the security guard reported gunshots and a bullet striking a building—direct evidence of a violent crime. Officer Davis then observed a vehicle on the unfinished freeway at the exact location and time of the incident. The .22-caliber casing—immediately recognizable as a spent firearm round—confirmed the presence of a weapon. This is not "mere presence" but corroborated evidence of criminal activity.  

The defense cites *Florida v. J.L.* to argue the tip lacked reliability, but again, that case is inapposite. *J.L.* involved an anonymous tip with no basis for reliability, whereas here the guard was a known source who reported a recent, specific crime. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "the reliability of an informant’s tip may be established by the corroboration of details that are not known to the public but are revealed by the informant." *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. at 330. The guard’s report of the *unfinished H-3 freeway portion* as the location of the gunshots was a detail not publicly known, and Officer Davis’s observation of a vehicle there immediately after the report corroborated it.  

Moreover, the defense’s reliance on *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 91 (1979), is misplaced. *Ybarra* involved a warrantless search of a patron in a bar, where no individualized suspicion existed. Here, the vehicle was directly tied to the crime scene: the guard reported gunshots at a specific location, and the vehicle was found there within minutes. This creates the requisite individualized suspicion for the stop and search.  

### IV. The Search of the Ukulele Case Was Lawful Under the Vehicle Exception  
Once probable cause was established for the vehicle, the officers were entitled to search any container within it that might conceal the object of the search. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565, 577 (1991). The ukulele case was a container that could reasonably hold a firearm, and the officers had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained a weapon based on the .22 casing and the context of the gunshots. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), confirms that "if probable cause justifies the search of a lawfully stopped vehicle, it justifies the search of every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search." The fact that the case was labeled a "ukulele case" does not shield it from search; the officers were not required to ignore a container that could hold a weapon simply because it was labeled innocuously.  

The defense claims the search "exceeded the scope" of probable cause, but this ignores the totality of circumstances. The security guard reported gunshots, a bullet hit a building, and the officers found a spent .22 casing in the vehicle. A firearm could easily be concealed in a musical instrument case—indeed, the rifle was later found there. The officers did not engage in a "fishing expedition"; they had specific, corroborated evidence that a weapon was present, and the ukulele case was a logical place to search for it.  

### V. Conclusion  
The circuit court’s suppression order was based on a fundamental misunderstanding of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. The security guard’s report provided specific, reliable information about a recent violent crime in a precise location. Officer Davis’s observation of a vehicle at that location within minutes of the incident established reasonable suspicion for the stop. The flashlight scan was a lawful observation under *Texas v. Brown*, revealing the .22 casing and black case, which together with the context of the gunshots established probable cause for the vehicle search. The ukulele case was properly searched under the vehicle exception.  

All evidence derived from these lawful actions must be admitted. The circuit court’s order should be reversed, and the motion to suppress denied.

## I. THE STOP WAS UNLAWFUL BECAUSE REASONABLE SUSPICION DID NOT EXIST

The prosecution contends that the security guard's tip provided reasonable suspicion for the stop. This argument fails because the tip lacked the specificity required under the Fourth Amendment.

### A. The Guard's Tip Was Insufficiently Specific

The prosecution attempts to distinguish this case from *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), by arguing that the guard was a "known, credible source." However, this misreads the holding of *J.L.* The Supreme Court in *J.L.* did not merely rely on the anonymity of the tip; it emphasized that the tip lacked predictive information. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. at 271 ("The tip in J.L. provided no predictive facts."). The critical deficiency here is not the guard's identity—it is the complete absence of any description or predictive information about the perpetrator or vehicle.

The prosecution cites *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990), for the proposition that tips from known informants carry greater weight. However, the prosecution ignores the crucial distinguishing factor in *Alabama v. White*: the informant provided **predictive information**—specifically, that the suspect would leave a particular apartment at a particular time and travel in a particular car. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. at 327. The Court upheld the stop precisely because the police corroborated these specific predictions. Here, by contrast, the guard provided no description of any vehicle, no description of any suspects, and no information about the direction of travel or number of individuals involved. The guard merely reported hearing gunshots and expressed a subjective belief about their origin.

This Court correctly noted in its ruling that "the guard made no predictions about the vehicle or its occupants." The prosecution's attempt to equate a generalized report of a crime in a specific area with the specific, predictive tip in *Alabama v. White* fundamentally misapplies the totality-of-circumstances analysis.

### B. The Vehicle's Presence Did Not Corroborate the Tip

The prosecution argues that Officer Davis's observation of a vehicle on the unfinished freeway corroborated the tip. This argument is unavailing. The mere presence of a vehicle in the general area where a crime was reported does not establish reasonable suspicion. As this Court correctly held, "the corroboration of the mere location of a crime does not establish reasonable suspicion to stop any vehicle found in that location."

In *Alabama v. White*, the Court emphasized that corroboration must relate to the **predictive information** in the tip, not merely the general allegations. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. at 331 ("Corroboration of the bare statement that 'White' would be leaving the apartment... does not suggest that the informant had special knowledge of White's illegal activity."). Here, there was no predictive information to corroborate. The officer simply found a car in the general area—a circumstance consistent with countless innocent explanations.

The prosecution cites *State v. Henley*, 61 Haw. 531, 537, 606 P.2d 813, 817 (1980), for the proposition that "the presence of a vehicle in a location where criminal activity has just occurred is a relevant factor." However, *Henley* does not stand for the proposition that such presence, alone, creates reasonable suspicion. In *Henley*, there was additional evidence connecting the vehicle to the crime—specifically, the defendant matching a description. Here, no such description existed.

### C. The Vehicle's Conduct Was Innocent

The prosecution notes that the vehicle was on an "unfinished freeway" and suggests this is inherently suspicious. However, the facts explicitly state that the vehicle was "traveling at a reasonable speed" with "headlights on." This conduct is entirely consistent with innocent activity. A driver may have numerous legitimate reasons for being on an unfinished roadway, including unfamiliarity with the area, navigational error, or curiosity. The prosecution's attempt to transform innocent conduct into suspicious conduct ignores the fundamental requirement of individualized suspicion.

As this Court correctly held in its ruling, "the vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on, conduct consistent with innocent activity, even on an unfinished roadway." The Fourth Amendment requires more than bare speculation; it requires specific, articulable facts. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

---

## II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN WAS AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL SEARCH

The prosecution argues that the flashlight scan was lawful under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983). This argument fails because it ignores the foundational requirement that the officer must be **lawfully present** before the plain view doctrine applies.

### A. The Plain View Doctrine Requires Lawful Presence

The Supreme Court in *Texas v. Brown* explicitly held that "the 'plain view' doctrine and the holding of *Texas v. Brown* presuppose that the officer is lawfully present in the place from which the observation is made." *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. at 739-40. The prosecution attempts to sidestep this requirement by arguing that the flashlight scan was permissible because it "merely enhanced" the officer's ability to observe. However, this argument assumes the validity of the initial stop.

Since the stop was unlawful—as demonstrated above—Officer Davis was not lawfully present at the vehicle. An unlawful stop taints all subsequent observations. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963). The prosecution cannot rely on *Texas v. Brown* to justify an observation made during an unlawful seizure.

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies *Texas v. Brown*

In *Texas v. Brown*, the stop was lawful—the officer had legitimately stopped the defendant's vehicle for a traffic violation. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. at 733. The flashlight scan was upheld because it occurred during a lawful traffic stop. Here, by contrast, the stop itself was unlawful. The prosecution's reliance on *Texas v. Brown* is therefore misplaced.

The prosecution's argument that the officers were "not rummaging through the car" is unavailing. The Fourth Amendment does not turn on the intensity of the intrusion. The officers scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight, moved the front seat to access the rear floor, and observed a black case. This was a search, regardless of whether it was exhaustive. *See Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 330-31 (2009) (holding that a search occurs when an officer looks into a vehicle's occupant areas that would not otherwise be visible without some form of intrusion).

---

## III. PROBABLE CAUSE DID NOT EXIST FOR THE VEHICLE SEARCH

The prosecution argues that probable cause existed based on the discovery of the .22-caliber casing during the flashlight scan. This argument fails because the probable cause was derived from an unlawful search.

### A. The Probable Cause Was Tainted by the Unlawful Stop and Search

The prosecution's entire probable cause argument depends on evidence obtained during the unlawful flashlight scan. The .22-caliber casing was discovered as a result of an unconstitutional search. Under the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine, evidence derived from an unlawful search must be suppressed. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. at 484-85.

The prosecution cannot bootstrap probable cause from unlawfully obtained evidence. As this Court correctly held, "the probable cause is 'fruit of the poisonous tree.'" The officers had no independent information suggesting the vehicle contained a weapon prior to the stop. The guard's tip did not describe a vehicle. Therefore, any probable cause assertion is tainted by the initial constitutional violation.

### B. The Prosecution Misreads *Illinois v. Gates*

The prosecution cites *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), for the standard of probable cause. However, *Gates* does not help the prosecution here. Probable cause requires "facts and circumstances within the officer's knowledge." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238. Here, the only facts within the officer's knowledge prior to the unlawful search were: (1) a report of gunshots in the area, and (2) the presence of a vehicle traveling at a reasonable speed with headlights on. These facts are insufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that contraband or evidence of a crime would be found in the vehicle.

The prosecution's argument that the .22-caliber casing "confirmed the presence of a weapon" assumes the lawfulness of the search that discovered it. This is circular reasoning. The casing was discovered only because the officers conducted an unlawful search. It cannot serve as the basis for probable cause.

## IV. THE SEARCH OF THE UKULELE CASE EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF THE VEHICLE EXCEPTION

The prosecution argues that the search of the ukulele case was lawful under the vehicle exception. This argument fails because the vehicle exception requires independent probable cause, untainted by constitutional violations.

### A. The Vehicle Exception Requires Independent Probable Cause

The Supreme Court in *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565, 577 (1991), held that the vehicle exception applies when police have "probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime." However, as demonstrated above, the probable cause here was derived entirely from the unlawful stop and unlawful flashlight scan. The officers had no independent information suggesting the vehicle contained a weapon prior to these violations.

The prosecution cannot rely on the vehicle exception to justify a search when the probable cause for that search was obtained through unconstitutional means. *See Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. at 484-85.

### B. The Search Exceeded the Scope of Probable Cause

Even assuming arguendo that probable cause existed, the search of the ukulele case exceeded the scope of the vehicle exception. The prosecution argues that "a firearm could easily be concealed in a musical instrument case." However, the officers had no information suggesting a weapon would be found in a ukulele case specifically. The mere fact that a container could potentially conceal a weapon does not create probable cause to search it.

In *California v. Acevedo*, the Court held that police may search a container within a vehicle if there is probable cause to believe the container holds the object of the search. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. at 575-76. Here, there was no probable cause to believe the ukulele case contained a weapon. The officers had no description of the weapon, no information about how it was concealed, and no reason to believe it would be found in a musical instrument case.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), is misplaced. In *Ross*, the Court upheld the search of a vehicle and its contents because there was probable cause to believe the vehicle contained drugs. Here, by contrast, there was no probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime—let alone that such evidence would be found in a ukulele case.

The prosecution's arguments fundamentally misapply Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. The stop was not justified by reasonable suspicion because the guard's tip lacked the specificity and predictive information required under *Terry v. Ohio*, *Florida v. J.L.*, and *Alabama v. White*. The flashlight scan was not a lawful plain view observation because it occurred during an unlawful seizure. The probable cause for the vehicle search was derived entirely from unlawful police conduct. And the search of the ukulele case exceeded the scope of any conceivable probable cause.

The circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress. The security guard's tip lacked the specificity and predictive detail necessary to provide reasonable suspicion for the stop. The stop was therefore unlawful, rendering the subsequent flashlight scan and the discovery of evidence unconstitutional violations of the Fourth Amendment. The fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine requires suppression of all evidence derived from these violations.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a trial court’s decision on a motion to suppress evidence, the appellate court must examine the facts presented to the trial court and determine whether the ruling was correct as a matter of law. *State v. Kealoha*, 79 Hawaii 109, 900 P.2d 130 (1995). The application of the Fourth Amendment to the facts is a question of law which is reviewed de novo. *Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690 (1996).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Stop of the Vehicle Was Unlawful**

The State contends that the stop was justified by the "totality of the circumstances," arguing that the security guard’s status as a known informant and the specific location of the reported crime provided reasonable suspicion. The Court remains unpersuaded.

The Fourth Amendment requires that a police officer have reasonable suspicion—specific, articulable facts suggesting criminal activity—before stopping a vehicle. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). Reasonable suspicion must be based on "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The State argues that the security guard was a "known, credible source" and that the tip was therefore reliable under *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990). While it is true that tips from known informants generally carry more weight than anonymous tips, *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325, 328 (1990), the tip here lacked the predictive detail necessary to link the Defendant’s vehicle to the reported crime. In *Alabama v. White*, the informant predicted the suspect’s future behavior, stating the suspect would leave a specific apartment at a specific time and travel in a specific car with cocaine. *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325, 327 (1990). The police corroborated these predictive details.

Here, the guard reported hearing gunshots and "felt" they were coming from the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway. The guard furnished no other information. Critically, the guard provided no description of a vehicle, no description of any suspects, and no information regarding the direction of travel or the number of individuals involved. The State argues that the vehicle’s presence on the unfinished freeway corroborated the tip. However, the corroboration of the mere location of a crime does not establish reasonable suspicion to stop any vehicle found in that location. The guard did not predict that a vehicle would be present; he merely reported that shots had been fired from that area. Finding a vehicle in that area after the fact does not link that specific vehicle to the crime. As the Supreme Court held in *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000), an anonymous tip indicating a person has a gun is insufficient to justify a stop without some indicia of reliability. While the guard here was not anonymous, the lack of any predictive information or description of the perpetrator renders the tip insufficient to distinguish the Defendant’s vehicle from any other vehicle that might have been in the area.

The State relies on *State v. Henley*, 61 Haw. 531, 537, 606 P.2d 813, 817 (1980), for the proposition that the presence of a vehicle at a crime scene is a relevant factor. However, *Henley* involved additional facts linking the defendant to the crime. Here, the vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on, conduct consistent with innocent activity, even on an unfinished roadway. Without a description connecting the vehicle to the reported gunshots, the stop was an impermissible seizure based on unparticularized suspicion. *See Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 91 (1979) (noting that the Fourth Amendment requires individualized suspicion).

Because the stop was unlawful, the subsequent detention and search of the vehicle are tainted.

**B. The Flashlight Scan Constituted an Unlawful Search**

The State argues that the flashlight scan was lawful under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), because the officer was lawfully present. However, as established above, the officer was not lawfully present at the vehicle because the stop itself violated the Fourth Amendment.

The "plain view" doctrine and the holding of *Texas v. Brown* presuppose that the officer is lawfully present in the place from which the observation is made. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 739-40 (1983). Since the initial stop was unlawful, the officers had no right to be at the vehicle scanning its interior. Consequently, the discovery of the .22-caliber casing and the black case was the result of an unconstitutional search.

**C. The Search of the Ukulele Case Violated the Fourth Amendment**

The State argues that the vehicle exception to the warrant requirement justified the search of the ukulele case. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991). However, the vehicle exception only applies if the police have probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime. *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565, 577 (1991).

The probable cause to search the vehicle was derived entirely from the unlawful stop and the subsequent unlawful flashlight scan. The officers had no independent information suggesting the vehicle contained a weapon prior to the stop. The guard’s tip did not describe a vehicle. Therefore, the probable cause is "fruit of the poisonous tree." *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963). The evidence discovered during the flashlight scan (the casing and the black case) cannot be used to bootstrap probable cause for a full vehicle search.

Furthermore, even if probable cause had existed independently, the search of the ukulele case exceeded the scope of the vehicle exception under the facts known to the officers at the time. The officers had no information suggesting a weapon would be concealed in a musical instrument case. While *California v. Acevedo* allows the search of containers if there is probable cause to search the vehicle, the probable cause here was entirely dependent on the unlawfully obtained evidence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress. The security guard’s tip lacked the specificity and predictive detail necessary to provide reasonable suspicion for the stop. The stop was therefore unlawful, rendering the subsequent flashlight scan and the discovery of the evidence unconstitutional violations of the Fourth Amendment. The "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine requires suppression of all evidence derived from these violations.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the State of Hawaii’s appeal is hereby DENIED. The circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is AFFIRMED.
