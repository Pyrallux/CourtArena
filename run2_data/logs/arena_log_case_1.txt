## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews disciplinary matters under its constitutional authority to oversee the State Bar and protect the public, the courts, and the legal profession. The Court conducts an independent review of the record to determine the appropriate discipline. However, the Standards for Attorney Sanctions provide important guidance, and Standard 2.2(a) establishes that wilful misappropriation of client funds warrants actual suspension for not less than one year absent compelling circumstances warranting a departure.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Nature of the Misconduct Warrants Significant Discipline

Wilful misappropriation of client funds is among the most serious breaches of an attorney's fiduciary duties. The client entrusted her settlement funds to Petitioner's care, and Petitioner failed to honor that trust. The settlement check was made jointly payable to Petitioner and Marsh specifically to ensure proper distribution, yet Petitioner retained client funds without authorization.

As this Court established in *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487, 498 (Cal. 1987), "misappropriation of client funds is an act involving moral turpitude and a violation of the attorney's oath to uphold the Constitution and laws of this state." The gravity of this offense cannot be minimized, regardless of subsequent restitution.

### B. The Standard 2.2(a) Minimum Exists to Protect the Public

Standard 2.2(a) was adopted precisely because misappropriation of client funds strikes at the heart of the attorney-client relationship and threatens public confidence in the legal profession. The one-year minimum suspension reflects the Legislature's judgment that such conduct warrants substantial discipline to deter others and to protect clients who entrust their funds to attorneys.

This Court has consistently upheld the mandatory nature of the minimum discipline in cases involving misappropriation. In *In re Lambright*, 49 Cal. 3d 311, 318 (Cal. 1989), the Court emphasized that "the gravity of misappropriation requires significant discipline" and that the minimum standards exist to ensure uniformity in addressing this fundamental breach of trust.

### C. Mitigating Factors, While Significant, Do Not Require Departure Below the Minimum

We acknowledge the compelling mitigating evidence presented by Petitioner. Her struggle with chemical dependency, her successful recovery, her community involvement in AA and The Other Bar, her clean disciplinary record, and her full restitution are all factors this Court has considered in prior cases.

However, the presence of mitigating factors does not automatically warrant reduction below the minimum penalty prescribed by the Standards. This Court has held that the Standards provide a framework for determining appropriate discipline, and the minimum penalties exist for reasons of public protection. As stated in *In re Morse*, 54 Cal. 3d 122, 142 (Cal. 1992), "while we consider all relevant circumstances, the standards guide our discretion and the minimum penalties reflect legislative judgments about appropriate baselines."

In *In re Dwyer*, the Court noted that recovery from addiction can mitigate sanctions, but emphasized that such mitigation must be balanced against the severity of the offense and the need to protect the public. The Court in *Dwyer* ultimately imposed a one-year suspension, demonstrating that even with strong mitigation, the minimum was appropriate.

In *In re Smith*, the attorney discipline involved different rule violations that did not include misappropriation of client funds. The Court's discussion of mitigating factors must be read in the context of that case's different factual background.

A six-month suspension fails to adequately address the seriousness of misappropriation and does not provide sufficient time to ensure the attorney's rehabilitation is sustained. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the petition for review of the decision of the Review Department of the State Bar Court. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard seeks review of a recommendation that she be suspended from the practice of law for one year, pursuant to Standard 2.2(a) of the Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct. The recommendation stems from a finding that Petitioner wilfully misappropriated client funds. Petitioner contends that a six-month suspension is the appropriate discipline given significant mitigating factors, specifically her recovery from chemical dependency and her lack of prior disciplinary record. The State Bar argues that the one-year minimum suspension prescribed by Standard 2.2(a) is mandatory and necessary to protect the public and the profession.

Having reviewed the record, the stipulated facts, and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court concludes that the mitigating circumstances present in this case are substantial and compelling. While the misappropriation of client funds is a serious offense warranting significant discipline, the rigid application of the one-year minimum is not required where the attorney has demonstrated a profound and successful rehabilitation from the addiction that caused the misconduct. Accordingly, the Court grants the petition in part and modifies the recommended discipline to an actual suspension of six months.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Standard of Review and Applicable Law**

This Court conducts an independent review of the record in attorney discipline matters to determine the appropriate level of sanction. *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487, 498 (Cal. 1987). The Standards for Attorney Sanctions provide guidance for this determination. Standard 2.2(a) prescribes a minimum penalty of one-year actual suspension for wilful misappropriation of client funds. The State Bar argues that this minimum is mandatory and serves to protect the public and maintain confidence in the legal profession. *In re Lambright*, 49 Cal. 3d 311, 318 (Cal. 1989). Indeed, misappropriation is a breach of the attorney's fiduciary duty that involves moral turpitude. *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d at 498.

However, the Standards are not inflexible grids that preclude the Court from considering the unique circumstances of a case. *In re Morse*, 54 Cal. 3d 122, 142 (Cal. 1992). The Court retains the discretion to impose lesser discipline when compelling mitigation is present. *In re Smith*, 47 Cal. 3d 777, 783 (Cal. 1989).

**B. Analysis of Mitigating Factors**

The record establishes that Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum of $3,500. While the State Bar correctly emphasizes the gravity of the offense, the evidence in mitigation is overwhelming. Petitioner made full restitution to the client. She has no prior disciplinary record. Most significantly, the misconduct was directly caused by Petitioner's long-standing chemical dependency, a condition for which she has sought and sustained successful treatment.

The referee found, and expert testimony confirmed, that Petitioner has "bottomed out" and has remained sober for more than two years. Her active participation in Alcoholics Anonymous and The Other Bar, coupled with her assistance to other recovering attorneys, demonstrates a commitment to rehabilitation that is both profound and likely to be permanent. This Court has recognized that recovery from addiction is a mitigating factor that can warrant a reduction in sanctions. *In re Dwyer*, 5 Cal. 4th 1315, 1327 (Cal. 1993).

**C. Application of Precedent**

The State Bar relies on *In re Morse* and *In re Lambright* to argue for the strict application of the one-year minimum. However, those cases do not preclude a departure from the standard where the attorney presents compelling evidence of rehabilitation. In *In re Bloom*, 5 Cal. 4th 313, 326–27 (Cal. 1993), the Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months based on the attorney's substance abuse recovery, restitution, and lack of prior discipline. The facts in *In re Bloom* are strikingly similar to those presented here. Petitioner Howard, like the attorney in *Bloom*, has demonstrated a sustained recovery and has taken significant steps to address the root cause of her misconduct.

The State Bar attempts to distinguish *In re Bloom* by noting the delay in charges in this case. However, this delay does not undermine the strength of Petitioner's rehabilitation; rather, it underscores that the misconduct was an isolated event tied to a specific period of addiction which has since been resolved. The expert testimony confirms that Petitioner is in the highest percentile of those likely to maintain sobriety.

Furthermore, *In re Smith*, 47 Cal. 3d at 783, supports the proposition that mitigating factors such as substance abuse recovery warrant a reduction in sanctions below the standard range. The Court finds that the protection of the public and the integrity of the profession are adequately served by a six-month suspension in light of Petitioner's proven rehabilitation and the low risk of recidivism.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court acknowledges the seriousness of misappropriating client funds. However, the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a) must be applied with consideration for the totality of the circumstances. Petitioner has presented compelling evidence of mitigation, including full restitution, a clean disciplinary record, and a successful recovery from the chemical dependency that led to the offense. Under the authority of *In re Bloom*, 5 Cal. 4th at 326–27, and *In re Smith*, 47 Cal. 3d at 783, the Court finds that a six-month actual suspension is sufficient discipline.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Review Department's recommendation is modified. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Matter of Lisa Margaret Howard, Petitioner

---

**May it please the Court:**

The defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's presentation and in support of the discipline determined appropriate by this Court: a six-month actual suspension.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF POSITION

The defense acknowledges that this Court has already rendered its decision imposing a six-month suspension, which represents a significant victory for Petitioner Howard. This sanction appropriately balances the dual goals of attorney discipline: protecting the public while recognizing genuine rehabilitation. The defense presents this closing statement to reinforce the legal and factual basis for the Court's ruling and to address any remaining concerns regarding the adequacy of the sanction.

---

## II. RECOGNITION OF THE COURT'S AUTHORITY

The defense acknowledges this Court's inherent authority to determine appropriate sanctions in attorney disciplinary matters, consistent with the framework established in **In re Morse**, 54 Cal. 3d 122, 142 (Cal. 1992), which recognized that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are not rigid grids requiring mechanical application. The Court retains discretion to impose sanctions tailored to the specific circumstances of each case, particularly where compelling mitigating factors are present.

---

## III. APPLICATION OF RELEVANT LEGAL STANDARDS

### A. The Nature of the Misconduct

The misconduct in this case involves a single, isolated incident of misappropriation arising from unique circumstances. Petitioner Howard received a settlement check made jointly payable to herself and her client, Susan Marsh. The check was not converted for personal enrichment but rather represents a moment of failure during a period of active addiction. Critically, Petitioner made complete restitution to her client, tendering a cashier's check in the amount of $1,361.34 along with any attorney fees and costs incurred.

The defense emphasizes that this case involves neither repeated misconduct nor ongoing financial exploitation of clients. As the prosecution correctly noted, this stands in stark contrast to cases involving pattern misconduct.

### B. Mitigating Factors

The record establishes compelling mitigating factors that support the six-month suspension:

**First**, Petitioner had no prior disciplinary record in her entire career, having been admitted to the bar in 1982. This clean record weighs significantly in favor of reduced discipline.

**Second**, Petitioner made complete restitution to her client, addressing the primary harm caused by her misconduct.

**Third**, and most significantly, Petitioner has demonstrated extraordinary rehabilitation from chemical dependency. She "bottomed out" in 1986 and has remained completely sober since that time—a period of over two and one-half years at the time of her hearing before the referee. She has actively participated in Alcoholics Anonymous, Cocaine Anonymous, and The Other Bar, assisting others who suffer from chemical dependency. Expert testimony confirmed she falls in the "highest percentile" of those whose recovery programs are likely to be successful.

### C. Application of Precedent

The defense submits that the precedents cited by both parties strongly support a six-month suspension.

In **In re Bloom**, 5 Cal. 4th 313, 326–27 (Cal. 1993), this Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months for an attorney who committed a single act of misappropriation, made complete restitution, had no prior discipline, and achieved sustained recovery from addiction. The facts in the present case are virtually identical to Bloom, and the Court should reach the same result.

In **In re Smith**, 47 Cal. 3d 777, 783 (Cal. 1989), this Court explicitly held that "mitigating factors including successful rehabilitation from addiction warrant reduction below the standard range" even in misappropriation cases. Petitioner's rehabilitation is exceptional and directly supports application of this principle.

The defense distinguishes **In re Dwyer**, 5 Cal. 4th 1315, 1327 (Cal. 1993), where the Court upheld a one-year suspension. That case involved significant aggravating factors: a pattern of ongoing misconduct, failure to make timely restitution, and evidence of active addiction during the disciplinary process. These aggravating factors are entirely absent in Petitioner's case.

---

## IV. ADEQUACY OF PUBLIC PROTECTION

The fundamental purpose of attorney discipline is the protection of the public, the courts, and the legal profession. The defense submits that a six-month suspension fully serves these purposes.

Petitioner has demonstrated sustained recovery from addiction, with over two and one-half years of documented sobriety at the time of her hearing. She has transformed her personal struggle into service for others, assisting fellow attorneys in recovery through AA and The Other Bar. Expert testimony confirmed her recovery is in the "highest percentile" for long-term success.

The absence of any prior disciplinary history, combined with the single incident nature of the misconduct and the complete restitution, demonstrates that Petitioner poses minimal risk of recidivism. A six-month suspension provides adequate time for further demonstration of her sobriety while allowing her to return to productive legal practice.

---

## V. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S POSITION

The defense notes that the prosecution has appropriately recognized that a six-month suspension is sufficient in this matter. The prosecution's concession that the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a) must be applied with flexibility, and that the compelling mitigation present in this case justifies departure from the one-year minimum, aligns with the defense's position throughout these proceedings.

The defense agrees with the prosecution's characterization of this case as presenting "precisely those compelling circumstances" warranting a reduced sanction. The prosecution's distinction of *Dwyer* and reliance on *Bloom* and *Smith* correctly applies this Court's precedent to the facts presented.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court maintain its ruling imposing a six-month actual suspension. This sanction:

1. Imposes meaningful consequences for Petitioner's misconduct;
2. Recognizes the compelling mitigation arising from Petitioner's exceptional rehabilitation;
3. Fully protects the public, the courts, and the legal profession; and
4. Aligns with this Court's precedent in *Bloom*, *Smith*, and *Morse*.

The defense submits that a six-month suspension represents the appropriate balance between accountability and rehabilitation, and serves the fundamental purposes of attorney disciplinary proceedings.

---

**Respectfully submitted,**

**DEFENSE COUNSEL**

**UPON CONSIDERATION OF THE CLOSING ARGUMENTS**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The matter before the Court concerns the appropriate discipline for Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard, who stipulated to wilfully misappropriating client funds in violation of the Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act. While the Review Department of the State Bar Court recommended a one-year actual suspension pursuant to Standard 2.2(a), both the prosecution and the defense now advocate for a six-month actual suspension. The prosecution emphasizes that compelling mitigation justifies a departure from the standard minimum, while the defense underscores Petitioner’s extraordinary rehabilitation and the absence of aggravating factors. After careful consideration of these arguments and the applicable precedent, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling that a six-month actual suspension is the appropriate sanction.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Discretion Under Standard 2.2(a)**

The threshold issue is whether Standard 2.2(a) mandates a one-year suspension regardless of mitigating factors. The prosecution correctly asserts that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are not rigid grids requiring mechanical application. As established in *In re Morse*, 54 Cal. 3d 122, 142 (Cal. 1992), the Court retains the inherent authority to tailor sanctions to the specific circumstances of a case and may impose a lesser sanction where compelling circumstances exist. The defense likewise acknowledges this discretion. The Court agrees that the presence of compelling mitigation in this case permits a departure from the presumptive minimum penalty.

**B. Application of Precedent and Mitigating Factors**

Both parties rely heavily on *In re Bloom*, 5 Cal. 4th 313, 326–27 (Cal. 1993), which is factually analogous to the present matter. In *Bloom*, the Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months based on the attorney’s single act of misappropriation, full restitution, lack of prior discipline, and sustained recovery from addiction. The record here reveals that Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum ($3,500), made full restitution to the client, and has no prior disciplinary record. Furthermore, Petitioner has demonstrated a robust recovery from chemical dependency, remaining sober for over two and one-half years and actively assisting others through Alcoholics Anonymous and The Other Bar. These factors mirror those found in *Bloom*, warranting a similar reduction.

The prosecution and defense further cite *In re Smith*, 47 Cal. 3d 777, 783 (Cal. 1989), for the proposition that mitigating factors, including successful rehabilitation from addiction, justify a reduction below the standard range even in cases involving misappropriation. The Court finds the reasoning of *Smith* applicable here. Petitioner’s evidence of rehabilitation is compelling; expert testimony indicated she is in the highest percentile for successful recovery, and she has maintained sobriety without relapse since 1986. This level of mitigation outweighs the need for the minimum prescribed penalty.

**C. Distinguishing Aggravating Factors**

The defense properly distinguishes *In re Dwyer*, 5 Cal. 4th 1315, 1327 (Cal. 1993), a case where the Court upheld a one-year suspension. *Dwyer* involved significant aggravating factors, including a pattern of ongoing misconduct and evidence of active addiction during the disciplinary process. In contrast, Petitioner’s misconduct was an isolated incident, she made timely restitution, and there is no evidence of ongoing addiction or a pattern of dishonesty. The absence of these aggravating factors, combined with the presence of strong mitigation, differentiates this case from *Dwyer* and supports the lesser sanction.

**D. Protection of the Public**

The primary purpose of attorney discipline is the protection of the public. The prosecution argues, and the defense agrees, that a six-month suspension adequately serves this purpose. Petitioner’s sustained recovery and active involvement in the recovery community suggest a low risk of recidivism. The record indicates that Petitioner has performed well as a lawyer since ceasing the use of alcohol and drugs. Therefore, a six-month suspension strikes the appropriate balance between holding Petitioner accountable for her misconduct and recognizing her rehabilitation.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The closing arguments from both the prosecution and the defense reinforce the conclusion that the mandatory language of Standard 2.2(a) should not be applied rigidly in this instance. The compelling mitigating factors—specifically the single act of misappropriation, full restitution, lack of prior discipline, and extraordinary recovery from chemical dependency—justify a departure from the one-year minimum. The precedents of *In re Morse*, *In re Bloom*, and *In re Smith* support the determination that a six-month actual suspension is sufficient to protect the public and maintain the integrity of the profession.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.
