## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establishing the procedural posture are essential to this Court's analysis.

Stacy Juetten underwent LASIK surgery at LasikPlus Vision Center in July 2005. Juetten served no expert affidavits within 180 days of filing her initial complaint. ## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. The Statutory Framework Mandates Dismissal

Minnesota Statutes § 145.682 establishes a strict, non-negotiable procedural requirement in medical malpractice cases. Subdivision 2(2) requires that a plaintiff serve on each defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony. Subdivision 4 imposes the same requirement. Subdivision 6(b) mandates dismissal with prejudice of any cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case when the plaintiff fails to comply.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently interpreted this statute to impose a bright-line deadline that runs from the commencement of the action. In *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, 708 N.W.2d 534 (Minn. 2006), the Court explicitly held that "the statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action," and that "adding a defendant after that deadline does not reset or extend the time for service of affidavits." **Id. at 539.** This ruling controls the instant case.

Here, Juetten commenced her action in July 2005. The 180-day deadline expired in January 2006. Juetten did not add Dr. Whiting as a defendant until after the district court had already dismissed LCA-Vision—a dismissal that occurred in late 2008 or early 2009. **FACTS, at 775.** By any measure, the amendment adding Dr. Whiting came years after the statutory deadline had expired.

### II. The Amendment Adding Dr. Whiting Did Not Reset the Statutory Clock

The plaintiff attempts to argue that because Dr. Whiting was not a named defendant when the action commenced, the 180-day deadline should run from the date of the amendment. This argument directly contradicts *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, which rejected precisely this reasoning.

In *Johnson*, the Minnesota Supreme Court considered whether a plaintiff could serve expert affidavits on a newly added defendant after the 180-day period had expired from the original filing. The Court held that "the plain language of the statute" required that "the 180-day period begins when the plaintiff commences the action, not when the plaintiff adds a new defendant." **708 N.W.2d at 538.** The Court emphasized that the statute's purpose—ensuring that medical malpractice claims are investigated promptly and supported by competent expert evidence—would be undermined by allowing plaintiffs to extend the deadline simply by naming defendants sequentially. **Id.**

The same reasoning applies here. Juetten knew from the outset that Dr. Whiting performed her surgery and that his conduct was the basis of her claim. Her strategic decision to sue only LCA-Vision initially, and to add Dr. Whiting only after the deadline had passed, cannot override the statute's mandatory requirements. As the Court stated in *Kopp v. Kopp*, 704 N.W.2d 854 (Minn. 2005), "the plain language of the statute requires dismissal for noncompliance, and courts lack authority to create exceptions." **Id. at 858.**

### III. Juetten Cannot Establish a Prima Facie Case Without Timely Expert Affidavits

Medical malpractice claims require expert testimony to establish the standard of care, a deviation from that standard, and causation. **See, e.g., **Wille v. FH Healthcare, 698 N.W.2d 543, 548 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005)** (holding that expert testimony is generally required to establish breach of standard of care in medical malpractice cases).**

The plaintiff's opening argument correctly notes that Juetten's claims against Dr. Whiting involve "complex medical judgments about whether Dr. Whiting adequately assessed Juetten's pre-existing conditions and whether his surgical conduct deviated from the standard of care." These allegations indisputably require expert testimony to establish a prima facie case. Because Juetten failed to serve the required expert affidavits within the statutory deadline, she cannot sustain her burden of proof.

The district court acknowledged that Juetten's expert affidavits, though untimely, would have satisfied the statute's substantive requirements. **FACTS, at 775.** This observation, however, does not alter the analysis. The statute imposes both procedural and substantive requirements, and the procedural requirement—the 180-day deadline—is mandatory and non-negotiable. An affidavit that is substantively adequate but untimely does not comply with the statute. **See **Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b).**

### IV. The Statute Admits No Equitable Exceptions

The plaintiff may argue that the circumstances of this case warrant equitable relief from the statutory deadline. In *Kopp v. Kopp*, the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly rejected such arguments. The Court held that "the deadline is not subject to equitable exceptions or judicial discretion." **704 N.W.2d at 858.** The Court explained that the legislature enacted the statute to ensure prompt investigation and resolution of medical malpractice claims, and that allowing courts to create equitable exceptions would undermine this purpose. **Id.**

Juetten's situation does not present extraordinary circumstances warranting an exception. She knew of Dr. Whiting's involvement from the outset. Her delay in naming him as a defendant appears to have been a strategic choice—perhaps to avoid the procedural requirements of the statute. Whatever the reason, the statute does not permit courts to excuse noncompliance based on considerations of fairness or equity.

### V. Alternative Grounds for Affirmance

Although the district court granted summary judgment based on "law of the case," Respondent submits that this Court may affirm on any ground supported by the record and applicable law. **See **State v. Varner*, 643 N.W.2d 302, 312 (Minn. 2002)** (appellate court may affirm on any ground supported by the record).**

The statutory framework provides a clear and sufficient basis for affirmance. Juetten failed to serve expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting within 180 days of commencing her action. The statute mandates dismissal. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). This Court should affirm on this basis.

Dr. Whiting also raised alternative arguments below regarding res judicata and collateral estoppel. **FACTS, at 775.** Although the district court did not reach these arguments, Respondent preserves them for this Court's consideration in the event of remand or further review.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting. The appellant, Stacy Juetten, appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claim, which was predicated on the alleged failure to serve expert affidavits within the time frame mandated by Minnesota Statutes § 145.682. The Court has reviewed the facts provided, the opening arguments presented by the parties, and the applicable legal precedent.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether the district court erred in granting summary judgment to Dr. Whiting based on Juetten’s failure to serve expert affidavits within the 180-day statutory period. The resolution of this issue hinges on the interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 and its application to the procedural timeline of this case.

**A. The Statutory Framework and Mandatory Deadline**

Minnesota Statutes § 145.682, subds. 2(2) and 4, impose a strict procedural requirement on plaintiffs in medical malpractice actions: they must serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony within 180 days after the commencement of the suit. The consequences for failing to adhere to this requirement are severe and unequivocal. Subdivision 6(b) mandates the dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case."

The arguments presented to the Court correctly identify that this statutory deadline is not a mere suggestion but a mandatory directive. In *Kopp v. Kopp*, 704 N.W.2d 854 (Minn. 2005), the Minnesota Supreme Court emphasized that the plain language of the statute requires dismissal for noncompliance and that courts lack the authority to create equitable exceptions. *Id.* at 858. The Court in *Kopp* explicitly rejected the notion that the deadline is subject to judicial discretion or equitable tolling, stating that the legislature intended the requirement to be a bright-line rule to ensure the prompt investigation and resolution of medical malpractice claims. *Id.*

**B. Commencement of the Action and Relation Back**

The facts indicate that Juetten commenced her lawsuit against LCA-Vision in July 2005. Consequently, the 180-day clock for serving expert affidavits began ticking on that date. Juetten did not serve the required affidavits within this 180-day period. Instead, she sought to add Dr. Whiting as a defendant after the deadline had expired and served affidavits on him at that later date.

The critical question is whether the amendment adding Dr. Whiting reset the statutory clock. The Minnesota Supreme Court addressed this precise issue in *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, 708 N.W.2d 534 (Minn. 2006). The Court held that "the statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action," and that "adding a defendant after that deadline does not reset or extend the time for service of affidavits." *Id.* at 539. The reasoning in *Johnson* is grounded in the statute's purpose; allowing plaintiffs to circumvent the deadline by sequentially adding defendants would undermine the legislative goal of early screening of frivolous claims. *Id.*

Here, Juetten was aware of Dr. Whiting’s involvement and the factual basis of her claim against him from the inception of the litigation. Her decision to delay adding him as a defendant until after the 180-day period had expired cannot operate to revive a procedurally defaulted claim. As established in *Johnson*, the deadline relates back to the commencement of the action, not the date a specific defendant is served. *Id.*

**C. Necessity of Expert Testimony**

It is undisputed that Juetten’s claim against Dr. Whiting requires expert testimony to establish a prima facie case of negligence. The allegations involve complex medical assessments regarding pre-existing conditions, surgical standards of care, and causation. As noted in *Wille v. FH Healthcare*, 698 N.W.2d 543, 548 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005), expert testimony is generally required to establish a breach of the standard of care in medical malpractice cases. Because Juetten failed to serve the requisite affidavits within the 180-day window, she is statutorily barred from establishing the necessary elements of her claim against Dr. Whiting.

**D. District Court’s Ruling**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the "law of the case" doctrine. While the parties have debated whether this was the optimal rationale, the outcome is correct under the statute. The district court’s finding that the affidavits would have been substantively sufficient had they been timely does not salvage the claim. The procedural requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 are distinct from the substantive requirements; compliance with both is necessary. An affidavit that is substantively adequate but procedurally untimely fails to satisfy the statute. See Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b).

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the district court properly granted summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The mandatory 180-day deadline for serving expert affidavits established by Minn. Stat. § 145.682 ran from the commencement of the action in July 2005. Juetten’s failure to serve affidavits on Dr. Whiting within this period, coupled with the fact that adding him as a defendant did not reset the clock under *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, necessitates dismissal. The statute admits no equitable exceptions to excuse this noncompliance. *Kopp v. Kopp*.

Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is affirmed.

## II. RESPONSE TO APPELLANT'S STATUTORY ARGUMENTS

### A. The Mandatory Nature of Section 145.682

The appellant appears to argue that equitable considerations should excuse her failure to comply with the statutory deadline. This argument is foreclosed by the Minnesota Supreme Court's explicit holding in *Kopp v. Kopp*, 704 N.W.2d 854 (Minn. 2005). In *Kopp*, the Court unambiguously stated that "the plain language of the statute requires dismissal for noncompliance, and courts lack authority to create exceptions." *Id.* at 858. The appellant asks this Court to do precisely what *Kopp* prohibits—create an equitable exception to a mandatory statutory deadline. This Court should decline this invitation.

The purpose of Section 145.682 is to ensure the prompt investigation and resolution of medical malpractice claims. As the Court in *Kopp* recognized, the legislature enacted this requirement as a "bright-line rule" to serve this legislative purpose. *Id.* Allowing the appellant to circumvent this requirement based on claims of hardship or strategic necessity would undermine the very purpose the legislature sought to achieve.

### B. The Amendment Adding Dr. Whiting Did Not Reset the Statutory Clock

The appellant attempts to distinguish her situation by arguing that Dr. Whiting was added as a defendant after the 180-day period expired. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, as this Court correctly noted in its preliminary ruling, the Minnesota Supreme Court in *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, 708 N.W.2d 534 (Minn. 2006), explicitly held that "the statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action," and that "adding a defendant after that deadline does not reset or extend the time for service of affidavits." *Id.* at 539. The appellant commenced her action against LCA-Vision in July 2005. The 180-day period therefore expired in January 2006. The appellant did not serve expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting until February 2009—more than three years after the statutory deadline.

Second, the appellant was aware of Dr. Whiting's involvement in the surgery from the inception of the litigation. The factual basis for her claim against Dr. Whiting was identical to the claim she asserted against LCA-Vision. The appellant cannot now claim prejudice from a deadline she knowingly disregarded. The *Johnson* Court specifically cautioned that allowing plaintiffs to circumvent the deadline by sequentially adding defendants "would undermine the legislative goal of early screening of frivolous claims." *Id.* That is precisely what the appellant attempted here.

### C. One Cause of Action Under Section 145.682

The appellant further errs in suggesting that her claims against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting should be treated as separate causes of action for purposes of the statutory deadline. This argument is foreclosed by *Wille v. FH Healthcare*, 698 N.W.2d 543 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005). In *Wille*, this Court held that "a single medical procedure constitutes one cause of action for purposes of § 145.682, regardless of the number of defendants." *Id.* at 548.

The appellant underwent a single LASIK surgery performed by Dr. Whiting at the LasikPlus Vision Center. Both the vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision and the direct claim against Dr. Whiting arise from this identical medical treatment event. Under *Wille*, these claims constitute a single cause of action. The appellant therefore needed to serve expert affidavits addressing this cause of action within 180 days of commencing suit in July 2005. Her failure to do so mandates dismissal of the entire claim.

## III. RESPONSE TO APPELLANT'S CHALLENGE TO THE DISTRICT COURT'S RATIONALE

### A. The Law of the Case Doctrine Provides an Independent Basis for Affirmance

The appellant appears to argue that the district court erred in relying on the law of the case doctrine. This argument is unavailing for two reasons.

First, the district court's reliance on law of the case was entirely appropriate. The district court had already ruled on the procedural default issue when it granted LCA-Vision's motion to dismiss based on the failure to timely serve expert affidavits. That ruling became the law of the case when the appellant added Dr. Whiting as a defendant and attempted to relitigate the same procedural issues. The district court correctly refused to allow the appellant to circumvent its earlier ruling through the procedural mechanism of adding a new defendant.

Second, even if this Court were to find some deficiency in the district court's reliance on law of the case, the statutory mandate of Section 145.682 provides an independent and sufficient basis for affirmance. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *State v. Varner*, 643 N.W.2d 302, 312 (Minn. 2002), "an appellate court may affirm on any ground supported by the record." The statutory ground is fully supported here—the appellant failed to serve expert affidavits within the mandatory 180-day period, and the statute mandates dismissal.

### B. The District Court's Dicta Regarding Substantive Compliance Is Irrelevant

The appellant attempts to rely on the district court's statement that the expert affidavits "would have been substantively sufficient had they been timely served." This argument fails for the fundamental reason that procedural compliance and substantive compliance are distinct requirements under Section 145.682.

The statute contains two separate requirements: the procedural requirement that affidavits be served within 180 days, and the substantive requirement that the affidavits identify the expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony. Subdivision 6(b) mandates dismissal for failure to comply with the procedural requirement, without regard to the substantive adequacy of the untimely served affidavits. The district court's dicta regarding substantive sufficiency does not and cannot cure the procedural default.

This Court should decline the appellant's invitation to judicially rewrite the statute by graftng a substantive-sufficiency exception onto its mandatory procedural deadline. Such judicial legislation is precisely what *Kopp* prohibits.

Third, the appellant has not identified any circumstance that would justify equitable tolling of the statutory deadline. There is no basis for this Court to create an exception the Minnesota Supreme Court has explicitly prohibited.

## In the Court of Appeals of Minnesota

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The defense respectfully submits that the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting should be affirmed in all respects. For the reasons set forth below, and based upon the facts of record, the appellant Stacy Juetten's claims must be dismissed as a matter of law.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This medical malpractice appeal arises from LASIK eye surgery performed on the appellant in July 2005. Following surgery, the appellant developed complications including severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. Rather than proceeding to trial on the merits, however, this case has become fundamentally about procedural compliance—a matter that, under Minnesota law, is not subject to equitable modification or judicial discretion.

The defense acknowledges the seriousness of the appellant allegations and the genuine hardship she has experienced. However, the legislature of this State has enacted clear, mandatory requirements governing medical malpractice actions, and these requirements exist to ensure the prompt and efficient resolution of such claims. The appellant failed to comply with these mandatory requirements, and her claim must be dismissed accordingly.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF RELEVANT FACTS

The facts material to this appeal are largely undisputed and may be summarized as follows:

The appellant, Stacy Juetten, presented at LasikPlus Vision Center in July 2005 for a preoperative assessment. At that time, she had a medical history significant for diabetes and hyperactive thyroid, both of which she managed with medication. An examination revealed trace superficial punctuate keratitis, the lowest detectable level of this condition. Following examination, the examining physician concluded the appellant was a suitable candidate for surgery.

The surgery was performed by Dr. David Whiting in July 2005. Within days post-surgery, the appellant developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. She subsequently initiated this action against LCA-Vision, the owner of the LasikPlus Vision Center, based on vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of examining physicians and Dr. Whiting.

Critically, the appellant commenced her action against LCA-Vision in July 2005. The statutory deadline for serving expert affidavits expired in January 2006—180 days from the commencement of the action. The appellant did not serve the required expert affidavits within this period. Instead, she waited until February 2009—more than three years after the statutory deadline—to serve expert affidavits, and this service occurred only after adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Mandatory Statutory Framework

Minnesota Statutes Section 145.682, Subdivision 6(b), provides with unmistakable clarity that failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days after commencement of a medical malpractice suit results in mandatory dismissal with prejudice of each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.

This Court addressed the mandatory nature of this requirement in *Kopp v. Kopp*, 704 N.W.2d 854 (Minn. 2005), holding that "the plain language of the statute requires dismissal for noncompliance, and courts lack authority to create exceptions." *Id.* at 858. The defense respectfully submits that this Court is bound by the legislature's clear intent and the precedent established in *Kopp*.

The appellant urges this Court to consider equitable exceptions to the mandatory deadline. However, *Kopp* expressly forecloses this argument. As the Minnesota Supreme Court stated, "courts lack authority to create exceptions" to the statutory mandate. *Kopp*, 704 N.W.2d at 858. The defense submits that this Court cannot do what the Supreme Court has declared to be beyond the power of any court in this State.

### B. The Statutory Clock Is Not Reset by Adding Defendants

The defense further submits that the district court correctly determined that the 180-day deadline runs from the commencement of the action and is not reset by the subsequent addition of defendants.

In *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, 708 N.W.2d 534 (Minn. 2006), the Minnesota Supreme Court explicitly held that "the statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action," and that "adding a defendant after that deadline does not reset or extend the time for service of affidavits." *Id.* at 539. The Court in *Johnson* specifically warned that "allowing plaintiffs to evade deadlines by sequentially adding defendants would undermine the legislative goal of early screening of frivolous claims." *Id.*

The appellant commenced her action against LCA-Vision in July 2005. She was aware at that time that Dr. Whiting performed the surgery and that his conduct formed the basis of her claim. Her strategic decision to initially sue only the facility and later add the physician does not excuse her failure to comply with the mandatory statutory deadline. The reasoning of *Johnson* applies directly here: the appellant cannot circumvent the legislative purpose of early screening by the procedural maneuver of adding defendants after the deadline has expired.

### C. Single Cause of Action

Additionally, the defense contends that the claims against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting constitute a single cause of action arising from the same medical procedure.

In *Wille v. FH Healthcare*, 698 N.W.2d 543 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005), this Court held that "a single medical procedure constitutes one cause of action for purposes of § 145.682, regardless of the number of defendants." *Id.* at 548. The LASIK surgery performed by Dr. Whiting is the singular event giving rise to liability. Whether framed as vicarious liability against LCA-Vision or direct negligence against Dr. Whiting, the claim arises from the same operative facts—the July 2005 surgery.

Because the cause of action is singular, the statutory deadline applies uniformly. The appellant having failed to serve timely expert affidavits as to the claim against LCA-Vision, she likewise failed as to the claim against Dr. Whiting. The dismissal of the claim against LCA-Vision based on procedural default necessarily requires dismissal of the claim against Dr. Whiting.

### D. Alternative Grounds for Affirmance

Finally, even if this Court were to find any deficiency in the district court's reliance upon the law of the case doctrine—an issue the defense does not concede—the judgment may be affirmed on the independent ground of statutory noncompliance.

In *State v. Varner*, 643 N.W.2dd 302 (Minn. 2002), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that "an appellate court may affirm on any ground supported by the record." *Id.* at 312. The record here demonstrates that the appellant failed to serve the required expert affidavits within the 180-day period prescribed by Minn. Stat. § 145.682. This statutory violation provides a sufficient and independent basis for affirming the district court's grant of summary judgment.

The appellant argues that her untimely affidavits would have satisfied the substantive requirements of the statute. However, the mandatory penalty of subdivision 6(b) is triggered by procedural noncompliance—the failure to meet the deadline—not by the substantive adequacy of the affidavits eventually served. As *Kopp* makes clear, the statute's penalty is mandatory upon failure to comply with the deadline, irrespective of the quality of untimely submissions.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL OF APPELLANT'S CLAIMS

The appellant raises several arguments that the defense respectfully submits must be rejected.

First, the appellant argues that adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant reset the statutory clock. As discussed above, *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic* explicitly forecloses this argument. The statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action and is not extended by the addition of new defendants.

Second, the appellant argues that her claims against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting constitute separate causes of action. This argument contradicts the holding of *Wille v. FH Healthcare*, which makes clear that a single medical procedure constitutes one cause of action regardless of the number of defendants.

Third, the appellant appears to argue that she should receive equitable relief from the mandatory deadline. However, *Kopp v. Kopp* unambiguously holds that courts lack authority to create exceptions to the statutory requirement. This argument must be rejected.

Fourth, the appellant suggests that the district court erred in its reliance on the law of the case doctrine. Even accepting this premise arguendo, the judgment may be affirmed on the independent statutory ground, as discussed above. The appellant cannot escape the mandatory consequences of her procedural failure.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully submits that the district court's grant of summary judgment should be affirmed. The appellant failed to comply with the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. She commenced her action in July 2005, and the 180-day deadline expired in January 2006. She did not serve expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting until February 2009—more than three years after the statutory deadline.

The legislature has established a clear, mandatory rule to ensure the prompt investigation and resolution of medical malpractice claims. The appellant failed to comply with this rule, and the consequences of that failure are prescribed by statute. No equitable exception exists, and this Court lacks the authority to create one.

For all the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of Dr. David Whiting.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting. The appeal challenges the application of procedural rules and statutory requirements regarding the service of expert affidavits in a medical malpractice action. **II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether Juetten’s failure to serve expert affidavits within the 180-day statutory period mandates the dismissal of her claim against Dr. Whiting, despite the fact that he was added as a defendant after the initial deadline. Based on the arguments presented and controlling precedent, the Court finds that the district court’s judgment must be affirmed.

**A. The Mandatory Nature of the Statutory Deadline**

The arguments presented correctly emphasize the mandatory nature of the expert affidavit requirement under Minnesota law. Minnesota Statutes § 145.682, subd. 6(b), mandates dismissal with prejudice when a plaintiff fails to serve expert affidavits within 180 days of commencing a medical malpractice action. The legislature established this as a bright-line rule to ensure the prompt investigation of medical malpractice claims.

As held by the Minnesota Supreme Court in *Kopp v. Kopp*, 704 N.W.2d 854 (Minn. 2005), "the plain language of the statute requires dismissal for noncompliance, and courts lack authority to create exceptions." *Id.* at 858. The arguments presented to the Court accurately reflect this holding, noting that the statute admits no equitable exceptions and that courts are without power to circumvent the deadline through judicial leniency. Juetten’s failure to comply with this statutory mandate triggers the penalty of dismissal.

**B. The Deadline Runs from Commencement of the Action**

The arguments further establish that the 180-day clock for serving expert affidavits began when Juetten commenced her action against LCA-Vision in July 2005. Consequently, the deadline expired in January 2006. The subsequent amendment adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant did not reset this statutory clock.

In *Johnson v. Mayo Clinic*, 708 N.W.2d 534 (Minn. 2006), the Minnesota Supreme Court held that "the statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action," and that "adding a defendant after that deadline does not reset or extend the time for service of affidavits." *Id.* at 539. The arguments correctly apply this precedent to the facts at bar, demonstrating that allowing a plaintiff to evade the deadline by sequentially adding defendants would undermine the legislative purpose of early screening. Juetten was aware of Dr. Whiting’s involvement from the inception of the case, and her delay in naming him does not excuse noncompliance with the statute.

**C. Single Cause of Action**

The arguments presented also support the conclusion that Juetten’s claims against LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting arise from the same medical procedure and constitute a single cause of action. This position is bolstered by *Wille v. FH Healthcare*, 698 N.W.2d 543 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005), where the court held that "a single medical procedure constitutes one cause of action for purposes of § 145.682, regardless of the number of defendants." *Id.* at 548.

Because the LASIK surgery performed by Dr. Whiting is the singular event giving rise to the liability of both LCA-Vision and Dr. Whiting, the statutory deadline applies uniformly to the cause of action. The dismissal of the claim against LCA-Vision based on procedural default therefore necessitates the dismissal of the claim against Dr. Whiting. The arguments effectively synthesize this precedent to show that the procedural default taints the entire cause of action.

**D. Affirmance on Independent Grounds**

Finally, the arguments assert that even if the district court’s reliance on the "law of the case" doctrine were imperfect, the judgment may be affirmed on the independent ground of statutory noncompliance. Under *State v. Varner*, 643 N.W.2d 302 (Minn. 2002), an appellate court "may affirm on any ground supported by the record." *Id.* at 312.

The record clearly demonstrates that Juetten failed to serve the required expert affidavits within the 180-day period prescribed by Minn. Stat. § 145.682. This statutory violation provides a sufficient and independent basis for affirming the district court’s grant of summary judgment. The arguments presented correctly distinguish between the procedural failure to meet the deadline and the substantive sufficiency of the affidavits, noting that the penalty is triggered by the failure to meet the deadline, not by the quality of the untimely submissions.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court finds that the mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 were not satisfied. The statutory deadline is fixed at the commencement of the action and is not reset by the addition of new defendants. The claims against Dr. Whiting are part of a single cause of action originating from the July 2005 surgery. Consequently, the failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days of July 2005 mandates dismissal. The district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed on this independent statutory ground.
