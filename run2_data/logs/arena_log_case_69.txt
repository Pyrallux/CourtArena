## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Does Not Provide a Proximity Preference

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that courts must ascertain and give effect to the intent of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 640 N.W.2d 120, 124 (Minn. 2002). When statutory language is clear and unambiguous, courts must apply it as written without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Id.* Here, the text of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is unambiguous and contains no reference whatsoever to city residency or proximity-based preferences.

The statute specifically enumerates certain preferences for admission to charter schools. However, the enumerated preferences do not include any preference for residents of the city where the charter school is located. Where the legislature has included specific preferences in the statute, the expressio unius est exclusio alterius canon of construction applies—the mention of one thing implies the exclusion of another. *See Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 738 N.W.2d 400, 407 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007) (holding that the express enumeration of certain exclusions in a statute implies the exclusion of others). Had the legislature intended to include a proximity preference for city residents, it would have done so explicitly, as it did with other preferences such as siblings of currently enrolled students.

The appellant asks this Court to read into the statute a preference that the legislature did not include. This the Court cannot do. As our Supreme Court held in *State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d 903, 907 (Minn. 1996), "courts may not rewrite statutes to add provisions that the legislature did not include." To do so would violate fundamental principles of separation of powers and judicial restraint. The legislature is presumed to have intended everything contained in the statute and to have excluded everything omitted therefrom. *See Minn. Stat. § 645.16 (2023)* (providing rules for construction of statutes).

### B. The District Court Correctly Applied Summary Judgment Standards

Summary judgment was properly granted in this case because the material facts are undisputed and the district court was presented with a pure question of law. Under Minnesota law, summary judgment is appropriate when "the only issue is a question of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact." *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 758, 761 (Minn. 1993). Here, the facts are not in dispute: PACT Charter School operates a lottery system for admissions, and Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not impose a duty to prioritize city residents. The sole issue before the district court was the proper interpretation of the statute—a question of law entirely appropriate for summary judgment.

The appellant cannot demonstrate any genuine dispute of material fact. The record establishes that the school used a lottery system, that Look's son was actually selected for admission through that system, and that Dehen's daughter was not selected. ### C. The Appellant's Interpretation Would Lead to Absurd Results

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s determination that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court’s judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

On appeal from a summary judgment, this Court must determine whether the district court erred in its application of the law. Whether a statute extends a specific preference is a question of statutory interpretation, which this Court reviews de novo. *See Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 758, 761 (Minn. 1993). Summary judgment is appropriate when the only issues presented are questions of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9**

The primary issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, creates a "proximity preference" for residents of the city where the charter school is located. The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation is that courts must ascertain and give effect to the intent of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 640 N.W.2d 120, 124 (Minn. 2002). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must apply the statute as written without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Id.*

Here, the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, is unambiguous. The statute enumerates specific preferences for admission to charter schools, such as siblings of currently enrolled students, but it is silent regarding any geographic or proximity-based criteria related to city residency. Because the legislature specifically included certain preferences while omitting others, the canon of construction *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* applies. As stated in *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 738 N.W.2d 400, 407 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), the express enumeration of certain exclusions in a statute implies the exclusion of others. Had the legislature intended to include a proximity preference for city residents, it would have done so explicitly.

Furthermore, courts are prohibited from rewriting statutes to add provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d 903, 907 (Minn. 1996). Appellants ask this Court to read a residency preference into the statute where none exists. To do so would violate the separation of powers doctrine and the principle of judicial restraint. The legislature is presumed to have intended everything contained in the statute and to have excluded everything omitted therefrom.

The Respondent further argues that interpreting the statute to include a proximity preference would lead to absurd results, requiring charter schools to track the residency of every applicant and implement complex geographic priority systems without statutory guidance. *See State ex rel. Nixon v. Taylor*, 95 S.W.3d 164, 172 (Mo. 2003) (courts should avoid interpretations that produce absurd results). Additionally, the statute must be read as a whole in harmony with the overall statutory scheme. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 774 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 2009). The charter school statutory scheme authorizes lottery systems for admissions when demand exceeds capacity; imposing a residency preference would contradict this framework.

**B. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

The district court properly granted summary judgment because the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is one of law. Under Minnesota law, summary judgment is appropriate when "the only issue is a question of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact." *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 758, 761 (Minn. 1993). The facts establish that PACT Charter School utilizes a lottery system for admissions and that the statute in question does not impose a duty to prioritize city residents. Appellants do not dispute these facts; rather, they challenge the legal interpretation of the statute. As this is a pure question of law appropriate for summary judgment resolution, the district court did not err.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court correctly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not establish a preference for admission based on residency within the city where the charter school is located. The plain language of the statute, the principles of statutory construction, and the absence of any genuine dispute of material fact support the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**ORDER**

Based on the arguments submitted and the law cited therein, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.

On appeal from a summary judgment, this Court must determine whether the district court erred in its application of the law. Summary judgment is appropriate when the only issues presented are questions of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Does Not Include a Proximity Preference**

The primary issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, creates a "proximity preference" for residents of the city where the charter school is located. The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation is that courts must ascertain and give effect to the intent of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 640 N.W.2d 120, 124 (Minn. 2002). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must apply the statute as written without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Id.*

The Respondent correctly argues that the plain language of the statute is unambiguous. The statute enumerates specific preferences for admission to charter schools, such as siblings of currently enrolled students and children of the school's staff, but it is silent regarding any geographic or proximity-based criteria related to city residency. Because the legislature specifically included certain preferences while omitting others, the canon of construction *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* applies. As stated in *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 738 N.W.2d 400, 407 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), the express enumeration of certain exclusions in a statute implies the exclusion of others. Had the legislature intended to include a proximity preference for city residents, it would have done so explicitly.

**B. Judicial Restraint and Legislative Intent**

Courts are prohibited from rewriting statutes to add provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d 903, 907 (Minn. 1996). Appellants ask this Court to read a residency preference into the statute where none exists. To do so would violate the separation of powers doctrine and the principle of judicial restraint. The legislature is presumed to have intended everything contained in the statute and to have excluded everything omitted therefrom. *See Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2023). The Respondent’s argument that the legislature knew how to create residency preferences had it wished to do so is persuasive. The absence of such language in the charter school statute is a clear indication of legislative intent to exclude it.

Taylor*, 95 S.W.3d 164, 172 (Mo. 2003) (courts should avoid interpretations that produce absurd results). Furthermore, the statute must be read as a whole in harmony with the overall statutory scheme. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 774 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 2009). The charter school statutory scheme authorizes lottery systems for admissions when demand exceeds capacity. *See Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 7*. This provision presupposes a neutral selection process. Imposing a residency preference would contradict this framework and create an irreconcilable conflict with the lottery-based admissions system.

The district court properly granted summary judgment because the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is one of law. Under Minnesota law, summary judgment is appropriate when "the only issue is a question of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact." *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 758, 761 (Minn. 1993). Appellants do not dispute these facts; rather, they challenge the legal interpretation of the statute. As this is a pure question of law appropriate for summary judgment resolution, the district court did not err.

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a pure question of statutory interpretation. The question is straightforward: does the plain language of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, create a preference for admission to charter schools based on residency in the city where the school is located? The answer, as the district court correctly determined, is no.

The appellant asks this Court to do what the legislature did not: create a residency preference where none exists. This Court should decline this invitation. The statute's plain language, established principles of statutory construction, and the overall statutory scheme all confirm that no such preference was intended by the legislature.

---

## II. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF THE STATUTE IS CONTROLLING

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation requires courts to ascertain and give effect to the intent of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 640 N.W.2d 120, 124 (Minn. 2002). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, this Court must apply the statute as written without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Id.*

Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, enumerates specific preferences for admission to charter schools. The statute provides that charter schools "may give preference for admission to siblings of currently enrolled students and to children of the school's staff." That is the complete list. The statute is silent regarding any geographic or proximity-based criteria related to city residency.

This Court must give effect to the legislature's deliberate choice. By including specific preferences while omitting others, the legislature demonstrated its intent to limit admissions preferences to those expressly stated. The canon of construction *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* applies with full force here. As this Court held in *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 738 N.W.2d 400, 407 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), the express enumeration of certain exclusions in a statute implies the exclusion of others.

The legislature knew precisely how to create preferences—it did so explicitly for siblings and staff. Had the legislature intended to include a proximity preference for city residents, it would have done so explicitly. The absence of such language is a clear indication of legislative intent to exclude it.

---

## III. JUDICIAL RESTRAINT PRECLUDES JUDICIAL LEGISLATION

Courts are prohibited from rewriting statutes to add provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d 903, 907 (Minn. 1996). The appellant asks this Court to read a residency preference into the statute where none exists. To do so would violate the separation of powers doctrine and the principle of judicial restraint.

The legislature is presumed to have intended everything contained in the statute and to have excluded everything omitted therefrom. *See* Minn. Stat. § 645.16 (2023). This presumption is particularly strong where, as here, the legislature has expressly enumerated certain preferences while remaining silent on others.

The appellant argues that a proximity preference would be reasonable or desirable. However, policy arguments of this nature are properly addressed to the legislature, not the courts. This Court cannot substitute its policy judgment for that of the legislature. *See State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d at 907. The legislature has made its choice: charter schools may prioritize siblings and staff, but not residents. That choice must be respected.

---

## IV. THE STATUTORY SCHEME SUPPORTS THE INTERPRETATION

The appellant['s] interpretation would lead to absurd results and conflict with the overall statutory scheme. Charter schools in Minnesota operate under a lottery system for admissions when demand exceeds capacity, as expressly mandated by Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 7. This provision presupposes a neutral selection process.

Imposing a residency preference would force charter schools to track the residency of every applicant, resolve complex boundary disputes, and implement geographic prioritization systems—all without statutory guidance. This would create an administrative burden the legislature never intended.

As the Missouri Supreme Court recognized in *State ex rel. Nixon v. Taylor*, 95 S.W.3d 164, 172 (Mo. 2003), courts must avoid interpretations that produce absurd or unworkable results. Here, the appellant['s] theory would undermine the charter school system's core purpose of providing educational choice and innovation through a neutral, lottery-based admissions process.

Furthermore, the statute must be read as a whole in harmony with the overall statutory scheme. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 774 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 2009). Imposing a residency preference would create an irreconcilable conflict with the lottery-based admissions system established in subdivision 7 of the same statute.

The district court correctly resolved this pure question of law. This Court should do the same.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon review of the closing arguments submitted by the parties. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School, arguing that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, should be interpreted to extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, which emphasize the plain language of the statute, established canons of construction, and the statutory framework governing charter school admissions, the Court finds no basis to disturb the district court’s decision. We affirm.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of summary judgment is reviewed de novo to determine whether the district court erred in its application of the law. *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 758, 761 (Minn. 1993). The interpretation of a statute presents a question of law, which this Court reviews independently. *State v. Thiel*, 640 N.W.2d 120, 124 (Minn. 2002). Summary judgment is appropriate when the only issues presented are questions of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact. *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d at 761.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of the Statute Controls**

The primary objective of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *State v. Thiel*, 640 N.W.2d at 124. When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must apply the statute as written without resorting to extrinsic aids. *Id.*

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, explicitly states that charter schools "may give preference for admission to siblings of currently enrolled students and to children of the school's staff." The statute is silent regarding any geographic or proximity-based criteria related to city residency. The Respondent correctly argues that this silence is deliberate. The legislature’s specific enumeration of these two preferences, to the exclusion of others, triggers the canon of construction *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing is the exclusion of another. As noted in *Anderson v. Target Corp.*, 738 N.W.2d 400, 407 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007), the express enumeration of certain items in a statute implies the exclusion of those not mentioned. The legislature demonstrated that it knew how to create admissions preferences; its failure to include a preference for city residents indicates an intent not to do so.

**B. Judicial Restraint and the Prohibition on Judicial Lawmaking**

The arguments presented underscore the principle that courts are prohibited from rewriting statutes to add provisions that the legislature did not include. *State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d 903, 907 (Minn. 1996). Appellants essentially ask this Court to judicially invent a residency preference where none exists. Such an action would violate the separation of powers doctrine. The legislature is presumed to have intended everything contained in the statute and to have excluded everything omitted therefrom. *See Minn. Stat. § 645.16* (2023). The policy arguments advanced by Appellants—that a proximity preference would be "reasonable" or "desirable"—are properly addressed to the legislature, not the judiciary. This Court cannot substitute its policy judgment for that of the legislature. *State v. Jones*, 555 N.W.2d at 907.

**C. Statutory Harmony and Avoidance of Absurd Results**

The Court must also consider the interpretation of the statute within the context of the overall statutory scheme. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 774 N.W.2d 756, 759 (Minn. 2009). The charter school statutory scheme includes a mandate for a lottery system when applications exceed capacity. *See Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 7*. This lottery system presupposes a neutral selection process. Imposing a residency preference, as argued by the Respondent, would create an irreconcilable conflict with this neutral lottery framework.

Furthermore, interpreting the statute to include a proximity preference would lead to absurd or unworkable results. *State ex rel. Nixon v. Taylor*, 95 S.W.3d 164, 172 (Mo. 2003). Without statutory guidance, charter schools would be forced to track the residency of every applicant, resolve complex boundary disputes, and implement ad hoc geographic prioritization systems. The administrative burden created by such a requirement suggests that the legislature did not intend for such a preference to exist.

**D. Appropriateness of Summary Judgment**

Finally, the district court properly granted summary judgment because the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is one of law. The parties agree that PACT Charter School utilizes a lottery system for admissions and that the statute contains no language creating a residency preference. As established in *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d at 761, summary judgment is appropriate when "the only issue is a question of law and there are no genuine issues of material fact." The district court correctly resolved this pure question of law.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the principles of statutory construction, the necessity of judicial restraint, and the harmony of the statute with the overall admissions scheme, the district court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. The statute does not create a proximity preference for residents of the city where the charter school is located.
