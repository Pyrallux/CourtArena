**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case requires the Court to determine whether A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), which establishes a presumption of paternity for a "man" married to the mother, may be applied to a female spouse in a same-sex marriage. The Petitioner, Turner, argues that the plain language of the statute restricts the presumption to men. The Respondent, Oakley, argues that the Court is bound by the precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (Ct. App. 2016), which held the statute must be applied gender-neutrally, and that equitable estoppel bars Turner from rebutting the presumption.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Family Court Was Bound by the Precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones***

The prosecution argues that the family court erred by applying A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to Oakley because the statute explicitly uses the terms "man" and "father." The prosecution relies on *State v. Superior Court*, 138 Ariz. 212, 673 P.2d 926 (1983), and *In re Adoption of Baby Boy B.*, 202 Ariz. 529, 47 P.3d 1081 (Ct. App. 2002), for the proposition that courts must apply gender-specific statutes literally. While these authorities correctly state the general principle of statutory construction, they cannot override the binding precedent of a prior panel of this Court.

The defense correctly cites *State v. Mitchell*, 180 Ariz. 568, 886 P.2d 262 (Ct. App. 1994), for the well-established principle that "one panel of this Court may not overrule another; only the Court en banc may do so." 180 Ariz. at 572, 886 P.2d at 266. In *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (Ct. App. 2016), a panel of this Court explicitly held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The prosecution asks this Court to disregard *McLaughlin* and revert to a strict textual interpretation. Under the mandate of *State v. Mitchell*, this Court lacks the authority to overrule *McLaughlin*. Therefore, the family court acted within its discretion and followed binding authority when it granted Oakley’s motion for reconsideration and applied the presumption of parentage gender-neutrally.

**B. The Doctrine of Equitable Estoppel Supports the Family Court’s Ruling**

The family court found that Turner represented to Oakley that they would raise C.T. together as co-parents. Oakley reasonably relied on these representations by participating in the artificial insemination process, attending medical appointments, and being present at the birth. Furthermore, Oakley has relied to her detriment by forming a parental bond with the child. As held in *McLaughlin*, it is inequitable to allow a party to deny a parental relationship they helped create. 240 Ariz. at 567 ¶ 21, 382 P.3d at 124. The prosecution’s argument that the birth certificate is merely an administrative document does not negate the weight of the other evidence demonstrating the parties' mutual intent to parent jointly.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution’s request for this Court to reverse the family court based on a strict textual reading of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) is denied because this Court is bound by the precedent of *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (Ct. App. 2016). Furthermore, the record supports the family court’s alternative finding that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting the presumption of parentage. The family court’s ruling granting Oakley presumed parent status is affirmed.

The prosecution asks this Court to take judicial notice of an alleged vacatur without providing the necessary record. ## II. CONSTITUTIONAL PRINCIPLES REQUIRE A GENDER-NEUTRAL READING OF A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)

The prosecution argues that constitutional principles do not require rewriting a gender-specific statute. The defense submits that this argument misapprehends the nature of constitutional interpretation and ignores the fundamental principle that statutes must be construed to avoid constitutional infirmities.

In *State v. Garcia*, 181 Ariz. 385, 390, 891 P.2d 934, 939 (1995), this Court held that "statutes must be construed, if possible, to avoid constitutional infirmities." This principle is fundamental to constitutional adjudication. When a statute can be read in a manner that avoids constitutional issues while giving effect to the legislature's intent, courts must adopt such a reading.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Superior Court*, 138 Ariz. 212, 215, 673 P.2d 926, 929 (1983), and *In re Adoption of Baby Boy B.*, 202 Ariz. 529, 533, 47 P.3d 1081, 1085 (Ct. App. 2002), is inapposite. Both cases were decided before the United States Supreme Court's landmark decision in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015). In *Obergefell*, the Supreme Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to treat same-sex couples equally in matters of marriage and its attendant benefits. 576 U.S. at 675. This constitutional mandate fundamentally changes the analysis.

To apply A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) only to opposite-sex spouses would effectively deny same-sex couples the legal protections of parentage afforded to different-sex couples. This result would violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The statute's purpose is to ensure that children born within a marriage have two legal parents. This purpose is equally served whether the spouses are opposite-sex or same-sex.

The prosecution argues that *Obergefell* did not authorize courts to rewrite statutes. However, *Obergefell* does not stand for the proposition that courts must blindly apply statutes that discriminate against same-sex couples. Rather, *Obergefell* establishes that the Constitution requires equal treatment. Reading the term "man" in A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to include "person" or "spouse" avoids the constitutional infirmity of discrimination while effectuating the statute's purpose. This is not judicial legislation; it is constitutional interpretation.

The prosecution's argument that the legislature could have extended the presumption to women if it intended to do so, as it did in A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(4), ignores the reality that the legislature has not amended A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to address same-sex marriage because *Obergefell* was decided only recently. It is the province of the courts, not the legislature, to interpret statutes in accordance with the Constitution. The defense respectfully submits that this Court should follow *Garcia* and adopt a gender-neutral reading that avoids constitutional infirmity.

## III. EQUITABLE ESTOPPEL INDEPENDENTLY BARS TURNER FROM DENYING OAKLEY'S PARENTAL RIGHTS

The prosecution argues that equitable estoppel cannot create statutory parentage where none exists. The defense submits that this argument misstates the nature of the family court's ruling and ignores the well-established doctrine of equitable estoppel in parentage proceedings.

The family court did not use equitable estoppel to "create" parentage. Rather, the family court found that Turner is equitably estopped from *rebutting* the presumption that Oakley is a legal parent. This distinction is crucial. The presumption under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) arises from the marriage itself. Turner's representations about Oakley's role as co-parent cannot be used to defeat this presumption after the fact.

The elements of equitable estoppel in Arizona are: (1) the party to be estopped must know the facts; (2) they must intend that their conduct be acted upon or act in a way that creates reasonable reliance; (3) the party asserting estoppel must be ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the party asserting estoppel must have relied on the conduct to their detriment. *Taylor v. State*, 194 Ariz. 113, 117, 978 P.2d 1221, 1225 (Ct. App. 1999).

The record fully supports the family court's finding that all elements are met. Turner knew the facts: she knew that Oakley was not the biological mother and had not formally adopted the child. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as the child's sole custodian, listed Oakley on the birth certificate, and publicly referred to Oakley as the child's mother on social media. These representations were intended to be acted upon and did create reasonable reliance.

Oakley relied on Turner's representations to her detriment. Oakley participated in the artificial insemination process, was present at the birth, cut the umbilical cord, and formed a parental bond with the child. Oakley reasonably believed that she was C.T.'s legal parent and made decisions based on that belief. To allow Turner now to deny Oakley's parental rights would be inequitable.

The prosecution argues that Turner did not misrepresent Oakley's legal status because she accurately stated Oakley was "not the biological or adoptive parent." This argument misses the point. Turner's representations were about her *intention* to raise the child with Oakley as co-parents, not merely about Oakley's biological or adoptive status. The fertility clinic document itself states that the child would be the "legal child of the recipient and recipient's husband or partner if applicable." This document contemplates that a "partner" may have parental rights. Turner's conduct in listing Oakley on the birth certificate, in her will, and on social media reinforced this understanding.

The prosecution's reliance on *In re Adoption of Baby Boy B.* is misplaced. In that case, the Court held that equitable estoppel does not permit a court to create a parent-child relationship where none exists under statutory law. 202 Ariz. at 534, 47 P.3d at 1086. However, the family court's ruling does not create a parent-child relationship where none exists. Rather, the family court found that Turner is estopped from denying the parental relationship that already exists under the statutory presumption. This is a critical distinction.

As this Court noted in *McLaughlin*, "it is inequitable to allow a party to deny a parental relationship they helped create." 240 Ariz. at 567 ¶ 21, 382 P.3d at 124. The facts of this case are even more compelling than *McLaughlin*. Here, Turner and Oakley were married when C.T. was born. Turner expressly represented that Oakley would be C.T.'s co-parent. Oakley relied on these representations and formed a parental bond with the child. The doctrine of equitable estoppel bars Turner from denying these facts now.
