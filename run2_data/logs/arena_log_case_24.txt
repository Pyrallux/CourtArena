# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits that the family court's ultimate ruling—that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting that presumption—should be affirmed. The plaintiff incorrectly argues that the statute's use of gendered language "man" and "father" unambiguously excludes women. However, this argument ignores the established principle that statutes must be interpreted in context and in harmony with the legislative purpose, particularly when application of a gender-specific interpretation would produce absurd and unjust results that the legislature could not have intended. Furthermore, the plaintiff cannot overcome the equitable estoppel findings, which are supported by substantial evidence in the record.

## I. THE STATUTE MUST BE INTERPRETED GENDER-NEUTRALLY AS APPLIED TO THIS CASE

The plaintiff urges this Court to apply A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) only to men based on the statute's use of the words "man" and "father." However, the plaintiff ignores the fundamental principle that Arizona courts must interpret statutes to give effect to legislative intent, and that intent must be determined by examining the statute's purpose, context, and the problem it was designed to address.

Arizona has long recognized that statutes should be interpreted to avoid absurd results. In *State v. Pima County*, 174 Ariz. 215, 848 P.2d 97 (App. 1993), the Arizona Court of Appeals held that "the primary goal of statutory construction is to give effect to the legislature's intent, and courts should interpret statutes to avoid absurd results." While the plaintiff cites *State v. Soto* for the proposition that clear language must be applied as written, this Court has equally recognized that "the spirit of the law controls the letter" when literal application would defeat the statute's purpose. *E.g.*, *Bilton v. Blade*, 156 Ariz. 35, 749 P.2d 432 (App. 1988).

The purpose of the presumption of parentage is to establish legal parent-child relationships for children born during marriage, ensuring that children have two legal parents who are responsible for their support and entitled to custody and parenting time. *See* A.R.S. § 25-814 (legislative findings). This purpose applies with equal force regardless of the gender of the spouse seeking parentage status. To hold that the statute applies only to male spouses would create a classification based solely on gender that denies married women the same presumption afforded to married men—precisely the type of gender-based discrimination that the Arizona Constitution and federal law prohibit.

The plaintiff relies heavily on *State v. Valdez*, but that case is distinguishable. In *Valdez*, the court was interpreting a criminal statute where gender-specific language described the specific offender. Here, the presumption statute addresses family relationships where the legislative purpose is clearly gender-neutral—to establish parentage for children born to married couples. The *Valdez* court itself acknowledged that context matters, stating that "the meaning of statutory language depends on the context in which it appears." 193 Ariz. at 270, 972 P.2d at 640.

## II. MCCLAUGHLY V. JONES CORRECTLY INTERPRETED THE STATUTE

The plaintiff asks this Court to "respectfully disagree with *McLaughlin*" and claim that decision "erred fundamentally." However, *McLaughlin v. Jones* represents the considered judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals on precisely this issue, and its reasoning is sound.

In *McLaughlin*, the Court held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The Court recognized that the statute's purpose—establishing parentage for children born during marriage—applies equally to same-sex and opposite-sex married couples following the United States Supreme Court's decision in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015). The *McLaughlin* court properly applied the principle that "the best indication of legislative intent is the language used in the statute, read in context," and concluded that the legislature could not have intended a gender-based discrimination that would deny married women the same parental rights as married men.

This Court should follow *McLaughlin*. That decision represents binding precedent from the Arizona Court of Appeals on the proper interpretation of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1). It correctly applied established principles of statutory construction to reach a result that is consistent with the statute's purpose and with constitutional guarantees of equal protection.

## III. EVEN IF THE STATUTE'S APPLICATION TO OAKLEY WERE QUESTIONABLE, EQUITABLE ESTOPPEL BARS TURNER'S CLAIM

The family court found that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting Oakley's presumed parentage. This finding is supported by substantial evidence and should be affirmed even if this Court harbors doubts about the statutory interpretation issue.

Equitable estoppel applies when: (1) the party to be estopped knows the true facts; (2) the party intends or expects the other party to rely on the representations; (3) the other party is ignorant of the true facts; and (4) the other party relies on the representations to his or her detriment. *See* *Chandler v. City of Phoenix*, 193 Ariz. 78, 82, 970 P.2d 928, 932 (App. 1998).

The record amply supports the family court's findings on each element. Turner knew that Oakley had no biological relationship to C.T. Yet Turner represented throughout her pregnancy and after C.T.'s birth that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as sole custodian if Turner died. Turner posted on social media calling Oakley C.T.'s mother. Turner listed Oakley on C.T.'s birth certificate in the "father" section, following the hospital nurse's instruction. Turner did not inform Oakley that formal adoption might be necessary to establish Oakley's legal rights.

Oakley relied on Turner's representations to her detriment. Oakley participated in the insemination process, was present at C.T.'s birth, cut the umbilical cord, and held herself out to the world as C.T.'s parent. Oakley did not formally adopt C.T. because Turner represented that being on the birth certificate was sufficient. Oakley formed a parent-child bond with C.T. based on Turner's assurances that they were co-parents.

The *McLaughlin* court recognized the applicability of equitable estoppel in precisely these circumstances. In *McLaughlin*, the court held that the biological mother was equitably estopped from disputing her wife's parental rights because the parties had agreed to raise the child together as co-parents, the wife was a beneficiary of the biological mother's will, and the wife had been a de facto parent for two years. 240 Ariz. at 565-67, 382 P.3d at 123-24. The facts here are even more compelling—Turner not only agreed to equal parenting rights but actively represented to Oakley, to medical providers, and to the world that Oakley was C.T.'s parent.

## IV. THE POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT AFFIRMANCE

The plaintiff warns that gender-neutral interpretation will lead to "rewriting" statutes "to conform to our own views of what the law should be." But the consequence of the plaintiff's position is far more troubling. Under the plaintiff's theory, a married woman who conceives a child through artificial insemination with her husband's or partner's consent could, upon dissolution, deny her spouse any parental rights—even if the spouse participated fully in the conception process, was present at birth, was named on the birth certificate, and acted as a parent from birth.

This result would be contrary to the best interests of children, who have a fundamental interest in maintaining relationships with both parents. It would also reward precisely the kind of representation that Turner made here—that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent—only to later argue that those representations were legally insufficient to establish parentage.

The legislature enacted A.R.S. § 25-814 to provide certainty regarding parent-child relationships and to protect children by ensuring they have legal parents. Applying the statute gender-neutrally serves these purposes. Denying the presumption to married women based solely on their gender does not.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's ruling that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting that presumption. The statute must be applied gender-neutrally to carry out its purpose of establishing parent-child relationships for children born during marriage. Alternatively, even if statutory interpretation were doubtful, the equitable estoppel doctrine independently requires affirmance because Turner knowingly represented that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent and Oakley reasonably relied on those representations to her detriment.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Based on the review of the facts provided and the opening arguments presented by the prosecution and defense, the Court preliminarily rules to **affirm** the family court’s ruling. The family court correctly determined that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and that Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting that presumption.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Statutory Interpretation of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)**

The central legal dispute is whether A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), which uses the gendered terms "man" and "father," may be applied to a female spouse in a same-sex marriage.

The prosecution argues for a strict textualist approach, asserting that the plain language of the statute unambiguously restricts the presumption of paternity to males. The prosecution relies on *State v. Soto*, 215 Ariz. 382, 160 P.3d 1071 (2007), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 212 Ariz. 153, 129 P.3d 391 (2006), for the proposition that courts must apply clear statutory language as written. Furthermore, the prosecution cites *State v. Valdez*, 193 Ariz. 267, 972 P.2d 637 (App. 1998), and *State v. Superior Court of Maricopa County*, 126 Ariz. 193, 613 P.2d 1292 (1980), to contend that the use of gender-specific terms like "man" or "husband" reflects a clear legislative intent to exclude women.

While the prosecution’s textual arguments are grounded in established principles of statutory construction, the defense persuasively counters that a strictly gendered reading would lead to absurd results and contravene the statute’s underlying purpose. The defense correctly notes that the primary goal of statutory construction is to give effect to legislative intent and avoid absurd results, as stated in *State v. Pima County*, 174 Ariz. 215, 848 P.2d 97 (App. 1993). The defense further argues that the "spirit of the law" must control when a literal application would defeat the statute’s purpose, citing *Bilton v. Blade*, 156 Ariz. 35, 749 P.2d 432 (App. 1988).

The purpose of A.R.S. § 25-814 is to establish legal parent-child relationships for children born during marriage, ensuring they have two legal parents responsible for their support and upbringing. To interpret the statute in a manner that grants a presumption of parentage to a non-biological male spouse but denies it to a non-biological female spouse—despite both being in a legally recognized marriage—would create an irrational classification based solely on gender. Such a result would arguably violate equal protection principles and the mandate of *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), which requires states to treat same-sex marriages equally to opposite-sex marriages.

Furthermore, the defense relies on *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), which directly addressed this issue and held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. The prosecution asks this Court to disregard *McLaughlin*, but the defense correctly identifies that *McLaughlin* represents binding precedent from the Court of Appeals and aligns with the constitutional necessity of treating same-sex couples equally under the law. Therefore, the defense’s argument that the statute must be interpreted gender-neutrally is more compelling than the prosecution’s strict textualist approach.

**II. Equitable Estoppel**

Even if the applicability of the statute were in doubt, the defense has demonstrated that equitable estoppel provides an independent and sufficient basis to affirm the family court’s ruling.

The defense cites *Chandler v. The record supports the finding that all elements are met. Turner knew Oakley was not the biological parent yet represented Oakley as a co-parent through various means, including drafting a will naming Oakley as the sole custodian, posting on social media that Oakley was C.T.’s mother, and listing Oakley on the birth certificate. Oakley relied on these representations by participating in the insemination process, being present at the birth, and forgoing formal adoption based on Turner’s assurance that the birth certificate was sufficient. Oakley has formed a significant parent-child bond with C.T., and severing that bond would constitute a detriment.

The prosecution does not address the equitable estoppel argument, choosing instead to rest entirely on the statutory interpretation issue. However, as established in *McLaughlin*, equitable estoppel is a proper doctrine to prevent a parent from going back on their word regarding the other parent's status when the other parent has relied on those assurances to their detriment. The facts here mirror those in *McLaughlin*, where the biological mother was estopped from disputing her wife's parental rights due to similar representations and the wife's role as a de facto parent.

**CONCLUSION**

The family court did not err in applying A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) gender-neutrally to recognize Oakley as a presumed parent. Furthermore, the doctrine of equitable estoppel bars Turner from rebutting this presumption given her clear representations and Oakley’s detrimental reliance. The prosecution’s strict reading of the statute is outweighed by the need to avoid absurd results and to uphold the equitable principles that protect the child’s relationship with their intended parent. Therefore, the family court’s ruling is affirmed.

The prosecution asks this Court to disregard McLaughlin v. Jones, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), as wrongly decided. This request must be rejected. McLaughlin is binding precedent from the Arizona Court of Appeals and represents the law of this jurisdiction until overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or superseded by legislative amendment. # DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE ARIZONA COURT OF APPEALS
### DIVISION ONE

---

**RE: The Matter of C.T., Minor Child**
**Turner v. Oakley**

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, this matter comes before this Court on special action review from the family court's ruling that Defendant-Appellant Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and that Plaintiff-Appellant Turner is equitably estopped from rebutting that presumption. The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's well-reasoned decision.

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the application of Arizona's parentage statutes to same-sex married couples who conceive children through artificial insemination. The defense submits that the family court correctly applied binding precedent from *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), and properly invoked equitable estoppel to prevent Turner from reneging on her representations that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF RELEVANT FACTS

The following facts, drawn from the record below, establish the defense's position:

1. **Intentional Conception and Shared Parenting Agreement**: Turner and Oakley began attempting to conceive a child through artificial insemination in 2013. Although they entered no formal written agreement, the parties discussed parenting roles and whether Oakley should formally adopt the child. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as sole custodian of any child born to her.

2. **Oakley's Active Participation**: Oakley played an active role in the artificial insemination process, including reviewing sperm donor profiles, accompanying Turner to medical appointments, and being present during the insemination procedures. While Oakley did not pay for services or sign clinic documents, this was a matter of convenience—Turner was the recipient of medical services—not an indication of intent to exclude Oakley from parenting.

3. **Birth Certificate and Hospital Representation**: Oakley was present at C.T.'s birth and cut the umbilical cord. A hospital nurse instructed Turner to list Oakley on the birth certificate in the section designated "father," which Turner did. This representation was made with full knowledge of the circumstances.

4. **Post-Birth Conduct**: Following C.T.'s birth, Turner held Oakley out as C.T.'s mother to family, friends, and the public through social media posts. The child was raised in a household with both women as parents.

5. **Turner's Litigation Position**: Only upon filing for dissolution did Turner suddenly assert that Oakley had no parental rights because she was neither the biological nor adoptive parent.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Family Court Correctly Applied A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) in a Gender-Neutral Manner

The defense acknowledges that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1), as originally enacted, employed gendered terminology referring to "men" and "fathers." However, as this Court held in *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d 118, 122 (App. 2016), the statute must be read and applied gender-neutrally to comply with constitutional requirements.

The prosecution's argument that this Court should apply the statute strictly according to its text, relying on *State v. Soto*, 215 Ariz. 382, 160 P.3d 1071 (2007), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 212 Ariz. 153, 129 P.3d 391 (2006), fails to account for the fundamental principle that statutory interpretation must yield to constitutional mandates. As this Court recognized in *McLaughlin*, the United States Supreme Court's decision in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), mandates that states treat same-sex marriages equally to opposite-sex marriages. Interpreting A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) to grant a presumption of parentage to a male spouse while denying it to a female spouse would result in unequal treatment based on the sex of the spouses, directly contravening *Obergefell*.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Valdez*, 193 Ariz. 267, 972 P.2d 637 (App. 1998), and *State v. Superior Court of Maricopa County*, 126 Ariz. 193, 613 P.2d 1292 (1980), is misplaced. Those cases stand for the unremarkable proposition that courts should not rewrite statutes to address perceived inequities. However, when a statute's plain text, read in a gendered manner, produces constitutional violations, the judiciary's duty is to interpret the statute in a manner that avoids such invalidity. As held in *State v. Pima County*, 174 Ariz. 215, 848 P.2d 97 (App. 1993), the absurd results doctrine permits courts to look beyond plain text when literal application would lead to irrational or unjust outcomes.

The defense further notes that *McLaughlin* is binding precedent from a panel of this Court. Under the doctrine of stare decisis, this Court must follow *McLaughlin* unless overruled by the Arizona Supreme Court or the Court sitting en banc. The prosecution has provided no authority from a higher court overruling *McLaughlin*, nor has it demonstrated that *McLaughlin* is factually distinguishable in any material respect. This Court is bound by *McLaughlin*'s conclusion.

### B. The Doctrine of Equitable Estoppel Properly Applies

The prosecution argues that equitable estoppel cannot apply because Oakley lacks a "valid underlying legal right." This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the doctrine and the sequence of the family court's analysis.

The family court did not use estoppel to "create" a parental right for Oakley. Rather, the court first determined that Oakley is a presumed parent under A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) (as interpreted gender-neutrally in *McLaughlin*). Having established Oakley's status as a presumed parent, the court then applied estoppel to prevent Turner from rebutting that presumption. This sequence is consistent with Arizona law.

The elements of equitable estoppel, as outlined in *Chandler v. City of Phoenix*, 193 Ariz. 78, 970 P.2d 928 (App. 1998), are met:

**First element**: Turner knew the true facts—that Oakley was not the biological parent. This is undisputed, as Turner was the one who underwent the insemination procedure.

**Second element**: Turner intended or expected Oakley to rely on her representations that Oakley was a co-parent. This is evidenced by multiple facts: Turner named Oakley as sole custodian in her will; Turner listed Oakley on the birth certificate; Turner held Oakley out as C.T.'s mother on social media; and Turner consistently represented to Oakley that they were co-parents.

**Third element**: Oakley was ignorant of the legal insufficiency of these representations. Oakley reasonably relied on Turner's assurances, the birth certificate listing, and the parties' shared understanding that they were raising a child together.

**Fourth element**: Oakley relied on these representations to her detriment. Oakley invested significant time, emotion, and resources into the parent-child relationship. She participated in the child's life as a parent, providing care, affection, and financial support. Oakley reasonably foregone formal adoption based on Turner's representations that such adoption was unnecessary.

The prosecution's argument that estoppel cannot "create" a right is a straw man. Estoppel here does not create a right; it prevents Turner from *denying* a right she previously acknowledged and which Oakley reasonably relied upon. This is precisely the function of equitable estoppel.

The facts here are virtually identical to those in *McLaughlin*, where this Court held that the biological mother was equitably estopped from disputing her wife's parental rights. In *McLaughlin*, as here, the parties had entered into an agreement guaranteeing equal parenting rights, the non-biological spouse was a beneficiary of the biological mother's will, and the non-biological spouse had been a de facto parent of the child. 240 Ariz. at 565-67 ¶¶ 16-23, 382 P.3d at 123-24. The family court's application of estoppel is supported by the record and consistent with binding precedent.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The plaintiff advances several arguments that the defense respectfully submits fail:

**First**, the plaintiff argues that the presumption of paternity applies only to men. This argument was expressly rejected in *McLaughlin*, which held that the statute must be applied gender-neutrally. This Court is bound by *McLaughlin*.

**Second**, the plaintiff argues that Oakley cannot be a parent because she is not biologically related to C.T. This argument ignores the statutory scheme, which creates presumptions of parentage based on marriage and birth, not solely on biology. The legislature has determined that children born to married couples benefit from the legal recognition of both spouses as parents, regardless of biological connection.

**Third**, the plaintiff argues that Oakley should have formally adopted C.T. This argument ignores the context in which the parties operated. The parties discussed adoption but were unsure if it was necessary, with Turner stating that "just being on the birth certificate was enough." Turner herself created the reliance by listing Oakley on the birth certificate and naming her in her will. It would be inequitable to allow Turner to now profit from her own representations.

**Fourth**, the plaintiff argues that the fertility clinic documents indicate that only the recipient is the legal parent. However, the document specifically states that the child would be the legal child of "the recipient and recipient's husband or partner if applicable." This language contemplates that a partner may be a legal parent. Moreover, the clinic's forms cannot override the statutory scheme or the parties' intent.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's ruling. The court correctly applied *McLaughlin v. Jones*, interpreting A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) gender-neutrally to include same-sex spouses within the scope of the presumption. The court properly applied equitable estoppel to prevent Turner from contradicting her prior representations that Oakley was C.T.'s co-parent.

To hold otherwise would permit a biological mother to misrepresent a co-parent's status, induce reliance on those misrepresentations, and then deny all parental rights upon the breakdown of the relationship. Such a result would be contrary to law, equity, and the best interests of the child.

The defense submits that the family court's decision is supported by the record, consistent with binding precedent, and correct in law.
