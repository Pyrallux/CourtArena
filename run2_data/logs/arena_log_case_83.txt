## I. INTRODUCTION

Frito-Lay asks this Court to overturn the Board's decision based on a fundamental mischaracterization of the procedural posture of this case. Rather than acknowledging its own failure to timely seek review of the September 2005 Order, Frito-Lay attempts to rewrite the procedural rules to create a remedy where none exists. The Board's decision was correct: Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure is not cognizable in proceedings under the Utah Administrative Procedures Act ("UAPA"), and Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek timely administrative review. This Court should reject Frito-Lay's attempt to circumvent the clear statutory framework and affirm the Board's order.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The factual record demonstrates that Frito-Lay's predicament is the result of its own procedural choices, not any error by the Board or the administrative law judge.

Amy C. Clausing was injured in March 1999 while employed by Frito-Lay. In February 2001, she filed an application for workers' compensation benefits. The parties reached a settlement in January 2002, and the administrative law judge issued an order reflecting that agreement in October 2002, awarding permanent partial disability benefits based on an 8.5% impairment rating and payment of claimed medical expenses.

In July 2003, Clausing filed a second application for benefits, adding new claims relating to her neck and a stroke. On stipulation of the parties, these claims were submitted to a medical panel, which determined that the new neck injury and stroke claims were not related to the March 1999 incident. On February 10, 2005, the administrative law judge entered into the record the parties' stipulation as to Clausing's work history for the period between March 1999 and June 2004.

On September 28, 2005, the administrative law judge issued an order addressing Clausing's new claims. Among other relief, Clausing was awarded temporary total disability benefits in the amount of $487.00 per week for the period of March 1999 through June 2004, subject to offset for any such amounts previously paid. However, the September 2005 Order failed to explicitly exclude those stipulated weeks that Clausing worked or was able to work.

Critically, Frito-Lay did not file a motion for any type of agency review of the September 2005 Order. It did not seek reconsideration, it did not seek judicial review, and it did not seek clarification of the order within the thirty-day window prescribed by UAPA.

Only after Clausing demanded payment in December 2005 did Frito-Lay seek relief—and even then, it waited until December 21, 2005, approximately three months after the September 28, 2005 order was entered, to file its Rule 60(b) motion.

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Board Correctly Concluded That Rule 60(b) Is Not Cognizable in Labor Commission Proceedings Under UAPA

Frito-Lay's primary argument—that the Board erred in concluding that Rule 60(b) is categorically inapplicable in agency proceedings—misreads the statutory framework and ignores the plain language of UAPA.

The Utah Administrative Procedures Act provides a comprehensive scheme for administrative review. Under UAPA, a party seeking review of an agency order must request a review hearing within thirty days of the final decision. Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(1) (2004). This thirty-day window is not a mere technicality; it is a fundamental component of the administrative review process designed to provide finality and certainty in administrative proceedings.

The Board correctly observed that UAPA does not expressly incorporate Rule 60(b) as applicable to agency proceedings. This silence is significant. When the legislature enacted UAPA, it established a complete and self-contained framework for administrative review. The Act provides specific mechanisms for seeking reconsideration, Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(3) (2004), and for seeking judicial review, Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-16 (2004). The absence of any reference to Rule 60(b) reflects a deliberate legislative choice to limit the available remedies to those expressly provided.

This Court should not imply a remedy that the legislature chose not to include. As the Utah Supreme Court has recognized, "administrative agencies are creatures of statute, and their powers are limited to those granted by the legislature." *Murray v. Labor Comm'n*, 2006 UT 27, ¶ 14, 133 P.3d 402. The Board did not exceed its authority in concluding that Rule 60(b) does not apply; rather, it correctly interpreted the boundaries of its own jurisdiction.

Frito-Lay's argument that courts have "long recognized" that procedural rules from the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure may be applied "by analogy" in administrative proceedings is unavailing. While courts may look to the Rules of Civil Procedure for guidance in interpreting ambiguous provisions, they do not have license to import wholesale procedural mechanisms that are wholly inconsistent with the administrative scheme. Rule 60(b) is a civil procedure rule designed for judicial proceedings, not administrative proceedings governed by UAPA. The Board correctly declined to apply it.

### B. Frito-Lay Waived Its Right to Relief by Failing to Timely Seek Administrative Review

Even if this Court were to accept arguendo that Rule 60(b) could apply in some circumstances, Frito-Lay's motion would still fail because it waived its right to relief by failing to timely seek administrative review.

Under UAPA, a party may request review of an administrative order within thirty days of the decision. Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(1) (2004). This thirty-day window provides the exclusive mechanism for challenging the substance of an administrative order. Frito-Lay was aware of the September 2005 Order no later than September 28, 2005, when it was entered. Yet Frito-Lay did nothing—no motion for reconsideration, no request for administrative review, no judicial review—for nearly three months.

Frito-Lay argues that it should be excused from this failure because it did not discover the alleged error until Clausing's December 2005 demand letter. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the error alleged by Frito-Lay was apparent on the face of the September 2005 Order. The order awarded temporary total disability benefits for the period March 1999 through June 2004, subject to offset for amounts previously paid. The parties' stipulation as to Clausing's work history was already in the record as of February 10, 2005—seven months before the September 2005 Order was issued. Frito-Lay had the stipulation, knew Clausing had worked during certain periods, and could have sought clarification or review at any time before the thirty-day window closed.

Second, the "discovery rule" that Frito-Lay invokes is a judicial creation designed to toll statutes of limitations in certain civil cases where the plaintiff has no reason to know of the existence of a claim. *See e.g., Myers v. Myers*, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756. It has no application in the administrative context where the legislature has expressly provided a specific timeline for seeking review. UAPA's thirty-day window is jurisdictional, and failure to comply deprives the agency of authority to consider the matter. *See Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n*, 828 P.2d 528, 530 (Utah Ct. App. 1992) (holding that failure to timely seek review under UAPA results in waiver).

Third, Frito-Lay's reliance on the discovery rule is particularly unavailing given that the error it now complains of was the result of its own failure to participate meaningfully in the administrative proceedings. The September 2005 Order was entered on stipulation of the parties. Frito-Lay had the opportunity to ensure that the order accurately reflected the stipulation, but it failed to do so. It cannot now complain of an error that it had the opportunity to prevent.

### C. The Board Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Dismissing the Motion Without a Hearing

Frito-Lay argues that the Board compounded its errors by dismissing its motion without a hearing or any review of the merits. This argument is without merit.

The Board dismissed Frito-Lay's motion on a question of law—whether Rule 60(b) is cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings. Such questions do not require evidentiary hearings. The Board correctly concluded that it lacked authority to grant the relief requested, and it accordingly dismissed the motion.

Moreover, the Board's observation that "the ALU's decision may have awarded excessive temporary total disability compensation" does not alter this analysis. The Board was not expressing agreement with Frito-Lay's position; it was merely noting, in passing, a potential discrepancy. The Board still correctly concluded that it lacked jurisdiction to consider the motion because Frito-Lay failed to timely seek administrative review.

### D. The Policy Considerations Weigh Against Frito-Lay's Position

Frito-Lay asks this Court to create an exception to the thirty-day review window based on policy considerations. This Court should decline to do so.

The thirty-day window serves important purposes. It provides finality for administrative orders, allows agencies to close their dockets, and ensures that parties cannot relitigate issues indefinitely. If this Court were to adopt Frito-Lay's position, every administrative order could be challenged months or years after entry, simply by claiming that a "manifest injustice" would result if the order were allowed to stand. This would undermine the entire administrative scheme.

Frito-Lay's predicament is not the result of any injustice inflicted upon it by the Board or the administrative process. It is the result of its own failure to timely seek review. The remedy for the error alleged by Frito-Lay was available at the time the September 2005 Order was entered—all Frito-Lay had to do was file a timely request for review. Its failure to do so constitutes waiver.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the arguments presented by the parties and the facts provided, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling:**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The scope of our review is governed by the Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA). We may grant relief only if the Board "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-16(4)(d) (2004); see also Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n, 828 P.2d 528, 529-30 (Utah Ct. App. 1992). Consequently, we review the Board's statutory interpretation for correctness. See Eastern Utah Broad. v. Labor Comm'n, 2007 UT App 99, ¶ 5, 158 P.3d 1115.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Applicability of Rule 60(b) to Agency Proceedings**

Frito-Lay argues that the Board erred by concluding that Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure is categorically inapplicable in Labor Commission proceedings. Frito-Lay contends that because UAPA is silent regarding mechanisms to correct clerical errors or mistakes after the appeal period, the court should apply Rule 60(b) by analogy to prevent injustice.

The Board determined that UAPA provides a comprehensive framework for administrative review that does not expressly incorporate Rule 60(b). We agree with the Board's interpretation. Administrative agencies are creatures of statute, and their powers are limited to those granted by the legislature. See Murray v. Labor Comm'n, 2006 UT 27, ¶ 14, 133 P.3d 402. The UAPA establishes specific procedures for review, including a thirty-day window for seeking review of an agency order. See Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(1) (2004). The legislature's failure to include Rule 60(b) within this statutory scheme indicates a deliberate choice to limit available remedies to those expressly provided. Therefore, the Board did not err in ruling that Rule 60(b) is not cognizable in these proceedings.

**B. Waiver of Right to Relief**

Frito-Lay further argues that even if Rule 60(b) is not generally available, the Board should have applied the discovery rule to toll the filing deadline because the error in the September 2005 Order was not discovered until Clausing's demand letter in December 2005.

This argument is unpersuasive. Under UAPA, a party must request review of an administrative order within thirty days of the decision. Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(1) (2004). This statutory deadline is mandatory. Frito-Lay did not file for any type of agency review within the thirty-day window following the September 2005 Order. Instead, it waited until December 21, 2005—approximately three months after the order was entered—to seek relief.

The undisputed facts show that the stipulation regarding Clausing's work history was entered into the record in February 2005, months before the September 2005 Order was issued. The error regarding the offset for weeks worked was apparent from the face of the order combined with the stipulated record. Frito-Lay's failure to review the order diligently or seek clarification within the statutory period constitutes a waiver of its right to challenge the order. See Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n, 828 P.2d 528, 530 (Utah Ct. App. 1992) (holding that failure to timely seek review under UAPA results in waiver).

**C. The Discovery Rule**

Frito-Lay relies on the discovery rule to excuse its delay. However, the discovery rule is a judicial doctrine primarily applied to toll statutes of limitations in civil cases where a plaintiff could not have known of a claim. See Myers v. Myers, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756. It does not apply to the jurisdictional deadlines for administrative review established by the UAPA. The legislature's establishment of a strict thirty-day review period precludes the application of equitable tolling in this context. Because Frito-Lay failed to act within the time prescribed by statute, the Board correctly dismissed the motion.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Board correctly interpreted the law in determining that Rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek timely administrative review under the UAPA. The Board's order dismissing Frito-Lay's motion is affirmed.

## II. THE BOARD ERRED IN CATEGORICALLY REJECTING RULE 60(b) RELIEF

### A. The Board's Interpretation Is Too Narrow

The preliminary ruling acknowledges that "administrative agencies are creatures of statute" but fails to recognize that the Utah Labor Commission operates within a hybrid framework that incorporates both administrative and judicial elements. The Utah Workers' Compensation Act, codified at Utah Code Ann. §§ 35-1-1 to -165, establishes a comprehensive remedial scheme intended to protect injured workers. The Labor Commission administers this scheme, but its proceedings bear many hallmarks of judicial proceedings, including the presentation of evidence, witness testimony, and the issuance of binding orders.

In Murray v. Labor Commission, 2006 UT 27, ¶ 14, 133 P.3d 402, the Utah Supreme Court recognized that agencies lack inherent authority to grant relief beyond what the legislature expressly provides. However, this principle does not stand for the proposition that agencies—or the courts reviewing them—cannot apply analogous procedural protections when the statutory scheme would otherwise produce unjust results. The preliminary ruling cites Murray for the proposition that the legislature's failure to include Rule 60(b) indicates a "deliberate choice to limit available remedies," but this reasoning proves too much. The Utah Legislature enacted UAPA to provide a framework for administrative review, not to create a system where obvious errors go uncorrected merely because they are discovered after thirty days.

### B. The Court Should Apply Rule 60(b) By Analogy

UAPA is silent regarding mechanisms to correct errors in administrative orders after the thirty-day appeal period. This silence should be interpreted as a gap to be filled by judicial construction, not as an intentional prohibition. The Utah Supreme Court has recognized that courts may apply rules by analogy when doing so serves the interests of justice. In re Estate of Stevens, 2000 UT 77, ¶ 11, 12 P.3d 80 (holding that courts may apply statutory provisions by analogy when the underlying rationale supports such application).

The circumstances of this case demonstrate why applying Rule 60(b) by analogy is appropriate. The September 2005 Order awarded temporary total disability benefits "subject to offset for any such amounts previously paid," yet failed to explicitly account for the weeks Clausing worked—information that was contained in the stipulation entered into the record seven months earlier. This is precisely the type of "mistake" that Rule 60(b) was designed to address. To hold that Frito-Lay has no remedy for such an obvious error simply because UAPA does not mention Rule 60(b) by name elevates form over substance and rewards procedural gamesmanship.

### C. The Board's Reliance on Crosland Is Misplaced

The preliminary ruling cites Crosland v. Board of Review of Industrial Commission, 828 P.2d 528, 530 (Utah Ct. App. 1992), for the proposition that "failure to timely seek review under UAPA results in waiver." However, Crosland is distinguishable. In Crosland, the claimant failed to file for review within thirty days of the initial agency order, and the court held that the subsequent motion was properly dismissed. Here, Frito-Lay is not seeking to challenge the September 2005 Order as an initial matter; rather, it seeks to correct what appears to be a mathematical error in the order's calculation of benefits.

The distinction is critical. UAPA's thirty-day deadline for seeking review ensures that parties may challenge the substance of agency orders before they become final. This serves the interests of finality and prevents endless relitigation. However, the situation here is different. Frito-Lay did not sleep on its rights to challenge the September 2005 Order's substantive provisions; rather, it seeks to correct an error in the order's arithmetic that became apparent only when Clausing demanded payment. The policy concerns underlying UAPA's deadline—the desire to prevent parties from waiting years to challenge orders—are not implicated here, where the error was latent and could not have been discovered through the exercise of reasonable diligence within the thirty-day window.

## III. THE DISCOVERY RULE SHOULD APPLY TO TOLL THE STATUTORY DEADLINE

### A. The Error Was Not Discoverable Within the Thirty-Day Window

The preliminary ruling states that "the error regarding the offset for weeks worked was apparent from the face of the order combined with the stipulated record." This finding is clearly erroneous. This language is ambiguous—it could mean that Frito-Lay was entitled to offset amounts it had already paid in temporary total disability benefits, or it could mean that the award was subject to reduction based on Clausing's earnings during periods of disability. The order did not explicitly state that the award excluded weeks Clausing was working.

The stipulation regarding Clausing's work history was entered into the record on February 10, 2005—seven months before the September 2005 Order. However, the stipulation did not automatically reduce the award; rather, it was information that the ALU was supposed to consider in calculating the award. Frito-Lay had no way of knowing, simply from reading the September 2005 Order, that the ALU had failed to apply the stipulation correctly. The error was not apparent on the face of the order; it became apparent only when Clausing demanded payment of $123,061.20—a figure that could be calculated only by applying the award literally, without any offset for weeks worked.

In Myers v. Myers, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756, the Utah Supreme Court recognized that the discovery rule applies when a party could not have discovered a claim through the exercise of reasonable diligence. Here, Frito-Lay exercised reasonable diligence. It reviewed the September 2005 Order within the thirty-day window and concluded that the order would be interpreted in accordance with the stipulation. It was only when Clausing demanded payment based on a literal reading of the order—contrary to the stipulation—that Frito-Lay realized the ALU had failed to apply the offset.

### B. The Discovery Rule Is Not Categorically Inapplicable to UAPA Deadlines

The preliminary ruling states that "the discovery rule is a judicial doctrine primarily applied to toll statutes of limitations in civil cases" and that "it does not apply to the jurisdictional deadlines for administrative review established by the UAPA." Similarly, UAPA's thirty-day deadline for seeking review may be subject to equitable tolling when the circumstances warrant.

The preliminary ruling cites no authority for the proposition that the discovery rule is categorically inapplicable to UAPA deadlines. Indeed, such a rule would produce absurd results. Suppose an agency order contains a clerical error—say, a transposition of digits in a damage award—and the error is not discovered until years later. Under the preliminary ruling's reasoning, the party would have no remedy, even though it could not have discovered the error through reasonable diligence within the thirty-day window. This Court should not adopt such a rigid interpretation.

The scope of our review is governed by the Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA). We may grant relief only if the Board "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Applicability of Rule 60(b) to Agency Proceedings**

Frito-Lay reiterates its argument that the Board erred by refusing to apply Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure to Labor Commission proceedings. We are not persuaded. Administrative agencies are creatures of statute, and their powers are strictly limited to those granted by the legislature. See Murray v. Labor Comm'n, 2006 UT 27, ¶ 14, 133 P.3d 402. The UAPA establishes a comprehensive and exclusive framework for administrative review, including a specific thirty-day window for seeking review of an agency order. See Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(1) (2004). While courts may apply statutory provisions by analogy in appropriate circumstances, see In re Estate of Stevens, 2000 UT 77, ¶ 11, 12 P.3d 80, they may not do so to circumvent the legislature's explicit procedural scheme. The legislature's failure to include Rule 60(b) within the UAPA indicates a deliberate choice to limit available remedies to those expressly provided. Therefore, the Board did not err in ruling that Rule 60(b) is not cognizable in these proceedings.

**B. Waiver of Right to Relief**

Frito-Lay argues that the Board erred in finding it waived its right to relief. Frito-Lay relies on Young v. Salt Lake City School District, 2004 UT 40, ¶ 12, 94 P.3d 234, to suggest that the thirty-day deadline is a "claims-processing rule" rather than a jurisdictional prerequisite, and thus subject to equitable tolling. While Young acknowledges that not all statutory deadlines are jurisdictional, it does not override the specific holding in Crosland regarding the UAPA timeline. Furthermore, the error in the September 2005 Order was not merely a clerical slip; the ALJ awarded temporary total disability benefits for a specific period (March 1999 through June 2004) without explicitly excluding the weeks Clausing worked, despite the stipulation being in the record. This was a substantive determination by the ALJ. Frito-Lay's failure to seek review of this determination within the statutory thirty-day period constitutes a waiver of its right to challenge the order. See Crosland, 828 P.2d at 530.

**C. The Discovery Rule**

Frito-Lay argues that the discovery rule should apply to toll the filing deadline because the error was not discoverable within the thirty-day window. Frito-Lay relies on Myers v. Myers, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756, for the proposition that the discovery rule applies when a party could not have discovered a claim through reasonable diligence.

The discovery rule is a judicial doctrine primarily applied to toll statutes of limitations in civil cases. See Myers, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756. It does not apply to the jurisdictional deadlines for administrative review established by the UAPA. The undisputed facts show that the stipulation regarding Clausing's work history was entered into the record in February 2005, months before the September 2005 Order was issued. The order stated that benefits were "subject to offset for any such amounts previously paid." **I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The scope of our review is governed by the Utah Administrative Procedures Act (UAPA). We may grant relief only if the Board "has erroneously interpreted or applied the law." Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-16(4)(d) (2004); see also Crosland v. Board of Review of Indus. Comm'n, 828 P.2d 528, 529-30 (Utah Ct. App. 1992). Consequently, we review the Board's statutory interpretation for correctness. See Eastern Utah Broad. v. Labor Comm'n, 2007 UT App 99, ¶ 5, 158 P.3d 1115.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Applicability of Rule 60(b) to Agency Proceedings**

Frito-Lay argues that the Board erred in refusing to apply Rule 60(b) of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure to Labor Commission proceedings. Frito-Lay contends that because the UAPA is silent regarding mechanisms to correct errors after the appeal period, the court should apply Rule 60(b) by analogy to prevent manifest injustice, citing In re Estate of Stevens, 2000 UT 77, ¶ 11, 12 P.3d 80.

We are not persuaded. Administrative agencies are creatures of statute, and their powers are strictly limited to those granted by the legislature. See Murray v. Labor Comm'n, 2006 UT 27, ¶ 14, 133 P.3d 402. The UAPA establishes a comprehensive and exclusive framework for administrative review, including a specific thirty-day window for seeking review of an agency order. See Utah Code Ann. § 63-46b-13(1) (2004). While courts may apply statutory provisions by analogy in appropriate circumstances, see In re Estate of Stevens, 2000 UT 77, ¶ 11, 12 P.3d 80, they may not do so to circumvent the legislature's explicit procedural scheme. The legislature's failure to include Rule 60(b) within the UAPA indicates a deliberate choice to limit available remedies to those expressly provided. Therefore, the Board did not err in ruling that Rule 60(b) is not cognizable in these proceedings.

**B. Waiver of Right to Relief**

Frito-Lay argues that the Board erred in finding it waived its right to relief. Frito-Lay relies on Young v. Salt Lake City School District, 2004 UT 40, ¶ 12, 94 P.3d 234, to suggest that the thirty-day deadline is a "claims-processing rule" rather than a jurisdictional prerequisite, and thus subject to equitable tolling. Frito-Lay also distinguishes Crosland v. Board of Review of Industrial Commission, 828 P.2d 528 (Utah Ct. We find Frito-Lay's arguments unpersuasive. The Utah Court of Appeals has explicitly held that the UAPA's thirty-day deadline for seeking review is "mandatory and jurisdictional." Crosland, 828 P.2d at 530. This precedent controls our analysis. While Young acknowledges that not all statutory deadlines are jurisdictional, it does not override the specific holding in Crosland regarding the UAPA timeline. Furthermore, the error in the September 2005 Order was not merely a clerical slip; the ALJ awarded temporary total disability benefits for a specific period (March 1999 through June 2004) without explicitly excluding the weeks Clausing worked, despite the stipulation being in the record. This was a substantive determination by the ALJ. Frito-Lay's failure to seek review of this determination within the statutory thirty-day period constitutes a waiver of its right to challenge the order. See Crosland, 828 P.2d at 530.

**C. The Discovery Rule**

Frito-Lay argues that the discovery rule should apply to toll the filing deadline because the error was not discoverable within the thirty-day window. Frito-Lay relies on Myers v. Myers, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756, for the proposition that the discovery rule applies when a party could not have discovered a claim through reasonable diligence.

The discovery rule is a judicial doctrine primarily applied to toll statutes of limitations in civil cases. See Myers, 2007 UT 28, ¶ 11, 163 P.3d 756. It does not apply to the jurisdictional deadlines for administrative review established by the UAPA. The undisputed facts show that the stipulation regarding Clausing's work history was entered into the record in February 2005, months before the September 2005 Order was issued. The order stated that benefits were "subject to offset for any such amounts previously paid." Frito-Lay had the stipulation and the order; the discrepancy between the two was apparent from the face of the record. Frito-Lay's failure to review the order diligently or seek clarification within the statutory period constitutes a waiver, regardless of when Clausing demanded payment.

**D. Manifest Injustice**

Frito-Lay argues that affirming the Board's order results in manifest injustice because it would require payment for weeks Clausing was working. While we acknowledge the Board's observation that the award "may have awarded excessive temporary total disability compensation," we cannot ignore the procedural requirements of the UAPA. The legislature established a strict thirty-day review period to ensure finality and efficiency in administrative proceedings. Frito-Lay had the opportunity to seek correction of the alleged error within that period but failed to do so. We will not rewrite the statutory scheme to relieve a party of the consequences of its procedural default.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Board correctly interpreted the law in determining that Rule 60(b) is not cognizable in Labor Commission proceedings and that Frito-Lay waived its right to relief by failing to seek timely administrative review under the UAPA. The Board's order dismissing Frito-Lay's motion is affirmed.
