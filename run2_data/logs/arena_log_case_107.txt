### Preliminary Statement

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response in support of Appellant's position and in opposition to the arguments advanced by the State. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the judgment below in part and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

---

### I. The State's Concession on Third Point Requires Remand

The Defense agrees with the State's concession that the Trial Court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without requisite notice and hearing. As the appellate court noted, this cause should be remanded to the Trial Court to vacate said order and for appropriate proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant. ### II. The Appellate Court Erred in Holding That Reversal Is Not Required Despite Officer Shugrue's Improper Comment

#### A. The Factual and Legal背景

The appellate court acknowledged that Officer Shugrue, with seventeen years of experience, should not have voluntarily commented on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions. The court stated: "Although the Court is sympathetic to the problems of police officers, we cannot in good conscience condone such conduct from officers who know or should know better." Despite this condemnation, the appellate court refused to reverse the conviction.

#### B. The Comment Violated the Defendant's Constitutional Rights

The Defense submits that the appellate court applied the wrong legal standard. Officer Shugrue's comment on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions violated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination and Article I, Section 9 of the Florida Constitution.

In *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 (2010), the United States Supreme Court held that the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects individuals from being compelled to testify against themselves or from having their silence used against them at trial. When a law enforcement officer comments on a defendant's exercise of this right, such comment constitutes reversible error.

The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that comments on a defendant's silence or refusal to answer questions constitute fundamental error requiring reversal. In *Henry v. State*, 688 So. 2d 320 (Fla. 1997), the Florida Supreme Court reversed a conviction where the prosecutor commented on the defendant's post-arrest silence. Similarly, in *Chandler v. State*, 732 So. 2d 172 (Fla. 1998), the Court held that any comment on the defendant's exercise of the right to remain silent constitutes fundamental error.

#### C. The Harmless Error Analysis Was Flawed

The appellate court applied a harmless error analysis, stating: "In view of the strength of the evidence against the Defendant, we doubt that the comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury to return a verdict of guilty."

The Defense submits that this analysis is flawed for several reasons:

First, the appellate court improperly shifted the burden to the Defendant to prove that the comment influenced the jury. Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the burden rests upon the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional violation did not contribute to the verdict.

Second, the appellate court minimized the significance of the comment by characterizing it as merely improper conduct by an officer. However, Officer Shugrue's comment was not merely improper—it was a direct comment on the Defendant's exercise of a constitutional right. The officer essentially told the jury that the Defendant refused to answer questions, which the jury could reasonably interpret as indicative of guilt.

Third, the appellate court's reliance on the strength of the evidence ignores the fact that the breathalyzer reading itself was subject to challenge. The reading of .12 and the blood alcohol reading exceeding .10 were the very evidence that the Defense was entitled to challenge through cross-examination and by examining the condition of the testing equipment.

In *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the Florida Supreme Court held that when there is a reasonable possibility that a constitutional error affected the verdict, the error is not harmless and requires reversal. The Defense submits that there is more than a reasonable possibility that Officer Shugrue's comment affected the verdict, particularly given the emphasis that juries place on officer testimony in DUI cases.

#### D. The Comment Was Not Invited by Defense Counsel

The appellate court noted that Officer Shugrue's comment came in response to a query by defense counsel as to whether the Officer knew how much sleep the Defendant had on the night preceding the arrest. The Defense submits that this does not constitute invited error.

Defense counsel's question regarding the Defendant's sleep was a legitimate attempt to establish a potential alternative explanation for the Defendant's appearance or behavior. It did not constitute an invitation for the officer to comment on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions. The officer's response exceeded the scope of the question and injected inadmissible evidence regarding the Defendant's exercise of constitutional rights.

### III. The Appellate Court Erred in Finding No Discovery Violation

#### A. The Factual Background

The Defense alleged that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to show defense counsel the breathalyzer's monthly inspection reports. The appellate court rejected this claim, reasoning that the rules do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facie case, and that the duty to show documentation arises only upon challenge by the defendant.

#### B. The Appellate Court Misapplied the Discovery Rules

The Defense submits that the appellate court misapplied the discovery rules. Under Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220, the State has a duty to disclose to the defense all evidence and information material to the preparation of the defense. This includes evidence that may be used to challenge the admissibility of test results.

In *State v. Heisser*, 720 So. 2d 537 (Fla. 1998), the Florida Supreme Court held that the State has a duty to disclose all evidence material to the defense, including evidence that might be used to impeach the State's evidence. The monthly inspection reports of the breathalyzer are precisely the type of evidence that could be used to challenge the reliability of the test results.

The appellate court's reasoning that the duty arises only upon challenge by the defendant places the defendant in an impossible position. How can the defendant challenge the condition of the equipment when the defendant is unaware of what the inspection reports show? This reasoning effectively nullifies the discovery rules and deprives the defendant of the ability to prepare a meaningful defense.

#### C. The Duty to Disclose Is Not Limited to Prima Facie Case

The appellate court stated: "the only duty placed upon the State in this case was to administer the breathalyzer tests in accordance with § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules" and that "these rules do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facie case."

The Defense submits that this reasoning confuses the elements of the offense with the defendant's right to prepare a defense. The State's obligation under the discovery rules exists independently of the elements necessary to prove a prima facie case. The discovery rules exist to ensure fairness and to allow the defendant to prepare a meaningful defense.

In *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), the United States Supreme Court held that the suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to the defendant upon request violates the defendant's due process rights where the evidence is material to either guilt or punishment. The monthly inspection reports fall squarely within the category of evidence that must be disclosed under Brady.

#### D. The Defendant Was Prejudiced by the Non-Disclosure

The Defense submits that the non-disclosure of the monthly inspection reports prejudiced the Defendant. Had the defense been provided with these reports, it could have used them to challenge the reliability of the breathalyzer reading. The reports might have revealed deficiencies in the maintenance or calibration of the device that could have cast doubt on the accuracy of the .12 reading.

In *United States v. Agurs*, 427 U.S. 97 (1976), the Supreme Court held that evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that the result of the proceeding would have been different had the evidence been disclosed. The Defense submits that there is a reasonable probability that the outcome would have been different had the inspection reports been disclosed, as they could have been used to challenge the most damaging piece of evidence against the Defendant.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the Prosecution's response to the Defense's initial arguments. The Prosecution's assertions fail to overcome the legal standards established by binding precedent and mischaracterize the nature of the errors at trial.

## II. REBUTTAL TO POINT I: OFFICER SHUGRUE'S TESTIMONY

### A. The Prosecution Fails to Meet Its Burden Under Chapman

The Prosecution argues that the error was harmless because the evidence of guilt was "overwhelming." However, this argument fundamentally misunderstands the burden established in Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967). The prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. The Prosecution has not carried this burden merely by pointing to other evidence of guilt.

In State v. DiGuilio, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the Florida Supreme Court emphasized that the prosecution must prove there is no reasonable possibility that the error affected the verdict. The Prosecution's argument that "no reasonable juror could have been swayed" is speculative and fails to satisfy the exacting standard required. The Prosecution has not demonstrated that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt; it has merely asserted that other evidence existed.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on State v. Murray Is Misplaced

The Prosecution cites State v. Murray, 443 So. 2d 954 (Fla. 1983), for the proposition that harmless error applies where breathalyzer results and field sobriety tests provide overwhelming evidence of intoxication. However, Murray is distinguishable. In that case, the court found harmless error where the testimony at issue was merely cumulative of other properly admitted testimony regarding the defendant's statements. Here, by contrast, Officer Shugrue's comment directly implicated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment right to remain silent—a constitutionally protected right that distinguishes this case from Murray.

The Prosecution's attempt to liken this case to Murray ignores the qualitative difference between improper commentary on a defendant's exercise of constitutional rights and testimony that is merely cumulative. The comment here was not cumulative; it was an improper comment on the Defendant's silence that could have improperly influenced the jury's perception of guilt.

### C. The Evidence Was Not So Overwhelming as to Render the Error Harmless

Even accepting arguendo the Prosecution's characterization of the evidence as "overwhelming," the Defense notes that the breathalyzer reading of .12 was the central evidence upon which the prosecution relied. Officer Shugrue's comment suggesting the Defendant refused to answer questions could have improperly bolstered the prosecution's case by implying consciousness of guilt. This is particularly significant where, as here, the breathalyzer reading formed the crux of the prosecution's prima facie case under Section 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985).

The Prosecution's argument that the comment was "isolated and non-repetitive" does not diminish its potential impact. A single comment violating a defendant's constitutional rights can constitute reversible error when it touches upon the very issue of guilt. See Henry v. State, 688 So. 2d 320 (Fla. 1997); Chandler v. State, 732 So. 2d 172 (Fla. 1998).

## III. REBUTTAL TO POINT II: DISCOVERY VIOLATION

### A. The Prosecution's Interpretation of Brady Is Fundamentally Flawed

The Prosecution argues that no discovery violation occurred because the Defendant never challenged the breathalyzer's reliability at trial. This argument turns Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), on its head. Brady requires the prosecution to disclose evidence favorable to the accused that is material to guilt or punishment, regardless of whether the defense requests such evidence. The prosecution cannot bootstrap its disclosure obligations onto the defense's ability to anticipate what evidence exists.

In State v. Heisser, 720 So. 2d 537 (Fla. 1998), the Florida Supreme Court held that the State must disclose all evidence material to the defense, including evidence that might be used to impeach the State's evidence. The Prosecution's argument that the inspection reports were not material because the defense did not challenge the equipment's reliability ignores the very purpose of Brady—to ensure that the defense has access to evidence that might undermine the prosecution's case.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220(b)(1) Is Misplaced

The Prosecution cites Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.220(b)(1) for the proposition that disclosure is required only when evidence is relevant to the defense's preparation. However, this argument ignores the mandatory disclosure obligations under Brady and its progeny. The rule cannot be interpreted to relieve the prosecution of its constitutional duty to disclose favorable evidence.

Moreover, the Prosecution's interpretation would create an impossible Catch-22: the defense cannot challenge the equipment's reliability without first knowing what the inspection reports reveal, but the prosecution argues the defense must challenge the reliability to obtain the reports. This circular reasoning eviscerates the discovery rules and violates the spirit of Brady.

### C. The Prosecution Misreads United States v. Agurs

The Prosecution argues that United States v. Agurs, 427 U.S. 97 (1976), requires a showing that the outcome would have been different had the evidence been disclosed. However, Agurs held that evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that the result of the proceeding would have been different. The State contends that while the comment was improper, it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt given the overwhelming evidence of guilt, specifically the breathalyzer reading of .12 and the Defendant’s failure to perform roadside tests. The State relies on *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and *State v. Here, Officer Shugrue’s comment directly implicated the Defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to remain silence. The Florida Supreme Court has held that comments on a defendant’s silence constitute fundamental error. See *Henry v. State*, 688 So. 2d 320 (Fla. 1997); *Chandler v. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the Appellant’s conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in admitting Officer Shugrue’s testimony regarding the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent Defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error as to the third point. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling in part and modifies its reasoning regarding Points I and II.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Point III: Imposition of Court Costs**

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Accordingly, the cause is remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order and for appropriate proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant.

**B. Point I: Admission of Officer Shugrue’s Testimony**

The Appellant argues that the trial court committed reversible error by allowing Officer Shugrue to comment on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions. The Prosecution, in its rebuttal, contends that while the comment was improper, it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt given the overwhelming evidence of guilt, specifically the breathalyzer reading of .12 and the Defendant’s failure to perform roadside tests. The State relies on *State v. Murray*, 443 So. 2d 954 (Fla. 1983), to argue that overwhelming physical evidence renders such errors harmless, and cites *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), regarding the burden of proof for harmless error.

The Defense correctly distinguishes *State v. Murray*, 443 So. 2d 954 (Fla. 1983). In *Murray*, the improper testimony was cumulative of other admissible evidence. Here, Officer Shugrue’s comment directly implicated the Defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. The Florida Supreme Court has held that comments on a defendant’s silence constitute fundamental error. See *Henry v. State*, 688 So. 2d 320 (Fla. 1997); *Chandler v. State*, 732 So. 2d 172 (Fla. 1998). Unlike the cumulative error in *Murray*, a comment on silence improperly suggests consciousness of guilt to the jury, striking at a core constitutional protection.

While the evidence of intoxication—the .12 breathalyzer reading and failed field sobriety tests—is strong, the State has not met its burden under *Chapman* and *DiGuilio* to prove that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. The State’s assertion that "no reasonable juror could have been swayed" is insufficient to satisfy the "reasonable possibility" standard required by *DiGuilio*. Because the error implicated a fundamental right and was not merely cumulative of other evidence, the Defense’s argument that the error was not harmless prevails.

**C. Point II: Discovery Violation**

The Appellant contends the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Prosecution’s rebuttal argument focused exclusively on the harmless error analysis regarding Officer Shugrue’s testimony and did not offer new arguments or authority to counter the Defense’s position on the discovery violation. Consequently, the Court relies on the arguments previously presented.

The Defense persuasively argues that the State’s interpretation creates an impermissible Catch-22. Under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), the prosecution is required to disclose evidence favorable to the accused that is material to guilt or punishment, regardless of whether a defense request is made. *State v. Heisser*, 720 So. 2d 537 (Fla. 1998), extends this duty to evidence that might be used to impeach the State's case. The State cannot condition its disclosure obligations on the Defendant’s ability to anticipate defects in the equipment that are only documented in the undisclosed reports.

The Defense’s reliance on *United States v. Agurs*, 427 U.S. 97 (1976), is well-taken. *Agurs* establishes that evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that the result of the proceeding would have been different had the evidence been disclosed. The inspection reports were the very evidence necessary to determine if the breathalyzer was functioning correctly. By withholding these reports, the State prevented the Defendant from assessing the machine's reliability to mount a challenge. The administrative rule cited by the State, § 316.1932(l)(f)(1), cannot supersede the constitutional discovery obligations outlined in *Brady*. Therefore, the State’s failure to disclose the reports constituted a discovery violation.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense has demonstrated that the error regarding Officer Shugrue’s testimony was not harmless and that the State’s failure to disclose the breathalyzer inspection reports violated the Defendant’s discovery rights. The State’s arguments, while supported by relevant authority regarding harmless error, do not overcome the specific constitutional infirmities identified by the Defense.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Trial Court is **REVERSED** on Points I and II, and the cause is **REMANDED** for a new trial. The cause is also **REMANDED** on Point III for proceedings consistent with the State’s concession.
