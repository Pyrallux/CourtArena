## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Standard of Review and Applicable Law

The Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial is fundamental and is made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. at 515, 92 S.Ct. at 2184. However, the right is necessarily relative and is consistent with delays. The United States Supreme Court has declined to establish specific time limits and instead adopted a balancing test that examines the conduct of both the prosecution and the defendant. *Id.* at 522, 92 S.Ct. at 2188.

Under *Barker v. Wingo*, courts must consider four factors: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant's assertion of the right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. *Id.* at 530, 92 S.Ct. at 2192. No single factor is dispositive; rather, courts must engage in a "difficult and sensitive balancing process." *Id.*

The Texas Constitution provides similar protections under Article I, Section 10, and Texas courts apply the *Barker* framework in analyzing speedy trial claims. The length of delay that triggers a speedy trial inquiry varies depending on the nature of the case, but a delay of approximately one year or more may be sufficient to trigger the analysis. *See State v. Munoz*, 991 S.W.2d 818, 825 (Tex. Crim. App. 1999).

### B. The Length of the Delay

The appellant contends that "more than three years elapsed before [he] was ever indicted and six years since the death of Tim Davila by the time trial actually came to fruition." However, the State agrees with the trial court's determination that any speedy trial analysis should not begin until the State executed the arrest warrant and took the appellant into custody on December 5, 2006.

This Court has recognized that the speedy trial clock begins running when a defendant is formally accused, either through arrest or indictment, whichever first occurs. *See* *State v. Olivares*, 982 S.W.2d 61, 65 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1998, no pet.). Here, the appellant was not arrested in Texas until December 5, 2006, despite being incarcerated in Ohio since February 2003. The appellant was indicted on March 1, 2007, and his trial began in August 2008. Thus, the period from arrest to trial was approximately twenty months.

While a twenty-month delay may be sufficient to trigger a speedy trial inquiry, it is not presumptively prejudicial in this context, particularly given the complexity of the case and the procedural history. The delay must be balanced against the other *Barker* factors.

### C. The Reason for the Delay

The reason for the delay is a critical factor in the speedy trial analysis. Different weights are assigned to different reasons. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531, 92 S.Ct. at 2193. A deliberate attempt to delay the trial to prejudice the defendant weighs heavily against the State. Conversely, a valid reason, such as a missing witness or missing evidence, weighs less heavily. *Id.*

The record demonstrates that the primary cause of delay in this case was the appellant himself. The trial court explicitly found that "the defendant is the primary cause of the delays" and explained:

> [I]n all fairness to the lawyers who have been appointed, they are entitled to have time to get ready for trial. They cannot be expected to go to trial even though you may be familiar with the facts of the case, they have an obligation and a duty to review the State's file, especially in light of the charges....

The trial court specifically noted that the appellant "filed grievances or made accusations against your lawyers," which contributed to the delays. This conduct by the appellant weighs heavily against his speedy trial claim.

The appellant argues that law enforcement acted in bad faith by transferring Detective Moffitt and waiting for his release from Ohio prison before arresting him. However, the record does not support a finding of deliberate delay by the State. The transfer of a detective to another unit does not constitute bad faith, particularly when a new officer was assigned to the case and was aware the appellant was incarcerated. Moreover, the appellant was serving a sentence in Ohio on unrelated charges, and the State was entitled to wait until the appellant was available to execute the arrest warrant.

### D. The Defendant's Assertion of the Right

The appellant filed his motion to dismiss for lack of speedy trial at the June 16, 2008, pretrial hearing—approximately nineteen months after his arrest and approximately five months before trial. While the appellant did assert his right to a speedy trial, his assertion came relatively late in the proceedings and was coupled with his repeated efforts to dismiss his attorneys, which contributed to the very delays he now complains of.

Furthermore, the appellant cannot claim prejudice from the delay when he was the primary cause of that delay through his repeated motions to dismiss counsel and his allegations of ineffective assistance and conflicts of interest.

### E. Prejudice to the Defendant

The appellant claims he was "severely" prejudiced because: (1) a key witness, Marco Toledo, died before trial; (2) the trial court admitted into evidence statements made by Toledo to other witnesses, even though the appellant had no opportunity to cross-examine Toledo; and (3) the testimony of a neighbor was unavailable.

The prejudice analysis focuses on the interests of the defendant that the speedy trial right was designed to protect: (1) to prevent oppressive pretrial incarceration; (2) to minimize anxiety and concern of the accused; and (3) to limit the possibility that the defense will be impaired. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 532, 92 S.Ct. at 2193.

The appellant has not demonstrated that the transfer of Detective Moffitt caused any delay in the proceedings or prejudiced his defense. The transfer, standing alone, does not support a finding of bad faith.

The appellant argues that law enforcement exhibited bad faith by waiting for his release from Ohio prison before arresting him. However, the appellant has not established that he ever communicated this waiver to Texas authorities or that he requested to be returned to Texas. Moreover, the appellant was serving a sentence in Ohio and was not available for trial until his release.

The State's decision to wait for the appellant to complete his Ohio sentence before executing the arrest warrant was a reasonable exercise of prosecutorial discretion and does not constitute bad faith.

### D. The Death of Marco Toledo Does Not Establish Prejudice

The appellant claims he was prejudiced because Marco Toledo died before trial and he was unable to cross-examine him. However, the appellant has not established that the State's delay caused Toledo's death. The record indicates that Toledo died of unrelated causes.

The appellant also argues that the trial court admitted statements made by Toledo to other witnesses. This presents a hearsay issue that is separate from the speedy trial claim. The appellant had the opportunity to object to the admission of these statements on hearsay grounds and to seek a limiting instruction. The appellant has not demonstrated that the admission of these statements violated his constitutional rights.

The appellant has failed to establish a violation of his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial. Under the *Barker* framework, the appellant has not demonstrated that: (1) the delay was unreasonable or purposeful; (2) the State caused the delay; (3) he timely asserted his right; or (4) he was prejudiced by the delay.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s denial of Appellant’s motion for a speedy trial. Appellant argues that his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial was violated due to a delay between the alleged offense and the trial, resulting in the unavailability of a key witness. The State contends that the delay analysis should be confined to the period following Appellant’s arrest on the Texas warrant and that any delay was caused by Appellant’s own conduct. Based on the facts and arguments provided, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the accused the right to a speedy trial, a protection applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 515, 92 S.Ct. 2182, 33 L.Ed.2d 101 (1972). In determining whether a defendant's right to a speedy trial has been violated, courts must engage in a balancing test involving four factors: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant's assertion of his right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. *Id.* at 530, 92 S.Ct. at 2192. No single factor is dispositive. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Length of the Delay**

The speedy trial clock generally begins when the accused is formally accused, either through arrest or indictment, whichever occurs first. *State v. Olivares*, 982 S.W.2d 61, 65 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1998, no pet.). The record indicates that Appellant was taken into custody by Texas officials on December 5, 2006, and was indicted on March 1, 2007. The trial commenced in August 2008. Therefore, the relevant delay is approximately twenty months.

While a delay of one year or more is generally sufficient to trigger a speedy trial inquiry, *State v. Munoz*, 991 S.W.2d 818, 825 (Tex. Crim. App. 1999), the length of the delay here, though significant, is not presumptively prejudicial such that it requires automatic reversal. The length of the delay merely triggers the *Barker* analysis.

**B. Reason for the Delay**

The record reveals that the primary cause of the delay between Appellant’s arrest in December 2006 and his trial in August 2008 was Appellant’s own conduct. Specifically, Appellant filed multiple motions to dismiss his court-appointed counsel, alleging ineffective assistance and conflicts of interest. These motions were filed in February 2007, March 2007, and February 2008, leading to the appointment of multiple attorneys and subsequent withdrawals. The trial court explicitly found that Appellant was "the primary cause of the delays."

Under *Barker*, different weights are assigned to different reasons for delay. A deliberate attempt to delay the trial to hamper the defense weighs heavily against the government, while a neutral reason such as negligence or overcrowded courts weighs less heavily. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531, 92 S.Ct. at 2193. However, a delay caused by the defendant himself weighs heavily against the defendant. *Id.* Here, the record supports the conclusion that the post-arrest delay was attributable to Appellant’s dissatisfaction with his counsel and the subsequent changes in representation. The State’s decision to wait for Appellant’s release from an Ohio prison before executing the arrest warrant does not demonstrate bad faith or an intentional delay designed to prejudice Appellant.

**C. Defendant’s Assertion of the Right**

Appellant filed his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial in June 2008, approximately nineteen months after his arrest and five months before the trial. While Appellant did assert his right, the assertion came late in the proceedings and only after he had contributed significantly to the delay through his motions regarding counsel. A failure to assert the right in a timely manner makes it difficult for a defendant to prove that he was denied a speedy trial. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 528-29, 92 S.Ct. at 2191-92.

**D. Prejudice to the Defendant**

Appellant claims he was "severely" prejudiced because a key witness, Marco Toledo, died of unrelated causes before trial, and he was unable to cross-examine him. The *Barker* court identified three interests protected by the speedy trial right: (i) to prevent oppressive pretrial incarceration; (ii) to minimize anxiety and concern of the accused; and (iii) to limit the possibility that the defense will be impaired. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 532, 92 S.Ct. at 2193.

Here, Appellant has not demonstrated that the State’s delay caused the death of Toledo. The record indicates Toledo died of unrelated causes. Furthermore, Appellant was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges from 2003 until 2006. The twenty-month delay following his Texas arrest, largely caused by his own motions to dismiss counsel, does not establish a nexus between the State's conduct and the witness's death. Therefore, Appellant has not shown that the delay impaired his defense in a manner sufficient to outweigh the other factors.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Appellant has not established a violation of his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial. The length of the delay triggers the inquiry, but the reason for the delay is largely attributable to the Appellant, his assertion of the right was late, and he has failed to demonstrate specific prejudice caused by the State. Accordingly, the trial court did not err in denying the motion for a speedy trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on Appellant’s appeal challenging the trial court’s denial of his motion for a speedy trial. Appellant contends that his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial was violated due to an excessive delay between the alleged offense and the trial, resulting in the unavailability of a key witness. The State argues that the delay analysis is confined to the period following Appellant’s arrest on the Texas warrant and that any delay was caused by Appellant’s own conduct. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties and the provided record, the Court affirms the trial court’s judgment.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the accused the right to a speedy trial, a protection applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 515, 92 S.Ct. 2182, 33 L.Ed.2d 101 (1972). To determine whether a defendant's right has been violated, courts engage in a balancing test of four factors: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant's assertion of his right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. *Id.* at 530, 92 S.Ct. at 2192. No single factor is dispositive. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Length of the Delay**

The speedy trial clock generally begins when the accused is formally accused, either through arrest or indictment, whichever occurs first. *State v. Olivares*, 982 S.W.2d 61, 65 (Tex. App.—San Antonio 1998, no pet.). The record indicates that Appellant was taken into custody by Texas officials on December 5, 2006, and was indicted on March 1, 2007. The trial commenced in August 2008. Therefore, the relevant delay is approximately twenty months.

Appellant argues, relying on *State v. Olivares*, that the clock should have started in February 2003 when the arrest warrant was issued, creating a delay of over five years. However, the provided facts state that the court agreed with the State’s assertion that the analysis should not begin until the State executed the arrest warrant and Appellant was taken into custody. The fact that Appellant was incarcerated in Ohio on unrelated charges during the interim period supports the conclusion that the speedy trial right did not attach until Texas authorities secured his person. Consequently, the twenty-month delay is the appropriate period for review. While a delay of one year or more is generally sufficient to trigger a speedy trial inquiry, *State v. Munoz*, 991 S.W.2d 818, 825 (Tex. Crim. App. 1999), the length of the delay here, though significant, is not presumptively prejudicial.

**B. Reason for the Delay**

The record reveals that the primary cause of the delay between Appellant’s arrest in December 2006 and his trial in August 2008 was Appellant’s own conduct. Appellant filed multiple motions to dismiss his court-appointed counsel, alleging ineffective assistance and conflicts of interest. These motions were filed in February 2007, March 2007, and February 2008, leading to the appointment of multiple attorneys and subsequent withdrawals. The trial court explicitly found that Appellant was "the primary cause of the delays."

Appellant argues that his motions to dismiss counsel were legitimate assertions of his right to effective representation and should not be counted against him. He also contends that the State exhibited bad faith by waiting three years to execute the warrant while he was in Ohio. Regarding the pre-arrest period, the court has already determined that the speedy trial clock did not commence until his arrest. Therefore, the State’s inaction during that period is not attributable to a violation of the speedy trial right. As for the post-arrest delay, under *Barker*, a delay caused by the defendant himself weighs heavily against the defendant. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531, 92 S.Ct. at 2193. The trial court’s finding that Appellant was the primary cause of the delay is supported by the record of his multiple motions to dismiss counsel and the resulting changes in representation.

**C. Defendant’s Assertion of the Right**

Appellant filed his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial in June 2008, approximately nineteen months after his arrest and five months before the trial. Appellant argues this assertion was timely given the instability of his counsel representation. However, the assertion came late in the proceedings and only after he had contributed significantly to the delay through his motions regarding counsel. A failure to assert the right in a timely manner makes it difficult for a defendant to prove that he was denied a speedy trial. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 528-29, 92 S.Ct. at 2191-92. The fact that Appellant was cycling through attorneys does not excuse the delay in asserting the right once he was represented and aware of the proceedings.

**D. Prejudice to the Defendant**

Appellant claims he was "severely" prejudiced because a key witness, Marco Toledo, died of unrelated causes before trial, and he was unable to cross-examine him. The *Barker* court identified three interests protected by the speedy trial right: (i) to prevent oppressive pretrial incarceration; (ii) to minimize anxiety and concern of the accused; and (iii) to limit the possibility that the defense will be impaired. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 532, 92 S.Ct. at 2193.

Appellant argues that the State "profited" from the delay by introducing Toledo’s statements at trial. However, the record indicates Toledo died of "unrelated causes." Appellant has not demonstrated that the State’s delay caused the death of Toledo. Furthermore, the twenty-month delay following his Texas arrest, which is the period subject to review, was largely caused by Appellant’s own motions to dismiss counsel. Appellant has failed to establish a nexus between the State's conduct and the witness's death. Therefore, Appellant has not shown that the delay impaired his defense in a manner sufficient to outweigh the other factors. The admission of Toledo’s statements presents a separate evidentiary issue regarding hearsay, which is distinct from the constitutional speedy trial claim.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Appellant has not established a violation of his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial. The length of the delay triggers the inquiry, but the reason for the delay is largely attributable to the Appellant, his assertion of the right was late, and he has failed to demonstrate specific prejudice caused by the State. Accordingly, the trial court did not err in denying the motion for a speedy trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on Appellant’s appeal challenging the trial court’s denial of his motion for a speedy trial. Appellant argues that his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial was violated due to a delay of over five years between the issuance of an arrest warrant and his trial. Specifically, Appellant contends the speedy trial clock began when the arrest warrant was issued in February 2003, and that the State’s failure to extradite him sooner constituted bad faith delay that prejudiced his defense by resulting in the unavailability of a key witness. The State counters that the clock did not begin until Appellant was taken into custody on the Texas warrant in December 2006, and that the subsequent delay was caused by Appellant’s own motions to dismiss counsel. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the record provided, the Court affirms the trial court’s judgment.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the accused the right to a speedy trial. *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514, 515, 92 S.Ct. 2182, 33 L.Ed.2d 101 (1972). This right is applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Id.* To determine whether a defendant's right has been violated, courts must engage in a balancing test of four factors: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant's assertion of his right; and (4) the prejudice to the defendant. *Id.* at 530, 92 S.Ct. at 2192. No single factor is dispositive, and the court must weigh the conduct of both the prosecution and the defendant. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Length of the Delay**

The threshold inquiry is whether the delay between accusation and trial is long enough to trigger a speedy trial analysis. Appellant argues the delay began in February 2003 when the arrest warrant was issued, resulting in a total delay of over five years. The State argues the delay began in December 2006 when Appellant was taken into custody, resulting in a delay of approximately twenty months.

The Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial attaches when a defendant becomes an "accused," which typically occurs at the initiation of formal judicial proceedings, such as an indictment, information, or arrest. *United States v. Marion*, 404 U.S. 307, 313, 92 S.Ct. 455, 460, 30 L.Ed.2d 468 (1971). However, the right to a speedy trial is primarily the right to a speedy trial, not merely a speedy accusation. Where a defendant is incarcerated on unrelated charges in another jurisdiction, the State's ability to bring him to trial is constrained.

The record indicates that Appellant was arrested in Ohio on unrelated charges in February 2003 and remained there until December 2006. While Appellant cites *United States v. Marion*, 404 U.S. 307, 313, 92 S.Ct. 455, 460, 30 L.Ed.2d 468 (1971), for the proposition that the clock starts at formal accusation, this does not control where the defendant is unavailable for trial due to incarceration elsewhere. The State cannot be held responsible for the delay incurred while Appellant was serving a sentence for unrelated crimes in Ohio. Consequently, the trial court correctly determined that the speedy trial clock began when Bexar County officials executed the arrest warrant and took Appellant into custody on December 5, 2006. The delay between this arrest and the trial in August 2008 was approximately twenty months. While a delay of this magnitude is sufficient to trigger a speedy trial inquiry, it is not presumptively prejudicial. *See Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647, 652 n.1, 112 S.Ct. 2686, 2690 n.1, 120 L.Ed.2d 520 (1992).

**B. Reason for the Delay**

The second factor requires an assessment of the reason for the delay. The record reveals that the significant portion of the delay following Appellant's arrest in December 2006 was attributable to Appellant himself. Appellant filed motions to dismiss his appointed counsel in February 2007, March 2007, and February 2008, leading to the appointment of multiple attorneys and the withdrawal of counsel. The trial court explicitly found that Appellant was "the primary cause of the delays."

Appellant argues that these motions were legitimate exercises of his right to effective assistance of counsel under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 104 S.Ct. 2052, 80 L.Ed.2d 674 (1984). While a defendant has a right to competent counsel, the right to a speedy trial is not suspended by the exercise of other rights. Delays caused by the defendant, even those undertaken for valid reasons, weigh against him in the *Barker* analysis. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 531, 92 S.Ct. at 2193.

Appellant also argues that the State engaged in "bad faith" by waiting to execute the warrant until after his release from an Ohio prison. However, as established above, the speedy trial clock did not run during the period of Appellant's incarceration in Ohio. The State's decision not to extradite him immediately, while perhaps resulting in a longer overall timeline, does not constitute a violation of the speedy trial right where the defendant was otherwise unavailable for trial on the Texas charges. Therefore, the reason for the delay during the relevant period (post-arrest) rests primarily with Appellant.

**C. Defendant’s Assertion of the Right**

Appellant filed his motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial in June 2008, approximately eighteen months after his arrest and five months before the trial. Appellant argues this assertion was timely given the instability of his legal representation. However, the right to a speedy trial must be asserted diligently. A failure to assert the right in a timely manner makes it difficult for a defendant to prove that he was denied a speedy trial. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 528-29, 92 S.Ct. at 2191-92. Appellant's assertion came late in the proceedings and only after he had contributed significantly to the delay through his own motions regarding counsel. This factor weighs against Appellant.

**D. Prejudice to the Defendant**

Appellant claims he was "severely" prejudiced because a key witness, Marco Toledo, died of unrelated causes before trial, and he was unable to cross-examine him. The *Barker* court identified three interests protected by the speedy trial right: (i) to prevent oppressive pretrial incarceration; (ii) to minimize anxiety and concern of the accused; and (iii) to limit the possibility that the defense will be impaired. *Barker*, 407 U.S. at 532, 92 S.Ct. at 2193.

Appellant argues that the State "profited" from the delay by introducing Toledo’s statements at trial. However, the record indicates Toledo died of "unrelated causes." Appellant has not demonstrated that the State’s delay caused the death of Toledo. Furthermore, the twenty-month delay following his Texas arrest, which is the period subject to review, was largely caused by Appellant’s own motions to dismiss counsel. Appellant has failed to establish a nexus between the State's conduct and the witness's death. Therefore, Appellant has not shown that the delay impaired his defense in a manner sufficient to outweigh the other factors. The admission of Toledo’s statements presents a separate evidentiary issue regarding hearsay, which is distinct from the constitutional speedy trial claim.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Appellant has not established a violation of his Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial. The length of the delay triggers the inquiry, but the reason for the delay is largely attributable to the Appellant, his assertion of the right was late, and he has failed to demonstrate specific prejudice caused by the State. Accordingly, the trial court did not err in denying the motion for a speedy trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
