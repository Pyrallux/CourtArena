FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1

The letters to the editor, on the other hand, are more opinionated. They do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt. Some are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race. We refuse, however, to find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society. But see Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. These few letters do not represent a society scornful of outsiders, as counsel Reardon would have us believe. Instead, by attempting to influence the public, these letters affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence.
One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

Your Honor, the defendants have failed to meet the stringent legal standard required to warrant a change of venue. Their motion rests on a mischaracterization of the record and a fundamental misunderstanding of the high threshold for establishing prejudice in a criminal trial. Under settled law, a change of venue is warranted only when pretrial publicity is so "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" that it creates an atmosphere where a fair trial is impossible—either through presumed prejudice or actual prejudice. Here, the defendants cite only six newspaper articles and editorials as evidence of prejudicial publicity, none of which the court deemed inflammatory. This falls far short of the exceptional circumstances required by precedent, and their cultural bias argument is unsupported by the record. For these reasons, the motion for change of venue was correctly denied, and this Court should affirm that ruling.

Presumed prejudice—the most stringent standard for establishing venue change—is "rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). It requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage" that renders the jury pool incapable of impartiality without needing voir dire. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). The Supreme Court has reversed convictions on this ground only once—in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963)—where a televised confession, recorded without counsel, was broadcast "several times to tens of thousands of people in the community," creating an "atmosphere" that made any proceeding "but a hollow formality." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727. Here, by contrast, the defendants cite merely six articles and editorials, none of which the court found inflammatory or sensationalized. The mere existence of opinionated letters to the editor—some "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race"—does not constitute the "exceptional" or "oversaturated" coverage required for presumed prejudice. As the court properly held, "we refuse... to find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society." The letters themselves "affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence," demonstrating that they reflect isolated views, not pervasive societal bias. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537 (rejecting similar claims where media coverage was not overwhelming). This case is lightyears removed from *Rideau* or other precedents where presumed prejudice was found; it is not "exceptional," and thus the burden remains unmet.

Actual prejudice—requiring "a showing that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured"—is equally inapplicable here. *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). Actual prejudice "is discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999). Yet no voir dire has occurred, making any such inquiry "premature." *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The defendants’ failure to even attempt this critical step—by not presenting juror responses or evidence of actual bias—dooms their claim. As the court noted, "if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors." The defendants cannot circumvent the foundational requirement of voir dire to prove actual prejudice; their motion is procedurally deficient.

The defendants offer no statistical data, expert testimony, or community surveys to support their sweeping assertion; instead, they rely on rhetorical characterizations that the court properly rejected. Due process requires more than speculation about bias—it demands concrete evidence of actual or presumed prejudice, which is entirely absent here.

Moreover, a change of venue is not a viable remedy in American Samoa. As this Court correctly recognized, the territory has a "small" population with "but one jury roll" encompassing all registered voters. *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (holding change of venue "unsuitable" in Puerto Rico due to "compact social structure" and "single jury pool"). Transferring the case within the territory would not alter the jury pool; it would merely "result in a physical change of venue" while leaving the "proceedings [the] same." *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981). A.S.C.A. § 46.0601—cited by the defendants—was enacted for "convenience of parties, witnesses or the court," such as holding court in Manu’a for local witnesses, not for "change of venue of the type sought." *Id.*; *Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) & (b)*. The statute does not contemplate venue transfer for juror bias; it is a logistical tool, not a remedy for prejudice. Thus, even if prejudice existed (which it does not), the defendants’ requested remedy is legally unavailable here.

Finally, the defendants ignore the Supreme Court’s core principle: "a fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process," but due process does not require a change of venue when the record lacks the necessary showing of prejudice. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). The court properly denied the motion, preserving the defendants’ right to a fair trial through alternative safeguards—such as a tailored voir dire questionnaire and individual questioning of jurors—without resorting to an impractical and legally unsupportable venue transfer. The defendants’ motion fails on all fronts: the publicity is not "massive, pervasive, or prejudicial," their cultural bias claim is speculative, and change of venue is not a feasible remedy in this jurisdiction. ## Introduction and Summary of Defense Position

The defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the prosecution's argument seeking affirmation of the trial court's denial of the defendants' motion for change of venue. The prosecution characterizes the pre-trial publicity as limited to "six newspaper articles and editorials." This characterization is incomplete and misleading. The trial court's order itself acknowledges not only the articles and editorials but also "letters to the editor" that "do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and are "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." The trial court's own recognition that some letters contained "matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" demonstrates that the publicity went beyond mere factual reporting and entered the realm of opinion that could prejudice potential jurors.

The prosecution argues that change of venue is not a viable remedy in American Samoa. The prosecution cites In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108 (1st Cir. 1981), for the proposition that change of venue is unsuitable in small jurisdictions. The trial court stated that the defendants "have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire." The court acknowledged this request but denied the motion outright without preserving the defendants' right to renew.

Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d at 362. If this is the correct standard, then it was error for the trial court to deny the motion without allowing the defendants the opportunity to examine juror responses.

The defense requests that this Court either (1) reverse the trial court's denial and grant the change of venue, or (2) reverse the trial court's denial and remand with instructions to allow the defendants to renew the motion after voir dire with the opportunity to present evidence of actual prejudice based on juror responses.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree and assault, argue that pre-trial publicity and cultural bias have tainted the jury pool to such an extent that a fair trial is impossible in American Samoa. The prosecution contends that the defendants failed to meet the high threshold required for a change of venue and that such a remedy is legally unavailable in the jurisdiction. Having reviewed the arguments of both parties and the underlying record, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**II. STANDARD FOR CHANGE OF VENUE**

A defendant’s right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is a fundamental requirement of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either presumed prejudice or actual prejudice. Presumed prejudice is a "rare" finding reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). It requires a showing of "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). Actual prejudice, conversely, requires a showing "that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured," which is generally discerned through the voir dire process. *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987); *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the cumulative effect of the publicity and the "compact social structure" of American Samoa creates an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. They rely on the trial court’s acknowledgment that letters to the editor contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race."

However, the standard for presumed prejudice is exacting. The Supreme Court has found such prejudice only in extreme circumstances, such as in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where a televised confession was broadcast multiple times to tens of thousands of people. Here, the record reflects only six newspaper articles and a limited number of opinionated letters. While the content of the letters is regrettable, the trial court correctly determined that they do not represent the beliefs of the entire society. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. The quantity and nature of the coverage fall far short of the "oversaturation" required to establish presumed prejudice. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272. The defense’s attempt to utilize the jurisdiction’s small size to amplify the impact of this limited publicity is insufficient to overcome the "exceptional" case threshold. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490.

**B. Actual Prejudice and Voir Dire**

The defense contends that the trial court erred by denying the motion outright without reserving judgment on the question of actual prejudice pending voir dire. The record indicates that no voir dire had occurred at the time of the motion. Under *Nevers*, actual prejudice is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers*, 169 F.3d at 362.

The trial court did not ignore the possibility of actual prejudice; rather, it found the inquiry premature. Furthermore, the court took proactive steps to safeguard the defendants' rights by ordering a detailed voir dire questionnaire and permitting individual questioning if necessary. This procedural mechanism is the appropriate method for identifying actual bias. The court’s denial of the motion for change of venue at this stage does not preclude the defendants from demonstrating actual bias during jury selection, at which point the court may employ other remedial measures. *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994).

**C. Availability of Change of Venue in American Samoa**

Even if the defendants had successfully demonstrated prejudice, the trial court correctly concluded that a change of venue is not a viable remedy in American Samoa. As recognized in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), a change of venue is unsuitable in jurisdictions with a "compact social structure" and a "single jury pool." American Samoa possesses exactly these characteristics; there is but one jury roll comprising all registered voters. Transferring the trial to another location within the Territory would result only in a physical change of venue, not a change in the jury pool. *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981).

The defendants’ reliance on A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 is misplaced. That statute authorizes the court to adjourn to a different location for the "convenience of parties, witnesses or the court," not to remedy prejudice. The trial court’s interpretation that the statute is a logistical tool, rather than a vehicle for changing the jury pool, is consistent with the statutory text and legislative history. Consequently, the trial court properly identified that alternative remedies, such as a rigorous voir dire or a bench trial, are the only available options to ensure due process.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defendants have not met the heavy burden of showing presumed prejudice, nor have they demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion for change of venue prior to voir dire. The trial court correctly identified that a change of venue is legally and logistically impracticable in American Samoa. The mechanisms put in place by the trial court, specifically the comprehensive voir dire questionnaire, are sufficient to address any potential actual prejudice.

Accordingly, the trial court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is **AFFIRMED**.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree and assault, appeal the denial of their motion to change venue. They argue that pre-trial publicity and cultural bias have tainted the jury pool, rendering a fair trial impossible in American Samoa. The prosecution opposes the appeal, arguing that the defendants failed to meet the high threshold for presumed prejudice and that a change of venue is legally unavailable. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both parties and the underlying record, the Court affirms the trial court’s order.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). A defendant’s right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is fundamental, but to warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either presumed prejudice or actual prejudice. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). Presumed prejudice is a "rare" finding reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272. It requires a showing of "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court erred in finding that the pre-trial publicity did not create an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. They contend that in the context of American Samoa’s small community with limited media outlets, six newspaper articles and opinionated letters to the editor constitute "oversaturation." The defense further argues that the "embarrassingly slanted" nature of the letters, which the trial court acknowledged were "slanted against the defendants on account of their race," demonstrates a level of prejudice that warrants intervention.

While the defense correctly notes that the impact of media coverage must be assessed relative to the community size, the legal standard for presumed prejudice remains exacting. The Supreme Court has found such prejudice only in extreme circumstances, such as in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where a televised confession was broadcast multiple times to tens of thousands of people. The defendants here rely on six articles and a limited number of letters. As the prosecution correctly notes, this quantity and nature of coverage fall far short of the "massive, pervasive" publicity required to establish presumed prejudice. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726.

The trial court found that the letters, while containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt," were "few" and did not represent the beliefs of the entire society. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. The defense’s argument that these letters indicate a broader "editorial tolerance for prejudicial content" or a "society scornful of outsiders" is speculative. The trial court’s determination that the letters represent a "very limited section of society" is a factual finding supported by the record. The defense has not provided evidence, such as community surveys or widespread demonstrations of bias, to overcome the high burden of showing presumed prejudice. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272.

**B. Actual Prejudice and Voir Dire**

The defense argues that the trial court’s reliance on voir dire to uncover actual prejudice is misplaced due to the "compact social structure" of American Samoa, where jurors may be reluctant to admit bias to neighbors. The defense also contends that the limitations placed on the voir dire questionnaire—restricting answers to "yes or no" and prohibiting questions on substantive arguments—will inadequately probe for bias.

These arguments are premature. Actual prejudice is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999). Because voir dire has not yet occurred, the defense cannot establish that an impartial jury cannot be secured. *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987).

The trial court took significant steps to address the defense’s concerns by ordering a bilingual voir dire questionnaire and permitting individual questioning if necessary. While the defense argues the questionnaire is too restrictive, the trial court has broad discretion in managing the voir dire process. The defense’s speculation that jurors will lie or that the questionnaire will fail is insufficient to prove actual prejudice at this juncture. The trial court’s approach allows for the identification of bias should it exist, and the defense may renew its motion if the voir dire process reveals that a fair jury cannot be seated. *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994).

**C. Availability of Change of Venue in American Samoa**

The defense argues that the trial court erred in concluding that a change of venue is unavailable. They suggest that because an in-territory transfer would not change the jury pool, the court should consider transferring the case to a federal court on the mainland United States.

This argument is without merit under the provided facts and statutory framework. The trial court correctly identified that American Samoa has a "compact social structure" and a "single jury roll." *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987); *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981). Transferring the case to another location within the Territory would not change the jury pool, as all registered voters are on the same roll.

The defense’s proposal to transfer the case to the mainland is not supported by the statutory authority cited, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601. That statute authorizes the court to adjourn to "any appropriate place in American Samoa" for the convenience of the parties or witnesses. It does not authorize transferring a territorial criminal case to a federal jurisdiction outside of American Samoa. The trial court’s interpretation that the statute is a logistical tool, rather than a vehicle for changing the venue to a different sovereign jurisdiction, is consistent with the text and the history of the enactment. Consequently, the trial court properly identified that alternative remedies, such as a rigorous voir dire or a bench trial, are the only available options to ensure due process. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defendants have not met the heavy burden of showing presumed prejudice, nor have they demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion for change of venue prior to voir dire. The trial court correctly identified that a change of venue is legally and logistically impracticable in American Samoa. The mechanisms put in place by the trial court, specifically the comprehensive voir dire questionnaire and the allowance for individual questioning, are sufficient to address any potential actual prejudice.

Accordingly, the trial court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is **AFFIRMED**.

However, this analysis ignores critical facts: (1) American Samoa has a population of approximately 55,000 people with a single newspaper and limited media outlets, making any coverage far more impactful than equivalent coverage in a mainland jurisdiction; (2) the trial court itself acknowledged that some letters were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race"; and (3) the prosecution's own cited precedent in *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985), recognized that the community context must be considered when assessing the impact of pre-trial publicity.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S CHARACTERIZATION OF PRE-TRIAL PUBLICITY

### A. The Quantity and Nature of Coverage Must Be Assessed in Context

The prosecution argues that six articles cannot constitute "oversaturation." This argument fails to account for the unique circumstances of American Samoa. In *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), the Eleventh Circuit emphasized that the impact of pre-trial publicity must be assessed relative to the community in which it occurs. In a territory of approximately 55,000 people with a single major newspaper, six articles representing sustained coverage over the relevant time period constitutes significant exposure.

The prosecution's reliance on *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), is misplaced. While *Rideau* involved a televised confession broadcast "several times to tens of thousands of people," the fundamental principle is that presumed prejudice arises when the community becomes so saturated with inflammatory material that the ability to obtain an impartial jury is inherently compromised. The prosecution asks this Court to apply a numerical threshold divorced from community context. This approach contradicts the reasoning in *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1490, which explicitly rejected such a rigid application of the standard.

### B. The Letters to the Editor Demonstrate Prejudicial Community Atmosphere

The prosecution attempts to dismiss the letters to the editor as representing "a very limited section of society." However, the trial court's own findings acknowledge that these letters contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "slanted against the defendants on account of their race." The trial court further noted that these letters were published by a newspaper in a community where media outlets constitute "virtual monopolies."

In *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537, the Eleventh Circuit conducted an extensive analysis of community prejudice and held that editorial tolerance for prejudicial content can be indicative of broader community bias. The prosecution's argument that isolated letters cannot establish community-wide prejudice ignores the reality of media influence in small communities. When a newspaper—a monopoly source of information—consistently publishes content that presumes guilt based on race, this creates an atmosphere that pervades the entire community's perception of the defendants.

The prosecution cites *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966), for the proposition that "every critical letter or editorial" does not automatically taint the community. However, *Sheppard* involved a situation where the trial court had implemented substantial remedial measures. Here, no such measures have been implemented, and the prosecution asks this Court to simply accept that the community will somehow remain impartial despite documented evidence of racial bias in published materials.

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING ACTUAL PREJUDICE AND VOIR DIRE

### A. The Prosecution's Reliance on Voir Dire Is Misplaced

The prosecution argues that the defense cannot establish actual prejudice without first conducting voir dire. This argument ignores the reality of American Samoa's "compact social structure," which the trial court itself acknowledged.

In *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999), the Sixth Circuit held that actual prejudice is discerned by reviewing "both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." However, the prosecution overlooks a critical component of this analysis: the nature of the publicity. Here, the publicity has explicitly racial overtones, and in a small community where "all the registered voters on the island are part of that roll," prospective jurors are likely to have direct connections to the parties, witnesses, and community members who have expressed public opinions about the case.

The prosecution speculates that "the trial court has taken appropriate steps" by ordering a bilingual questionnaire. However, the trial court explicitly limited the questionnaire to "yes or no" responses and prohibited questions on "substantive arguments of the case." These restrictions severely undermine the ability to uncover actual prejudice. A juror who has been exposed to prejudicial material but wishes to conceal that bias can easily answer "no" to a binary question about media exposure. The prosecution's argument that the defense's concerns are "speculative" ignores the documented reality that:

1. The community is small, with all registered voters comprising the jury pool;
2. The media landscape is dominated by a single newspaper that published prejudicial content;
3. The social structure is "compact," meaning potential jurors are likely connected to those who expressed public opinions.

### B. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Burden

The prosecution argues that the defense bears the burden of showing that "an impartial jury cannot be seated." However, this misstates the applicable standard. In *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961), the Supreme Court held that actual prejudice requires a showing "that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." This is a forward-looking assessment based on the totality of circumstances, not a requirement that the defense prove impossibility with certainty.

In American Samoa, where the entire voting population constitutes the jury pool and where the media has published racially biased content, the defense has established circumstances that make it "reasonably likely" that an impartial jury cannot be secured. The prosecution's argument to the contrary asks the defense to prove a negative—essentially requiring demonstration that every potential juror is biased—before any voir dire has occurred. This places an impossible burden on the defense and ignores the prophylactic purpose of the change of venue remedy.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING AVAILABILITY OF CHANGE OF VENUE

### A. The Prosecution Incorrectly Concludes That Change of Venue Is Unavailable

The prosecution argues that change of venue is "legally and logistically impracticable" in American Samoa. This argument contradicts the fundamental principle that the right to a fair trial is constitutionally guaranteed and must be protected through available remedies.

In *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), the First Circuit recognized that a change of venue "is unsuitable" in jurisdictions with compact social structures. However, the prosecution misreads this precedent. The First Circuit did not hold that change of venue is prohibited; it merely acknowledged the practical difficulty. The court explicitly stated that "alternative remedies appropriate" must be explored. *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981).

The prosecution's argument that the defendants' proposal to transfer to the mainland is "not supported by the statutory authority cited" misses the point. The defense has argued throughout that the trial court should exercise its inherent authority to ensure due process. In *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977), the court found that jury trials in American Samoa are "practical and not anomalous." However, this finding was made in the context of establishing the right to a jury trial, not in limiting the remedies available when that right is threatened.

### B. The Prosecution's Interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 Is Too Narrow

The prosecution argues that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 authorizes only physical relocation within American Samoa for convenience. However, this statute must be read in conjunction with the constitutional guarantee of due process. The trial court acknowledged that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives." This recognition that alternatives exist undermines the prosecution's argument that no remedy is available.

The prosecution cites *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), for the proposition that the Constitution guarantees "a fair trial—not a perfect one." However, this principle supports the defense's position, not the prosecution's. The question is whether the current circumstances permit a fair trial. Given the documented pre-trial publicity, the acknowledged racial bias in published materials, and the unique challenges of the jury pool in American Samoa, the defense has established that the current venue cannot assure a fair trial.

---

## V. THE DEFENSE HAS ESTABLISHED PRESUMED PREJUDICE

The combined circumstances of this case establish presumed prejudice. The factors supporting this finding include:

1. **Extent of Publicity**: Six newspaper articles in a community with a single newspaper and approximately 55,000 people represents significant saturation.

2. **Nature of Publicity**: The trial court acknowledged that letters to the editor contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "slanted against the defendants on account of their race."

3. **Community Size and Structure**: American Samoa has a single jury roll comprising all registered voters, making it impossible to escape the reach of prejudicial publicity.

4. **Media Monopoly**: The territory's "virtual monopolies" in media ensure that the published content reaches the entire potential jury pool.

5. **Racial Dimension**: The bias is not merely against the defendants as individuals but is explicitly based on their national origin and race, as acknowledged by the trial court.

In *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1490, the Eleventh Circuit held that presumed prejudice requires a showing of "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." While the coverage here may not be "sensationalized" in the traditional sense, it is explicitly prejudicial based on race, and in a community of this size, any coverage has the potential to saturate the entire population.

## VI. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm the denial of the change of venue motion based on a rigid application of standards developed for mainland jurisdictions with larger populations and multiple media sources. This approach ignores the unique circumstances of American Samoa and effectively nullifies the defendants' constitutional right to an impartial jury.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **Reverse** the trial court's Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue;

2. **Grant** the defendants' motion for change of venue to a jurisdiction outside of American Samoa where the jury pool has not been exposed to the prejudicial pre-trial publicity;

3. **Alternatively**, if this Court finds that transfer to the mainland is not available, order that the trial court implement enhanced voir dire procedures including:

   a. Individual voir dire of all prospective jurors outside the presence of other panel members;

   b. Expansion of the questionnaire to allow open-ended responses regarding media exposure and bias;

   c. Disclosure of jury information sheets to allow defense counsel to identify potential connections to parties or witnesses;

   d. A change of venue to a different island within American Samoa, if such a transfer would provide any meaningful difference in the jury pool.

The fundamental principle underlying the defendants' motion is simple: the right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is a cornerstone of due process. When the circumstances of a community make it impossible to secure an impartial jury, the Constitution requires intervention. The prosecution's argument that the defendants must await the voir dire process before raising this issue puts the cart before the horse: if the community is already tainted, voir dire cannot undo that taint.

The defense has met the heavy burden of showing presumed prejudice. The trial court erred in denying the motion, and this Court should reverse that decision.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree and assault, appeal the denial of their motion. They argue that pre-trial publicity and racial bias have tainted the jury pool to such an extent that a fair trial is impossible in American Samoa. The defendants further contend that the trial court’s proposed voir dire procedures are insufficient to uncover this bias and that a change of venue to the mainland United States is necessary to protect their due process rights. The prosecution opposes the appeal, arguing that the defendants failed to meet the high threshold for presumed prejudice and that a change of venue is legally unavailable under American Samoa law. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties and the underlying record, the Court affirms the trial court’s order.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). A defendant’s right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is fundamental, but to warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either presumed prejudice or actual prejudice. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). Presumed prejudice is a "rare" finding reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272. It requires a showing of "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court erred in finding that the pre-trial publicity did not create an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. They contend that in the context of American Samoa’s small community with limited media outlets, six newspaper articles and opinionated letters to the editor constitute "oversaturation." The defense emphasizes the "embarrassingly slanted" nature of the letters, which the trial court acknowledged were "slanted against the defendants on account of their race."

While the defense correctly notes that the impact of media coverage must be assessed relative to the community size, the legal standard for presumed prejudice remains exacting. The Supreme Court has found such prejudice only in extreme circumstances, such as in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where a televised confession was broadcast multiple times to tens of thousands of people. The defendants here rely on six articles and a limited number of letters. As the prosecution correctly notes, this quantity and nature of coverage fall far short of the "massive, pervasive" publicity required to establish presumed prejudice. *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726.

The trial court found that the letters, while containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt," were "few" and did not represent the beliefs of the entire society. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. The defense’s argument that these letters indicate a broader "society scornful of outsiders" is speculative. The trial court’s determination that the letters represent a "very limited section of society" is a factual finding supported by the record. The defense has not provided evidence, such as community surveys or widespread demonstrations of bias, to overcome the high burden of showing presumed prejudice. *Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 272. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the "oversaturation" standard articulated in *Coleman* requires more than the factual record here supports.

**B. Actual Prejudice and Voir Dire**

The defense argues that the trial court’s reliance on voir dire to uncover actual prejudice is misplaced due to the "compact social structure" of American Samoa, where jurors may be reluctant to admit bias to neighbors. The defense also contends that the limitations placed on the voir dire questionnaire—restricting answers to "yes or no" and prohibiting questions on substantive arguments—will inadequately probe for bias.

These arguments are premature. Actual prejudice is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999). Because voir dire has not yet occurred, the defense cannot establish that an impartial jury cannot be secured. *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987).

The trial court took significant steps to address the defense’s concerns by ordering a bilingual voir dire questionnaire and permitting individual questioning if necessary. While the defense argues the questionnaire is too restrictive, the trial court has broad discretion in managing the voir dire process. The defense’s speculation that jurors will lie or that the questionnaire will fail is insufficient to prove actual prejudice at this juncture. The trial court’s approach allows for the identification of bias should it exist, and the defense may renew its motion if the voir dire process reveals that a fair jury cannot be seated. *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The defense’s request for enhanced voir dire procedures, while noted, is a matter of trial court discretion and does not warrant appellate intervention at this stage.

**C. Availability of Change of Venue in American Samoa**

The defense argues that the trial court erred in concluding that a change of venue is unavailable. They suggest that because an in-territory transfer would not change the jury pool, the court should consider transferring the case to a federal court on the mainland United States.

This argument is without merit under the provided facts and statutory framework. The trial court correctly identified that American Samoa has a "compact social structure" and a "single jury roll." *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987); *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981). Transferring the case to another location within the Territory would not change the jury pool, as all registered voters are on the same roll.

The defense’s proposal to transfer the case to the mainland is not supported by the statutory authority cited, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601. That statute authorizes the court to adjourn to "any appropriate place in American Samoa" for the convenience of the parties or witnesses. It does not authorize transferring a territorial criminal case to a federal jurisdiction outside of American Samoa. The trial court’s interpretation that the statute is a logistical tool, rather than a vehicle for changing the venue to a different sovereign jurisdiction, is consistent with the text and the history of the enactment. The defense’s reliance on the court’s "inherent authority" to override this statutory limitation is unpersuasive. Consequently, the trial court properly identified that alternative remedies, such as a rigorous voir dire or a bench trial, are the only available options to ensure due process. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defendants have not met the heavy burden of showing presumed prejudice, nor have they demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion for change of venue prior to voir dire. The trial court correctly identified that a change of venue is legally and logistically impracticable in American Samoa. The mechanisms put in place by the trial court, specifically the comprehensive voir dire questionnaire and the allowance for individual questioning, are sufficient to address any potential actual prejudice. The Constitution guarantees a fair trial, not a perfect one, and the trial court’s order ensures that the defendants' rights are protected within the bounds of the law. *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975).

Accordingly, the trial court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is **AFFIRMED**.
