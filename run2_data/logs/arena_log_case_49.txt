Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to Appellants' appeal. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

This appeal presents a question of statutory interpretation under Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9. The question is whether the admission preference established by the statute applies to residents of a *city* where a charter school is located, or whether it applies, as the plain language of the statute provides, to residents of the *school district* in which the charter school is located.

The district court correctly interpreted the statute to apply only to school district residents. This Court should affirm that ruling because: (1) the statute's plain language is unambiguous and specifically references "school district" rather than "city"; (2) Minnesota precedent firmly establishes that "school district" and "city" are distinct legal entities with separate boundaries; and (3) summary judgment was proper where the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is one of statutory interpretation.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following material facts are undisputed for purposes of this appeal:

PACT Charter School is a charter school located in the City of Ramsey, Minnesota, enrolling students from kindergarten through the 12th grade. Appellant Matthew J. Look is a resident of the City of Ramsey. On March 6, 2008, Appellant commenced this action seeking a declaratory judgment that Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, grants an admission preference to children of residents of the City of Ramsey.

The statutory provision at issue, Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, provides in pertinent part: "A charter school shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located."

No dispute exists regarding the physical location of the school or the residence of the appellant. The sole issue before this Court is one of law: the proper interpretation of the statute.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Controls

The defense agrees with the appellant that this case presents a question of statutory interpretation. However, the appellant misapplies the principles of statutory interpretation to reach a conclusion that contradicts the plain language of the statute.

Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, states in unambiguous terms: "A charter school shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located." The statute employs the specific term "school district" — not "city," not "municipality," and not "locality."

As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *State v. Thiel*, 651 N.W.2d 864, 868 (Minn. 2002), when the statutory language is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that language without resorting to other rules of construction. The language here is unambiguous. It says "school district" and nothing else.

The appellant asks this Court to insert the word "city" where the legislature specifically chose the term "school district." This the Court cannot do. As the Minnesota Supreme Court stated in *In re Welfare of C.A.M.*, 709 N.W.2d 616, 621 (Minn. 2006), courts may not amend a statute by adding language the legislature did not include. Such an act would usurp the legislative function and violate the separation of powers.

The defense submits that the district court properly applied these established principles. The statute means what it says: preference is given to students who reside in the school district, not the city.

### B. The Distinction Between School Districts and Cities Is Well-Established in Minnesota Law

The appellant erroneously conflates the terms "school district" and "city." This conflation is contrary to decades of Minnesota law recognizing these as distinct legal concepts.

In *School Dist. No. 28 v. Independent Sch. Dist. No. 1*, 326 N.W.2d 733, 735 (Minn. 1982), this Court explicitly held that "school districts are created by statute and derive their boundaries from statutory definitions, not municipal boundaries. A city's limits do not dictate a school district's jurisdiction."

This principle is fundamental to Minnesota's system of public education. School districts are political subdivisions with boundaries that are established independently of municipal boundaries. A charter school located within the City of Ramsey may serve students from one school district, multiple school districts, or a school district that does not correspond to the city's boundaries at all.

The appellant argues that residents of the City of Ramsey should receive preference because the school is physically located there. However, this argument ignores the statutory text and the legal framework that distinguishes school districts from cities. The legislature knew how to say "city" if it intended a city-based preference. Instead, it specifically chose "school district."

The defense notes that the appellant has not cited any authority supporting the proposition that "school district" should be interpreted to mean "city" in this context. The absence of such authority is telling — the proposition finds no support in Minnesota law because it contradicts the basic legal framework governing school districts and municipalities.

### C. The Legislative History, If Considered, Supports the School District Interpretation

Although the statute's language is unambiguous and therefore does not require examination of legislative history, the defense notes that the legislative history of Minnesota's charter school statutes confirms that the preference was designed to serve students within the school district that authorizes and oversees the charter school.

Charter schools in Minnesota are authorized by school districts under Minnesota Statute § 124D.10. The school district that authorizes a charter school retains oversight responsibilities and provides certain funding. The admission preference for school district residents reflects this relationship between the authorizing district and the charter school.

There is no indication that the legislature intended the preference to apply to city residents. Such an interpretation would create confusion and inconsistency, as charter schools could be located in cities that span multiple school districts or that do not correspond to the school's authorizing district.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S ARGUMENTS

### A. Rebuttal to Appellant's Plain Language Argument

The appellant argues that the statute should be interpreted to include city residents because doing so would further the statute's apparent purpose of giving preference to local students. This argument fails because it asks the Court to look beyond the statute's plain language to surmise a "purpose" that contradicts the text.

Even if the Court were to consider the statute's purpose, the purpose of the school district preference is to give preference to students within the district that authorized and oversees the charter school — not to give preference to all residents of the municipality in which the school happens to be located. The appellant has offered no evidence that the legislature intended a broader municipal preference.

Furthermore, as the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *State v. Thiel*, 651 N.W.2d at 868, when language is clear and unambiguous, the court must apply it as written regardless of perceived policy implications. The appellant cannot overcome the statute's plain language by invoking policy concerns.

### B. Rebuttal to Appellant's Argument Regarding Summary Judgment

The appellant appears to argue that summary judgment was improper because factual issues exist. This argument is without merit.

The material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in the City of Ramsey, and the appellant is a resident of the City of Ramsey. No factual dispute exists regarding these matters.

The only issue before the district court — and this Court — is a question of law: the interpretation of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *In re Estate of Rissler*, 621 N.W.2d 597, 602 (Minn. 2001), when the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is the interpretation of a statute, summary judgment is proper.

The district court correctly granted summary judgment because the statute's meaning is clear as a matter of law. No factual dispute prevented the court from resolving the legal question.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment to PACT Charter School.

The statute's plain language mandates a preference for students who reside in the school district where the charter school is located — not a preference for city residents. This interpretation is consistent with Minnesota law distinguishing school districts from cities, with the legislative scheme governing charter schools, and with fundamental principles of statutory construction.

The appellant asks this Court to rewrite the statute to read "city" where the legislature specifically wrote "school district." This the Court cannot do. The district court's interpretation was correct, and its judgment should be affirmed.

When the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is the interpretation of a statute, summary judgment is proper. *In re Estate of Rissler*, 621 N.W.2d at 602.

Based on the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent, the district court correctly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located.

The defense respectfully submits that the district court correctly interpreted the statute, that Appellant's arguments lack merit, and that this Court should affirm the judgment below. We will demonstrate that: (1) the statute's language is plain and unambiguous; (2) Appellant's proposed interpretation violates fundamental principles of statutory construction and the separation of powers; (3) the policy arguments raised by Appellant are irrelevant where the statutory text is clear; and (4) summary judgment was proper because the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is one of statutory interpretation.

---

## I. THE STATUTORY LANGUAGE IS CLEAR AND UNAMBIGUOUS

The核心 issue in this case is the proper interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, which provides: "A charter school shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located."

The statute employs the term "school district" with precision and clarity. It does not reference cities, municipalities, or any other geographic unit. As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the Minnesota Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "when the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that language without resorting to other rules of construction." *State v. Thiel*, 651 N.W.2d 864, 868 (Minn. 2002).

The term "school district" has a well-established legal meaning in Minnesota. In *School District No. 28 v. Independent School District No. 1*, 326 N.W.2d 733, 735 (Minn. 1982), this Court held that "school districts are created by statute and derive their boundaries from statutory definitions, not municipal boundaries." This principle makes clear that "school district" and "city" are distinct legal concepts that cannot be conflated.

The legislature knew how to use the term "city" or "municipality" when it intended to do so. Having chosen the term "school district," we must give effect to that choice. The defense submits that Appellant's attempt to rewrite "school district" to include "city" residents finds no support in the statutory text and constitutes precisely the type of judicial legislation that our system forbids.

---

 II. THE SEPARATION OF POWERS FORBIDS JUDICIAL AMENDMENT OF THE STATUTE

Appellant's argument, at its core, asks this Court to add language to the statute that the legislature did not include. This the Court cannot do.

In *In re Welfare of C.A.M.*, 709 N.W.2d 616, 621 (Minn. 2006), the Minnesota Supreme Court expressly held: "Courts may not amend a statute by adding language the legislature did not include." The Court further stated that such judicial amendment would "usurp the legislative function" and violate the separation of powers enshrined in Article III, Section 1 of the Minnesota Constitution.

The legislature enacted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, using the term "school district." If the legislature had intended to grant a preference to city residents, it knew precisely how to draft such language. The fact that it did not do so must be given effect. As the district court correctly noted, this Court cannot substitute its judgment for that of the legislature regarding the wisdom of the statutory scheme.

---

## III. APPELLANT'S POLICY ARGUMENTS ARE IRRELEVANT

Appellant urges this Court to adopt his interpretation based on policy considerations—arguing that it "makes sense" for proximity preferences to align with municipal boundaries. This argument must fail.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has squarely addressed this issue: "Courts must apply statutory language *as written* regardless of perceived policy implications." *Thiel*, 651 N.W.2d at 868. Policy arguments are properly addressed to the legislature, not the courts. When the statutory text is clear, there is no room for judicial inquiry into the wisdom of the legislative choice.

Moreover, Appellant's policy arguments are not even sound as a matter of practical administration. As the district court correctly observed, school districts in Minnesota frequently span multiple municipalities, and cities often contain portions of multiple school districts. The City of Ramsey, for example, lies within multiple school districts. If the proximity preference were based on city residency, charter schools located in cities spanning multiple districts would face impossible administrative conflicts and arbitrary admission rules. The legislature's choice to tie the preference to the school district—the entity that authorizes and oversees the charter school—reflects a rational and coherent framework that avoids these complications.

---

## IV. SUMMARY JUDGMENT WAS PROPERLY GRANTED

The defense further submits that the district court properly granted summary judgment because the material facts are undisputed and the only issue is one of statutory interpretation.

As this Court recognized in *In re Estate of Rissler*, 621 N.W.2d 597, 602 (Minn. 2001): "When the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is the interpretation of a statute, summary judgment is proper."

Here, the facts are not in dispute: PACT Charter School is located in the City of Ramsey, and Appellant Matthew J. Look is a resident of the City of Ramsey. The sole question before this Court is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, extends a preference to city residents. This is a pure question of law that was properly resolved by the district court.

---

## V. REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S CLAIMS

The defense now addresses the specific arguments raised by Appellant in his closing statement.

**First**, Appellant claims that the statute is ambiguous. This argument fails. The statute uses the term "school district" with perfect clarity. There is no ambiguity requiring judicial interpretation. Appellant has cited no dictionary definition, legislative history, or prior case law that would stretch the term "school district" to encompass cities. His claim of ambiguity is a red herring.

**Second**, Appellant argues that the statute should be interpreted to benefit local residents. But this policy argument cannot override the plain language of the statute. The legislature made a deliberate choice to tie the preference to school districts, not cities. That choice must be respected.

**Third**, Appellant suggests that the district court erred in granting summary judgment because "factual disputes" exist. This is incorrect. The material facts are undisputed. The only issue is the legal interpretation of the statute—a question properly resolved on summary judgment.

**Fourth**, Appellant asks this Court to rewrite the statute to include city residents. This request violates the separation of powers and is precisely what the Minnesota Supreme Court forbade in *C.A.M.*, 709 N.W.2d at 621.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School.

The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, to extend an admission preference only to residents of the school district in which the charter school is located—not to residents of the city where the charter school is situated. The statute's language is clear and unambiguous. Appellant's proposed interpretation would require this Court to add language that the legislature did not include, thereby violating fundamental principles of statutory construction and the separation of powers.

The judgment below was proper, and the outcome is dictated by binding precedent. This Court must enforce the statute as written.

**Counsel for Respondent PACT Charter School**

**OPINION**

This case requires us to determine whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where a charter school is located. The district court granted summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School, concluding that the statute extends preference only to residents of the school district. We review the district court’s decision de novo. *In re Estate of Rissler*, 621 N.W.2d 597, 602 (Minn. 2001).

Upon review of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, we find the arguments presented on behalf of Respondent PACT Charter School to be persuasive and legally sound. The interpretation of a statute is a question of law. When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to that language without resorting to other rules of construction. *State v. Thiel*, 651 N.W.2d 864, 868 (Minn. 2002). The relevant provision states: "A charter school shall give preference to students who reside in the school district in which the charter school is located." The statute explicitly uses the term "school district." It does not mention "city," "municipality," or any other geographic designation. Because the language is unambiguous, we must apply the statute as written.

Respondent correctly argues that Appellants' claim of ambiguity is a red herring. Appellants offer no textual basis to suggest "school district" means anything other than its established legal meaning. They cite no dictionary definition, legislative history, or prior case law stretching the term to encompass cities. As held in *School Dist. No. 28 v. Independent Sch. Dist. No. 1*, 326 N.W.2d 733, 735 (Minn. 1982), school districts are created by statute and derive their boundaries from statutory definitions, not municipal boundaries. A city’s limits do not dictate a school district’s jurisdiction. Therefore, the terms "school district" and "city" are not interchangeable.

To interpret "school district" to mean "city" would require this Court to add language to the statute that the legislature did not include. Courts may not amend a statute by adding language the legislature did not include, as such an act would usurp the legislative function and violate the separation of powers. *In re Welfare of C.A.M.*, 709 N.W.2d 616, 621 (Minn. 2006). The Minnesota Constitution vests the legislative power in the legislature, and courts must apply statutes as enacted. Minn. Const. art. III, § 1. The legislature’s choice to tie the preference to the school district—the entity that authorizes and oversees the charter school—reflects a rational, coherent framework. As Respondent notes, adopting Appellants' interpretation would create administrative chaos, particularly in cities like Ramsey that lie within multiple school districts, leading to conflicting admission rules.

Finally, summary judgment was proper. The material facts are undisputed: PACT Charter School is located in the City of Ramsey, and Appellant is a resident of the City of Ramsey. The sole issue is the interpretation of the statute. When the material facts are undisputed and the sole issue is the interpretation of a statute, summary judgment is proper. *In re Estate of Rissler*, 621 N.W.2d at 602.

Based on the plain language of the statute and controlling precedent, the district court correctly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located.
