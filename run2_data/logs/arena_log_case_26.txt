**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard’s appeal from the recommendation of the Review Department of the State Bar Court. The Review Department recommended a one-year actual suspension, the minimum penalty under Standard 2.2(a) for wilful misappropriation of entrusted funds. The hearing referee recommended a three-month actual suspension. In the preliminary ruling, this Court determined that a six-month actual suspension was the appropriate discipline. Both the prosecution and the defense have submitted closing arguments urging the Court to adopt their respective positions. The prosecution argues for the affirmation of the six-month suspension, while the defense urges a reduction to three months. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, the record, and the applicable precedent, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Standard for Wilful Misappropriation and Departure Therefrom**

The Standards for Attorney Sanctions for Professional Misconduct prescribe a one-year actual suspension as the minimum penalty for wilful misappropriation of entrusted funds. (Std. 2.2(a).) However, this Court retains the discretion to depart from this standard where compelling mitigation exists. As established in *In re Sutter*, 52 Cal. 3d 100 (Cal. 1990), a rigid application of the minimum standard is inappropriate where the evidence demonstrates that the attorney's conduct was an aberration and that the attorney has successfully addressed the underlying causes of the misconduct.

**B. Analysis of Precedent Regarding Mitigated Sanctions**

The defense and prosecution disagree on the appropriate baseline for a mitigated sanction for wilful misappropriation. The defense relies heavily on *In re Rose*, 12 Cal. 4th 1045 (Cal. 1996), to argue that Petitioner’s mitigation—specifically her two-and-one-half years of sobriety—distinguishes her case and warrants a sanction lower than that imposed in *Rose*. In *In re Rose*, the Court reduced the sanction to six months where the attorney had maintained sobriety for only one year. The defense posits that because Petitioner’s sobriety is more than double that of the attorney in *Rose*, a further reduction to three months is logically compelled.

The Court finds this argument unpersuasive. While *In re Rose* establishes that six months is an appropriate departure from the one-year minimum where compelling mitigation exists, it does not establish a sliding scale where the duration of sobriety automatically reduces the sanction below six months. To the contrary, *In re Rose* and *In re Lax*, 53 Cal. 3d 212 (Cal. 1991), establish six months as the floor for mitigated misappropriation cases. In *In re Lax*, the Court approved a six-month suspension and explicitly stated that the gravity of misappropriation lies not in the dollar value but in the fundamental betrayal of trust inherent in the attorney-client relationship. *In re Lax*, 53 Cal. 3d 212 (Cal. 1991).

The prosecution correctly points out that there is no authority where a court reduced a sanction for wilful misappropriation to three months. The defense’s request for a three-month suspension relies entirely on a "logical implication" derived from the facts of *In re Rose*, rather than on binding precedent. This Court is constrained by the disciplinary framework established by prior decisions. The absence of any case supporting a three-month suspension for this type of misconduct indicates that such a sanction would be inconsistent with the standards governing the profession.

**C. Evaluation of Mitigating Factors**

The Court acknowledges the strength of Petitioner’s mitigation. Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum of $1,300. She made full restitution. She has presented compelling evidence of rehabilitation, including two-and-one-half years of sobriety at the time of the hearing and active participation in Alcoholics Anonymous and The Other Bar. The referee found her evidence compelling and credited expert testimony regarding her high likelihood of continued recovery.

These factors are significant and justify a departure from the one-year minimum prescribed by Standard 2.2(a). They distinguish this case from those involving disbarment or longer suspensions. However, the question is whether these factors justify a departure below the six-month benchmark established by *In re Rose* and *In re Lax*. The Court finds they do not. The distinction between one year of sobriety and two-and-one-half years of sobriety, while noteworthy, does not override the precedent setting six months as the appropriate sanction for mitigated misappropriation. The protection of the public and the integrity of the profession require that the sanction for misappropriation, even when mitigated by addiction and recovery, remain within the bounds established by controlling authority.

**D. Weight of the Referee’s Recommendation**

The defense urges the Court to give substantial weight to the referee’s recommendation of three months. The Court respects the referee’s ability to assess credibility and the compelling nature of Petitioner’s rehabilitation evidence. However, the determination of appropriate discipline is a legal conclusion subject to independent review. While the referee’s recommendation is informative, it is not binding on this Court, particularly where it conflicts with the disciplinary framework established by the Review Department and appellate precedent. The fact that two members of the Review Department dissented in favor of the referee’s recommendation indicates that the issue is close, but it does not compel a departure from the six-month standard established in analogous cases.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Petitioner has presented compelling evidence of rehabilitation, and her sustained sobriety is a significant mitigating factor. However, the Court finds that these factors do not justify a reduction to three months, a sanction for which there is no supporting precedent in cases of wilful misappropriation. The six-month actual suspension strikes the appropriate balance between acknowledging Petitioner’s recovery and maintaining the standards of the profession. It aligns with the precedent set forth in *In re Rose*, 12 Cal. 4th 1045 (Cal. 1996), and *In re Lax*, 53 Cal. 3d 212 (Cal. 1991).

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling is affirmed. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months. This suspension shall be actual.
