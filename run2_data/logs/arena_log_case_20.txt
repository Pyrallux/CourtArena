# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

### STATE OF WASHINGTON

---

**Respondents/Cross-Appellants:** John George and Tommy George

**Cross-Respondent:** State of Washington

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in denying the motion to dismiss and that the Court of Appeals should reverse the conviction for attempted first degree theft by deception. The State failed to establish a prima facie case because it could not prove the essential element of market value—that the truck, as represented, was worth significantly less than the purchase price. The prosecution's case rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and a failure to meet its burden of proof on all elements of the crime.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The undisputed facts establish that the Georges were *purchasers*, not *sellers*, of the vehicle in question. Jerome Potter, the original owner, approached Tommy George in June 2003 about purchasing the 1974 Chevrolet half-ton pickup truck. Potter made full disclosure of the vehicle's condition: the rear wheel differential was defective, rendering the truck inoperable for two years; the truck had sat outside, uncovered, during that period; and Potter had replaced the original 350 engine with a 400 engine. Potter represented that the truck had 185,000 miles on it, though the odometer read 70,000. Tommy George was thus fully informed of the vehicle's mechanical condition, mileage discrepancy, and history of neglect.

John George subsequently negotiated with Potter and purchased the truck for $1,800. The Georges made improvements to the truck after acquiring it.

The detectives who investigated the case were not defrauded *by* the Georges—they encountered the Georges *after* the Georges had already purchased the vehicle. The State's theory appears to be that the Georges then misrepresented the truck's condition to the detectives (impersonating potential buyers), but this theory fails for multiple reasons discussed below.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The State Failed to Prove the Essential Element of Value

Under Washington law, first-degree theft requires that the property stolen be worth more than $5,000. RCW 9A.56.030. Attempted first-degree theft requires proof that the defendant *attempted* to commit theft of property worth more than $5,000. RCW 9A.56.030; RCW 9A.28.020.

The critical deficiency in the State's case is its complete failure to establish the market value of the truck at the time of the alleged deception. As the trial court record reflects, Detective Stokke could not find standardized pricing information for the vehicle due to its age. The *only* evidence the State presented regarding value was the amount the Georges paid Potter—$1,800. This establishes the *purchase price*, not the *market value* of the truck as represented.

In **State v. Gocken**, 127 Wash. 2d 95 (1995), the Washington Supreme Court held that the State must prove the actual market value of property in theft cases, not merely the amount paid or requested. The State bears the burden of establishing value through competent evidence, not inference. Here, the State presented no evidence whatsoever regarding what the truck would have been worth *if* the representations had been true.

The Georges' motion to dismiss specifically argued that after purchasing the truck, they made improvements to it, and the State offered no evidence showing the truck *as sold* was worth less than the sale price. This argument is well-taken. The State's evidence—$1,800—actually tends to demonstrate *under*valuation, not overvaluation, particularly given that the Georges subsequently invested in improvements.

### B. The Georges Were the Victims, Not the Perpetrators, of Any Deception

The facts establish that the Georges purchased the truck from Potter, who disclosed all material defects. If any deception occurred, it would have been Potter deceiving the Georges about the truck's condition or history. The Georges cannot be held liable for theft by deception when they were the ones who received full disclosure and paid fair consideration.

The State's theory appears to be that the Georges later misrepresented the truck's condition to the detectives. However, this theory fails because:

1. The detectives approached the Georges *after* the Georges had already purchased the truck;
2. The Georges had no obligation to disclose to the detectives what Potter had already disclosed to them;
3. The detectives were not "purchasers" in any legal sense—they were law enforcement officers conducting an investigation; and
4. No evidence establishes that the Georges obtained *any* money from the detectives.

In **State v. Smith**, 115 Wash. 2d 434 (1990), the Washington Supreme Court held that theft by deception requires that the defendant obtain property of another *by means of* deception. The Georges obtained the truck from Potter, not from the detectives, and they obtained it only after Potter disclosed the defects.

### C. The State Failed to Establish the Deception Element

Even accepting the detectives' testimony that the Georges made certain representations about the truck being garaged and having low mileage, the State must prove these representations were *material* and *actually deceived* someone. The detectives knew the true condition of the truck before they ever spoke to the Georges—they had already investigated and determined the actual history. As in **State v. Johnson**, 68 Wash. 2d 228 (1974), a deception claim cannot stand where the alleged victim had actual knowledge of the truth or could have discovered the truth through reasonable investigation.

### D. The Evidence Fails to Establish Attempted First-Degree Theft

Given the State's failure to prove the truck was worth more than $5,000, the most that could be established is attempted third-degree theft (property worth less than $250), as the Georges argued in their motion to dismiss. The trial court acknowledged this issue by giving lesser-included instructions, but the State failed to present evidence sufficient to support any theft conviction.

---

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution will likely argue that the $1,800 purchase price, combined with representations of low mileage and good condition, establishes that the Georges sought to obtain money based on false representations. This argument fails because:

1. **No money was obtained from the detectives**: The Georges completed their purchase from Potter before meeting the detectives. They never received any money from the investigators.

2. **The purchase price does not establish overvaluation**: The State presented no evidence that $1,800 exceeded the fair market value of a truck with 185,000 miles, a defective differential, and a replaced engine that had sat outside for two years. In fact, the Georges' payment of $1,800 for such a vehicle suggests they may have *overpaid*, not defrauded anyone.

3. **The Georges' statements to the detectives are irrelevant to the theft charge**: Even if the Georges made misleading statements to the detectives, this does not establish that they obtained property by deception *from* anyone. The detectives gave the Georges no money.

---

## CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that the Court of Appeals reverse the convictions for attempted first degree theft, dismiss the charges with prejudice, or in the alternative, reduce the offenses to attempted third-degree theft and remand for resentencing. The State failed to establish a prima facie case because it could not prove the market value of the truck exceeded $5,000, and the evidence does not support the essential elements of theft by deception.

Respectfully submitted,

**DEFENSE COUNSEL**

**A. The State Failed to Establish the Value Element for Any Degree of Theft**

The State bears the burden of proving the actual market value of the property at the time and place of the offense. As established in *State v. Gocken*, 127 Wash. 2d 95, 105 (1995), this burden requires "competent evidence" of value; it cannot be met by merely presenting the price paid in a prior transaction.

Without competent evidence of value, the State failed to establish the degree of theft, rendering the evidence insufficient for a conviction of first, second, or third-degree theft under RCW 9A.56.020.

**B. The State Failed to Prove the "By Means of" Element of Theft by Deception**

Theft by deception requires that the defendant obtain property "by means of" deception. *State v. Smith*, 115 Wash. 2d 434, 442 (1990). Even if improvements had been made, the State failed to present any evidence of the truck's value *with* those improvements at the time of the offense.

The Prosecution's concessions are well-founded.
