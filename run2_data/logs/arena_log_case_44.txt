## INTRODUCTION

Appellants John George and Tommy George respectfully submit this opening response in defense of their appeal, challenging the Court of Appeals' affirmance of their convictions for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the theft statute's "value" element. ## I. THE PROPER MEASURE OF VALUE IN THEFT BY DECEPTION MUST CONSIDER THE ACTUAL CONDITION OF THE PROPERTY AT THE TIME OF THE OFFENSE, INCLUDING IMPROVEMENTS MADE BY THE DEFENDANTS

### A. The Prosecution's Interpretation of "Value" Is Contrary to the Plain Language of the Statute and Legislative Intent

The prosecution urges this Court to adopt a rigid interpretation of "value" under RCW 9A.56.010(19) that would divorce the property's condition from its valuation entirely. This Court should reject such an interpretation.

RCW 9A.56.010(19) defines "value" as "the market value of the property at the time and place of the crime or, if such cannot be satisfactorily ascertained, the cost of replacement of the property within a reasonable time after the crime." The statute explicitly requires assessment of the property's value "at the time and place of the crime." This temporal requirement is critical.

As this Court held in *State v. Johnson*, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011), "the value of the property stolen in a theft by deception is the actual market value of the property at the time of the offense." However, the prosecution's argument conflates "actual market value" with a hypothetical value that ignores the defendants' legitimate improvements to the property.

The facts establish that after purchasing the truck from Jerome Potter for $1,800, the Georges made improvements to the vehicle. While the exact nature of these improvements was not fully developed at trial due to the State's failure to pursue this evidentiary avenue, the record reflects that the Georges invested labor and resources to transform the inoperable vehicle into one they believed had value. The prosecution's position would permit the State to profit from the defendants' labor and investment by assigning the truck a value based solely on its condition when acquired from Potter—ignoring the transformative efforts that made the sale possible.

### B. The Improvements Made by the Georges Are Relevant to Determining Value

The trial court erred by refusing to consider the defendants' improvements when assessing the truck's value. The prosecution's own evidence demonstrated that the Georges advertised the truck as being in "perfect condition" and "great shape"—representations that, if true, would have justified a higher market value. The State cannot rely on the defendants' representations about the truck's condition to establish the deception while simultaneously ignoring the physical reality of the truck at the time of sale.

In *State v. Blevins*, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007), this Court clarified that "the value of the property for theft by deception is the actual value of the property as it was at the time of the offense, not the amount the victim paid due to the deception." However, the Blevins Court did not hold that the property's condition at the time of the offense must be assessed without reference to any improvements made by the defendant. To the contrary, "actual value" must reflect the property's genuine worth in the marketplace at the relevant time.

The evidence presented at trial established that the Georges had invested in the truck following their purchase. The prosecution's failure to present any evidence regarding the truck's value following these improvements left the jury without a complete picture of the property's actual market value. The trial court's instruction to assess value based solely on the truck's "documented condition" without considering improvements was fundamentally flawed.

---

## II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE FIRST-DEGREE THEFT

### A. The State Failed to Establish That the Property Was Worth $5,000 or More

First-degree theft under RCW 9A.56.030 requires proof that the property stolen was worth "five thousand dollars or more." The prosecution bears the burden of establishing every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. The State presented no competent evidence establishing the truck's actual market value at the time of the offense.

Detective Stokke testified that due to the vehicle's age, he could not find standardized pricing information. The State presented no expert testimony regarding the truck's value. No evidence was introduced establishing what the truck was worth following the defendants' improvements. The prosecution relied entirely on the $5,500 sale price to establish the value threshold—a methodology this Court rejected in *Johnson* and *Blevins*.

However, the prosecution's error was more fundamental: even accepting the prosecution's theory that value must be assessed based on the property's condition, the State failed to prove what that condition actually was at the time of sale. The evidence established that the Georges represented the truck as having 70,000 miles, being garaged, and being in perfect condition. The prosecution's evidence regarding the truck's actual condition was limited to:

1. Potter's statements about the truck's condition when he sold it to the Georges;
2. The odometer discrepancy; and
3. Representations made during the sale to the detectives.

This evidence does not establish the truck's actual market value at the time of the offense. The prosecution failed to present evidence regarding the truck's value as improved, the cost of comparable vehicles, or any other basis for determining market value. The trial court erred in denying the Georges' motion to dismiss.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on the Sale Price Was Improper

The prosecution urges this Court to affirm based on the jury's ability to "assess market value" from the State's evidence. However, the prosecution never established what the truck was actually worth. The $5,500 sale price represents the amount the undercover officers paid—not the property's market value. As this Court recognized in *Johnson*, 161 Wash. App. at 538, value is not measured by "the amount paid by the victim."

The prosecution's position would permit convictions for first-degree theft whenever the sale price exceeds $5,000, regardless of the property's actual value. This interpretation eviscerates the statutory requirement and creates perverse incentives for overcharging in deception cases. A defendant who deceives a victim into paying $5,100 for property worth $100 has committed first-degree theft under the prosecution's theory, while a defendant who deceives a victim into paying $4,900 for property worth $1 has committed only second-degree theft. Such a result contradicts the statute's graduated structure.

## III. THE THEFT STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AS APPLIED

### A. The Statute Fails to Provide Fair Notice

The prosecution argues that RCW 9A.56.010(19) is not vague because "value" is defined by reference to "market value," a term known to commerce and law. However, the statute's application in this case demonstrates the precise vagueness problem the Georges identify.

The prosecution contends that "market value" must be assessed based on the property's condition at the time of the offense, without reference to the defendants' improvements. Yet the prosecution presented no evidence establishing what the truck's market value actually was. The statute provides no guidance regarding how to assess market value when:

1. The defendant has made improvements to the property;
2. No standardized pricing information exists;
3. No expert testimony is presented; and
4. The only evidence of "value" is the sale price induced by the deception.

As this Court determined in *State v. Smith*, 143 Wash. 2d 477, 485 (2001), the theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague when "value" is defined by reference to "market value." However, the Smith Court addressed the statute's facial validity, not its application to circumstances like those presented here. Where, as here, the State cannot establish market value through any recognized method, the statute fails to provide the notice required by due process.

### B. The Statute Creates Arbitrary Enforcement

The prosecution's interpretation permits arbitrary enforcement based on the victim's subjective assessment of value rather than any objective standard. Under the prosecution's theory, a defendant who deceives a victim into overpaying by $1 has committed theft in the first degree if the victim paid $5,001, but only third-degree theft if the victim paid $4,999. The property's actual worth is irrelevant.

This Court should hold that the statute requires the State to prove actual market value through competent evidence—not merely the amount paid by the victim. Where, as here, the State fails to present such evidence, the statute cannot support a conviction for first-degree theft.

## IV. THE LESSER INCLUDED OFFENSE INSTRUCTIONS WERE INSUFFICIENT

The trial court instructed the jury on lesser included offenses of attempted second and third-degree theft. However, the court failed to provide any guidance regarding how the jury should assess the property's value. The jury was instructed to determine "value" but received no instruction on how to calculate market value when the property's condition has been improved by the defendant.

The instruction provided no basis for the jury to distinguish between first, second, and third-degree theft. Without guidance on whether improvements are relevant to value, the jury had no basis for determining which offense, if any, was established. This Court should reverse and remand for a new trial with proper instructions.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding the appeal of the convictions of John George and Tommy George for attempted first degree theft by deception. The central issue on appeal is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a conviction for first degree theft, specifically regarding the element of the value of the property stolen. Additionally, the Court must address the defense's claim that the theft statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied in this case.

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The defense argues that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction for first degree theft because the State failed to prove that the property stolen was worth $5,000 or more. The prosecution counters that the jury was properly instructed to determine the actual market value of the truck based on its condition and that the Court of Appeals correctly affirmed the conviction.

The Court agrees with the defense that the evidence was insufficient to support a conviction for first degree theft. Under Washington law, the value of property in a theft by deception case is defined as the actual market value of the property at the time and place of the crime. As stated in *State v. Johnson*, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011), "the value of the property stolen in a theft by deception is the actual market value of the property at the time of the offense, not the amount paid by the victim." This principle was reaffirmed in *State v. Blevins*, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007), which clarified that "the value of the property for theft by deception is the actual value of the property as it was at the time of the offense, not the amount the victim paid due to the deception."

Applying this standard to the facts provided, the "property stolen" in this transaction was the 1974 Chevrolet pickup truck sold by the Georges to the undercover detectives. The State's evidence established that the truck was inoperable due to a faulty rear wheel differential, had approximately 185,000 miles (despite the odometer reading of 70,000), had not been garaged, and had a replaced engine rather than the original "350" engine. The State presented no evidence regarding the truck's value following the alleged improvements made by the Georges, nor did they present standardized pricing information, as Detective Stokke was unable to locate any.

To sustain a conviction for first degree theft under RCW 9A.56.030, the State was required to prove that the value of the property stolen was $5,000 or more. The State's evidence regarding the truck's condition—high mileage, inoperability, and exposure to the elements—supports a finding that the truck's actual market value was significantly less than $5,000. While the Georges attempted to sell the truck for $5,500, the amount asked for or paid by the victim is not the measure of value under *Johnson* and *Blevins*. Because the State failed to introduce competent evidence establishing that the truck's actual market value exceeded $5,000, the evidence was insufficient to support the element of value required for first degree theft. The State's evidence may have supported a conviction for a lesser degree, but it failed to meet the threshold for first degree.

**III. CONSTITUTIONAL VAGUENESS**

The defense argues that RCW 9A.56.010(19) is unconstitutionally vague as applied because it failed to provide fair notice regarding how to assess the market value of a unique, aged vehicle with no standardized pricing. The Court disagrees.

The Washington Supreme Court has previously addressed this challenge. In *State v. Smith*, 143 Wash. 2d 477, 485 (2001), the Court held that "the theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague because 'value' is defined by reference to market value, a standard term with a well-settled meaning in commerce and law." The fact that the State may have difficulty obtaining specific pricing data for a 1974 truck does not render the statute vague; it merely requires the jury to assess the value based on the evidence of the property's condition. The statute provides clear standards for enforcement and fair notice to persons of ordinary intelligence. Therefore, the defense's vagueness challenge is rejected.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the evidence was insufficient to sustain a conviction for attempted first degree theft. The State proved the deception and the sale, but failed to prove the value of the property exceeded $5,000 as required by law. The conviction for first degree theft cannot stand. The Court of Appeals' decision affirming the first degree conviction is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling, which may include the entry of a judgment for a lesser included offense if supported by the record.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding the sufficiency of the evidence to support the convictions of John George and Tommy George for attempted first degree theft by deception. The specific issue before this Court is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to prove that the value of the property stolen exceeded $5,000, as required for a first degree theft conviction under RCW 9A.56.030. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling that the evidence was insufficient to sustain the conviction for first degree theft.

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The prosecution argues that the $5,500 sale price, the advertisement, and the defendants' representations that the truck was in "perfect condition" provided ample evidence for the jury to conclude the market value exceeded $5,000. The prosecution further contends that the jury could infer value from the "improvements" made by the defendants. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive in light of controlling precedent and the evidentiary record.

**A. The Sale Price and Representations Cannot Establish Actual Market Value**

The prosecution relies heavily on the $5,500 asking price and the Georges' representations to the detectives as evidence of the truck's value. However, under Washington law, the value of property in a theft by deception case is the actual market value of the property at the time of the offense, not the amount paid or asked for by the victim. As stated in *State v. Johnson*, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011), "the value of the property stolen in a theft by deception is the actual market value of the property at the time of the offense, not the amount paid by the victim." This principle was reaffirmed in *State v. Blevins*, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007), which clarified that "the value of the property for theft by deception is the actual value of the property as it was at the time of the offense, not the amount the victim paid due to the deception."

To allow the State to use the $5,500 price—induced by the very deception charged—as proof of the value element would create circular reasoning that eviscerates the statutory requirement. The Georges' representations that the truck was in "perfect condition," "always garaged," and had only 70,000 miles were the very misrepresentations that formed the basis of the fraud charge. Because the State's evidence established that these representations were false—the truck was actually inoperable, had sat outside for two years, and had approximately 185,000 miles—these statements cannot serve as competent evidence of the truck's actual market value. The State effectively proved that the truck was *not* worth the $5,500 the defendants asked for, rather than proving it was.

**B. Lack of Evidence Regarding Improvements**

The prosecution suggests that the truck's market value should include any "legitimate improvements" made by the Georges. While the law requires assessing the property's condition at the time of the offense, the burden remains on the State to prove the value of those improvements. The record reflects that the State presented no evidence regarding what improvements, if any, were made to the truck or the monetary value of such improvements. The prosecution cannot now ask this Court to speculate that the repairs to the differential or the engine replacement raised the truck's value to the $5,000 threshold. Without competent evidence on this issue, the jury could not reasonably find that the value exceeded $5,000 based on improvements.

**C. The State's Evidence Supported a Finding of Low Value**

The State's evidence regarding the truck's actual condition pointed toward a value significantly lower than $5,000. The evidence showed the truck was a 1974 model that had been inoperable for two years, had high mileage (185,000 miles), and had been exposed to the elements. While the prosecution argues the jury could infer value from the asking price, *Johnson* and *Blevins* prohibit this approach in deception cases. Because the State failed to introduce standardized pricing, expert testimony, or other evidence of the truck's actual worth following the alleged improvements, the evidence was insufficient to support a finding that the value exceeded $5,000. The State's evidence was sufficient to support a finding of value for a lesser degree of theft, but it failed to meet the threshold for first degree.

**III. CONSTITUTIONAL VAGUENESS**

The defense concedes, and the Court agrees, that RCW 9A.56.010(19) is not unconstitutionally vague. As held in *State v. Smith*, 143 Wash. 2d 477, 485 (2001), "the theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague because 'value' is defined by reference to market value, a standard term with a well-settled meaning in commerce and law." The prosecution's rebuttal correctly notes that the jury is capable of determining market value based on the evidence. However, the jury's capability to determine value does not cure the lack of evidence presented by the State to support the specific value required for first degree theft.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the evidence was insufficient to sustain a conviction for attempted first degree theft. The prosecution's reliance on the deceived sale price and unproven improvements cannot substitute for the requisite proof of actual market value. The conviction for first degree theft is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

## I. THE LEGAL FRAMEWORK GOVERNING THEFT BY DECEPTION

The charged offense of attempted first degree theft by deception requires the State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that: (1) the defendants obtained property of another; (2) by means of a deception; (3) with intent to cause the owner to pass title; and (4) that the property stolen had a value exceeding $5,000. RCW 9A.56.030; RCW 9A.56.020. The value element is critical—it distinguishes first degree theft (a Class B felony) from lesser degrees of theft.

Under Washington law, the value of property in a theft by deception case is the *actual market value* of the property at the time of the offense, not the amount paid or asked for by the victim. *State v. Johnson*, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011). This principle exists to prevent the very circular reasoning that would otherwise infect deception-based theft prosecutions. As this Court explained in *State v. Blevins*, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007), "the value of the property for theft by deception is the actual value of the property as it was at the time of the offense, not the amount the victim paid due to the deception."

---

## II. THE STATE FAILED TO PRESENT COMPETENT EVIDENCE OF VALUE EXCEEDING $5,000

### A. The Sale Price Cannot Establish Actual Market Value

The prosecution correctly acknowledges that the $5,500 sale price cannot serve as evidence of actual market value. This admission is fatal to the State's case. The sale price of $5,500 was induced by the very misrepresentations that form the basis of the theft by deception charge. To allow the State to use this price as proof of the value element would create an impermissible cycle of reasoning: the State argues the defendants deceived the detectives into paying $5,500, and then argues that $5,500 proves the truck was worth more than $5,000.

This Court in *State v. Johnson* explicitly rejected such reasoning, holding that "the value of the property stolen in a theft by deception is the actual market value of the property at the time of the offense, not the amount paid by the victim." 161 Wash. App. at 538. The prosecution's brief before this Court correctly cites this binding precedent. The Georges' representations that the truck was in "perfect condition," had "always been garaged," and had only 70,000 miles were the very misrepresentations that formed the basis of the fraud charge. Because the State's evidence established that these representations were false—the truck was actually inoperable, had sat outside for two years, and had approximately 185,000 miles—these statements cannot serve as competent evidence of the truck's actual market value.

### B. The Advertisement and Representations Cannot Establish Value

The advertisement's $5,500 asking price is merely an offer, not proof of value. As this Court recognized in *State v. Johnson*, an asking price alone does not establish market value. 161 Wash. App. at 538. More critically, the Georges' representations to the detectives—that the truck was in "perfect condition," had "always been garaged," and had 70,000 miles—were demonstrably false. The evidence showed the truck was inoperable for two years, exposed to the elements, had 185,000 miles, and contained a replaced engine.

These false statements cannot simultaneously form the foundation of the deception charge and serve as proof of the property's legitimate worth. To permit this would allow the State to bootstrap its own fraud into evidence, which is legally impermissible. The prosecution's brief before this Court correctly concedes this point.

### C. No Evidence of Improvements Was Presented

The prosecution suggests that the truck's market value should include any "legitimate improvements" made by the Georges. While the law requires assessing the property's condition at the time of the offense, the burden remains on the State to prove the value of those improvements. The record reflects that the State presented no evidence regarding what improvements, if any, were made to the truck or the monetary value of such improvements.

The prosecution cannot now ask this Court to speculate that the repairs to the differential or the engine replacement raised the truck's value to the $5,000 threshold. Speculation is not evidence. The burden rests squarely on the State to prove every element of the crime, including value. Without competent evidence of improvements or their cost, the jury could not reasonably infer the truck's value exceeded $5,000.

## III. THE EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENTED SUPPORTED A FINDING OF LOW VALUE

The State's own evidence regarding the truck's actual condition pointed toward a value significantly lower than $5,000. The evidence showed the truck was a 1974 model that had been inoperable for two years, had high mileage (185,000 miles), and had been exposed to the elements. While the prosecution argues the jury could infer value from the asking price, binding precedent prohibits this approach in deception cases.

Because the State failed to introduce standardized pricing, expert testimony, or other evidence of the truck's actual worth following the alleged improvements, the evidence was insufficient to support a finding that the value exceeded $5,000. The State's evidence was sufficient to support a finding of value for a lesser degree of theft, but it failed to meet the threshold for first degree.

## IV. THE CONSTITUTIONAL VAGUENESS ARGUMENT

The defense acknowledges that RCW 9A.56.010(19) is not unconstitutionally vague. As this Court held in *State v. However, the statute's clarity does not cure the State's failure to present evidence meeting that standard.

The prosecution correctly notes that the jury is capable of determining market value based on the evidence. However, the jury's capability to determine value does not cure the lack of evidence presented by the State to support the specific value required for first degree theft. Where no evidence of value is introduced, the jury cannot manufacture that evidence from whole cloth.

The State failed to meet its burden of proving that the truck's actual market value exceeded $5,000 at the time of the offense. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense regarding the sufficiency of the evidence to support the convictions of John George and Tommy George for attempted first degree theft by deception. In a notable turn of events, the prosecution has conceded that the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish the value element necessary for a first degree theft conviction. After reviewing the record and the applicable law, the Court finds that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the value of the truck exceeded $5,000.

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The sole dispositive issue in this appeal is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to prove that the value of the property stolen exceeded $5,000, as required for a conviction of first degree theft under RCW 9A.56.030. The prosecution correctly concedes that it failed to meet this burden.

**A. The Sale Price Cannot Establish Actual Market Value**

The State’s case relied heavily on the fact that the defendants asked for and agreed to sell the truck for $5,500. However, in a prosecution for theft by deception, the value of the property is determined by its actual market value at the time of the offense, not the price the victim was induced to pay through fraud. As held in *State v. Johnson*, 161 Wash. App. 532, 538 (2011), "the value of the property stolen in a theft by deception is the actual market value of the property at the time of the offense, not the amount paid by the victim."

The prosecution acknowledges that using the $5,500 price to prove value would constitute circular reasoning. The price was derived entirely from the defendants' misrepresentations that the truck was in "perfect condition," "always garaged," and had only 70,000 miles. Because the State proved these representations were false—the truck was actually inoperable, had sat outside for two years, and had approximately 185,000 miles—the asking price cannot serve as competent evidence of the truck's actual market value. This principle is reaffirmed in *State v. Blevins*, 141 Wash. App. 323, 330 (2007), which states that "the value of the property for theft by deception is the actual value of the property as it was at the time of the offense, not the amount the victim paid due to the deception."

**B. Lack of Evidence Regarding Improvements**

The defendants argued that they had made improvements to the truck which might have increased its value. However, the State presented no evidence regarding the nature or cost of these improvements. The prosecution concedes that speculation regarding the value of repairs to the differential or the engine replacement is insufficient to meet the State's burden of proof. Without competent evidence—such as receipts, expert testimony, or market data—establishing the value added by these improvements, the jury could not reasonably find that the truck's value exceeded the $5,000 threshold.

**C. The State's Evidence Supported a Finding of Low Value**

The evidence presented by the State regarding the truck's actual condition pointed toward a value significantly lower than $5,000. The truck was a 1974 model that had been inoperable for two years, had high mileage, and had been exposed to the elements. Because the State failed to introduce standardized pricing or expert testimony to counter this evidence, the record supports only a finding of value consistent with third degree theft.

**III. CONSTITUTIONAL VAGUENESS**

The defense raised a challenge to the constitutionality of RCW 9A.56.010(19), arguing the term "value" is unconstitutionally vague. Both parties agree that this argument is without merit. As established in *State v. Smith*, 143 Wash. 2d 477, 485 (2001), "the theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague because 'value' is defined by reference to market value, a standard term with a well-settled meaning in commerce and law." While the statute provides a clear standard, the State’s failure to present evidence meeting that standard is fatal to the first degree conviction.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the evidence was insufficient to sustain a conviction for attempted first degree theft. The prosecution correctly concedes that the State failed to prove the value of the truck exceeded $5,000 independent of the deception. The conviction for attempted first degree theft is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
