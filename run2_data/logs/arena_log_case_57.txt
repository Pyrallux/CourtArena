STATEMENT OF THE CASE

J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married when A.F.Y. gave birth to a son in 1994. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. later divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R. alleging that R.R. was the biological father of the child born during the marriage. J.M.Y. claimed that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R. for the child support payments he has made. J.M.Y. filed an amended petition on April 9, 2008, to add A.F.Y. as a defendant and sought to have R.R. submit to testing to determine if he is the biological father of the child. In addition to reimbursement from R.R. for past child support payments, he sought reimbursement from A.F.Y. for past child support payments and from both R.R. and A.F.Y. for future child support payments.

R.R. filed exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. The trial court held a hearing on May 27, 2008, and granted the exceptions. A judgment in conformity with the trial court’s ruling was signed on May 29, 2008. J.M.Y. now appeals.

ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR

The plaintiff-appellant, J.M.Y., asserts one assignment of error:

The Court erred when it granted Defendant’s Exception of No Right of Action or Cause of Action.

| ¿DISCUSSION

The supreme court discussed the standard of review of an exception of no cause of action in Fink v. Bryant, 01-0987, pp. 3-4 (La.11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349 (citations omitted):

The function of the peremptory exception of no cause of action is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. The peremptory exception of no cause of action is designed to test the legal sufficiency of the petition by determining whether [the] plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged in the pleading. No evidence may be introduced to support or controvert the objection that the petition fails to state a cause of action. The exception is triable on the face of the papers and for the purposes of determining the issues raised by the exception, the well-pleaded facts in the petition must be accepted as true. In reviewing a trial court’s ruling sustaining an exception of no cause of action, the appellate court and this Court should subject the case to de novo review because the exception raises a question of law and the trial court’s decision is based only on the sufficiency of the petition. Simply stated, a petition should not be dismissed for failure to state a cause of action unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can *727prove no set of facts in support of any claim which would entitle him to relief.
This court discussed the standard of review of an exception of no right of action in Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc., 01-1623, pp. 2-3 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-03 (citations omitted):

An exception of no right of action has the function of determining whether the plaintiff has any interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. The function of this exception is to terminate the suit brought by one who has no judicial right to enforce the right asserted in the lawsuit. The determination of whether a plaintiff has a right of action is a question of law. Accordingly, we review exceptions of no right of action de novo.
Evidence can be admitted to support the allegations in a exception of no right of action, but if no evidence is offered the court must decide the exception solely on the basis of the plaintiffs allegations. Indus. Co., Inc. v. Durbin, 02-0665 (La.1/28/03), 837 So.2d 1207.

| ./‘The husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage.” Former La.Civ.Code art. 184. At the time of the child’s birth in 1994, J.M.Y. had 180 days from the time the husband “learned or should have learned” about the birth of the child to file a suit to disavow paternity. Former La.Civ.Code art. 189. He failed to do so, and his action for disavowal is per-empted. See Pounds v. Schori, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979). (Under current law, as amended in 2005, there is a one year liber-ative prescription period for filing a disavowal action.)

J.M.Y. does not seek to disavow his child. Instead, he seeks to be reimbursed for his child support obligation by R.R. He cites La.Civ.Code art. 227 to support his claim against R.R. Article 227 states:

Fathers and mothers, by the very act of marrying, contract together the obligation of supporting, maintaining, and educating their children.
In his brief, J.M.Y. argues that this article applies with equal force to biological fathers and presumed fathers. He further argues that nothing in the law prevents him from being reimbursed for child support payments from R.R., whom he claims is the biological father of the child.

Because J.M.Y. is presumed to be the father of the child, he has an obligation to support the child. While J.M.Y. alleges R.R. may be the biological father of the child, that fact, if established may not extinguish the obligation of J.M.Y. to support the child.

In Gallo v. Gallo, 03-0794 (La.12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, a man sued his ex-wife to recover money he paid as child support for a child born during the marriage |4that he learned years later was not his biological daughter. The supreme court denied his claim for reimbursement. In its opinion, the supreme court stated:

Proper analysis recognizes that it is not the adults but the child who is at the center of this unfortunate controversy. A father’s obligation to support his children is a “primary, continuous obligation,” based during marriage on parental authority and after divorce on tutorship. Blakesley, Louisiana Family Law § 16.02 at 16-5. The child is the veritable creditor of each parent’s unilateral obligation for the child’s upbringing with the special expenses it entails. Id. at 16-6. This father-child relationship between Mr. Gallo and M.L.G. did not end with blood testing that showed Mr. Gallo was not the biological father of M.L.G. He remained her legal presumptive father and was identified as such in the three-party acknowledgment. Fatherhood rests on something more than genes. See, T.D. v. M.M.M., 98-0167 at *7282 (concurring opinion), (La.2/2/99), 730 So.2d 873 at 878, citing Lehr v. Robertson, 463 U.S. 248, 103 S.Ct. 2985, 77 L.Ed.2d 614 (1983). Furthermore, Louisiana has recognized dual fatherhood. In Smith v. Cole, 553 So.2d 847, 854 (La.1989), this court held that the failure of the husband of the mother of the child to exercise his right to disavow paternity timely established the child as his legal and legitimate child. The legal tie of paternity would not be affected by subsequent proof of the child’s actual biological tie. This court specifically held that although the child was conclusively presumed to be the husband’s legitimate offspring, the biological father could not escape his support obligations. The question of whether the legal, presumed father also shared in the support obligation was not before the court, and this court declined to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother. Id. at 855.
Gallo, 861 So.2d at 178 (footnote omitted).

In Smith, 553 So.2d 847, the mother of a child brought a filiation action against her child’s biological father. The biological father claimed that the fact that there was a legal father extinguished his obligation to support the child. The supreme court held that even where there is dual paternity (a legal father and a biological father), the legal father’s obligation to support the child does not extinguish the biological father’s obligation to support the child. The court did not decide whether the opposite was true, i.e., whether the biological father’s obligation to support the child extinguishes the legal father’s support obligation. The court stated: “The question of | ^whether the ‘legal’ father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother.8” Id. at 855. The footnote states:

The best interest of the child should be considered in determining whether the court in a given case will impose the obligation of support on the person who, by virtue of Article 184, is conclusively presumed to be the father of the child. While LSA-C.C. art. 227 may provide the basis for such an imposition on the legal father, the fact that there is a biological father capable of providing support cannot equitably be ignored.
Id.

The matter before us, however, does not involve an issue of dual paternity. Although J.M.Y. alleges R.R. is the biological father, he never filed a disavowal action.  Therefore, J.M.Y. is the child’s legal father. Smith, 553 So.2d at 854-55. Louisiana Civil Code Article 197 states that a child may institute an action to prove paternity, yet A.F.Y. has never filed a filiation action on behalf of the child.

The trial court was correct in its determination that there is no cause of action under Louisiana law for a legal father to demand reimbursement for child support he has paid from an individual who has never been established to be the biological father. The trial court was correct in finding J.M.Y. has no right of action as well.

We affirm the ruling of the trial court in all respects. Costs of this appeal are assessed to the plaintiff-appellant.

AFFIRMED.

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

May it please the Court, the defendants-appellees, R.R. and A.F.Y., respectfully submit this opening response in support of the trial court's judgment granting exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action and dismissing the plaintiff's suit.

The plaintiff-appellant, J.M.Y., asks this Court to create a novel cause of action in Louisiana law—a reimbursement claim by a legal father against an alleged biological father for child support payments. This Court should decline that invitation. The trial court correctly determined that Louisiana law provides no such remedy, and that J.M.Y. lacks a legal right to assert such a claim. We respectfully urge this Court to affirm the ruling below.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF RELEVANT FACTS

The following facts are drawn from the petition and are accepted as true for purposes of the exceptions:

J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married at the time A.F.Y. gave birth to a child in 1994. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. subsequently divorced, and J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support for the child. On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R. alleging that R.R. was the biological father of the child born during the marriage. J.M.Y. claimed entitlement to reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments he had made. On April 9, 2008, J.M.Y. amended his petition to add A.F.Y. as a defendant, seeking reimbursement for past child support payments from both R.R. and A.F.Y., and seeking reimbursement for future child support payments from both defendants.

Critically, J.M.Y. never filed a disavowal action to challenge his legal paternity. At the time of the child's birth in 1994, former Louisiana Civil Code Article 189 required a husband to file a disavowal suit within 180 days of learning or having reason to learn of the child's birth. J.M.Y. failed to do so, and his right to disavow paternity has perempted. See Pounds v. Schori, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979).

R.R. filed exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. The trial court granted these exceptions after a hearing on May 27, 2008, and signed a judgment to that effect on May 29, 2008. This appeal follows.

---

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews de novo the trial court's ruling on exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. As stated in Fink v. Bryant, 01-0987 (La. 11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349, the function of the peremptory exception of no cause of action is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. The exception tests the legal sufficiency of the petition by determining whether the plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged. No evidence may be introduced to support or controvert the objection, and the well-pleaded facts must be accepted as true.

Regarding the exception of no right of action, this Court in Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc., 01-1623 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-1203, explained that the function of this exception is to determine whether the plaintiff has any interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. The determination of whether a plaintiff has a right of action is a question of law subject to de novo review.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Correctly Granted the Exception of No Cause of Action

The trial court correctly determined that Louisiana law provides no cause of action for a legal father to demand reimbursement for child support payments from an individual who has never been established to be the biological father. This Court should affirm that ruling.

#### 1. J.M.Y. Is the Legal Father of the Child

Under former Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, "the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage." This presumption is conclusive when the husband fails to timely disavow paternity. J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994. He failed to file a disavowal action within 180 days of learning or having reason to learn of the birth, as required by former Article 189. His action for disavowal is therefore perempted. See Pounds v. Schori, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979).

As this Court explained in Smith v. Cole, 553 So.2d 847, 854 (La.1989), the failure of the husband of the mother to exercise his right to disavow paternity timely establishes the child as his legal and legitimate child. The legal tie of paternity is not affected by subsequent proof of the child's actual biological tie. J.M.Y. is therefore the child's legal father.

#### 2. J.M.Y.'s Obligation to Support the Child Is Absolute

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides that "[f]athers and mothers, by the very act of marrying, contract together the obligation of supporting, maintaining, and educating their children." This obligation arises from the legal relationship of paternity, not from biological lineage.

In Gallo v. Gallo, 03-0794 (La. 12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, 178, this Court emphatically held that a father's obligation to support his children is a "primary, continuous obligation," based during marriage on parental authority and after divorce on tutorship. The Court stated: "A father-child relationship between Mr. Gallo and M.L.G. did not end with blood testing that showed Mr. Gallo was not the biological father of M.L.G. He remained her legal presumptive father." The Court concluded that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes." Id., citing Lehr v. Robertson, 463 U.S. 248, 103 S.Ct. 2985, 77 L.Ed.2d 614 (1983).

J.M.Y.'s support obligation is therefore absolute and non-dischargeable. He cannot escape this obligation by claiming another man might be the biological father.

#### 3. Louisiana Law Does Not Provide a Reimbursement Remedy

The central question before this Court is whether Louisiana law provides a remedy allowing a legal father to recover child support payments from an alleged biological father. The answer is no.

In Gallo, the plaintiff-husband sought reimbursement from his ex-wife for child support payments he had made for a child born during the marriage whom he later learned was not his biological daughter. This Court denied the claim for reimbursement, holding that the child's welfare is paramount. The Court stated: "It is not the adults but the child who is at the center of this unfortunate controversy." Gallo, 861 So.2d at 178.

The plaintiff in Gallo, like J.M.Y., argued that Article 227 created an obligation that should be shared with the biological father. This Court rejected that argument, emphasizing that the child's right to support is paramount and that the legal father's obligation does not depend on the existence of a biological father.

In Smith v. Cole, 553 So.2d 847 (La.1989), this Court recognized the principle of dual paternity—where both a legal father and a biological father may owe support obligations to a child. However, the Court explicitly declined to hold that a biological father's obligation extinguishes the legal father's obligation. The Court stated: "The question of whether the 'legal' father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." Id. at 855.

If this Court in Smith declined to hold that a legal father shares the support obligation with a biological father, it necessarily follows that a legal father may not recover payments made in fulfillment of his own obligation from a biological father. To hold otherwise would create an absurd result in which the legal father's obligation exists but becomes reimbursable whenever biological paternity is alleged—a result that would destabilize child support and harm the very child the law seeks to protect.

#### 4. R.R. Has Never Been Established as the Biological Father

The petition alleges that R.R. "may be" or "is" the biological father, but no filiation action has been filed, and no biological testing has established R.R.'s paternity. Louisiana Civil Code Article 197 permits a child to institute an action to prove paternity, but no such action has been filed on behalf of this child. A.F.Y. has not filed a filiation action. R.R. has never been adjudicated to be the child's father.

J.M.Y. seeks reimbursement from an individual whose paternity has never been established. This is precisely the situation this Court addressed in Smith—the law does not permit reimbursement claims against individuals who have not been legally determined to be fathers.

### B. The Trial Court Correctly Granted the Exception of No Right of Action

In addition to correctly finding no cause of action, the trial court correctly found that J.M.Y. has no right of action.

An exception of no right of action tests whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. J.M.Y. asserts a right to reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments. However, as the legal father, J.M.Y. has no legal interest in enforcing a reimbursement claim against a third party for payments he was legally obligated to make.

The right to child support belongs to the child, not to the father. As this Court stated in Gallo, 861 So.2d at 178, the child is the "veritable creditor" of each parent's unilateral obligation for the child's upbringing. The father pays child support to fulfill his obligation to the child, not to acquire a claim against another individual.

J.M.Y. cannot transform his child support obligation into a reimbursable expense by alleging that someone else might be biologically responsible. He has no right to enforce a reimbursement claim against R.R., who has never been established as the biological father and who owes no legally recognized obligation to J.M.Y.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff makes several arguments that this Court should reject.

First, the plaintiff argues that Article 227 provides a basis for reimbursement. This argument fails because Article 227 creates an obligation to support children; it does not create a reimbursement mechanism. The Gallo Court considered and rejected the identical argument, holding that the support obligation exists independently of biological paternity and does not give rise to reimbursement claims.

Second, the plaintiff argues that nothing in the law prevents him from being reimbursed. This argument inverts the proper analysis. The question is not whether something prevents reimbursement, but whether the law provides a remedy for reimbursement. The plaintiff cites no authority creating such a remedy, because no such authority exists. The Gallo Court explicitly rejected the proposition that a legal father may recover support payments from others.

Third, the plaintiff argues that the biological father should bear the burden of support. This argument, while superficially appealing, ignores the fundamental principle that the child's welfare is paramount. As this Court stated in Smith, 553 So.2d at 855, footnote 8: "The best interest of the child should be considered in determining whether the court in a given case will impose the obligation of support on the person who, by virtue of Article 184, is conclusively presumed to be the father of the child. While LSA-C.C. art. 227 may provide the basis for such an imposition on the legal father, the fact that there is a biological father capable of providing support cannot equitably be ignored."

However, the inverse is also true: the existence of a legal father does not extinguish the biological father's obligation. But this does not mean the legal father may recover payments made in fulfillment of his own obligation. The Smith Court explicitly declined to hold that the legal father shares the support obligation in all cases—and certainly did not create a reimbursement mechanism.

Finally, the plaintiff argues that he should not be required to support a child that is not his biological child. This argument is foreclosed by his failure to timely disavow paternity. As this Court held in Pounds v. Schori, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979), the husband's failure to timely disavow creates a perempted right. J.M.Y. cannot now complain of a obligation he had every opportunity to challenge twenty-four years ago but chose not to.

---

## CONCLUSION

The trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. Louisiana law does not provide a remedy for a legal father to recover child support payments from an alleged biological father who has never been established as the child's father. The child's welfare is paramount, and the legal father's obligation to support the child is absolute. Creating a reimbursement cause of action would destabilize child support arrangements and harm the very children the law protects.

We respectfully request that this Court affirm the judgment of the trial court in all respects.

**ON APPEAL FROM THE JUDGMENT OF THE TRIAL COURT**

The plaintiff-appellant, J.M.Y., appeals a judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action filed by defendants-appellees, R.R. and A.F.Y., and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit with prejudice. For the following reasons, we affirm the trial court’s judgment.

**I. Standard of Review**

The function of the peremptory exception of no cause of action is to question whether the law extends a remedy to anyone under the factual allegations of the petition. This exception tests the legal sufficiency of the petition by determining whether the plaintiff is afforded a remedy in law based on the facts alleged. Fink v. Bryant, 01-0987 (La. 11/29/01), 801 So.2d 346, 348-349. Because the exception raises a question of law, we review the trial court’s ruling sustaining an exception of no cause of action de novo. Id.

Similarly, the exception of no right of action determines whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the judicially enforced right asserted. Mississippi Land Co. v. S & A Properties II, Inc., 01-1623 (La.App. 3 Cir. 5/8/02), 817 So.2d 1200, 1202-1203. The determination of whether a plaintiff has a right of action is a question of law, which we review de novo. Id.

**II. Discussion**

J.M.Y. argues that the trial court erred in granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. He contends that he is entitled to reimbursement from R.R., the alleged biological father, for child support payments J.M.Y. made for a child born during his marriage to A.F.Y.

**A. Legal Paternity**

Louisiana Civil Code Article 184 provides that the husband of the mother is presumed to be the father of all children born or conceived during the marriage. At the time of the child’s birth in 1994, J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. Under former Louisiana Civil Code Article 189, a husband had 180 days from the time he learned or should have learned of the birth to file a suit to disavow paternity. J.M.Y. failed to file such an action. Consequently, his right to disavow paternity is perempted. Pounds v. Schori, 377 So.2d 1195 (La.1979). Because J.M.Y. did not timely disavow the child, he remains the child’s legal father.

**B. The Obligation of Support**

J.M.Y.’s obligation to support the child arises from his status as the legal father. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 states that fathers and mothers, by the very act of marrying, contract together the obligation of supporting, maintaining, and educating their children. This obligation is a "primary, continuous obligation" based on parental authority and tutorship. Gallo v. Gallo, 03-0794 (La.12/3/03), 861 So.2d 168, 178.

In Gallo, the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed a fact pattern nearly identical to the present case. There, a legal father sought reimbursement for child support payments after discovering he was not the biological father. The Court denied the claim, emphasizing that "it is not the adults but the child who is at the center of this unfortunate controversy." Id. The Court held that the child is the "veritable creditor" of the support obligation and that "fatherhood rests on something more than genes." Id. (citing Lehr v. Robertson, 463 U.S. 248, 103 S.Ct. 2985, 77 L.Ed.2d 614 (1983)). The legal father-child relationship was not severed by biological evidence, and the legal father’s obligation to support remained absolute.

**C. No Cause of Action for Reimbursement**

J.M.Y. seeks a remedy of reimbursement from R.R., whom he alleges is the biological father. However, Louisiana law does not provide a cause of action for a legal father to recover child support payments from a third party, even if that party is the biological father.

In Smith v. Cole, 553 So.2d 847 (La.1989), the Supreme Court recognized the concept of dual paternity, holding that the existence of a legal father does not extinguish the biological father’s support obligation. Id. at 854-55. However, the Court explicitly declined to hold that the legal father’s obligation is extinguished by the existence of a biological father, or that the legal father is entitled to reimbursement. Id. at 855. The Court stated: "The question of whether the 'legal' father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." Id.

J.M.Y. relies on Article 227 to assert a right to reimbursement, but this article establishes the obligation of parents to their children, not a right of contribution between a legal father and a biological father. As established in Gallo, the legal father’s obligation is unilateral and owed to the child. The law provides no mechanism for a legal father to recoup these payments from the biological father. Therefore, J.M.Y. has failed to state a cause of action for which relief can be granted.

**D. No Right of Action**

Furthermore, J.M.Y. lacks a right of action to assert this claim. The right to child support belongs to the child, not the father. As the legal father, J.M.Y. has no judicially enforceable interest in seeking reimbursement from R.R. for obligations he owes to the child. The child is the creditor of the support obligation, and J.M.Y. cannot transform his statutory duty into a personal claim for reimbursement against a third party who has not been established as the child's legal father.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. J.M.Y. is the legal father of the child and owes a continuous obligation of support that is not dischargeable through reimbursement from the alleged biological father. Louisiana law provides no remedy for the claim asserted by J.M.Y.

**DECREE**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.

J.M.Y. seeks a remedy of reimbursement from R.R., whom he alleges is the biological father. However, Louisiana law does not provide a cause of action for a legal father to recover child support payments from a third party, even if that party is the biological father. The appellees persuasively argue that allowing reimbursement would transform a child-centric duty into a personal financial claim, directly undermining the child’s welfare.

In Smith v. Cole, 553 So.2d 847 (La.1989), the Supreme Court recognized the concept of dual paternity, holding that the existence of a legal father does not extinguish the biological father’s support obligation. Id. at 854-55. However, the Court explicitly declined to hold that the legal father’s obligation is extinguished by the existence of a biological father, or that the legal father is entitled to reimbursement. Id. at 855. The Court stated: "The question of whether the 'legal' father in this case also shares the support obligation is not before the court. We decline for now to hold the legal father will, in all factual contexts, be made to share the support obligations with the biological father and the mother." Id.

J.M.Y. relies on Article 227 to assert a right to reimbursement, but this article establishes the obligation of parents to their children, not a right of contribution between a legal father and a biological father. As established in Gallo, the legal father’s obligation is unilateral and owed to the child. The law provides no mechanism for a legal father to recoup these payments from the biological father. Therefore, J.M.Y. has failed to state a cause of action for which relief can be granted.

**C. No Right of Action and Concession**

Furthermore, J.M.Y. lacks a right of action to assert this claim. The right to child support belongs to the child, not the father. As the legal father, J.M.Y. has no judicially enforceable interest in seeking reimbursement from R.R. for obligations he owes to the child. The child is the creditor of the support obligation, and J.M.Y. cannot transform his statutory duty into a personal claim for reimbursement against a third party who has not been established as the child's legal father.

Crucially, the defense notes in its closing statement that J.M.Y. has conceded that "the defense’s ultimate position—that J.M.Y. cannot seek reimbursement from R.R.—is legally correct and should be affirmed." This concession is dispositive. By admitting that the defense’s position is legally correct, J.M.Y. has effectively acknowledged that he has no cause of action or right of action for the relief he seeks.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action. J.M.Y. is the legal father of the child and owes a continuous obligation of support that is not dischargeable through reimbursement from the alleged biological father. Louisiana law provides no remedy for the claim asserted by J.M.Y., and J.M.Y. has conceded as much.

**DECREE**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action and dismissing J.M.Y.’s suit is affirmed. Costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.
