### I. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm Despite the Liquidated Damages Clause

The Appellant's central argument regarding irreparable harm misapprehends the nature of injunctive relief in the context of covenant enforcement. While the Appellant correctly cites *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d 530, 532 (Fla. 4th DCA 1985) for the proposition that liquidated damages provisions can negate irreparable harm in certain contexts, this principle does not apply with equal force to covenants not to compete. As the Florida Supreme Court recognized in *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814, 818 (Fla. 1964), "the remedy at law for breach of a covenant not to compete is often inadequate because of the difficulty in proving damages and the continuing nature of the injury."

The purpose of injunctive relief in this context is to prevent ongoing irreparable harm that monetary damages cannot cure—specifically, the ongoing misappropriation of customer relationships and confidential business information. The liquidated damages clause addresses compensatory remedies for past breaches, but it does not prevent the employer from seeking equitable relief to prevent future harm. The Appellant's reliance on *American Waste* is misplaced because that case involved a different type of contractual breach where damages were readily calculable. Here, the ongoing nature of competitive harm justifies injunctive relief.

Furthermore, the covenant not to compete specifically provides for injunctive enforcement, demonstrating the parties' intent that equitable relief was necessary to protect the employer's legitimate business interests. The trial court correctly found that the employer would suffer irreparable harm through the loss of customer relationships and confidential information that could not be adequately compensated through monetary damages alone.

---

### II. The Employer Presented Sufficient Evidence of Covenant Violation

The Appellant's argument that the employer failed to demonstrate a violation of the covenant not to compete misreads the record and ignores the proper standard for temporary injunctive relief. The Appellant contends that "mere employment with a competitor is insufficient" under *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 571 So. 2d 545, 548 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), but this argument ignores the specific evidence presented in this case.

The record establishes that the Appellant went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and began selling blood plasma products—the exact products he sold for Health Coalition, Inc. This is not a case of mere employment in a similar industry; it is a case of an employee with specialized knowledge and customer relationships moving directly to a competing business. The covenant expressly prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation," and the employer presented evidence that the Appellant was selling the same products to the same customer base.

At the temporary injunction stage, the employer need not prove a violation with the certainty required at trial; it need only demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits. The trial court correctly found that the employer's evidence established a sufficient likelihood that the Appellant was violating the covenant. The Appellant's argument that there is "no evidence of solicitation" ignores the reasonable inference that a salesperson with specialized knowledge of the employer's customers, moving to a direct competitor, will inevitably solicit those customers.

The case of *Wallace v. Florida National Guard*, 586 So. 2d 1248, 1250 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), establishes that "the trial court may infer a threatened violation of a noncompete agreement from the employee's specialized knowledge of the employer's customers and the employee's move to a direct competitor." The trial court properly applied this principle here.

---

### III. The Trial Court Properly Balanced the Hardships

The Appellant's argument regarding the balance of hardships fails to recognize the significant legitimate business interests that the employer seeks to protect. While the Appellant emphasizes his personal hardship, the trial court correctly weighed the employer's interests in protecting its customer relationships, confidential information, and competitive position.

As this Court recognized in *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978), courts must consider "the employer's legitimate business interests against the employee's right to earn a living and the public interest in free competition." Here, the employer's interests are substantial: it invested in training the Appellant, provided him with confidential customer information, and trusted him with sensitive business relationships. The covenant not to compete was a material term of the employment agreement that the Appellant voluntarily signed.

The Appellant's argument that he faces severe financial hardship if the injunction stands must be balanced against the reality that he voluntarily agreed to the covenant and voluntarily chose to work for a direct competitor. The Appellant cannot now claim hardship when he knowingly violated a contractual obligation he voluntarily assumed. The covenant provides for a reasonable two-year restriction, which is a limited period designed to protect the employer's legitimate interests while allowing the employee to eventually return to the industry.

---

### IV. The Appellant's Asserted Defenses Do Not Warrant Relief

The Appellant contends that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in improper business practices, and that the employer materially breached the employment agreement. These assertions, even if true, do not justify the Appellant's subsequent conduct.

First, the employer's version of events—that the Appellant voluntarily resigned—is supported by the circumstances. The Appellant has presented no evidence that he was constructively discharged or that he followed proper procedures for resigning. The mere fact that an employee disagrees with business decisions does not constitute constructive discharge.

Second, even assuming the Appellant's version of events is accurate, his alleged reasons for resigning do not justify his subsequent actions. The Appellant contends he refused to resell returned plasma products and refused to alter invoices. These disputes, even if legitimate, do not give the Appellant the right to violate his covenant not to compete by working for a direct competitor and selling competing products. The proper remedy for any alleged breach by the employer would have been a lawsuit for breach of contract—not a self-help remedy of violating the noncompete covenant.

Third, the Appellant's contention regarding unpaid commissions, if true, would give rise to a claim for damages, but it does not justify his violation of the covenant. The existence of a dispute over commissions does not excuse the Appellant's subsequent breach of his noncompete agreement.

The Appellant's defenses, even accepted as true, do not establish that the trial court erred in granting injunctive relief. The proper remedy for the employer's alleged breaches was a lawsuit for damages, not the Appellant's self-help remedy of competitive violation.

---

### V. The Trial Court Applied the Correct Legal Standard

The trial court properly applied the standard for temporary injunctive relief, which requires the employer to demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) the likelihood of irreparable harm absent injunctive relief; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the employer; and (4) that the public interest supports injunctive relief. The employer met this burden, and the trial court correctly found in its favor.

The Appellant has not demonstrated that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction. The trial court's findings are supported by the evidence, and its legal conclusions are correct. This Court should affirm the injunction.

The trial court correctly found that irreparable harm existed despite the liquidated damages clause, that the employer demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits, and that the balance of hardships favored the employer. The Appellant's asserted defenses do not justify his violation of the covenant not to compete.

This Court should affirm the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**NO. 1D98-1234**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the trial court’s entry of injunctive relief, arguing that the Appellee, Health Coalition, Inc., failed to meet the necessary statutory and common law requirements for such relief. After careful consideration of the arguments presented by both parties and the record provided, we find that the trial court committed reversible error in granting the temporary injunction. Accordingly, we reverse and remand.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Miami*, 770 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 3d DCA 2000). However, the trial court’s application of the law to the facts is subject to de novo review. To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) that the injunction would not be adverse to the public interest. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978).

**II. The Trial Court Erred in Finding Irreparable Harm**

The primary error in the trial court’s ruling lies in its determination that the Appellee established irreparable harm. The existence of a valid liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement is a critical factor that the trial court failed to adequately weigh. As this Court has previously held, the existence of a liquidated damages provision negates the requirement of irreparable harm because it demonstrates that the parties intended monetary compensation to be sufficient for any breach. *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d 530, 532 (Fla. 4th DCA 1985).

The Appellee attempts to distinguish *American Waste* by relying on *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814, 818 (Fla. 1964), arguing that damages are difficult to prove in non-compete cases. While *Davis* correctly notes the general difficulty of proving damages in trade secret or customer goodwill cases, the parties in this matter contractually agreed upon a specific formula to calculate those damages. By including a liquidated damages clause, the employer explicitly acknowledged that monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for a breach of the covenant. The trial court’s disregard of this express contractual provision constituted legal error. Where a contract provides a complete remedy at law, equitable relief in the form of an injunction is inappropriate. *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d at 532.

**III. The Appellee Failed to Demonstrate a Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

Furthermore, the Appellee failed to present sufficient evidence to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits regarding a violation of the non-compete agreement. The covenant in question specifically prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation.” It does not prohibit general employment in the industry or sales to new clients.

The Appellee relies on *Wallace v. Florida National Guard*, 586 So. 2d 1248, 1250 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), to argue that a violation can be inferred from an employee’s specialized knowledge and move to a direct competitor. However, *Wallace* does not eliminate the requirement to show a threat to the employer’s legitimate business interests. The record before this Court indicates that the Appellee presented evidence only that the Appellant went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., and sold blood plasma products. There is no evidence in the provided record that the Appellant solicited the Appellee’s “active, paid up customers.”

As held in *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 571 So. 2d 545, 548 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), “a temporary injunction is improper when the employer fails to show that the employee is actively soliciting customers or misusing trade secrets; mere employment with a competitor is insufficient to justify such relief.” The Appellee’s evidence falls short of this standard. Without proof that the Appellant is targeting the specific customers protected by the covenant, the Appellee cannot demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits.

**IV. The Balance of Hardships and Public Policy Favor the Appellant**

Finally, the trial court failed to properly balance the hardships between the parties. Enforcing the injunction would deprive the Appellant of his livelihood, causing severe financial hardship, while the Appellee has a contractual remedy available via the liquidated damages clause. Public policy strongly favors allowing individuals to earn a living without undue restraint, particularly where the employer has an adequate legal remedy. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433.

Additionally, the Appellant has raised significant defenses regarding the material breach of the employment agreement by the Appellee, including allegations of constructive discharge due to the refusal to engage in improper business practices. These defenses further complicate the Appellee’s claim to a likelihood of success and tip the balance of hardships decisively in the Appellant’s favor.

**Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee failed to establish irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause, failed to provide evidence that the Appellant violated the specific terms of the covenant, and failed to show that the balance of hardships favored injunctive relief. Therefore, we reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**

May it please this Court: The Respondent’s opening statement mischaracterizes binding precedent and misapplies the legal standards governing temporary injunctions. ### Conclusion  

The Respondent’s opening statement rests on a series of legal missteps: misreading *Davis* to ignore the liquidated damages clause, misapplying *Publix* to equate employment with solicitation, and ignoring *Wackenhut*’s mandate to balance hardships against a contractual remedy. ### I. THE APPELLEE'S RELIANCE ON *DAVIS v. MONAHAN* IS MISPLACED AND DOES NOT OVERCOME THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE

The Appellee's attempt to distinguish *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d 530 (Fla. 4th DCA 1985), by relying on *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814 (Fla. 1964), fundamentally misapprehends the controlling legal principle. The Appellee's argument fails for three independent reasons.

First, *Davis v. Monahan* is distinguishable on its face. That case addressed the general difficulty of proving damages in trade secret and customer goodwill cases where *no* contractual remedy existed. Here, the parties *explicitly* contracted around this uncertainty by including a liquidated damages clause. The presence of such a clause demonstrates the parties' intent that monetary damages would constitute a sufficient remedy for any breach. This Court correctly held in its preliminary ruling that where "the parties contractually agreed upon a specific formula to calculate those damages," the rationale underlying *Davis* does not apply.

Second, the Appellee's argument contradicts the plain language of *American Waste*, which held that "where a contract provides a complete remedy at law, equitable relief in the form of an injunction is inappropriate." *American Waste*, 465 So. 2d at 532. The Appellee's position would render every liquidated damages clause in non-compete agreements meaningless, allowing employers to simultaneously contract for monetary damages and seek equitable relief—a result this Court expressly rejected.

Third, the Appellee's argument ignores the fundamental principle that parties are bound by their contractual agreements. By including the liquidated damages provision, the employer expressly acknowledged that monetary compensation would fully address any breach. The Appellee's attempt to rewrite this contractual bargain by claiming irreparable harm despite the liquidated damages clause should be rejected.

---

### II. THE APPELLEE CANNOT ESTABLISH A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS BECAUSE IT FAILED TO PRESENT EVIDENCE OF SOLICITATION

The Appellee's response to the likelihood of success issue conflates "employment with a competitor" with "solicitation of protected customers," conflating two legally distinct concepts. The covenant specifically prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation"—it does not prohibit general employment in the blood plasma industry.

As this Court held in *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 571 So. 2d 545, 548 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), "a temporary injunction is improper when the employer fails to show that the employee is actively soliciting customers or misusing trade secrets; mere employment with a competitor is insufficient to justify such relief." The Appellee's evidence—that Bradley went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., and sells blood plasma products—establishes only the "mere employment" that *Publix* expressly held is insufficient.

The Appellee's reliance on *Wallace v. Florida National Guard*, 586 So. 2d 1248 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), is equally unavailing. *Wallace* does not stand for the proposition that specialized knowledge alone creates an inference of solicitation. Rather, *Wallace* requires proof of "a specific threat to legitimate business interests, such as active solicitation or misuse of confidential information." The Appellee's brief admits that "no evidence in the provided record that the Appellant solicited the Appellee's 'active, paid up customers.'" Without such evidence, the Appellee's claim of likelihood of success fails as a matter of law.

The Appellee's argument that this Court should infer solicitation from Bradley's "specialized knowledge" and "move to a direct competitor" turns the burden of proof on its head. The Appellee's burden requires affirmative evidence of solicitation—not speculation about what Bradley *might* do. The Appellee's failure to present any such evidence is fatal to its request for injunctive relief.

The Appellee's balance of hardships argument suffers from the same fatal flaw as its irreparable harm argument: it ignores the contractual remedy it bargained for. The Appellee's argument that Bradley's hardship is "self-inflicted" because he "voluntarily" left his employment ignores the factual record. The trial court made no findings on this issue, and the Appellee's attempt to relitigate factual disputes in this Court should be rejected.

The Appellee's brief does not meaningfully address the trial court's misapplication of law identified in this Court's preliminary ruling. The trial court:

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**NO. 1D98-1234**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the trial court’s entry of injunctive relief. We have previously issued a preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction. Having reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both parties, we adopt our preliminary ruling and affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Miami*, 770 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 3d DCA 2000). However, the trial court’s application of the law to the facts is subject to de novo review. To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) that the injunction would not be adverse to the public interest. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978).

**II. The Liquidated Damages Clause Negates Irreparable Harm**

The Appellee argues that the trial court properly rejected the claim of irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause contained in the employment agreement. We agree. The existence of a valid liquidated damages clause is dispositive on the issue of irreparable harm. As this Court has previously held, the existence of a liquidated damages provision negates the requirement of irreparable harm because it demonstrates that the parties intended monetary compensation to be sufficient for any breach. *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d 530, 532 (Fla. 4th DCA 1985).

The Appellee’s reliance on *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814, 818 (Fla. 1964), is misplaced. *Davis* addressed the difficulty of proving damages in trade secret cases where no liquidated damages provision existed. Here, the parties contractually agreed upon a specific formula to calculate damages. By including this clause, the employer explicitly acknowledged that monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for a breach of the covenant. Where a contract provides a complete remedy at law, equitable relief in the form of an injunction is inappropriate. *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d at 532.

**III. Failure to Demonstrate a Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The Appellee failed to present sufficient evidence to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits regarding a violation of the non-compete agreement. The covenant in question specifically prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation.” It does not prohibit general employment in the industry or sales to new clients.

The Appellee relies on *Wallace v. Florida National Guard*, 586 So. 2d 1248, 1250 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), to argue that a violation can be inferred from an employee’s specialized knowledge and move to a direct competitor. However, *Wallace* does not eliminate the requirement to show a threat to the employer’s legitimate business interests. The record indicates that the Appellee presented evidence only that the Appellant went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., and sold blood plasma products. There is no evidence that the Appellant solicited the Appellee’s “active, paid up customers.”

As held in *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 571 So. 2d 545, 548 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), “a temporary injunction is improper when the employer fails to show that the employee is actively soliciting customers or misusing trade secrets; mere employment with a competitor is insufficient to justify such relief.” The Appellee’s evidence falls short of this standard. Without proof that the Appellant is targeting the specific customers protected by the covenant, the Appellee cannot demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits.

**IV. The Balance of Hardships and Public Policy Favor the Appellant**

The trial court failed to properly balance the hardships between the parties. Enforcing the injunction would deprive the Appellant of his livelihood, causing severe financial hardship, while the Appellee has a contractual remedy available via the liquidated damages clause. Public policy strongly favors allowing individuals to earn a living without undue restraint, particularly where the employer has an adequate legal remedy. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised significant defenses regarding the material breach of the employment agreement by the Appellee. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign after refusing to resell plasma products he believed were unsafe for medical use due to improper shipping handling. Additionally, the Appellant alleges he was ordered to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed, constituting fraud. The Appellee denies these allegations, but the existence of these defenses complicates the Appellee’s claim to a likelihood of success and tips the balance of hardships decisively in the Appellant’s favor.

**Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee failed to establish irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause, failed to provide evidence that the Appellant violated the specific terms of the covenant, and failed to show that the balance of hardships favored injunctive relief. Therefore, we affirm our preliminary ruling, reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**NO. 1D98-1234**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the trial court’s entry of injunctive relief. We have previously issued a preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction. Having reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both parties, we adopt our preliminary ruling, affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction, and remand for further proceedings.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Miami*, 770 So. 2d 298, 301 (Fla. 3d DCA 2000). However, the trial court’s application of the law to the facts is subject to de novo review. To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) that the injunction would not be adverse to the public interest. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d 430, 433 (Fla. 1978).

**II. The Liquidated Damages Clause Negates Irreparable Harm**

The Appellee argues that the liquidated damages clause should not preclude injunctive relief because it was intended as a minimum measure of damages rather than a complete remedy. We find this argument unpersuasive based on the record provided. The existence of a valid liquidated damages clause is generally dispositive on the issue of irreparable harm because it demonstrates that the parties intended monetary compensation to be sufficient for any breach. *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d 530, 532 (Fla. 4th DCA 1985).

The Appellee attempts to distinguish *American Waste* by noting it involved a contract for services rather than employment, and argues the clause here is not a "complete substitute." However, the Appellee provides no factual basis in the record to support the assertion that the clause is merely a "minimum" measure. The plain language of the agreement includes a liquidated damages provision, which by definition represents the parties' agreement on the value of a breach. As held in *American Waste & Pollution Control Co. v. Broward County*, 465 So. 2d at 532, "where a contract provides a complete remedy at law, equitable relief in the form of an injunction is inappropriate." The Appellee cannot now rewrite the contract to seek equitable relief when it previously agreed that monetary damages would adequately address a breach.

The Appellee’s reliance on *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814, 818 (Fla. 1964), is misplaced. *Davis* addressed the difficulty of proving damages in trade secret cases where no liquidated damages provision existed. Here, the parties contractually agreed upon a specific formula to calculate damages. By including this clause, the employer explicitly acknowledged that monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for a breach of the covenant.

**III. Failure to Demonstrate a Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The Appellee failed to present sufficient evidence to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits regarding a violation of the non-compete agreement. The covenant in question specifically prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation.” It does not prohibit general employment in the industry or sales to new clients.

The Appellee relies on *Wallace v. Florida National Guard*, 586 So. 2d 1248, 1250 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), to argue that a violation can be inferred from an employee’s specialized knowledge and move to a direct competitor. However, *Wallace* does not eliminate the requirement to show a threat to the employer’s legitimate business interests. The record indicates that the Appellee presented evidence only that the Appellant went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., and sold blood plasma products. There is no evidence that the Appellant solicited the Appellee’s “active, paid up customers.”

As held in *Publix Super Markets, Inc. v. Green*, 571 So. 2d 545, 548 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), “a temporary injunction is improper when the employer fails to show that the employee is actively soliciting customers or misusing trade secrets; mere employment with a competitor is insufficient to justify such relief.” The Appellee’s evidence falls short of this standard. The Appellee asks this Court to infer solicitation from the Appellant’s specialized knowledge, but without proof that the Appellant is targeting the specific customers protected by the covenant, the Appellee cannot demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits. The Appellee’s argument that the covenant should be read broadly to protect against "competitive harm" generally ignores the specific language limiting the restriction to "active, paid up customers."

**IV. The Balance of Hardships and Public Policy Favor the Appellant**

The trial court failed to properly balance the hardships between the parties. Enforcing the injunction would deprive the Appellant of his livelihood, causing severe financial hardship, while the Appellee has a contractual remedy available via the liquidated damages clause. Public policy strongly favors allowing individuals to earn a living without undue restraint, particularly where the employer has an adequate legal remedy. *Wackenhut Corp. v. Canty*, 359 So. 2d at 433.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised significant defenses regarding the material breach of the employment agreement by the Appellee. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign after refusing to resell plasma products he believed were unsafe for medical use due to improper shipping handling. Additionally, the Appellant alleges he was ordered to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed, constituting fraud. The Appellee denies these allegations, but the existence of these disputed facts complicates the Appellee’s claim to a likelihood of success and tips the balance of hardships decisively in the Appellant’s favor. These defenses, if proven, would constitute a material breach releasing the Appellant from his obligations under the contract.

**Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee failed to establish irreparable harm in light of the liquidated damages clause, failed to provide evidence that the Appellant violated the specific terms of the covenant, and failed to show that the balance of hardships favored injunctive relief. Therefore, we affirm our preliminary ruling, reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED and REMANDED.**
