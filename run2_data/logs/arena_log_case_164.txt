### I. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND A SUBSTANTIAL LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS

The plaintiff argues that the trial court erred in finding a likelihood of success on the merits because Bradley presented a material breach defense. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

#### A. The Trial Court Did Not Ignore Bradley's Defenses; It Properly Weighed the Evidence

At the injunction hearing, the trial court heard competing evidence regarding the circumstances of Bradley's departure from Health Coalition. The employer presented evidence that Bradley voluntarily resigned in April 1994. Bradley countered that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in alleged improper business practices. The employer specifically denied that any instruction was given to alter invoices.

Under Florida law, the trial court, sitting as the finder of fact at an injunction hearing, is entitled to assess the credibility of witnesses and resolve conflicts in the evidence. *See* Joseph v. Florida Parishes Bank, 118 So. 2d 436, 438 (Fla. 2d DCA 1960) (holding that the trial court, sitting without a jury, is the sole judge of witness credibility). The trial court's decision to credit the employer's version of events—particularly the denial that any improper instruction was given—does not constitute error. It reflects the proper function of the trial court as the initial finder of fact.

Bradley's reliance on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Kast*, 637 So. 2d 929 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994), is misplaced. In *Kast*, the court held that an employee's defense of material breach may defeat a motion for injunctive relief *if the breach is established by substantial evidence*. Here, the trial court found that Bradley failed to carry his burden of proving material breach. The evidence was contested, and the trial court resolved the conflict against Bradley. This Court should not reweigh the evidence on appellate review. *See* Canell v. Florida Power Corp., 396 So. 2d 1147, 1151 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981) (appellate courts do not reweigh evidence or substitute their judgment for that of the trial court).

#### B. The Alleged Breach Did Not Occur Until After Bradley's Employment Ended

Even assuming *arguendo* that Health Coalition failed to pay certain commissions, this alleged breach occurred *after* Bradley's employment terminated. The covenant not to compete was designed to protect Health Coalition's customer relationships *during* and *after* the employment relationship. The time sequence is critical: the covenant became operative only upon termination of employment.

Under Florida law, a covenant not to compete is enforceable when the employer has a legitimate business interest to protect and the covenant is reasonable in scope, duration, and geographic reach. *See* Mason v. Hillsborough County Bar Ass'n, 412 So. 2d 411, 413 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982). The covenant in this case protected "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition—a legitimate protectable interest. Bradley cannot escape his obligations under the covenant by pointing to post-termination disputes about commission payments that arose *after* he began working for a competitor.

Moreover, the employment agreement contained a liquidated damages clause specifically addressing breaches of the covenant. This provision reflects the parties' intent that monetary damages would be the exclusive remedy for breaches of the restrictive covenant—not a defense to its enforcement.

#### C. The Alleged Invoice Alteration Instruction Was Denied and Not Substantiated

Bradley claims he was instructed to alter invoices to charge customers higher prices than agreed upon, but the employer *specifically denied* that any such instruction was given. This is a classic example of a disputed fact issue that the trial court resolved against the moving party. The trial court was entitled to credit the employer's denial. Bradley's self-serving allegations, without corroboration, do not constitute the "uncontroverted evidence" required to defeat a motion for temporary injunction.

### II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND IRREPARABLE HARM

The plaintiff argues that the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm because the noncompetition agreement contained a liquidated damages clause. This argument reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of Florida law regarding injunctive relief.

#### A. Liquidated Damages Clauses Do Not Automatically Negate Irreparable Harm in Noncompete Cases

The plaintiff cites *B & B Mobile Home Sales, Inc. v. Pate*, 431 So. 2d 1007 (Fla. 2d DCA 1983), for the proposition that a liquidated damages clause negates irreparable harm. However, *B & B Mobile Home Sales* is distinguishable. That case involved a contract for the sale of goods where the sole issue was monetary compensation for breach. Noncompetition agreements present different considerations.

Florida courts have consistently recognized that noncompetition agreements involve unique circumstances where money damages may be inadequate. *See* Florida-Georgia LLC v. H.W. Bedford, 870 So. 2d 90, 96 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004) (holding that loss of customers and goodwill constitutes irreparable harm that cannot be adequately compensated by money damages). The covenant here specifically protects "active, paid up customers"—a business asset that is unique and not readily replaceable. Once a competitor solicits these customers, the harm is done and cannot be undone through monetary compensation.

The trial court was therefore correct in finding that irreparable harm would result from Bradley's continued solicitation of Health Coalition's customers. The liquidated damages clause provides a measure of damages *if* the covenant is breached, but it does not prevent Health Coalition from seeking injunctive relief to *prevent* the breach from occurring in the first place.

#### B. The Nature of the Protected Interest Supports the Irreparable Harm Finding

The covenant not to compete protects the confidentiality of corporate information and prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Customer relationships are recognized under Florida law as protectable business interests that justify injunctive relief. *See* Capraro v. Tilton, 549 So. 2d 1212, 1215 (Fla. 4th DCA 1989) (holding that customer lists and relationships constitute legitimate business interests protected by noncompetition agreements).

The plaintiff presented no evidence that the liquidated damages clause was unreasonable or that it would adequately compensate Health Coalition for the loss of its customer relationships. Speculation about the adequacy of monetary remedies is insufficient to overcome the presumption of irreparable harm in cases involving customer relationships. *See* Davis v. Monahan, 832 So. 2d 791, 795 (Fla. 2002) (recognizing that injunctive relief is appropriate to protect customer relationships).

---

### III. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY APPLIED THE STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

#### A. The Standard for Temporary Injunctions in Florida

To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) the likelihood of irreparable harm absent injunctive relief; (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) that the public interest supports injunctive relief. *See* Freedom Communications, Inc. v. Matas, 841 So. 2d 472, 475 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003). The trial court found that Health Coalition met all four requirements.

The plaintiff argues that the trial court "ignored" his defenses. However, the record demonstrates that the trial court considered Bradley's arguments and simply found them unpersuasive. This is not error; it is the proper function of the trial court to evaluate evidence and determine the weight to be given to competing arguments.

#### B. The Trial Court Did Not Shift the Burden of Proof

The plaintiff argues that the trial court "shifted the burden of proof" to Bradley. This argument is without merit. The movant (Health Coalition) bears the initial burden of demonstrating entitlement to injunctive relief. The trial court found that Health Coalition met this burden by presenting evidence of the valid noncompetition agreement, Bradley's employment with a competitor, and the threat of solicitation of protected customers.

Once Health Coalition established a prima facie case for injunctive relief, Bradley was entitled to present his defenses. The trial court considered those defenses but found them unpersuasive based on the evidence presented. This is not a "shift" of the burden; it is the ordinary process of adjudication.

---

### IV. THE COVENANT NOT TO COMPETE IS REASONABLE AND ENFORCEABLE

#### A. The Covenant Satisfies Florida's Requirements for Enforceability

Florida courts enforce noncompetition agreements when they are reasonable in duration, geographic scope, and scope of activity. *See* Wallace v. St. Augustine's Medical Clinic, 498 So. 2d 488, 489 (Fla. 1st DCA 1986). The covenant in this case prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers" for a period of two years—a reasonable restriction designed to protect legitimate business interests.

The plaintiff argues that the covenant should not be enforced because of alleged misconduct by the employer. However, this argument conflates two separate issues. The covenant was designed to protect Health Coalition's customer relationships regardless of the circumstances surrounding Bradley's departure. Even if Bradley had valid claims for unpaid commissions, those claims do not automatically invalidate the restrictive covenant.

#### B. The Doctrine of Unclean Hands Does Not Apply

The plaintiff suggests that Health Coalition cannot enforce the covenant because it allegedly engaged in improper conduct. This argument invokes the doctrine of unclean hands. However, the doctrine applies only when the plaintiff's own conduct is directly related to the subject matter of the litigation. *See* First Atlantic Funding Corp. v. GDC Properties, LLC, 958 So. 2d 1024, 1028 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007).

Here, Bradley's alleged concerns about invoice alterations relate to his own conduct during employment—not to the enforceability of the covenant. The covenant protects Health Coalition's customer relationships; it does not relate to the specific invoices Bradley claims he was asked to alter. Even assuming *arguendo* that such an instruction was given (which the employer denied), this does not constitute a defense to enforcement of the covenant.

The covenant not to compete became effective immediately upon the termination of employment. A breach occurring after the covenant has already been triggered cannot retroactively excuse the employee from obligations that vested at the moment of termination. The noncompete was designed to protect Health Coalition’s customer relationships during the post-employment period. Bradley’s argument that a post-termination payment dispute invalidates the restrictive covenant is unavailing. See *Mason v. ### I. INTRODUCTION  
The trial court erred in modifying and extending the temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete against Appellant Martin J. Bradley. ### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN REJECTING BRADLEY’S MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSE  

#### A. The Defense Misstates the Law on Material Breach and Fails to Acknowledge Breaches Occurring *During* Employment  
The defense claims Bradley’s material breach defense was properly rejected because any alleged breach "occurred after termination." This is a fundamental mischaracterization of the record. Bradley presented evidence that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement *during* the employment relationship by instructing him to engage in improper business practices—a breach that occurred *before* the covenant not to compete became effective. As this Court recognized in *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Kast*, 637 So. 2d 929, 931 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994), "an employer’s material breach of an employment agreement excuses the employee from performing under a noncompetition covenant." Here, the employer’s conduct—specifically, its instructions to Bradley to engage in improper business practices—constituted a breach of the employment agreement that arose *while Bradley was still employed*, not after termination.  

The trial court erroneously conflated this with the post-termination commission dispute. The defense’s opening statement ignores that the material breach defense includes *both* the commission dispute *and* the improper business practices alleged to have occurred during employment. By dismissing the latter based solely on "credibility assessments," the trial court failed to recognize that Bradley’s evidence created a genuine issue of material fact on whether a breach occurred *before* the covenant’s effective date. Under Florida law, when a genuine factual dispute exists regarding material breach, the movant cannot establish a "substantial likelihood of success on the merits"—the first prerequisite for a temporary injunction. *See* Freedom Communications, Inc. v. Matas, 841 So. 2d 472, 475 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003) (requiring "substantial likelihood of success on the merits" for injunctive relief). The trial court’s unilateral crediting of Health Coalition’s denial—without considering whether Bradley’s evidence was sufficient to raise a triable issue—constituted reversible error.  

#### B. The Defense Ignores That the Trial Court Misapplied *Kast* and *Mason*  
The defense cites *Mason v. Hillsborough County Bar Ass’n*, 412 So. 2d 411 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982), to argue that the covenant is enforceable because Health Coalition has a "legitimate business interest." But *Mason* does not address material breach defenses; it merely holds that covenants are enforceable if reasonable and protecting legitimate interests. Crucially, *Kast*—which the defense dismisses as "misplaced"—directly controls here. *Kast* holds that material breach by the employer *excuses* the employee from the covenant, regardless of whether the employer has a legitimate interest. 637 So. 2d at 931. By failing to address whether Health Coalition’s alleged breaches during employment rendered the covenant unenforceable, the trial court ignored binding precedent. This error alone requires reversal.  

---

### III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING IRREPARABLE HARM  

#### A. The Liquidated Damages Clause Negates Irreparable Harm as a Matter of Law  
The defense argues that "liquidated damages clauses do not automatically negate irreparable harm in noncompete cases," citing *Florida-Georgia LLC v. H.W. Bedford*, 870 So. 2d 90 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004), and *Capraro v. Tilton*, 549 So. 2d 1212 (Fla. 4th DCA 1989). This argument ignores controlling precedent. In *B & B Mobile Home Sales, Inc. v. Pate*, 431 So. 2d 1007, 1009 (Fla. 2d DCA 1983), this Court squarely held that "when a contract contains a liquidated damages clause, it is generally presumed that the parties have agreed that damages would be the exclusive remedy for breach, and thus irreparable harm cannot be shown." Here, the noncompetition agreement explicitly includes a liquidated damages clause for breaches of the covenant. This clause demonstrates that the parties intended monetary damages to be the sole remedy for any violation—making irreparable harm legally impossible to prove.  

*Florida-Georgia LLC* and *Capraro* are inapposite because neither case involved a liquidated damages clause for the noncompete breach. *Florida-Georgia LLC* addressed whether customer relationships constitute irreparable harm *in the absence of a liquidated damages clause*—a distinction the trial court overlooked. 870 So. 2d at 96. Similarly, *Capraro* upheld an injunction where no liquidated damages clause existed. 549 So. 2d at 1215. By ignoring *B & B Mobile Home Sales*, the trial court misapplied Florida law and erroneously found irreparable harm where none could exist as a matter of law.  

#### B. The Defense Misrepresents the Purpose of Liquidated Damages Clauses  
The defense claims that the liquidated damages clause "provides a measure of damages *if* the covenant is breached, but it does not prevent Health Coalition from seeking injunctive relief to *prevent* the breach." This is incorrect. *B & B Mobile Home Sales* holds that liquidated damages clauses *preclude* injunctive relief because they establish money damages as the exclusive remedy. 431 So. 2d at 1009. The clause here was included precisely to avoid the need for injunctions; it reflects the parties’ agreement that monetary compensation suffices for any breach. Permitting injunctions in such cases would render the clause meaningless and contradict the parties’ contractual intent. *See* Davis v. Monahan, 832 So. 2d 791, 795 (Fla. 2002) (noting that parties’ contractual remedies govern the scope of relief). The trial court’s finding of irreparable harm was legally unsupportable.  

---

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN BALANCING EQUITIES AND PUBLIC INTEREST  

#### A. The Balance of Hardships Heavily Favors Bradley  
The defense asserts that the "balance of hardships tips in Health Coalition’s favor," but this ignores the stark reality: Bradley faces immediate economic harm from being barred from employment, while Health Coalition has a contractual remedy (liquidated damages) for any breach. *See* Canakaris v. Canakaris, 382 So. 2d 1197, 1203 (Fla. 1980) (requiring courts to balance hardships when issuing injunctions). The trial court’s injunction effectively deprives Bradley of his livelihood without justification, as Health Coalition’s alleged harm is fully compensable by money damages. This imbalance alone warrants reversal.  

#### B. The Public Interest Favors Enforcing Contractual Remedies Over Injunctions  
The defense’s claim that the "public interest supports injunctive relief" is contradicted by Florida law. Public policy favors upholding contractual liquidated damages clauses as they promote certainty and discourage litigation. *See* B & B Mobile Home Sales, 431 So. 2d at 1009. Enforcing an injunction here would undermine this policy by allowing Health Coalition to bypass its agreed-upon remedy. Moreover, noncompetition agreements that restrict employment opportunities raise significant public interest concerns—particularly when no irreparable harm exists. *See* Freedom Communications, 841 So. 2d at 475 (public interest requires courts to avoid unnecessary restrictions on trade).

The trial court committed reversible error in three critical respects:

### II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY REJECTED BRADLEY'S MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSE

**OPINION ON REBUTTALS**

This Court has carefully considered the rebuttals filed by both Appellant Martin J. Bradley and Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. regarding the trial court’s order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. After reviewing the arguments presented and the applicable law, we find that the trial court did not abuse its discretion. Accordingly, we affirm the temporary injunction.

**I. Standard of Review**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *See* Canakaris v. Canakaris, 382 So. 2d 1197, 1203 (Fla. 1980). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the balance of equities tips in the movant’s favor; and (4) that the injunction is in the public interest. *See* Freedom Communications, Inc. v. Matas, 841 So. 2d 472, 475 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003).

**II. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Rejecting the Material Breach Defense**

Bradley argues that the trial court erred in rejecting his defense of material breach, specifically contending that Health Coalition instructed him to alter invoices to defraud customers during his employment. He relies on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Kast*, 637 So. 2d 929 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994), for the proposition that an employer’s material breach excuses the employee from non-compete obligations.

However, the trial court heard conflicting evidence regarding this allegation and was entitled to assess the credibility of the witnesses. As this Court has previously held, the trial court is the sole arbiter of witness credibility in injunction proceedings. *See* Joseph v. Florida Parishes Bank, 118 So. 2d 436, 438 (Fla. 2d DCA 1960). The trial court apparently credited the employer’s denial that any such instruction was given. It is not the role of this Court to reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court regarding witness credibility. *See* Canell v. Florida Power Corp., 396 So. 2d 1147, 1151 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981).

Unlike in *Kast*, where the material breach was established by undisputed evidence, the record here reflects a classic "he-said, she-said" dispute which the trial court resolved in favor of Health Coalition. Because Bradley failed to establish the material breach by substantial evidence, the trial court correctly found that Health Coalition had a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. Furthermore, the dispute regarding unpaid commissions arose after the termination of employment and could not retroactively excuse obligations that vested at the moment of termination.

**III. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm**

Bradley argues that the existence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement precludes a finding of irreparable harm, citing *B & B Mobile Home Sales, Inc. v. Pate*, 431 So. 2d 1007 (Fla. 2d DCA 1983). While *B & B Mobile Home Sales* held that a liquidated damages clause in a lease agreement may indicate that money damages are the exclusive remedy, that case is distinguishable from the matter at hand.

Florida law recognizes a distinction in the context of noncompetition agreements: the loss of customer relationships and goodwill constitutes a unique form of injury that cannot be fully compensated by monetary damages, even when a liquidated damages clause exists. *See* Florida-Georgia LLC v. H.W. Bedford, 870 So. 2d 90, 96 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004) (holding that loss of customers and goodwill constitutes irreparable harm that cannot be adequately compensated by money damages). The presence of a liquidated damages clause provides a measure of damages for a breach that has already occurred but does not prevent the employer from seeking injunctive relief to prevent the irreparable harm of losing customer relationships in the first instance. *See* Capraro v. Tilton, 549 So. 2d 1212, 1215 (Fla. 4th DCA 1989).

The covenant in this case specifically protects "active, paid up customers," a classic example of a legitimate business interest that warrants injunctive relief. *See* Mason v. Hillsborough County Bar Ass'n, 412 So. 2d 411, 413 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982). Once Bradley solicits these customers, the damage to Health Coalition’s business relationships is immediate and difficult, if not impossible, to quantify or repair with money alone. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding that irreparable harm was likely.

**IV. The Balance of Equities and Public Interest**

Bradley argues that the balance of hardships favors him because he faces immediate economic harm, whereas Health Coalition has a contractual remedy. However, the trial court properly weighed these competing interests. The loss of established customer relationships is a permanent injury that cannot be rectified by money damages, whereas Bradley’s restriction is limited in duration and scope. *See* Freedom Communications, Inc. v. Matas, 841 So. 2d 472, 475 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003). The public interest supports the enforcement of reasonable noncompetition agreements that protect legitimate business interests. *See* Mason v. Hillsborough County Bar Ass'n, 412 So. 2d 411, 413 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982).

**V. Conclusion**

The trial court properly applied the law to the facts and did not err in granting the temporary injunction. The defenses raised by Bradley regarding material breach and the adequacy of legal remedies were factually unsupported or legally insufficient to overcome the employer’s showing of need for equitable relief. The trial court’s findings regarding witness credibility and the existence of irreparable harm are supported by the record and applicable precedent.

**Accordingly, we affirm the order of the trial court modifying and extending the temporary injunction.**

Here, Bradley presented credible evidence that Health Coalition breached the agreement *during* his employment—by withholding earned commissions and directing him to falsify invoices—and the trial court improperly dismissed this as a mere "he-said, she-said" dispute.  

The law is clear: A trial court cannot resolve factual disputes at the injunction stage. *Joseph v. Florida Parishes Bank*, 118 So. 2d 436, 438 (Fla. 2d DCA 1960), confirms that while credibility assessments are within the trial court’s purview, it must accept the non-moving party’s evidence as true when determining whether a genuine issue of material fact exists. Yet the trial court here ignored this principle, weighing evidence and crediting the employer’s denial without any evidentiary basis. This is not judicial discretion—it is judicial overreach. *Canell v. Florida Power Corp.*, 396 So. 2d 1147, 1151 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981), forbids appellate courts from substituting their judgment for the trial court on credibility, but it equally forbids trial courts from resolving factual disputes that belong at trial. By doing so, the trial court violated the very standard of review it was bound to follow.

If a plaintiff’s own evidence creates a genuine dispute about the employer’s misconduct, the noncompete cannot be enforced preliminarily. The clause here explicitly provides for a predetermined remedy for breach—meaning the parties agreed that monetary compensation, not injunctive relief, is the proper recourse.

**I. Standard of Review**

Appellant argues that the trial court erred in rejecting his defense of material breach, specifically contending that Health Coalition instructed him to alter invoices to defraud customers and refused to pay earned commissions. He relies on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Kast*, 637 So. 2d 929 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994), for the proposition that an employer’s material breach excuses the employee from non-compete obligations.

We find no error in the trial court’s ruling. The trial court heard conflicting evidence regarding the alleged instruction to alter invoices. As this Court has previously held, the trial court is the sole arbiter of witness credibility in injunction proceedings. *See* Joseph v. Florida Parishes Bank, 118 So. 2d 436, 438 (Fla. 2d DCA 1960). The trial court was entitled to credit the employer’s denial that any such instruction was given. It is not the role of this Court to reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court regarding witness credibility. *See* Canell v. Florida Power Corp., 396 So. 2d 1147, 1151 (Fla. 1st DCA 1981).

Appellant’s reliance on *Joseph* to argue that the trial court must accept the non-moving party’s evidence as true is misplaced. *Joseph* establishes the trial court’s role in assessing credibility, not a requirement that the court accept the non-movant’s version of events as undisputed fact. Unlike in *Kast*, where the material breach was established by undisputed evidence, the record here reflects a dispute of fact which the trial court resolved in favor of Health Coalition. Because Appellant failed to establish the material breach by substantial evidence, the trial court correctly found that Health Coalition had a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

Furthermore, the dispute regarding unpaid commissions arose after the termination of employment. The covenant not to compete became enforceable upon the termination of employment in April 1994. Any alleged breach occurring thereafter could not retroactively excuse obligations that vested at the moment of termination.

**III. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm**

Appellant argues that the existence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement precludes a finding of irreparable harm, citing *B & B Mobile Home Sales, Inc. v. Pate*, 431 So. 2d 1007 (Fla. 2d DCA 1983). While *Pate* held that a liquidated damages clause in a lease agreement may indicate that money damages are the exclusive remedy, that case is distinguishable from the matter at hand.

Florida law recognizes a distinction in the context of noncompetition agreements: the loss of customer relationships and goodwill constitutes a unique form of injury that cannot be fully compensated by monetary damages, even when a liquidated damages clause exists. *See* Florida-Georgia LLC v. H.W. Bedford, 870 So. 2d 90, 96 (Fla. 2d DCA 2004) (holding that loss of customers and goodwill constitutes irreparable harm that cannot be adequately compensated by money damages).

Appellant’s attempt to distinguish *Florida-Georgia LLC* and *Capraro v. Tilton*, 549 So. 2d 1212 (Fla. 4th DCA 1989), is unavailing. In *Capraro*, this Court held that the presence of a liquidated damages clause provides a measure of damages for a breach that has already occurred but does not prevent the employer from seeking injunctive relief to prevent the irreparable harm of losing customer relationships in the first instance. *Capraro*, 549 So. 2d at 1215. The covenant in this case specifically protects "active, paid up customers," a classic example of a legitimate business interest that warrants injunctive relief. *See* Mason v. Hillsborough County Bar Ass'n, 412 So. 2d 411, 413 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982). Once Appellant solicits these customers, the damage to Health Coalition’s business relationships is immediate and difficult, if not impossible, to quantify or repair with money alone. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding that irreparable harm was likely.

**IV. The Balance of Equities and Public Interest**

Appellant argues that the balance of hardships favors him because he faces immediate economic harm, whereas Health Coalition has a contractual remedy. However, the trial court properly weighed these competing interests. The loss of established customer relationships is a permanent injury that cannot be rectified by money damages, whereas Appellant’s restriction is limited in duration and scope. *See* Freedom Communications, Inc. v. Matas, 841 So. 2d 472, 475 (Fla. 2d DCA 2003). The public interest supports the enforcement of reasonable noncompetition agreements that protect legitimate business interests. *See* Mason v. Hillsborough County Bar Ass'n, 412 So. 2d 411, 413 (Fla. 2d DCA 1982).

**V. Conclusion**

The trial court properly applied the law to the facts and did not err in granting the temporary injunction. The defenses raised by Appellant regarding material breach and the adequacy of legal remedies were factually unsupported or legally insufficient to overcome the employer’s showing of need for equitable relief. The trial court’s findings regarding witness credibility and the existence of irreparable harm are supported by the record and applicable precedent.

**Accordingly, we affirm the order of the trial court modifying and extending the temporary injunction.**
