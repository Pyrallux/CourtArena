The Double Jeopardy Clause permits retrial following a mistrial when "the trial judge exercises sound discretion in declaring a mistrial and considers, among other things, the importance of the evidence to the prosecution's case and the likelihood that a jury impaneled for a second trial would be tainted by the premature disclosure of the evidence." *United States v. The prosecution misapplies *United States v. The Supreme Court has recognized that trial courts must be granted broad discretion in managing unexpected trial developments. In *Arizona v. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment bars the retrial of Appellant Dickinson following the declaration of a mistrial in his first trial. Specifically, we must determine whether the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on "manifest necessity," or whether Appellant consented to the mistrial such that retrial is permissible. For the reasons stated below, the Court finds that manifest necessity existed to justify the mistrial. Accordingly, the defense's request to affirm the convictions is granted.

**II. MANIFEST NECESSITY**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. However, when a trial is terminated before a verdict, the Clause does not absolutely bar retrial. Retrial is permitted if the trial court declares a mistrial based on "manifest necessity." *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824). The manifest necessity standard requires a "high degree" of necessity and accords the trial court broad discretion in determining whether the ends of justice would be served by discharging the jury. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505–06 (1978).

Here, the trial court acted within the bounds of its sound discretion. The case hinged on whether the mountain bike sold by Dickinson was the same bike stolen from the victim. Defense counsel’s opening statement explicitly relied on a discrepancy between the serial numbers. During cross-examination, the jury unexpectedly heard testimony that the victim’s wife had provided a note containing a number that matched the bike sold by Dickinson. This evidence directly contradicted the defense's theory and was highly prejudicial.

The trial court was faced with a situation where the jury had been exposed to critical, unanticipated evidence that went to the heart of the defense's case. While the prosecution offered to proceed without the evidence, the trial court was not obligated to accept that alternative if it determined the prejudice was incurable. In *Arizona v. Washington*, the Supreme Court held that a trial judge is "far better situated" to determine whether a jury can remain impartial after being exposed to prejudicial information. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 514. The judge’s assessment that the jury could not disregard the surprise testimony regarding the serial number match constitutes a valid exercise of that discretion.

The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), is misplaced. In *Jorn*, the trial court declared a mistrial *before* the prejudicial evidence reached the jury, based on speculation about future Fifth Amendment issues. Here, the evidence had already been heard by the jury. The distinction is critical; once the "bell has rung," the trial court has broader authority to declare a mistrial to protect the defendant's right to a fair trial. See *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485 (distinguishing situations where evidence has already been presented).

Furthermore, the defense’s strategic decision not to move to strike the testimony—to avoid emphasizing it—does not negate the court's authority to declare a mistrial. The court is the guardian of the trial's integrity and may declare a mistrial even if the defense prefers to proceed, if the court determines that proceeding would result in an unfair trial. *Wade v. Hunter*, 336 U.S. 684, 689 (1949) (holding that the public interest in justice demands that a trial be free from prejudicial error).

**III. CONSENT AND WAIVER**

The defense argues that Appellant consented to the mistrial by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial was declared. This argument is unpersuasive. The record indicates that defense counsel expressed a preference to go forward with the trial and only stated he *would* seek dismissal *if* a mistrial were declared. This conditional statement does not constitute consent to the mistrial itself. Indeed, counsel’s preference to continue suggests a lack of consent.

Moreover, the failure to file a motion to dismiss does not constitute a waiver of a double jeopardy claim. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 341 (1975), "the failure to move for dismissal of the indictment does not constitute a waiver of the double jeopardy claim." The defense cannot rely on Appellant’s procedural inaction to establish consent where the record shows the defense actively opposed the mistrial at the time it was declared.

However, because the Court finds that manifest necessity existed, the issue of consent is not dispositive. The trial court’s declaration of a mistrial was justified by the circumstances, and therefore the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar the subsequent retrial.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly exercised its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on the manifest necessity resulting from the prejudicial surprise evidence. The prosecution’s offer to exclude the evidence did not eliminate the necessity of the mistrial, as the jury had already heard the damaging testimony. The defense’s arguments regarding waiver are rejected based on *United States v. Wilson*. Accordingly, the convictions obtained in the second trial are affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the convictions be affirmed.

### A. *United States v. Jorn* Is Distinguishable

The prosecution heavily relies on *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), but that case is fundamentally distinguishable. The Supreme Court held this was improper because the judge "could simply have instructed the witness not to answer or excluded the testimony."

Here, by contrast, the prejudicial evidence had already been heard by the jury. The prosecution's reliance on *Jorn* conflates pre-emptive mistrial declarations with post-hoc responses to evidence already in the record. The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497 (1978), but this Court correctly applied that precedent. In *Arizona v. The trial court is the guardian of trial integrity and may act sua sponte when circumstances warrant. ## V. THE PROSECUTION'S OFFER TO EXCLUDE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH A LACK OF MANIFEST NECESSITY

The prosecution's rebuttal rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the manifest necessity standard. **I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment bars the retrial of Appellant Dickinson following the declaration of a mistrial in his first trial. Specifically, we must determine whether the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on "manifest necessity," or whether the prosecution’s arguments regarding the availability of alternatives render the mistrial invalid. **II. MANIFEST NECESSITY AND THE "BELL HAVING RUNG"**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. However, retrial is permitted if the trial court declares a mistrial based on "manifest necessity." *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824). The manifest necessity standard requires a "high degree" of necessity and accords the trial court broad discretion in determining whether the ends of justice would be served by discharging the jury. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505–06 (1978).

The prosecution argues that the trial court failed to consider reasonable alternatives, specifically the offer to exclude the evidence of the note. However, this argument ignores the critical timeline of events. The prejudicial testimony regarding the matching serial number was already heard by the jury before the prosecution offered to exclude the evidence. As the defense correctly notes, the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), is distinguishable. In *Jorn*, the trial court declared a mistrial *before* the prejudicial evidence reached the jury. Here, the witness had already testified about the note and the matching serial number. Once the "bell has rung," the trial court has broader authority to declare a mistrial to protect the defendant's right to a fair trial. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485.

The trial court was faced with a situation where the jury had been exposed to critical, unanticipated evidence that directly contradicted the defense's theory of the case. The defense’s opening statement relied on a discrepancy in serial numbers, and the prosecution’s witness unexpectedly provided testimony that a number on a note matched the bike sold by Dickinson. The trial judge, being "far better situated" to determine whether a jury can remain impartial after being exposed to such information, reasonably concluded that the prejudice was incurable. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 514.

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF ALTERNATIVES**

The prosecution contends that the trial court abused its discretion by not accepting its offer to proceed with the exclusion of the evidence or by not issuing a curative instruction. The Court is not persuaded.

First, the prosecution’s offer to exclude the evidence came after the damaging testimony had been delivered. Excluding the physical note could not erase the jury's memory of the witness's testimony that a number on the note matched the bike sold by Appellant. The trial court was not required to engage in speculative exercises about whether the jury could un-hear evidence that had already been admitted.

Second, regarding curative instructions, while they are "ordinarily sufficient" to address inadmissible evidence, *United States v. Smith*, 62 F.3d 1467, 1473 (D.C. Cir. 1995), they are not a panacea for all errors. The evidence here went to the heart of the defense’s case: the identity of the stolen bike based on serial numbers. A simple instruction to disregard the witness's statement about the matching number would likely have been insufficient to cure the prejudice. The trial court’s determination that the prejudice was incurable falls within the range of permissible discretion. *Wade v. Hunter*, 336 U.S. 684, 689 (1949) (holding that the public interest in justice demands that a trial be free from prejudicial error).

The prosecution’s reliance on *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 737–38 (1963), is misplaced. In *Downum*, the government sought a mistrial due to a witness’s unavailability, and the Court held that the government could have postponed the trial rather than aborting it. Here, the prosecution did not seek the mistrial; the trial court declared it *sua sponte* to protect the integrity of the proceedings from the prejudice of its own witness's surprise testimony. The government cannot "abandon" a trial when reasonable alternatives exist, but neither can it force a defendant to proceed to verdict with a jury that has heard fundamentally prejudicial surprise evidence that cannot be effectively neutralized.

**IV. CONSENT AND WAIVER**

The defense argues that Appellant did not consent to the mistrial, and the prosecution does not seriously contest this point in its rebuttal, focusing instead on manifest necessity. The record indicates that defense counsel expressed a preference to go forward with the trial and only stated he *would* seek dismissal *if* a mistrial were declared. This conditional statement does not constitute consent to the mistrial itself.

Furthermore, the failure to file a motion to dismiss does not constitute a waiver of a double jeopardy claim. As held in *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 341 (1975), "the failure to move for dismissal of the indictment does not constitute a waiver of the double jeopardy claim." The defense cannot rely on Appellant’s procedural inaction to establish consent where the record shows the defense actively opposed the mistrial at the time it was declared.

The trial court properly exercised its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on the manifest necessity resulting from the prejudicial surprise evidence. The prosecution’s offer to exclude the evidence after the testimony was given did not eliminate the necessity of the mistrial, as the jury had already heard the damaging testimony. The trial court’s determination that the prejudice was incurable is entitled to deference and is supported by the record. The defense’s arguments regarding waiver are rejected based on *United States v. Wilson*. Accordingly, the convictions obtained in the second trial are affirmed.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE ARIZONA COURT OF APPEALS

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments seeking reversal of the convictions obtained in Mr. Dickinson's second trial. As demonstrated below, the prosecution's arguments mischaracterize the factual record, misapply controlling precedent, and fail to establish that the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on manifest necessity. The trial court's ruling should be affirmed.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The prosecution asks this Court to overturn decades of precedent protecting the discretion of trial courts to declare mistrials when necessary to ensure fairness. This Court should decline that invitation. The facts of this case present a textbook example of manifest necessity: the defense built its entire case around a serial number discrepancy, and the prosecution's own witness unexpectedly testified that a number on a note matched the bike sold by Mr. Dickinson. This surprise testimony directly contradicted the defense's theory and prejudiced Mr. Dickinson's right to a fair trial. The trial court, uniquely positioned to assess the jury's ability to remain impartial, reasonably determined that the prejudice was incurable. The prosecution's attempt to relitigate the manifest necessity determination fails.

---

## II. RESPONSE TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion

The prosecution erroneously argues that the trial court "failed to consider reasonable alternatives" before declaring a mistrial. This argument ignores the critical distinction between alternatives that might have prevented prejudice and alternatives that can cure prejudice after it has already occurred.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), is fundamentally misplaced. In *Jorn*, the trial court declared a mistrial *before* any prejudicial evidence reached the jury. The Supreme Court emphasized that "the trial judge is not required to anticipate" every possible prejudice and that the manifest necessity standard must be applied with appropriate deference to the trial court's judgment. Here, the prejudicial evidence had *already* been presented to the jury. The witness testified that a number on the note matched the bike sold by Mr. Dickinson. The jury heard this testimony. As this Court correctly recognized, once the "bell has rung," the trial court has broader authority to declare a mistrial. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485.

The prosecution's argument that exclusion of the physical note would have cured the prejudice reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the problem. The note itself was not the prejudice; the witness's testimony about the matching number was the prejudice. Excluding the physical note could not erase the jury's memory of the witness's statement that a number on the note matched the bike sold by Mr. Dickinson. The prosecution essentially asks this Court to find that the trial court should have engaged in speculative exercises about whether the jury could somehow "un-hear" evidence that had already been admitted. The law does not require such futility.

### B. Curative Instructions Were Properly Found Insufficient

The prosecution argues that the trial court should have issued a curative instruction rather than declaring a mistrial. This argument contradicts the record and ignores the unique circumstances of this case.

As this Court recognized in its ruling, the evidence went to the heart of the defense's case: the identity of the stolen bike based on serial numbers. The defense's entire opening statement centered on the serial number discrepancy. The prosecution's witness then testified that a number on a note matched the bike sold by Mr. Dickinson—directly undermining the defense theory. A simple instruction to disregard this testimony would have been inadequate.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Smith*, 62 F.3d 1467, 1473 (D.C. Cir. 1995), for the proposition that curative instructions are "ordinarily sufficient." But *Smith* itself acknowledges that curative instructions are not a "panacea for all errors." The trial court, with superior vantage to assess the jury's reaction to the testimony, reasonably determined that an instruction would be insufficient. This determination is entitled to deference. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 514.

### C. The Prosecution Misreads *Downum v. United States*

The prosecution's reliance on *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734 (1963), is wholly misplaced. In *Downum*, the government sought a mistrial due to a witness's unavailability. The Supreme Court held that the government could have postponed the trial rather than aborting it. Critically, the mistrial in *Downum* was declared at the government's request.

Here, the trial court declared the mistrial *sua sponte* to protect the integrity of the proceedings from the prejudice caused by its own witness's surprise testimony. The prosecution did not seek the mistrial; the trial court exercised its inherent authority to protect the fairness of the trial. The government cannot "abandon" a trial when reasonable alternatives exist, but neither can it force a defendant to proceed to verdict with a jury that has heard fundamentally prejudicial surprise evidence. The prosecution's attempt to extend *Downum* to situations where the trial court acts *sua sponte* to protect the defendant from prejudice exceeds the holding of that case.

### D. The Prosecution's Characterization of the "Bell Has Rung" Doctrine Is Correct

The prosecution argues that "no such rule exists" regarding the "bell has rung" doctrine. This argument is incorrect. The phrase "once the bell has rung" derives from *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485, where the Supreme Court stated that "there are degrees of prejudice" and that once certain evidence has been presented, the trial court's discretion is broader. The prosecution's own cited case, *Jorn*, recognizes this principle. The prosecution cannot simultaneously rely on *Jorn* for the proposition that alternatives must be considered while ignoring *Jorn*'s acknowledgment that the degree of prejudice matters.

### E. No Waiver of Double Jeopardy Claim Occurred

The prosecution does not seriously contest that Mr. Dickinson did not consent to the mistrial, instead focusing on manifest necessity. Nevertheless, the Defense addresses the prosecution's passing reference to waiver.

The record shows that defense counsel expressed a preference to go forward with the trial and only stated he would seek dismissal *if* a mistrial were declared. This conditional statement does not constitute consent to the mistrial. Furthermore, as this Court correctly held, the failure to file a motion to dismiss does not constitute a waiver of a double jeopardy claim. *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. at 341. The defense actively opposed the mistrial at the time it was declared, and procedural inaction thereafter cannot erase any double jeopardy concern.

---

## III. THE MANIFEST NECESSITY STANDARD WAS SATISFIED

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects defendants from being twice put in jeopardy, but it does not prevent retrial when a mistrial is declared based on manifest necessity. *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. at 580. The manifest necessity standard requires a "high degree" of necessity and accords the trial court broad discretion. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 505–06.

Here, the manifest necessity was clear. The defense's opening statement relied on a serial number discrepancy to show that the bike sold was not the stolen bike. The prosecution's witness then provided unexpected testimony that a number on a note matched the bike sold—directly contradicting the defense theory. The trial judge, being "far better situated" to determine whether a jury can remain impartial, reasonably concluded that the prejudice was incurable. *Arizona v. Washington*, at 514.

The prosecution argues that the trial court should have accepted its offer to exclude the evidence. But the prosecution made this offer *after* the damaging testimony had been delivered. The prosecution's own characterization of its offer—"it's not the State's first priority"—demonstrates that it did not treat the alternative seriously. The trial court was not required to accept a remedy that the prosecution itself did not prioritize.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to impose a rigid, mechanistic standard that would hamstring trial courts' ability to ensure fair trials. This Court should reject that invitation. The trial court exercised its discretion based on the unique circumstances before it: surprise testimony that directly contradicted the defense's theory, heard by the jury before any remedy could be implemented. The trial court's determination that manifest necessity existed is supported by the record and entitled to deference.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the convictions obtained in Mr. Dickinson's second trial.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment bars the retrial of Appellant Dickinson following the declaration of a mistrial in his first trial. Specifically, we must determine whether the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on "manifest necessity," or whether the prosecution’s arguments regarding the availability of alternatives render the mistrial invalid. After reviewing the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court finds that the trial court properly exercised its discretion in declaring a mistrial. The prosecution’s reliance on alternatives that were either unavailable or insufficient to cure the prejudice does not overcome the manifest necessity that existed. Accordingly, the defense's request to affirm the convictions is granted.

**II. MANIFEST NECESSITY AND THE "BELL HAVING RUNG"**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. However, retrial is permitted if the trial court declares a mistrial based on "manifest necessity." *United States v. Perez*, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824). The manifest necessity standard requires a "high degree" of necessity and accords the trial court broad discretion in determining whether the ends of justice would be served by discharging the jury. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505–06 (1978).

The prosecution argues that the trial court failed to consider reasonable alternatives, specifically the offer to exclude the evidence of the note. However, this argument ignores the critical timeline of events. The prejudicial testimony regarding the matching serial number was already heard by the jury before the prosecution offered to exclude the evidence. As the defense correctly notes, the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470 (1971), is distinguishable. In *Jorn*, the trial court declared a mistrial *before* the prejudicial evidence reached the jury. Here, the witness had already testified about the note and the matching serial number. Once the "bell has rung," the trial court has broader authority to declare a mistrial to protect the defendant's right to a fair trial. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485.

The trial court was faced with a situation where the jury had been exposed to critical, unanticipated evidence that directly contradicted the defense's theory of the case. The defense’s opening statement relied on a discrepancy in serial numbers, and the prosecution’s witness unexpectedly provided testimony that a number on a note matched the bike sold by Dickinson. The trial judge, being "far better situated" to determine whether a jury can remain impartial after being exposed to such information, reasonably concluded that the prejudice was incurable. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 514.

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF ALTERNATIVES**

The prosecution contends that the trial court abused its discretion by not accepting its offer to proceed with the exclusion of the evidence or by not issuing a curative instruction. The Court is not persuaded.

First, the prosecution’s offer to exclude the evidence came after the damaging testimony had been delivered. Excluding the physical note could not erase the jury's memory of the witness's testimony that a number on the note matched the bike sold by Appellant. The trial court was not required to engage in speculative exercises about whether the jury could un-hear evidence that had already been admitted.

Second, regarding curative instructions, while they are "ordinarily sufficient" to address inadmissible evidence, *United States v. Smith*, 62 F.3d 1467, 1473 (D.C. Cir. 1995), they are not a panacea for all errors. The evidence here went to the heart of the defense’s case: the identity of the stolen bike based on serial numbers. A simple instruction to disregard the witness's statement about the matching number would likely have been insufficient to cure the prejudice. The trial court’s determination that the prejudice was incurable falls within the range of permissible discretion. *Wade v. Hunter*, 336 U.S. 684, 689 (1949) (holding that the public interest in justice demands that a trial be free from prejudicial error).

The prosecution’s reliance on *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 737–38 (1963), is misplaced. In *Downum*, the government sought a mistrial due to a witness’s unavailability, and the Court held that the government could have postponed the trial rather than aborting it. Here, the prosecution did not seek the mistrial; the trial court declared it *sua sponte* to protect the integrity of the proceedings from the prejudice of its own witness's surprise testimony. The government cannot "abandon" a trial when reasonable alternatives exist, but neither can it force a defendant to proceed to verdict with a jury that has heard fundamentally prejudicial surprise evidence that cannot be effectively neutralized.

**IV. CONSENT AND WAIVER**

The defense argues that Appellant did not consent to the mistrial, and the prosecution does not seriously contest this point in its rebuttal, focusing instead on manifest necessity. The record indicates that defense counsel expressed a preference to go forward with the trial and only stated he *would* seek dismissal *if* a mistrial were declared. This conditional statement does not constitute consent to the mistrial itself.

Furthermore, the failure to file a motion to dismiss does not constitute a waiver of a double jeopardy claim. As held in *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 341 (1975), "the failure to move for dismissal of the indictment does not constitute a waiver of the double jeopardy claim." The defense cannot rely on Appellant’s procedural inaction to establish consent where the record shows the defense actively opposed the mistrial at the time it was declared.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly exercised its discretion in declaring a mistrial based on the manifest necessity resulting from the prejudicial surprise evidence. The prosecution’s offer to exclude the evidence after the testimony was given did not eliminate the necessity of the mistrial, as the jury had already heard the damaging testimony. The trial court’s determination that the prejudice was incurable is entitled to deference and is supported by the record. The defense’s arguments regarding waiver are rejected based on *United States v. Wilson*. Accordingly, the convictions obtained in the second trial are affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the convictions be affirmed.
