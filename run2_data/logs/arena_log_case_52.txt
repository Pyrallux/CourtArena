FACTS:
Appellee, a government contractor identified herein as John Doe, underwent military detention in Iraq. After his release, he filed this action in the district court against, inter alia, then-Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and a Bivens action for violation of his due process rights. Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. The district court granted the motion as to the claims under the DTA and some other claims, but did imply an action under the Bivens due process theory and denied Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss as to those claims. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals from the denial of his motion, arguing both that the claims are barred by qualified immunity and that the court erred in implying such a cause of action in the first instance. Because we agree that the district court erred in implying such a cause of action, we reverse the order of the district court.

Background

Because this case arises out of a motion to dismiss, we, like the district court, accept the well-pleaded factual allegations set forth in Doe’s complaint as true for purposes of this stage of the litigation and construe reasonable inferences from those allegations in Doe’s favor, although we are not required to accept Doe’s legal conclusions as true. See, e.g., Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges the following facts:

*392In December 2004, Doe, a United States citizen and employee of an American-owned defense contracting firm, traveled to Iraq to work as a civilian Arabic translator and was detailed to a United States Marine Corps Human Exploitation Team operating along the Iraqi-Syrian border. Doe’s job was to develop intelligence through contacts with local Iraqis and to discover threats to the Marine unit. In July 2005, he made contact and developed a relationship with Iraqi Sheikh Abd AlSattar Abu Risha. Doe maintains that he became the unit’s point of contact with AlSattar and that, through a series of secretive meetings, he cultivated Al-Sattar as a United States “ally.”
On October 20, 2005, Doe returned to a United States military camp in preparation to depart for his annual leave. There, a Navy Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agent asked to interview Doe. Doe agreed and discussed his work -with the agent.

Two weeks later, Doe traveled to A1 Asad, a United States military base, from which he was scheduled to depart for his leave. At that point, three NCIS agents, including the one who previously interviewed him, along with another United States official, detained Doe and interrogated him for four hours.Doe alleges that the agents denied his requests to have an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit present for his questioning. 

Doe was transferred into the custody of the Marine Corps. After seventy-two hours he was flown to Camp Cropper, a United States military facility near Baghdad International Airport used to hold high-value detainees, where he was confined for nine months. For the first three months, Doe alleges he was kept in solitary confinement; thereafter, he was transferred into a cell housing suspected hostile al Qaeda and Arab Socialist Ba’ath Party members. Doe alleges that the military officers publicized his affiliation with the Department of Defense  During his detainment, United States government officials interrogated Doe multiple times and denied his requests for an attorney to be present at those interrogations.

In December 2005, the Detainee Status Board held a hearing and deemed Doe a threat to the Multi-National Forces in Iraq. In July 2006, the Board held a second hearing, after which Doe was transported to Jordan and ultimately to the United States, where he was released. Doe was never formally charged with a crime. He alleges that his property was not returned to him and that he has been placed on watch lists, preventing contracting firms from hiring him and causing customs officials to interrogate him when he returns from international travel.

Procedural History

In November 2008, Doe filed this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, other United States government officers and agents, and the United States government. Relevant to this appeal, Doe asked the district court to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for violating Doe’s rights under the DTA and for violating Doe’s constitutional rights guaranteed under the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments on the theory that Secretary Rumsfeld developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm.
*393Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss Doe’s claims against him for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F.Supp.2d 94, 100 (D.D.C.2011). The district court dismissed Doe’s claims arising under the DTA, holding that the DTA does not provide a private cause of action. Id. at 104-05. The district court also dismissed Doe’s procedural due process and access to court claims, holding that Doe had not pled sufficient facts to overcome Secretary Rumsfeld’s qualified immunity defense. Id. at 113-14.
The court held, however, that Doe could maintain a federal cause of action for his substantive due process claims under Bivens v. Secretary Rumsfeld filed this interlocutory appeal of the district court’s partial denial of his motion to dismiss Doe’s claims against him. He argues that the district court erred first by implying a Bivens action arising out of the sensitive context of a military detention in a foreign warzone and second by denying him qualified immunity against Doe’s substantive due process claims. Jurisdiction
Although the jurisdiction of courts of appeals ordinarily extends only to review of “final decisions” of the district courts, we have jurisdiction over the current interlocutory appeal. **OPENING ARGUMENT**  

May it please the Court:  

Today, this Court confronts a fundamental question of separation of powers: whether federal judges may create a new damages remedy for constitutional violations arising from military detention operations in a foreign warzone. The district court answered yes. That answer was legally erroneous. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly held, Bivens remedies are disfavored in new contexts—especially where national security, military command, and foreign policy are at stake. Here, the district court’s decision to imply a Bivens cause of action for substantive due process claims in the context of military detention in Iraq constitutes an impermissible judicial intrusion into areas reserved for the political branches. This Court must reverse.  

### I. BIVENS DOES NOT APPLY IN THIS SENSITIVE CONTEXT—SPECIAL FACTORS COUNSEL HESITATION  

The Supreme Court has made clear that Bivens is not a "general remedy for constitutional violations." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 675 (2009). Instead, it is a "last resort" remedy available only when Congress has not provided an alternative and no "special factors counselling hesitation" exist. *Id.* The district court erred by failing to recognize that military detention in a foreign warzone is the very definition of a "new context" where special factors demand judicial restraint.  

In *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, the Court squarely held that "when the claim arises in a new context, the Court considers whether there are 'special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress.'" 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). There, the Court refused to extend Bivens to claims arising from post-9/11 detentions, emphasizing that national security, foreign policy, and the separation of powers "counsel hesitation" in such sensitive settings. *Id.* at 1858. This case involves the same core concerns: military detention in an active warzone during a time of armed conflict. The Constitution does not permit federal courts to second-guess military decisions about detainee handling in such environments. As the Court explained in *Ziglar*, "the judiciary has no special competence to resolve military questions," and "[t]he political branches are best equipped to address the complex policy tradeoffs inherent in wartime detention." *Id.*  

*Chappell v. Wallace* controls here. 462 U.S. 296 (1983). There, the Court refused to extend Bivens to military personnel suing superiors for constitutional violations, holding that "the unique structure of the military" and "the need for discipline" require that such claims be resolved within the military justice system—not through judicially created damages actions. *Id.* at 304–05. Though the plaintiff here is a civilian contractor, his detention occurred entirely within the military chain of command during active hostilities in Iraq. The Supreme Court has consistently treated military operations in foreign warzones as a singular context where judicial interference is forbidden. In *United States v. Stanley*, the Court reiterated that "the peculiar and special relationship of the soldier to his superiors" makes such claims unsuitable for judicial review. 483 U.S. 669, 683 (1987). The same logic applies to civilian contractors embedded with military forces in combat zones. The district court’s attempt to carve out an exception for contractors ignores the Supreme Court’s uniform teaching: in military detention operations, "the special factors counselling hesitation are overwhelming." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.  

### II. CONGRESS HAS NOT AUTHORIZED A REMEDY—JUDICIAL CREATION OF BIVENS VIOLATES SEPARATION OF POWERS  

The district court’s error is compounded by its refusal to heed Congress’s explicit choice not to create a private cause of action for detainee treatment claims. The court correctly dismissed Doe’s claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), holding that the statute does not provide a private right of action. That conclusion is pivotal: Congress deliberately chose *not* to authorize judicially enforceable damages for such claims. By implying a Bivens remedy, the district court effectively rewrote the statute and created a cause of action Congress expressly withheld. That is a classic violation of separation of powers.  

As the Supreme Court stated in *Iqbal*, "We have repeatedly refused to extend Bivens to new contexts where Congress has not authorized such a remedy." 556 U.S. at 675. Here, Congress addressed detainee treatment through the DTA—but specifically declined to create a private damages remedy. Instead, it established military review processes and statutory safeguards. The district court’s decision to judicially implant a Bivens remedy ignores this congressional judgment and usurps the legislative function. *See also Minneci v. Pollard*, 565 U.S. 118, 129 (2012) (refusing to extend Bivens where "alternative remedies exist" and Congress has "structured the legal landscape"). The DTA’s silence on private suits is not an oversight—it is a policy choice. Courts cannot override it.  

### III. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY APPLIES EVEN IF BIVENS APPLY—BUT IT DOES NOT  

Even if this Court were to reach qualified immunity—which it should not, given Bivens is inapplicable—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to dismissal. Qualified immunity shields officials from liability unless they violated a "clearly established" constitutional right. *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 735, 741 (2011). Here, no reasonable official in 2005 could have known that interrogating a contractor in a warzone violated substantive due process. The Supreme Court has held that constitutional standards in military detention contexts are inherently fluid and subject to "military judgment," making it impossible to define a "clearly established" right. *See, e.g., Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 531 (2004) (noting the "deference due to the Executive" in wartime). The district court’s assertion that a "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established is legally unsound. In *Brosseau v. Haugen*, the Court rejected qualified immunity claims where "the right was not clearly established at the time of the conduct," and here, the military’s operational discretion in Iraq precluded such clarity. 543 U.S. 194, 198–99 (2004).  

### CONCLUSION  

The district court’s decision to imply a Bivens remedy in this military detention context is a profound error. It disregards Supreme Court precedent, violates separation of powers, and invites judicial interference in matters reserved for the political branches. This Court must reverse.

[Appellant’s Counsel]

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY FOR SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS CLAIMS

### A. The Legal Framework for Bivens Claims

The Supreme Court has consistently held that *Bivens* is a "last resort" remedy, to be inferred by courts only when "(1) no alternative statutory remedy exists, and (2) no special factors counsel hesitation." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 675 (2009). The Court has emphasized that *Bivens* "is not a general remedy for constitutional violations" and that "expanding the Bivens remedy ... is 'a significant extension of the doctrine... which Congress... has not necessarily foreclosed, but which Congress has definitely not authorized.'" *Id.* at 675 (quoting *Bush v. Lucas*, 462 U.S. 367, 378 (1983)).

When a claim arises in a "new context," courts must consider whether "special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress" exist. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). The question is not whether the context is *new* in some abstract sense, but whether the context involves "the kind of political-branch decisionmaking that has been committed to those branches and not subject to judicial review." *Id.* at 1862.

### B. Military Detention in a Foreign Warzone Is a Context Where Special Factors Counsel Hesitation

The district court erred in concluding that this case did not present a "new context" for *Bivens* purposes. Military detention in an active warzone during armed conflict presents precisely the type of context where special factors overwhelmingly counsel judicial hesitation.

In *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, the Supreme Court refused to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from post-9/11 detentions at Guantanamo Bay, emphasizing that national security, foreign policy, and separation of powers "counsel hesitation" in such sensitive settings. 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Court explained that "the decisionmaker in these matters is the political branch, not the judicial branch" and that "the judiciary has no special competence to resolve" such military and national security questions. *Id.* at 1858-59.

The Appellee's detention occurred in Iraq—an active theater of armed conflict—where United States forces were engaged in combat operations. The policies challenged here relate to the handling of detainees in military custody during wartime. This context is indistinguishable in all material respects from *Ziglar*.

The district court attempted to distinguish *Ziglar* on the ground that Doe is a civilian contractor rather than a military detainee. This distinction is unavailing. The critical factor is not the status of the detainee but the nature of the activity: military detention operations in a foreign warzone. The Supreme Court has consistently treated such operations as areas where judicial interference is forbidden, regardless of whether the detainee is a soldier, civilian, or contractor.

As this Court noted in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697 (D.C. Cir. 2008), the "sensitive context" inquiry requires examining whether the claims involve "the kind of political-branch decisionmaking that has been committed to those branches." *Id.* at 707. The decision to detain, interrogate, and release individuals in an active warzone is quintessentially a military and political decision committed to the political branches.

### C. The Unique Military Context Supports Hesitation

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the military occupies a unique constitutional position. In *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), the Court refused to extend *Bivens* to military personnel suing their superiors for constitutional violations, holding that "the unique structure of the military" and "the need for discipline" require that such claims be resolved within the military justice system. *Id.* at 304-05.

While Doe is technically a civilian contractor, his detention occurred entirely within the military chain of command. He was held at Camp Cropper, a military facility used for high-value detainees. He was interrogated by military personnel and military intelligence agencies. The policies allegedly violated were military policies implemented in a combat zone.

The reasoning of *Chappell* applies with equal force: allowing damages actions against senior military officials for conduct in a warzone would impermissibly intrude on military judgment and discipline. The district court erred in failing to apply this reasoning.

### D. Congress's Silence Is Determinative

The district court's error is compounded by Congress's explicit choice not to create a private cause of action for detainee treatment claims. As the district court correctly held, the DTA does not provide a private right of action. Congress addressed detainee treatment but deliberately declined to authorize judicially enforceable damages.

In *Minneci v. Pollard*, 565 U.S. 118 (2012), the Supreme Court refused to extend *Bivens* where "alternative remedies exist" and Congress has "structured the legal landscape." *Id.* at 129. Here, Congress created administrative remedies and military review processes but declined to create a private damages remedy. This silence is a policy choice—not an oversight—and courts cannot override it.

As the Court stated in *Iqbal*, "We have repeatedly refused to extend Bivens to new contexts where Congress has not authorized such a remedy." 556 U.S. at 675. The district court's decision to judicially implant a *Bivens* remedy ignores this congressional judgment and usurps the legislative function.

## III. EVEN IF A BIVENS REMEDY WERE AVAILABLE, SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The Qualified Immunity Standard

Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability unless they violated a "clearly established" constitutional right of which a reasonable official would have known. *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 735, 741 (2011). This standard protects "all but the plainly incompetent or those who knowingly violate the law." *Malley v. Briggs*, 475 U.S. 335, 341 (1986).

### B. No Clearly Established Substantive Due Process Right Was Violated

The district court held that Doe possessed a substantive due process right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." *Doe*, 800 F. Supp. 2d at 115. This holding was erroneous.

To defeat qualified immunity, the right must be "clearly established" in a "particularized" sense—meaning "its contours [are] sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). This is an "exacting standard." *al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. at 742.

The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently vague and provides little guidance to government officials. As the Supreme Court explained in *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 848-49 (1998), the standard is "deliberately responsive to the facts and circumstances of each case" and "necessarily straightforward in application only in the sense that there are no standards."

Critically, no case prior to Doe's detention in 2005 established that a civilian contractor detained in a warzone had a clearly established substantive due process right to be free from the specific detention and interrogation practices at issue. The district court relied on cases establishing general principles, but "general statements of the law are insufficient to defeat qualified immunity." *Brosseau v. Haugen*, 543 U.S. 194, 198-99 (2004).

### C. Military Necessity and Operational Discretion

The context of military detention in an active warzone further defeats any claim of clearly established rights. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 531 (2004), there is "deference due to the Executive" in wartime. The military must have operational discretion to make rapid decisions about detainee handling in dangerous environments.

The district court's assertion that a "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established in this context is legally unsound. Constitutional standards in military detention contexts are inherently fluid and subject to military judgment. A reasonable official in 2005 could not have known that specific interrogation or detention practices violated substantive due process.

### D. The Claims Against Secretary Rumsfeld Specifically Fail

Moreover, Doe's claims against Secretary Rumsfeld personally fail because he has not alleged sufficient facts to establish that Rumsfeld directly participated in any constitutional violation. *See Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 675 ("A vicarious liability theory cannot reproduce the specific elements of the constitutional violation."). Doe alleges that Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm," but this conclusory allegation is insufficient. *Id.* at 678-79 (holding that courts need not accept "threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action" supported by "mere conclusory statements").

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff has argued that this Court should affirm the district court. For the reasons stated above, this Court should not do so. The plaintiff's arguments are unavailing for the following additional reasons:

### A. The Plaintiff Mischaracterizes the Scope of Bivens

The plaintiff suggests that *Bivens* provides a routine remedy for constitutional violations. This is incorrect. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized, *Bivens* is a "last resort" to be inferred only when Congress has not provided a remedy and no special factors counsel hesitation. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 675. The plaintiff cannot overcome these requirements here.

### B. The Plaintiff Cannot Distinguish This Case from Ziglar

The plaintiff will likely argue that *Ziglar* is distinguishable because it involved detainees at Guantanamo Bay rather than in Iraq. This distinction is without merit. The critical feature of *Ziglar* was not the location of the detention but the nature of the activity: military detention during wartime. Both cases involve decisions committed to the political branches. The plaintiff cannot escape *Ziglar* through geographic technicalities.

### C. The Qualified Immunity Standard Is Met

The plaintiff will argue that qualified immunity should not apply because his rights were clearly established. This argument fails because no case has established the particular right the plaintiff asserts—a substantive due process right to be free from wartime detention and interrogation practices in Iraq. The plaintiff cannot point to a single case that gave Secretary Rumsfeld fair notice that his conduct violated the Constitution.

For the foregoing reasons, Secretary Rumsfeld respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying dismissal of Doe's substantive due process claims, and remand with instructions to dismiss those claims with prejudice.

The district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone. This error violates separation of powers and disregards Supreme Court precedent. Even if a *Bivens* action were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because no clearly established constitutional right was violated.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**PANEL:** [Appellate Court Panel Designation]

**DECISION:** REVERSED AND REMANDED

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This case comes before the Court on an interlocutory appeal from the district court’s denial of Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss. Appellee John Doe, a civilian contractor, alleges that he was detained and subjected to harsh interrogation practices by the United States military in Iraq, in violation of his substantive due process rights. The district court held that Doe could maintain a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), and that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to qualified immunity. For the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this context. Accordingly, we reverse.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In conducting this review, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept the plaintiff’s legal conclusions. *Id.*

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY**

The Supreme Court has long instructed that *Bivens* actions are a "last resort," not a "general remedy for constitutional violations." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 675, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Before implying a *Bivens* remedy in a new context, a court must determine whether there are any "special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857, 198 L.Ed.2d 290 (2017). We find that such special factors are present here, precluding the creation of a *Bivens* remedy.

**A. New Context and Special Factors**

The detention of a civilian contractor by the military in an active warzone constitutes a "new context" distinct from the facts of *Bivens*. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. In *Ziglar*, the Supreme Court refused to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from the detention and treatment of terrorism suspects, emphasizing the sensitivity of national security and foreign policy decisions. *Id.* at 1858. The Court noted that "the judiciary has no special competence to resolve" such matters and that "the political branches are best equipped to address the complex policy tradeoffs inherent in wartime detention." *Id.*

The present case involves the same core concerns. Doe was detained at Camp Cropper, a military facility near Baghdad International Airport, during active hostilities. The decisions regarding his detention and interrogation were made by military officials operating in a theater of war. This context triggers the "special factors counselling hesitation" identified in *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304-05, 103 S.Ct. 2382, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983). In *Chappell*, the Court held that the "unique structure of the military" and the "need for discipline" preclude *Bivens* actions by military personnel against their superiors. *Id.* While Doe is a civilian contractor, his detention was managed entirely within the military chain of command. The reasoning of *Chappell* applies with equal force here; allowing damages actions against senior military officials for detention decisions made in a combat zone would intrude upon military discipline and judgment. See *United States v. Stanley*, 483 U.S. 669, 683, 107 S.Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 406 (1987) (reiterating the "peculiar and special relationship of the soldier to his superiors" and the unsuitability of such claims for judicial review).

**B. Congressional Silence**

Furthermore, Congress has not authorized a damages remedy for these claims. The district court correctly dismissed Doe’s claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., acknowledging that the DTA does not provide a private right of action. This silence is significant. In *Minneci v. Pollard*, 565 U.S. 118, 129, 132 S.Ct. 517, 181 L.Ed.2d 390 (2012), the Supreme Court refused to extend *Bivens* where "alternative remedies exist" and Congress has "structured the legal landscape." Here, Congress established military review processes and statutory safeguards for detainee treatment but chose not to create a private damages remedy. It is not the role of the judiciary to override this policy choice. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 675.

Because the context of military detention in a foreign warzone presents special factors counselling hesitation, and because Congress has not authorized a *Bivens* remedy in this sphere, the district court erred in implying a cause of action.

**III. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are shielded from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731, 741, 131 S.Ct. 2074, 179 L.Ed.2d 1149 (2011).

The district court determined that Doe had a right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." However, the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently vague and fact-dependent. *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 848-49, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998). To defeat qualified immunity, the right must be defined with sufficient specificity that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violated it. *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 562 (1987).

No prior case put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that detaining a civilian contractor suspected of posing a threat to military forces in a warzone, and subjecting him to the conditions alleged, violated substantive due process. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 531, 124 S.Ct. 2633, 159 L.Ed.2d 578 (2004), "deference [is] due to the Executive" in matters of military detention. Given the fluid nature of military judgment in active combat zones, the contours of any alleged right were not clearly established in 2005. *Brosseau v. Haugen*, 543 U.S. 194, 198-99, 125 S.Ct. 596, 160 L.Ed.2d 583 (2004) (holding that qualified immunity applies where the right was not clearly established at the time of the conduct).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

The plaintiff has not alleged that any specific policy adopted by Rumsfeld caused the specific harm he suffered. Rather, he has alleged that general detention policies existed and that he was harmed. The plaintiff dismisses the defense's arguments regarding military necessity as "legally indefensible." This characterization is incorrect. **DECISION**

**PANEL:** [Appellate Court Panel Designation]

**DECISION:** AFFIRMED IN PART, REVERSED IN PART, AND REMANDED

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on an interlocutory appeal from the district court’s denial of Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss. Appellee John Doe, a civilian contractor, alleges that he was detained and subjected to harsh interrogation practices by the United States military in Iraq, in violation of his substantive due process rights. The district court held that Doe could maintain a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), and that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to qualified immunity. We have reviewed the parties’ rebuttals to the opening arguments. For the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this context. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s order denying the motion to dismiss.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In conducting this review, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept the plaintiff’s legal conclusions. *Id.*

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY**

The Supreme Court has instructed that *Bivens* actions are a "last resort," not a "general remedy for constitutional violations." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 675, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Before implying a *Bivens* remedy in a new context, a court must determine whether there are any "special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857, 198 L.Ed.2d 290 (2017). We find that such special factors are present here, precluding the creation of a *Bivens* remedy.

**A. The Context of Military Detention Triggers Special Factors**

The Appellee argues that *Ziglar* and *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2382, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), are inapplicable because he is a civilian contractor, not a member of the military. While *Chappell* explicitly reserved the question of whether a *Bivens* remedy would be available for a civilian employed by the military, *Id.* at 305 n.6, the reasoning of that case and *Ziglar* extends to the circumstances presented here.

The "special factors" analysis is not solely dependent on the status of the plaintiff, but heavily weighs the context of the alleged constitutional violation. In *Ziglar*, the Supreme Court refused to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from the detention of terrorism suspects, emphasizing the sensitivity of national security and foreign policy decisions. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Court noted that "the judiciary has no special competence to resolve" such matters and that "the political branches are best equipped to address the complex policy tradeoffs inherent in wartime detention." *Id.*

The present case involves the same core concerns. Doe was detained at Camp Cropper, a military facility near Baghdad International Airport, during active hostilities. The decisions regarding his detention and interrogation were made by military officials operating in a theater of war. The Appellee’s distinction based on his citizenship and employment status does not negate the fact that his detention was an integral part of military operations in a combat zone. The reasoning of *Chappell*—that the "unique structure of the military" and the "need for discipline" preclude *Bivens* actions—applies with equal force here. *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 304-05. Allowing damages actions against senior military officials for detention decisions made in a combat zone would intrude upon military discipline and judgment, regardless of whether the detainee is a soldier or a civilian contractor embedded with the unit. See *United States v. Stanley*, 483 U.S. 669, 683, 107 S.Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 406 (1987) (reiterating the "peculiar and special relationship of the soldier to his superiors" and the unsuitability of such claims for judicial review).

**B. Congressional Silence**

The Appellee argues that Congress’s silence in the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., regarding civilian contractors does not bar a *Bivens* remedy. We disagree. The absence of a statutory remedy for a constitutional violation does not automatically mean that a *Bivens* remedy should be implied. *Minneci v. Pollard*, 565 U.S. 118, 129, 132 S.Ct. 517, 181 L.Ed.2d 390 (2012). To the contrary, when Congress has established a comprehensive statutory scheme for military detainee matters but chosen not to create a private damages remedy, this silence is a "special factor counselling hesitation." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. It is not the role of the judiciary to override this policy choice and imply a cause of action that could interfere with military operations. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 675.

**III. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are shielded from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731, 741, 131 S.Ct. 2074, 179 L.Ed.2d 1149 (2011).

**A. The Right Was Not Clearly Established**

The Appellee relies on *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 124 S.Ct. 2633, 159 L.Ed.2d 578 (2004), to argue that the right to be free from prolonged detention without charge was clearly established. However, *Hamdi* recognized that "the exigencies of the conflict may render the process that would be optimal in peacetime impossible in wartime." *Id.* at 535. *Hamdi* did not clearly establish that detaining a civilian contractor suspected of posing a threat to military forces in a warzone for nine months, with the procedural safeguards provided here (Detainee Status Board hearings), violated substantive due process.

To defeat qualified immunity, the right must be defined with sufficient specificity that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violated it. *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 562 (1987). The "shock the conscience" standard cited by the Appellee, *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 848-49, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), is inherently vague and fact-dependent. No prior case put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that the specific detention and interrogation conditions alleged, applied to a civilian contractor in a warzone, crossed the constitutional line. *Brosseau v. Haugen*, 543 U.S. 194, 198-99, 125 S.Ct. 596, 160 L.Ed.2d 583 (2004).

**B. Supervisory Liability**

The Appellee argues that Secretary Rumsfeld is liable because he "developed, authorized, and implemented" the policies at issue. However, as established in *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009), supervisory liability requires more than a vicarious liability theory; a plaintiff must allege that the supervisor "adopted a policy" and that the policy was the "moving force" behind the constitutional violation. *Id.* at 678. The Appellee’s complaint contains only generalized allegations about Rumsfeld’s role in setting detention policies, insufficient to establish personal participation in the specific alleged violations. This failure to plead facts sufficient to support supervisory liability further supports the grant of qualified immunity.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a question of exceptional importance: whether the federal judiciary may create a Bivens remedy against the Secretary of Defense for decisions made in the context of military detention during an active armed conflict. The district court erred in answering this question in the affirmative. The context of military detention in a foreign warzone constitutes "special factors counselling hesitation" that preclude the implication of a Bivens remedy, and Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity even if such a remedy were available.

The defense acknowledges that this Court has now issued its ruling reversing the district court. This closing statement is submitted to reinforce the legal bases for that reversal and to address the appellee's remaining arguments.

---

## II. RELEVANT FACTS

The following facts, drawn from the appellee's complaint and accepted as true for purposes of this motion, establish the context that mandates dismissal:

1. **Military Context of Detention**: The appelee, John Doe, was detained at Camp Cropper, a United States military facility near Baghdad International Airport used to hold high-value detainees, during active hostilities in Iraq. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F Supp. 2d 94, 100 (D.D.C. 2011).

2. **Military Decision-Making**: The decisions regarding appellee's detention and interrogation were made by military officials operating in a theater of war, including the Marine Corps and the Detainee Status Board, which conducted two hearings regarding appellee's status. Id.

3. **Procedural Protections**: The appelee was afforded two Detainee Status Board hearings—one in December 2005 and another in July 2006—before being transferred to Jordan and ultimately released to the United States. Id. at 101.

4. **No Formal Charges**: The appelee was never formally charged with a crime, but the military determined he posed a threat to Multi-National Forces in Iraq based on intelligence assessments. Id.

5. **Secretary Rumsfeld's Role**: The complaint contains only generalized allegations that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented" policies that caused appellee's harm, without alleging personal participation in the specific decisions affecting Doe. Id. at 115.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Context of Military Detention Triggers Special Factors Counseling Hesitation

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens is a "last resort," not a "general remedy for constitutional violations." Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 675 (2009). Before implying a Bivens remedy in a new context, courts must determine whether there are any "special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress." Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017).

The military detention context itself constitutes such special factors. In Ziglar, the Supreme Court refused to extend Bivens to claims arising from the detention of terrorism suspects, emphasizing that "the judiciary has no special competence to resolve" national security and foreign policy decisions and that "the political branches are best equipped to address the complex policy tradeoffs inherent in wartime detention." Id. at 1858. The Court further noted that "the Executive's authority over military operations in a foreign territory is at its greatest." Id.

The appellee's attempt to distinguish his case based on his status as a civilian contractor must fail. While Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), reserved the question of whether a Bivens remedy would be available for a civilian employed by the military, the reasoning of that case extends to the circumstances presented here. The "unique structure of the military" and "the need for discipline" counsel hesitation regardless of the detainee's status. Id. at 304-05. As this Court correctly recognized, allowing damages actions against senior military officials for detention decisions made in a combat zone would intrude upon military discipline and judgment, regardless of whether the detainee is a soldier or a civilian contractor.

The appelee was detained by the Marine Corps, held at a military facility, and subjected to military detention procedures under the Detainee Status Board. The decisions regarding his detention and interrogation were integral to military operations in a combat zone. United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 683 (1987) (emphasizing the "peculiar and special relationship of the soldier to his superiors" and the judiciary's lack of competence to review such matters).

### B. Congressional Silence Is a Special Factor Counseling Hesitation

The appellee's argument that Congress's failure to include civilian contractors in the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) means a Bivens remedy must exist reverses the law. The Supreme Court has held that "the absence of a statutory remedy for a constitutional violation does not automatically mean that a Bivens remedy should be implied." Minneci v. Pollard, 565 U.S. 118, 129 (2012). When Congress establishes a comprehensive statutory scheme for military detainee matters but chooses not to create a private damages remedy, that silence is itself a "special factor counselling hesitation." Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

The DTA, the Military Commissions Act, and military regulations collectively reflect a deliberate policy choice to leave detention decisions in wartime to the Executive Branch. It is not the judiciary's role to override that choice and imply a remedy that would interfere with military operations. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 675.

### C. Qualified Immunity Applies Even If a Bivens Remedy Were Available

Government officials are shielded from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. Ashcroft v. al-Kidd, 563 U.S. 731, 741 (2011).

The right the appelee invokes—the right to be free from prolonged detention without charge—was not clearly established as applied to civilian contractors in a warzone. While Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), recognized a right to due process for U.S. citizens detained as enemy combatants, it explicitly recognized that "the exigencies of the conflict may render the process that would be optimal in peacetime impossible in wartime." Id. at 535. The Court emphasized that the judiciary must "accord the Executive substantial latitude to conduct operations in wartime." Id. at 531.

The appelee was afforded two Detainee Status Board hearings—a process that exceeds what Hamdi required for a U.S. citizen detained as an enemy combatant. No prior case put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that detaining a civilian contractor suspected of posing a threat to military forces in a combat zone violated substantive due process.

The "shock the conscience" standard the appelee invokes is inherently vague and fact-specific. County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 848-49 (1998). It cannot serve as the basis for defeating qualified immunity when no prior case has applied it to similar circumstances. The right must be defined with sufficient specificity that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violated it. Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). The appelee points to no such case.

### D. Supervisory Liability Is Unproven

The appelee's claim that Secretary Rumsfeld is liable for "developing, authorizing, and implementing" detention policies fails under Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009). Supervisory liability requires more than vicarious liability—a plaintiff must allege that the supervisor "adopted a policy" and that the policy was the "moving force" behind the specific constitutional violation. Id. at 678.

The appellee's complaint contains only generalized allegations about Rumsfeld's role in broad detention policies. It does not allege that Rumsfeld personally participated in or directed the specific decisions affecting Doe. This failure alone requires dismissal.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

### A. Rebuttal: Bivens Remedy

The appelee's claim that this Court should imply a Bivens remedy fails because:

1. **Special Factors Abound**: The military detention context, the wartime setting, the involvement of military decision-makers, and the need for military discipline all counsel hesitation. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857-58; Chappell, 462 U.S. at 304-05; Stanley, 483 U.S. at 683.

2. **Congressional Silence**: Congress has provided statutory schemes for detainee matters but declined to create a private damages remedy. This silence indicates a policy choice that the judiciary should not override. Minneci, 565 U.S. at 129; Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

3. **Status Does Not Matter**: Whether the detainee is a soldier or civilian contractor, the military's institutional concerns apply with equal force. The appellee's distinction based on citizenship and employment status does not negate the fact that his detention was an integral part of military operations in a combat zone.

### B. Rebuttal: Qualified Immunity

The appelee's claim that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity fails because:

1. **No Clearly Established Right**: No prior case put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that detaining a civilian contractor in a warzone, with the procedural protections provided, violated substantive due process. Hamdi, 542 U.S. at 535.

2. **Vague Standard**: The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently vague and fact-dependent. It cannot provide the specificity required to defeat qualified immunity. Brosseau v. Haugen, 543 U.S. 194, 198-99 (2004).

3. **Supervisory Liability**: The appelee has not alleged personal participation by Secretary Rumsfeld in the specific decisions affecting him. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the reversal of the district court's order and dismiss all claims against Secretary Rumsfeld. The law is clear: special factors counsel hesitation in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone, congressional silence forecloses judicial intervention, and no clearly established right exists here. Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

The Constitution does not empower courts to second-guess military decisions in an active warzone. Allowing the appellee's claim to proceed would invite endless litigation over military decisions in combat zones—undermining discipline, operational security, and the Executive's ability to conduct warfare.

**Counsel for Appellant Secretary Donald Rumsfeld**

**DECISION**

**PANEL:** [Appellate Court Panel Designation]

**DECISION:** REVERSED

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This matter comes before the Court on an interlocutory appeal from the district court’s denial of Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss. Appellee John Doe, a civilian contractor, alleges that he was detained and subjected to harsh interrogation practices by the United States military in Iraq, in violation of his substantive due process rights. The district court held that Doe could maintain a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), and that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to qualified immunity. We have reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties. For the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this context. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s order denying the motion to dismiss.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We review de novo a district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In conducting this review, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). However, we are not bound to accept the plaintiff’s legal conclusions. *Id.*

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY**

The Supreme Court has instructed that *Bivens* actions are a "last resort," not a "general remedy for constitutional violations." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 675, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Before implying a *Bivens* remedy in a new context, a court must determine whether there are any "special factors counselling hesitation in the absence of affirmative action by Congress." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857, 198 L.Ed.2d 290 (2017). We find that such special factors are present here, precluding the creation of a *Bivens* remedy.

**A. The Context of Military Detention Triggers Special Factors**

The Appellant argues that *Ziglar v. Abbasi* and *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2382, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), control this case, regardless of Doe’s status as a civilian contractor. We agree. In *Ziglar*, the Supreme Court refused to extend *Bivens* to claims arising from the detention of terrorism suspects, emphasizing the sensitivity of national security and foreign policy decisions. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Court noted that "the judiciary has no special competence to resolve" such matters and that "the political branches are best equipped to address the complex policy tradeoffs inherent in wartime detention." *Id.*

The present case involves the same core concerns. Doe was detained at Camp Cropper, a military facility near Baghdad International Airport, during active hostilities. The decisions regarding his detention and interrogation were made by military officials operating in a theater of war. The Appellee’s distinction based on his citizenship and employment status does not negate the fact that his detention was an integral part of military operations in a combat zone. The reasoning of *Chappell*—that the "unique structure of the military" and the "need for discipline" preclude *Bivens* actions—applies with equal force here. *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 304-05. Allowing damages actions against senior military officials for detention decisions made in a combat zone would intrude upon military discipline and judgment, regardless of whether the detainee is a soldier or a civilian contractor embedded with the unit. See *United States v. Stanley*, 483 U.S. 669, 683, 107 S.Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 406 (1987) (reiterating the "peculiar and special relationship of the soldier to his superiors" and the unsuitability of such claims for judicial review).

**B. Congressional Silence**

The Appellant further contends that Congress’s silence in the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., regarding civilian contractors does not bar a *Bivens* remedy. We agree with the Appellant that this silence is a "special factor counselling hesitation." The absence of a statutory remedy for a constitutional violation does not automatically mean that a *Bivens* remedy should be implied. *Minneci v. Pollard*, 565 U.S. 118, 129, 132 S.Ct. 517, 181 L.Ed.2d 390 (2012). To the contrary, when Congress has established a comprehensive statutory scheme for military detainee matters but chosen not to create a private damages remedy, this silence is a "special factor counselling hesitation." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. It is not the role of the judiciary to override this policy choice and imply a cause of action that could interfere with military operations. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 675.

**III. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are shielded from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731, 741, 131 S.Ct. 2074, 179 L.Ed.2d 1149 (2011).

**A. The Right Was Not Clearly Established**

The Appellee relies on *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507, 124 S.Ct. 2633, 159 L.Ed.2d 578 (2004), to argue that the right to be free from prolonged detention without charge was clearly established. However, *Hamdi* recognized that "the exigencies of the conflict may render the process that would be optimal in peacetime impossible in wartime." *Id.* at 535. *Hamdi* did not clearly establish that detaining a civilian contractor suspected of posing a threat to military forces in a warzone for nine months, with the procedural safeguards provided here (Detainee Status Board hearings), violated substantive due process.

To defeat qualified immunity, the right must be defined with sufficient specificity that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violated it. *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640, 107 S.Ct. 3034, 97 L.Ed.2d 562 (1987). The "shock the conscience" standard cited by the Appellee, *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 848-49, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), is inherently vague and fact-dependent. No prior case put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that the specific detention and interrogation conditions alleged, applied to a civilian contractor in a warzone, crossed the constitutional line. *Brosseau v. Haugen*, 543 U.S. 194, 198-99, 125 S.Ct. 596, 160 L.Ed.2d 583 (2004).

**B. Supervisory Liability**

The Appellee argues that Secretary Rumsfeld is liable because he "developed, authorized, and implemented" the policies at issue. However, as established in *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009), supervisory liability requires more than a vicarious liability theory; a plaintiff must allege that the supervisor "adopted a policy" and that the policy was the "moving force" behind the constitutional violation. *Id.* at 678. The Appellee’s complaint contains only generalized allegations about Rumsfeld’s role in setting detention policies, insufficient to establish personal participation in the specific alleged violations. This failure to plead facts sufficient to support supervisory liability further supports the grant of qualified immunity.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
