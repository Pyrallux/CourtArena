The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against successive prosecutions, but it does not bar retrial following a properly declared mistrial. A mistrial may be declared when "there is a manifest necessity for doing so." The Supreme Court has long recognized that the trial court retains broad discretion in determining whether manifest necessity exists, and this Court must review that determination with considerable deference. See Arizona v. Washington, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978).

Here, the prosecution dramatically understates the prejudice arising from the surprise testimony. The buyer unexpectedly revealed during cross-examination that his wife possessed a note containing numbers from the theft victim that matched numbers on the bike sold by Dickinson. In State v. Schack, 154 Ariz. 169, 741 P.2d 296 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1987), this Court held that manifest necessity exists when surprise evidence so fundamentally undermines the defense's trial strategy that fairness requires a new trial. The surprise note here was precisely such evidence. Defense counsel had built his entire opening statement around the serial number discrepancy defense. The note, revealing that the buyer had a matching number, eviscerated that defense in real time.

The prosecution's reliance on Downum v. United States, 372 U.S. 734, 83 S. Ct. 1033, 10 L. Ed. 2d 100 (1963), is misplaced. In Downum, the government knew before trial that its key witness was unavailable, yet proceeded to trial anyway—creating the very prejudice the defendant suffered. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during Dickinson’s first trial. The central issue is whether the mistrial was declared with manifest necessity and with the defendant’s consent, such that the subsequent retrial did not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-4033.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from multiple prosecutions for the same offense. A defendant who successfully moves for a mistrial may generally be retried. However, if the trial court declares a mistrial over the defendant’s objection, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial unless there was a “manifest necessity” for the mistrial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978). The manifest necessity standard is a high one, requiring that the ends of justice would be defeated if the trial were to continue. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971). Furthermore, the trial judge must consider all reasonable alternatives to a mistrial before declaring one. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Dickinson Did Not Consent to the Mistrial**

The State argues that Dickinson’s counsel impliedly consented to the mistrial by raising it as a potential resolution and failing to object when the court indicated its intention to grant one. We disagree.

While it is true that defense counsel initially noted a mistrial was a possibility that would allow him to start over, the record clearly shows that counsel subsequently and repeatedly "made clear that he preferred instead to proceed with the same jury." After the prosecutor suggested options, defense counsel "again indicat[ed] his preference to go forward with trial." A defendant’s mere discussion of a mistrial as a strategic option does not constitute consent when the defendant explicitly expresses a preference to continue the trial. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 96 S. Ct. 1075, 47 L. Ed. 2d 267 (1976). The fact that counsel stated he would seek dismissal with prejudice if the court deemed a mistrial necessary underscores his position: he wanted to proceed, but if the court forced a mistrial, he would seek the ultimate remedy. This is not consent; it is a conditional objection. Therefore, the mistrial was declared over Dickinson’s objection.

**B. Manifest Necessity Was Lacking**

Because the mistrial was declared over Dickinson’s objection, the State must demonstrate manifest necessity. The trial court has broad discretion in determining manifest necessity, but that discretion is not unlimited. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978). A key component of this inquiry is whether the trial court exhausted reasonable alternatives before aborting the trial. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971).

The record reveals that the surprise testimony regarding the scrap of paper created a strategic dilemma for the defense. However, the trial court had viable alternatives to a mistrial. The prosecutor explicitly stated that the State could "proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence" and that such exclusion was "an option for the Court." Crucially, defense counsel agreed with this course of action, stating he would not move to strike the testimony and preferred to proceed without further reference to the note.

When both the prosecution and the defense agree on a specific remedy—here, the exclusion of the evidence and continuation of the trial—it is difficult to conclude that manifest necessity existed to declare a mistrial. The trial court’s authority to declare a mistrial is not a license to disregard the parties’ agreed-upon strategy when a less drastic alternative is readily available. *State v. Williams*, 133 Ariz. 220, 650 P.2d 1202 (Ariz. 1982). The court’s concern that the surprise evidence could not be cured is undermined by the fact that the party most prejudiced by the evidence—the defense—was willing to proceed with the exclusion of that evidence.

The defense relies on *State v. Schack*, 154 Ariz. 169, 741 P.2d 296 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1987), to argue that the surprise nature of the evidence created manifest necessity. While *Schack* acknowledges that surprise evidence can necessitate a mistrial, it does not hold that surprise *automatically* mandates one where the defense waives the prejudice and opts to proceed. Here, the defense was willing to absorb the risk of the surprise testimony to avoid a mistrial. The trial court’s failure to honor this preference and adopt the mutually agreed-upon alternative of exclusion constitutes a lack of manifest necessity.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial over Dickinson’s objection without first exhausting the reasonable alternative of excluding the surprise evidence—a alternative both parties were willing to accept. Under the standards set forth in *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978), and *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971), manifest necessity was not established. Therefore, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars Dickinson’s retrial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s motion to dismiss the charges with prejudice is granted. The convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

The Supreme Court in *Jorn* made clear that “the trial judge must exercise sound discretion” and “consider all reasonable alternatives” before declaring a mistrial. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485. Here, the court did not consider exclusion—it simply assumed exclusion was insufficient without any inquiry into whether curative instructions or a cautionary admonition could mitigate the prejudice. The Arizona Supreme Court in *State v. Williams*, 133 Ariz. 220, 223, 650 P.2d 1202, 1205 (Ariz. 1982), squarely rejected this approach, holding that when “both parties agree on a specific remedy short of mistrial, the court’s failure to adopt it demonstrates a lack of manifest necessity.” The defense’s claim that the court “retained the authority to determine that the prejudice could not be cured” is contradicted by the record: the defense—the party most prejudiced—was willing to proceed with exclusion, and the prosecution did not object to this course. The trial court’s failure to explore even basic curative measures—such as instructing the jury to disregard the note—was arbitrary and legally insufficient. As *Arizona v. Washington* emphasized, “the trial judge’s discretion is not unbounded,” and courts must not “abandon the trial without exhausting reasonable alternatives.” *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 506.

### IV. Double Jeopardy Violations Are Structural Errors Subject to No Harmless Error Review

The defense’s fallback argument—that any error was “harmless”—is legally untenable. The Double Jeopardy Clause is a structural safeguard that “prohibits successive prosecutions for the same offense,” and when a mistrial is declared without manifest necessity, retrial is barred as a matter of constitutional law. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 675, 102 S. Ct. 2083, 2089, 72 L. Ed. 2d 416 (1982) (noting that double jeopardy violations are “structural defects” that “affect the framework within which the trial proceeds” and cannot be excused by harmless error analysis). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “if there is no manifest necessity for the mistrial, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial regardless of the trial court’s good faith or the defendant’s subsequent conviction.” *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. at 485. The defense’s attempt to characterize the mistrial as a “procedural irregularity” subject to harmless error review misreads binding precedent. As *Arizona v. Washington* made clear, “the question is not whether the error affected the outcome of the trial, but whether the mistrial was justified under the Constitution.” *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 505. Here, the mistrial was unjustified, rendering retrial a constitutional violation that cannot be cured by any subsequent proceedings.

The trial court violated Dickinson’s double jeopardy rights by declaring a mistrial without manifest necessity and over his objection. The record shows the parties agreed on a clear alternative—exclusion of the evidence—and the court’s failure to adopt it was arbitrary and legally impermissible. Dickinson did not consent to the mistrial; he repeatedly and explicitly objected to it. The defense’s harmless error argument fails because double jeopardy violations are structural errors that cannot be excused by any review of trial outcomes. For these reasons, this Court must reverse Dickinson’s convictions and remand with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during Dickinson’s first trial. The central issue is whether the mistrial was declared with manifest necessity and with the defendant’s consent, such that the subsequent retrial did not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-4033. Having reviewed the rebuttals of the parties, we affirm our preliminary ruling granting Dickinson’s motion to dismiss.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from multiple prosecutions for the same offense. A defendant who successfully moves for a mistrial may generally be retried. However, if the trial court declares a mistrial over the defendant’s objection, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial unless there was a “manifest necessity” for the mistrial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978). The manifest necessity standard is a high one, requiring that the ends of justice would be defeated if the trial were to continue. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971). Furthermore, the trial judge must consider all reasonable alternatives to a mistrial before declaring one. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Dickinson Did Not Consent to the Mistrial**

The State argues that Dickinson’s counsel impliedly consented to the mistrial by failing to object when the court indicated its intention to grant one and by engaging in discussions about it. We disagree.

While the State relies on *Illinois v. Somerville*, 410 U.S. 458, 93 S. Ct. 1066, 35 L. Ed. 2d 425 (1973), for the proposition that failure to object constitutes consent, that case is distinguishable. *Somerville* involved a legal defect in the indictment apparent on the face of the record, where the defendant did not object to the mistrial declaration. Here, the record clearly shows that Dickinson’s counsel explicitly and repeatedly expressed a preference to proceed with the trial. Counsel stated that he "preferred instead to proceed with the same jury" and "again indicat[ed] his preference to go forward with trial." *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 96 S. Ct. 1075, 47 L. Ed. 2d 267 (1976), holds that a defendant’s mere discussion of a mistrial as a strategic option does not constitute consent when the defendant explicitly expresses a preference to continue the trial.

The State’s characterization of counsel’s statement that he would "seek a dismissal with prejudice" if a mistrial were deemed necessary as a "conditional threat" ignores the legal reality of the situation. This was a conditional objection, not consent. It placed the court on notice that the defense believed a mistrial was unwarranted and that, if the court forced one, the defense would seek the ultimate remedy of dismissal. Therefore, the mistrial was declared over Dickinson’s objection.

**B. Manifest Necessity Was Lacking**

Because the mistrial was declared over Dickinson’s objection, the State must demonstrate manifest necessity. The State argues that the trial court possessed broad discretion to declare a mistrial based on the prejudicial nature of the surprise testimony regarding the serial numbers, citing *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978). While we acknowledge the trial court’s discretion, that discretion is not unbounded and must be exercised within the framework of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The State contends that the surprise testimony itself created manifest necessity because the damage to the defense’s theory could not be cured. However, the record reveals that the State itself offered a viable alternative: proceeding with the exclusion of the evidence regarding the scrap of paper. The prosecutor explicitly stated, "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." Crucially, defense counsel agreed with this course of action.

The State argues that the trial court adequately considered alternatives by engaging in a "lengthy discussion." However, *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971), requires the trial judge to consider and adopt reasonable alternatives if they are available. When both the prosecution and the defense agree on a specific remedy—here, the exclusion of the evidence and continuation of the trial—it is difficult to conclude that manifest necessity existed to declare a mistrial. *State v. Williams*, 133 Ariz. 220, 650 P.2d 1202 (Ariz. 1982). The court’s failure to adopt the mutually agreed-upon alternative, despite the State’s assurance that it could proceed, demonstrates a lack of manifest necessity. The court’s concern that the surprise evidence could not be cured is undermined by the fact that the party seeking to introduce the evidence (the State) was willing to proceed without it.

**C. The Double Jeopardy Violation Is Not Subject to Harmless Error Review**

Finally, the State argues that even if the mistrial was improperly declared, the error was harmless because the evidence of guilt was overwhelming. The State relies on *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 82, 98 S. Ct. 2187, 57 L. Ed. 2d 65 (1978), and *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 102 S. Ct. 2083, 72 L. Ed. 2d 416 (1982). This argument is misplaced.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects an accused from the harassment and burden of multiple trials. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 102 S. Ct. 2083, 72 L. Ed. 2d 416 (1982). When a mistrial is declared without the defendant’s consent and without manifest necessity, the prohibition against double jeopardy bars any subsequent trial, regardless of the strength of the evidence. This is a structural error affecting the framework within which the trial proceeds, not a trial error subject to harmless error analysis. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971). The State’s reliance on *Scott* is inapposite because *Scott* involved a dismissal requested by the defendant, not a mistrial declared over the defendant’s objection.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial over Dickinson’s objection without first exhausting the reasonable alternative of excluding the surprise evidence—an alternative both parties were willing to accept. Under the standards set forth in *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978), and *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971), manifest necessity was not established. Therefore, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars Dickinson’s retrial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s motion to dismiss the charges with prejudice is granted. The convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND JURISDICTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments. The issue on appeal remains whether the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during Dickinson’s first trial violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment. Specifically, we must determine if the mistrial was declared with manifest necessity and with the defendant’s consent. Having reviewed the closing arguments presented by both parties, we affirm our preliminary ruling granting Dickinson’s motion to dismiss. We have jurisdiction pursuant to Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-4033.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from multiple prosecutions for the same offense. A defendant may be retried after a mistrial only if the trial court declares the mistrial with the defendant’s consent or, if declared over the defendant’s objection, there was a “manifest necessity” for the mistrial. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978). The manifest necessity standard is stringent, requiring that the ends of justice would be defeated if the trial were to continue. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971). Furthermore, the trial judge must consider all reasonable alternatives to a mistrial before declaring one. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Dickinson Did Not Consent to the Mistrial**

The arguments presented confirm that Dickinson did not consent to the declaration of a mistrial. The record clearly demonstrates that Dickinson’s counsel explicitly and repeatedly expressed a preference to proceed with the trial. Counsel stated that he "preferred instead to proceed with the same jury" and "again indicat[ed] his preference to go forward with trial."

As argued, *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 96 S. Ct. 1075, 47 L. Ed. 2d 267 (1976), controls this situation. In *Dinitz*, the Supreme Court held that a defendant’s mere discussion of a mistrial as a strategic option does not constitute consent when the defendant explicitly expresses a preference to continue the trial. Here, counsel’s statement that he would "seek a dismissal with prejudice" if a mistrial were deemed necessary was a conditional objection, not consent. It placed the court on notice that the defense believed a mistrial was unwarranted. The State’s reliance on *Illinois v. Somerville*, 410 U.S. 458, 93 S. Ct. 1066, 35 L. Ed. 2d 425 (1973), is misplaced, as that case involved a failure to object to a mistrial based on a facial defect in the indictment, a scenario absent here where the defendant actively advocated against the mistrial.

**B. Manifest Necessity Was Lacking**

Because the mistrial was declared over Dickinson’s objection, the State was required to demonstrate manifest necessity. The arguments highlight that the trial court failed to meet this high standard because it ignored a mutually agreed-upon alternative to a mistrial.

The record reveals that the State itself offered a viable alternative: proceeding with the exclusion of the evidence regarding the scrap of paper. The prosecutor explicitly stated, "The State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." Defense counsel agreed with this course of action. Under *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971), a trial judge must consider all reasonable alternatives to a mistrial. When both the prosecution and the defense agree on a specific remedy—here, the exclusion of the evidence and continuation of the trial—it is difficult to conclude that manifest necessity existed to declare a mistrial.

This conclusion is supported by Arizona precedent in *State v. Williams*, 133 Ariz. 220, 650 P.2d 1202 (Ariz. 1982), which holds that when the prosecution and defense agree on a remedy short of mistrial, the court must implement it unless no alternative exists. The trial court’s failure to adopt the mutually agreed-upon alternative, despite the State’s assurance that it could proceed, demonstrates a lack of manifest necessity. The court’s concern that the surprise evidence could not be cured is undermined by the fact that the party seeking to introduce the evidence was willing to proceed without it.

**C. The Double Jeopardy Violation Is Not Subject to Harmless Error Review**

Finally, the arguments correctly assert that any error in declaring the mistrial was not harmless. The Double Jeopardy Clause protects an accused from the harassment and burden of multiple trials. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 102 S. Ct. 2083, 72 L. Ed. 2d 416 (1982). When a mistrial is declared without the defendant’s consent and without manifest necessity, the prohibition against double jeopardy bars any subsequent trial, regardless of the strength of the evidence. This is a structural error affecting the framework within which the trial proceeds, not a trial error subject to harmless error analysis. *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971). The State’s reliance on *United States v. Scott*, 437 U.S. 82, 98 S. Ct. 2187, 57 L. Ed. 2d 65 (1978), is inapposite because *Scott* involved a dismissal requested by the defendant, not a mistrial declared over the defendant’s objection.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial over Dickinson’s objection without first exhausting the reasonable alternative of excluding the surprise evidence—an alternative both parties were willing to accept. Under the standards set forth in *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 98 S. Ct. 824, 54 L. Ed. 2d 717 (1978), and *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 91 S. Ct. 547, 27 L. Ed. 2d 543 (1971), manifest necessity was not established. Therefore, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars Dickinson’s retrial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Dickinson’s motion to dismiss the charges with prejudice is granted. The convictions are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.
