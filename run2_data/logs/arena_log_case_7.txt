## In the Court of Appeals of Ohio, Ninth Appellate District

### STATE OF OHIO, Plaintiff-Appellee, v. KITTLE, Defendant-Appellant.

---

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

Defendant-Appellant Ms. Kittle respectfully submits this opening response to affirm the appellate court's review of the trial court's evidentiary ruling admitting NPLEX records. The prosecution's argument fails for three principal reasons: (1) the NPLEX records do not qualify as business records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) because they were created specifically for prosecution and thus fall outside the business records exception; (2) Officer Lemonier was not a qualified custodian or keeper of records under the rule and lacked the requisite foundation knowledge to authenticate these documents; and (3) the Confrontation Clause analysis requires de novo review because the admissibility of testimonial business records presents a constitutional question this Court must resolve independently. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment.

---

## I. THE NPLEX RECORDS DO NOT QUALIFY AS BUSINESS RECORDS UNDER OHIO EVIDENCE RULE 803(6)

The prosecution contends that the NPLEX records satisfy the business records exception. This argument fails because the records were not made in the regular course of business but rather were generated specifically for use in prosecution.

### A. The Regular Course of Business Requirement

Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) provides that business records are admissible if they were "made at or near the time of the act, condition, or event, by, or from information transmitted by, a person with knowledge of the act, condition, or event, kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity of a business, organization, occupation, or calling, whether or not for profit." The rule requires that the records be made in the "regular course of business" and that their primary purpose not be litigation. See *State v. Davis*, 62 Ohio St. 3d 326, 581 N.E.2d 1362 (1991).

The key requirement is that records must be created for routine, administrative purposes, not for the purpose of establishing facts for litigation. As the Supreme Court explained in *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), business records are admissible because they are "by their nature objects that are not testimonial." However, this characterization applies only when records are created in the regular course of business operations, not when they are created specifically for prosecution.

### B. NPLEX Records Were Created for Prosecution, Not in Regular Course of Business

The NPLEX (National Precursor Log Exchange) system tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine products. While such tracking may serve administrative purposes, the records generated in this case were obtained specifically to investigate and prosecute Ms. Kittle. The prosecution cannot establish that these records were made in the regular course of business independent of their use in this criminal proceeding.

In *State v. Vondran*, 2015-Ohio-1483, 2015 WL 1383637 (2nd Dist.), the court held that records created specifically for law enforcement purposes do not fall within the business records exception. The court reasoned that when the primary purpose of creating or maintaining records is to investigate or prosecute criminal activity, the records lose their business records character.

Here, the NPLEX records were obtained after Officer Lemonier initiated the investigation and specifically to establish that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine for J.S. This is precisely the type of investigatory record that falls outside the business records exception.

---

## II. OFFICER LEMONIER WAS NOT PROPERLY QUALIFIED TO AUTHENTICATE THE NPLEX RECORDS

### A. The Proper Foundation Requirement

Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) requires authentication through testimony of the custodian of records or another qualified witness. The prosecution cites *State v. Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, 153 Ohio App. 3d 532, 794 N.E.2d 588 (8th Dist.), for the proposition that any witness familiar with the record-keeping system may authenticate records. However, the defense submits that *Strothers* does not go as far as the prosecution suggests and that Officer Lemonier fails even under that relaxed standard.

### B. Officer Lemonier's Lack of Qualification

In *Strothers*, the court held that a witness may authenticate business records if they are "familiar with the record-keeping system" and can "testify to its regularity." However, the prosecution's reliance on this case is misplaced because Officer Lemonier's involvement in the investigation does not make him familiar with the NPLEX record-keeping system maintained by the Ohio Bureau of Criminal Identification and Investigation.

The *Strothers* court specifically required that the witness understand "the record-keeping system." Officer Lemonier is a police officer, not a records custodian for the NPLEX system. He has no knowledge of how the NPLEX database operates, how entries are made, who makes them, or what quality control measures exist. His testimony regarding the "regularity" of the system amounts to speculation.

In *State v. Brown*, 2014-Ohio-496, 2014 WL 555380 (10th Dist.), the court reversed the admission of similar pharmaceutical tracking records because the testifying officer could not establish the necessary foundation. The court held that the officer's lack of personal knowledge about the record-keeping system rendered his authentication testimony insufficient.

Here, Officer Lemonier simply obtained records from the NPLEX system—he did not create them, maintain them, or have any operational knowledge of how they are generated. This falls far short of the requirement that a qualified witness understand the record-keeping system.

### C. The Prosecution Failed to Establish the Required Elements

Under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6), the proponent must establish: (1) the record was made at or near the time of the act; (2) by, or from information transmitted by, a person with knowledge; (3) kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity; (4) making the record was a regular practice of that activity; and (5) all these circumstances must appear authentic. The prosecution failed to establish any of these elements through Officer Lemonier's testimony.

---

## III. THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE ANALYSIS REQUIRES DE NOVO REVIEW

### A. The Constitutional Question Is Not Subject to Deference

The prosecution argues that this Court should apply abuse-of-discretion review to the trial court's evidentiary ruling. This is incorrect. When a defendant's Sixth Amendment confrontation rights are at stake, this Court must conduct de novo review of the legal question of whether the admitted evidence is testimonial.

In *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), the Supreme Court held that the Confrontation Clause applies to forensic laboratory reports, rejecting the government's argument that such reports are not testimonial. The Court emphasized that the determination of whether evidence is testimonial is a constitutional question requiring independent appellate review.

Similarly, in *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647, 131 S. Ct. 2705, 180 L. Ed. 2d 610 (2011), the Court again emphasized that the admissibility of testimonial forensic reports under the Confrontation Clause is a legal question subject to de novo review.

### B. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial

Even if the NPLEX records could somehow satisfy the business records exception, they are testimonial for Confrontation Clause purposes. The prosecution argues that records created in the regular course of business are not testimonial, citing *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015). However, *Clark* is distinguishable.

In *Ohio v. Clark*, the Court held that statements made to teachers by a child victim were not testimonial because they were made in a context that lacked the primary purpose of creating evidence for prosecution. The Court emphasized the informal, spontaneous nature of the statements.

Here, the NPLEX records were created specifically to track pharmaceutical purchases that could be used in criminal prosecutions. The primary purpose of the NPLEX system is to provide law enforcement with evidence of pseudoephedrine purchases. This is fundamentally different from the business records at issue in cases like *United States v. Lugan*, 2008 WL 294537 (N.D. Ohio 2008), where records were created for purely administrative purposes.

The NPLEX records are more analogous to the forensic laboratory reports in *Melendez-Diaz* and *Bullcoming*—they were created to establish facts for prosecution and thus are testimonial. Ms. Kittle has the right to confront the persons who created and maintain these records.

### C. The Prosecution's Citation to *Ohio v. Clark* Is Misplaced

The prosecution's reliance on *Ohio v. Clark* is misplaced. In *Clark*, the Supreme Court explicitly distinguished records created specifically for law enforcement purposes. The Court stated that "statements made to a person not involved in the crime or the investigation may be less likely to be testimonial than statements to law enforcement officers." The NPLEX records were created for law enforcement purposes and are therefore testimonial.

The prosecution's argument that the records were created in the "ordinary course of police operations" conflates two separate concepts. The question is not whether police were involved in obtaining the records, but whether the records were created for the primary purpose of prosecution. Here, the NPLEX database exists specifically to assist in drug investigations and prosecutions.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT'S RULING WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY THE RECORD

### A. The Prosecution Failed to Lay Proper Foundation

The trial court admitted the NPLEX records based solely on Officer Lemonier's testimony. However, the record demonstrates that Officer Lemonier could not establish the necessary foundation. He testified that he obtained the records from the NPLEX system but had no personal knowledge of how that system operates or how the records are generated and maintained.

Under Ohio law, the failure to establish proper foundation constitutes reversible error. See *State v. Easterling*, 2014-Ohio-3439, 2014 WL 3855171 (8th Dist.) (holding that admission of business records without proper foundation is reversible error).

### B. The Error Was Not Harmless

Even if this Court were to find some error in the trial court's ruling, such error was not harmless. The core of the prosecution's case against Ms. Kittle was her alleged purchase of pseudoephedrine for J.S. The NPLEX records were the only direct evidence of such purchases. Without these records, the prosecution's case rests solely on Ms. Kittle's statements during the interrogation—statements that were obtained after her arrest and raise separate constitutional concerns.

Under the harmless error analysis, this Court must determine whether the error "did not affect the substantial rights of the appellant." See *State v. Perry*, 101 Ohio St. 3d 205, 2004-Ohio-455, 803 N.E.2d 835 (2004). Here, the NPLEX records were essential to establishing the essential element of the charged offense—possession or assembly of chemicals for drug manufacturing. The error in admitting these records was not harmless.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant Ms. Kittle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and remand this matter for a new trial. The trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records because: (1) the records do not qualify as business records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6); (2) Officer Lemonier was not properly qualified to authenticate the records; and (3) the Confrontation Clause bars admission of these testimonial records. The prosecution has not met its burden of establishing that the records were properly admissible, and Ms. Kittle's conviction cannot stand.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the Prosecution and the Defense regarding the admissibility of the NPLEX records and the subsequent conviction of Ms. Kittle. Based on the provided facts and the legal arguments presented, the Court finds that the Defense’s arguments regarding the violation of the Confrontation Clause and the improper authentication of the NPLEX records are persuasive. Consequently, the judgment of the trial court is likely to be reversed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The Defense correctly identifies that the determination of whether evidence is "testimonial" under the Confrontation Clause is a legal question subject to *de novo* review. In *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647, 131 S. Ct. 2705, 180 L. Ed. 2d 610 (2011), the Supreme Court held that the Confrontation Clause guarantees a defendant the right to confront witnesses who make "testimonial" statements, and the applicability of this constitutional guarantee is a matter for independent appellate review. While the Prosecution cites *State v. Sage*, 31 Ohio St. 3d 173, 510 N.E.2d 343 (1987), for the proposition that evidentiary rulings are reviewed for an abuse of discretion, this deference does not extend to the constitutional determination of whether the admitted evidence was testimonial in nature. Therefore, this Court must conduct a *de novo* review of the Confrontation Clause issue.

**II. Confrontation Clause Analysis**

The central issue is whether the NPLEX records are "testimonial" under the Sixth Amendment. The Prosecution relies on *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), arguing that the records are non-testimonial business records. However, the Defense persuasively distinguishes *Clark*, noting that it involved statements made to teachers by a child victim, where the primary purpose was not to create evidence for prosecution.

Here, the NPLEX records are fundamentally different. As argued by the Defense, the NPLEX system is a law enforcement tool designed specifically to track pseudoephedrine purchases for the purpose of investigating and prosecuting drug crimes. In *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), the Supreme Court held that forensic laboratory reports are testimonial because they are created solely for the purpose of establishing a fact at trial. Similarly, the NPLEX records in this case were generated to establish that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine, an element of the offense. The primary purpose of these records is to aid in prosecution, placing them within the scope of "testimonial" evidence as defined in *Melendez-Diaz*. Therefore, the admission of these records without the testimony of the individual who created or maintained them violated Ms. Kittle's rights under the Confrontation Clause.

**III. Business Records Exception and Authentication**

Even if the Confrontation Clause were not implicated, the Defense has demonstrated that the NPLEX records fail to qualify for the business records exception under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6).

**A. Regular Course of Business**

The Prosecution argues the records are admissible under *State v. Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, 153 Ohio App. 3d 532, 794 N.E.2d 588 (8th Dist.). However, the Defense correctly cites *State v. The facts provided indicate that Officer Lemonier was the lead investigator but do not establish that he had any operational knowledge of the NPLEX database, its input procedures, or its quality control measures. Based on the foregoing analysis, the Defense has established that the trial court likely erred in admitting the NPLEX records. # DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the prosecution's mischaracterization of both the nature of the NPLEX system and the applicable legal standards. The prosecution's arguments fail to overcome the well-reasoned analysis set forth in the preliminary ruling, and this Court should affirm the reversal of the trial court's judgment.

## I. The Prosecution's Attempt to Distinguish Vondran Fails

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *State v. Vondran*, 2015-Ohio-1483, 2015 WL 1383637 (2nd Dist.), by arguing that NPLEX is a "state-mandated regulatory database" rather than an internal police report. This distinction is unpersuasive because it ignores the fundamental nature of the NPLEX system as applied in this case.

The facts establish that Officer Lemonier accessed the NPLEX database specifically to investigate Ms. Kittle's involvement in methamphetamine production. The records were not generated as part of routine regulatory compliance by pharmacists or pharmaceutical companies. Rather, the officers retrieved these records as part of their criminal investigation following the discovery of the methamphetamine lab. The prosecution's argument that the records serve a "regulatory purpose" ignores the reality that in this case, the records were obtained and used solely for prosecution.

As this Court correctly noted in the preliminary ruling, records created specifically for law enforcement purposes do not fall within the business records exception. *Vondran* at ¶ 25. The fact that the database exists pursuant to a statutory scheme does not transform its use in a particular case into a regulatory function. When Officer Lemonier queried the NPLEX system to identify Ms. Kittle's pseudoephedrine purchases, he was engaged in investigative activity, not regulatory oversight.

## II. Strothers Does Not Control This Case

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, 153 Ohio App. 3d 532, 794 N.E.2d 588 (8th Dist.), is misplaced. In *Strothers*, the court addressed whether NPLEX records were admissible, but the facts of that case are distinguishable.

In *Strothers*, the defendant was charged with trafficking in pseudoephedrine, and the court focused on the records' admissibility under the business records exception. However, the preliminary ruling correctly identified the critical distinction: the *Strothers* court did not conduct a thorough Confrontation Clause analysis, as the defendant in that case did not raise a Sixth Amendment challenge. The prosecution cannot bootstrap a case where the Confrontation Clause was not at issue to establish that the Clause is inapplicable here.

Moreover, the prosecution overreads *Strothers* when it claims the court "explicitly permitted" officer authentication. The *Strothers* court found authentication sufficient under the specific facts of that case, where the officer testified about his familiarity with the NPLEX system. Here, the facts establish only that Officer Lemonier "retrieved" records from the database. There is no indication that he had any familiarity with the record-keeping system, its input procedures, or its quality control measures.

The defense maintains that *State v. Brown*, 2014-Ohio-496, 2014 WL 555380 (10th Dist.), provides the correct standard. In *Brown*, the court reversed admission of pharmaceutical tracking records because the testifying officer lacked personal knowledge of the record-keeping system. The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Brown* on the basis that it involved "private pharmacy records" is unavailing. The principle underlying *Brown—that a witness must be familiar with the record-keeping system to establish the foundation required by Evidence Rule 803(6)—applies with equal force to state-operated databases.

## III. The Confrontation Clause Applies to the NPLEX Records

The prosecution's argument that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial because they were created for "regulatory purposes" misapplies the analytical framework established by the Supreme Court.

In *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), the Supreme Court held that forensic laboratory reports are testimonial because they are created "for the purpose of proving" facts at trial. The Court emphasized that the critical inquiry is the "primary purpose" of the statement or record. *Id.* at 2521.

The prosecution relies on *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), but that case is distinguishable. In *Clark*, the Court held that statements made by a child victim to teachers were not testimonial because their primary purpose was not to create evidence for prosecution but to protect the child. The Court explicitly distinguished business records created in the regular course of business from those created for litigation purposes.

Here, the NPLEX records were generated specifically to track pseudoephedrine purchases—chemicals that can be used to manufacture methamphetamine. The system was created as a tool to investigate and prevent drug crimes. When Officer Lemonier accessed these records, his purpose was to establish that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine, an essential element of the charged offense. The primary purpose of these records, as applied in this criminal prosecution, was to prove facts at trial.

The prosecution's argument that "the mere fact that the records were later used in a criminal prosecution does not automatically render them inadmissible" (quoting *Strothers*) conflates the business records analysis with the Confrontation Clause analysis. Even if records satisfy the business records exception, they may still be testimonial and subject to the Sixth Amendment's requirements.

The defense further notes that the prosecution's reliance on *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647, 131 S. Ct. 2705, 180 L. Ed. 2d 610 (2011), is not "misplaced," as the prosecution suggests. In *Bullcoming*, the Court held that a forensic laboratory report analyzing blood alcohol content was testimonial. The reasoning in *Bullcoming—that reports created to establish facts at trial are testimonial—applies with equal force to NPLEX records created to establish that a defendant purchased pseudoephedrine.

## IV. The Error Was Not Harmless

The prosecution's final argument—that Ms. Kittle's admission renders any error harmless—fails for two reasons.

First, the admission itself must be properly corroborated. Ms. Kittle's statement that she "bought pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine" was corroborated by the NPLEX records. Without those records, the prosecution's evidence of the actual purchase—distinct from the admission—diminishes significantly.

Second, and more importantly, the prosecution conflates sufficiency of evidence with harmless error analysis. Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 87 S. Ct. 824, 17 L. Ed. 2d 705 (1967), and Ohio's harmless error standard in *State v. Perry*, 101 Ohio St. 3d 205, 2004-Ohio-455, 803 N.E.2d 835 (2004), the question is whether the error "did not contribute to the conviction." The prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the error was harmless.

Here, the NPLEX records established the objective fact of Ms. Kittle's pseudoephedrine purchases. Her admission, while significant, was a subjective statement that required corroboration. The prosecution cannot establish beyond a reasonable doubt that the erroneous admission of the NPLEX records did not contribute to the conviction when those records provided the only independent corroboration of the essential element of the offense.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, and those set forth in the defense's opening argument, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the preliminary ruling reversing the trial court's judgment. The NPLEX records were testimonial in nature, requiring confrontation under the Sixth Amendment, and were improperly authenticated under the business records exception. The error was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

The prosecution has failed to overcome the persuasive analysis set forth in the preliminary ruling, and its arguments mischaracterize both the nature of the NPLEX system and the controlling precedent. This Court should affirm the reversal and remand for further proceedings.

**UPDATED RULING**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court finds that the Prosecution’s arguments regarding the admissibility of the NPLEX records are persuasive. The preliminary ruling is withdrawn, and the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The determination of whether evidence is "testimonial" under the Confrontation Clause is a legal question subject to *de novo* review. *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647, 131 S. Ct. 2705, 180 L. Ed. 2d 610 (2011). However, the admission of evidence under the business records exception to the hearsay rule is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *State v. Sage*, 31 Ohio St. 3d 173, 510 N.E.2d 343 (1987).

**II. Business Records Exception**

The central issue is whether the NPLEX records qualify as business records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6). The Defense relies on *State v. Vondran*, 2015-Ohio-1483, 2015 WL 1383637 (2nd Dist.), arguing that records created for law enforcement purposes are inadmissible. However, the Prosecution correctly identifies *State v. Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, 153 Ohio App. 3d 532 (8th Dist.), as the controlling authority regarding NPLEX records.

In *Strothers*, the court held that NPLEX records are maintained by the Ohio Bureau of Criminal Identification and Investigation (BCI) for regulatory compliance under R.C. 3719.41, and are thus created in the regular course of business for administrative purposes rather than solely for litigation. *Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, at ¶ 20. The court explicitly stated that "the mere fact that the records were later used in a criminal prosecution does not automatically render them inadmissible." *Id.* This distinguishes the case at hand from *Vondran*, which involved internal police reports created specifically for investigative purposes. Because the NPLEX system is a state-mandated regulatory database designed to monitor pseudoephedrine sales for public safety, the records fall within the business records exception. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting them.

**III. Confrontation Clause Analysis**

The Defense argues that the NPLEX records are "testimonial" under the Sixth Amendment, citing *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009). However, the Prosecution persuasively argues that the records are non-testimonial under the framework established in *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015).

In *Clark*, the Supreme Court held that statements made in the course of regular business operations—or for the purpose of public safety and regulatory compliance—are not testimonial. *Id.* at 2180. Unlike the forensic laboratory reports in *Melendez-Diaz*, which were created solely for the purpose of establishing a fact at trial, the NPLEX records are generated by pharmacies to comply with statutory obligations regarding the sale of pseudoephedrine. The primary purpose of these records is regulatory, not prosecutorial. Therefore, the Confrontation Clause does not bar their admission. The Defense's attempt to characterize the NPLEX system solely as a law enforcement tool ignores the statutory mandate that drives its creation, as recognized in *Strothers*.

**IV. Authentication**

The Defense contends that Officer Lemonier was unqualified to authenticate the records because he lacked personal knowledge of the record-keeping system, relying on *State v. Brown*, 2014-Ohio-496, 2014 WL 555380 (10th Dist.). However, *Strothers* directly addresses this issue and holds otherwise.

In *Strothers*, the court found that an officer’s testimony that he obtained the records from the NPLEX database maintained by BCI was a sufficient foundation for authentication. *Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, at ¶ 21. The rule does not require the witness to be the custodian of records; it only requires the witness to be familiar with the record-keeping system or able to testify that the record was made in the regular course of business. Officer Lemonier’s testimony that he retrieved the records from the state-maintained system satisfies this requirement. *Brown* is distinguishable because it involved private pharmacy records where the officer had no connection to the record-keeping process, whereas NPLEX is a centralized state system.

**V. Harmless Error**

Finally, even if the admission of the NPLEX records was erroneous, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Ms. Kittle admitted during the police interview that she purchased pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine. This admission provided independent evidence of the element of possession or assembly of chemicals. Under *State v. Perry*, 101 Ohio St. 3d 205, 2004-Ohio-455, 803 N.E.2d 835 (2004), the prosecution has met its burden of showing that the error did not contribute to the conviction.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admitted the NPLEX records under the business records exception, and their admission did not violate the Confrontation Clause. The records were properly authenticated, and any potential error was harmless. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

May it please the Court, the Defense respectfully submits that the prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving that the NPLEX records were properly admissible, and that even if such records were admitted, the evidence was insufficient to support Ms. Kittle's conviction. The prosecution's arguments rest on a fundamental mischaracterization of the NPLEX system and ignore critical distinctions between regulatory record-keeping and the business records exception to the hearsay rule. The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## I. THE NPLEX RECORDS DO NOT FALL WITHIN THE BUSINESS RECORDS EXCEPTION

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records qualify as business records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6). This Court should reject this argument for several reasons.

### A. The Primary Purpose of NPLEX Records Is Law Enforcement, Not Business Administration

The business records exception under Evidence Rule 803(6) requires that records be made in the "regular course of business." As this Court recognized in *State v. Vondran*, 2015-Ohio-1483, 2015 WL 1383637 (2nd Dist.), records created primarily for law enforcement purposes do not fall within the business records exception. In *Vondran*, the Second District Court of Appeals held that internal police reports and records created specifically for investigative purposes are inadmissible because their primary purpose is litigation, not business administration.

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Vondran* by arguing that NPLEX records are created pursuant to R.C. 3719.41 for regulatory compliance. However, this argument ignores the practical reality of how NPLEX functions. The system was designed and implemented specifically to assist law enforcement in tracking purchases of pseudoephedrine—a precursor chemical used in methamphetamine production. While the statute may provide a regulatory framework, the practical effect and primary function of NPLEX is to gather evidence for criminal prosecutions.

The Defense submits that *State v. Vondran*, 2015-Ohio-1483, controls here. The NPLEX system, while technically maintained by the Ohio Bureau of Criminal Identification and Investigation, serves a fundamentally law enforcement function. Pharmacists are required to report these sales precisely because the information is valuable to law enforcement in combating methamphetamine production. The mere fact that a statutory mandate exists does not transform law enforcement records into business records.

### B. The Prosecution Misreads *State v. Strothers*

The prosecution relies heavily on *State v. Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, 153 Ohio App. 3d 532 (8th Dist.), to support its position. However, the Defense submits that *Strothers* is distinguishable and does not control the instant case.

In *Strothers*, the Eighth District Court of Appeals addressed whether NPLEX records could be admitted as business records. The court in *Strothers* found that because the records were maintained for regulatory compliance under R.C. 3719.41, they were created in the regular course of business. However, the *Strothers* court failed to conduct a thorough analysis of whether the primary purpose of these records was truly administrative or whether it was, in fact, law enforcement.

The Defense submits that *Strothers* was incorrectly decided and that this Court should follow the reasoning in *Vondran* instead. The NPLEX system exists to track purchases of a chemical that is primarily used in the illegal manufacture of drugs. The regulatory framework exists precisely because law enforcement needs this information to investigate and prosecute drug crimes. To hold that such records are "business records" simply because they are mandated by statute ignores the reality of why such mandates exist.

Even if *Strothers* is binding precedent, it is distinguishable on its facts. In *Strothers*, the court was presented with a different factual scenario involving different types of records. The fundamental principle from *Strothers*—that records created solely for litigation are inadmissible—should govern here. The NPLEX records were created for the purpose of aiding law enforcement, not for the administration of a business enterprise.

---

## II. THE NPLEX RECORDS ARE TESTIMONIAL AND VIOLATE THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE

The prosecution argues that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial under the framework established in *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015). This Court should reject this argument.

### A. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial Under *Melendez-Diaz* and *Bullcoming*

In *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), the Supreme Court held that forensic laboratory reports are testimonial and that the Confrontation Clause requires that the analyst who created the report testify unless the defendant has an opportunity to cross-examine. Similarly, in *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647, 131 S. Ct. 2705, 180 L. Ed. 2d 610 (2011), the Court held that a forensic laboratory report analyzing blood alcohol content was testimonial and could not be admitted through the testimony of a different analyst who did not perform the test.

The prosecution attempts to distinguish these cases by arguing that NPLEX records are not forensic reports. However, this distinction is unavailing. The critical question under the Sixth Amendment is whether the statements were made with the primary purpose of establishing or proving past events potentially relevant to a later criminal prosecution. See *Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813, 126 S. Ct. 2266, 165 L. Ed. 2d 224 (2006).

The NPLEX records are created when a pharmacy reports the sale of pseudoephedrine to a government database. While the prosecution argues this is done for regulatory compliance, the reality is that these records are created precisely because law enforcement needs them to investigate drug crimes. The primary purpose of these records is to establish a paper trail that can be used in criminal prosecutions. Under *Melendez-Diaz* and *Bullcoming*, such records are testimonial.

### B. *Ohio v. Clark* Does Not Authorize Admission of These Records

The prosecution relies on *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), for the proposition that records created for regulatory purposes are non-testimonial. However, *Clark* is distinguishable.

In *Clark*, the Supreme Court held that statements made to school officials by a student victim were not testimonial because they were made in the context of a crisis involving potential child abuse, not solely for the purpose of establishing facts for trial. The Court emphasized that the statements were made in the context of an ongoing emergency and were aimed at addressing a immediate threat to child welfare.

The NPLEX records are fundamentally different. They are not generated in response to an emergency or to address an immediate threat. Rather, they are routine records created after every transaction involving pseudoephedrine, stored indefinitely in a database accessible to law enforcement. The primary purpose of these records is not public safety in the immediate sense, but rather the generation of evidence for future criminal prosecutions.

The Defense submits that *Clark* should be interpreted narrowly and that the NPLEX records do not fall within its scope. The records were created for the purpose of building criminal cases, not for addressing an immediate public safety crisis.

---

## III. OFFICER LEMONIER WAS NOT PROPERLY QUALIFIED TO AUTHENTICATE THE NPLEX RECORDS

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier's testimony was sufficient to authenticate the NPLEX records. The Defense submits that this argument fails.

### A. The Requirements for Authentication Under Ohio Law

Under Ohio Evidence Rule 901(A), authentication requires "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what its proponent claims." To lay a foundation for business records under Rule 803(6), the proponent must show that: (1) the record was made by a person with knowledge of the act, event, or condition recorded; (2) the record was made at or near the time of the act, event, or condition; (3) the record is kept in the regular course of business; and (4) making the record was a regular practice of that person.

As this Court recognized in *State v. Brown*, 2014-Ohio-496, 2014 WL 555380 (10th Dist.), a witness must have personal knowledge of the record-keeping system to authenticate records. In *Brown*, the Tenth District Court of Appeals held that an officer who had no connection to the pharmacy's record-keeping process could not authenticate pharmacy records.

### B. Officer Lemonier Lacked the Requisite Knowledge

In this case, Officer Lemonier testified that he obtained the NPLEX records from the database maintained by BCI. However, there is no evidence that Officer Lemonier had any personal knowledge of how the NPLEX system operates, who enters the data, or whether the records are made in the regular course of business.

The prosecution argues that *Strothers* holds that an officer's testimony that he obtained records from the NPLEX database is sufficient. However, the Defense submits that *Strothers* incorrectly applied the authentication standard. Simply retrieving records from a database does not establish that the records were made in the regular course of business or that the person who entered the information had personal knowledge of the transaction.

Officer Lemonier is not a pharmacist. He did not witness the sale of pseudoephedrine. He has no knowledge of the pharmacy's record-keeping practices. Under *Brown*, his testimony is insufficient to authenticate the NPLEX records.

---

## IV. EVEN IF ADMITTED, THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO CONVICT

The prosecution argues that even if the NPLEX records were improperly admitted, the error was harmless because Ms. Kittle made an admission. The Defense submits that this argument fails for two reasons.

### A. The Admission Does Not Establish All Elements of the Offense

Ms. Kittle admitted that she "bought pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine." However, this admission, even if credited, does not establish that Ms. Kittle possessed or assembled chemicals for the manufacture of drugs.

Under Ohio Revised Code Section 2925.041(A), the offense of illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs requires that the defendant "possess or have under the person's control any quantity of any" chemical compound that may be used in the manufacture of a controlled substance. The statute requires that the defendant actually possess or control the chemicals.

Ms. Kittle's admission establishes only that she purchased pseudoephedrine for J.S. It does not establish that she possessed the chemicals at any time, that she was present at the methamphetamine lab, or that she participated in the manufacturing process. The prosecution still must prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

### B. The Harmless Error Analysis Is Inappropriate Here

The prosecution argues that any error in admitting the NPLEX records was harmless. However, the harmless error doctrine applies only where the prosecution can prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the conviction. See *State v. Perry*, 101 Ohio St. 3d 205, 2004-Ohio-455, 803 N.E.2d 835 (2004).

Here, the NPLEX records were the only evidence linking Ms. Kittle to the pseudoephedrine purchases. Without these records, the prosecution's case rests entirely on Ms. Kittle's admission—a statement that, standing alone, does not establish all elements of the offense. The prosecution cannot meet its burden of proving harmless error.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court affirms the judgment of the trial court. **I. BUSINESS RECORDS EXCEPTION**

The Defense contends that the NPLEX records are inadmissible because their primary purpose is law enforcement, relying on *State v. Vondran*, 2015-Ohio-1483, 2015 WL 1383637 (2nd Dist.). In *Vondran*, the court excluded internal police reports created specifically for the purpose of investigating a defendant. However, this distinction is critical. The NPLEX records at issue are not internal police reports; they are pharmacy logs generated pursuant to a statutory mandate under R.C. 3719.41.

As the Prosecution correctly notes, *State v. Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, 153 Ohio App. 3d 532 (8th Dist.), is the controlling authority on this issue. In *Strothers*, the court held that NPLEX records are maintained by the Ohio Bureau of Criminal Identification and Investigation (BCI) for regulatory compliance and are thus created in the regular course of business. *Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, at ¶ 20. The court explicitly rejected the argument that the potential use of these records by law enforcement renders them inadmissible, stating, "the mere fact that the records were later used in a criminal prosecution does not automatically render them inadmissible." *Id.*

The Defense asks this Court to disregard *Strothers* or find it distinguishable based on the "practical reality" that the system aids law enforcement. However, the legal test for the business records exception under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) focuses on whether the record was made in the regular course of business. The statutory requirement for pharmacies to log pseudoephedrine sales creates a regularized, administrative duty. The fact that the legislature designed this regulatory scheme to aid law enforcement does not transform the pharmacy’s business records into law enforcement investigatory documents. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records under the business records exception.

**II. CONFRONTATION CLAUSE**

The Defense argues that the NPLEX records are "testimonial" under the Sixth Amendment, citing *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527, 174 L. Ed. 2d 314 (2009), and *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647, 131 S. Ct. 2705, 180 L. Ed. 2d 610 (2011). Those cases involved forensic analysts creating reports specifically for use in criminal trials. The NPLEX records, by contrast, are created by third-party pharmacies during the ordinary course of commercial transactions.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), guides the analysis here. In *Clark*, the Court held that statements made to teachers by a child victim were not testimonial because they were made in the context of an ongoing emergency and for the purpose of public safety, rather than for the primary purpose of establishing evidence for trial. *Id.* at 2180. Similarly, NPLEX records are generated to comply with a public safety regulatory scheme (R.C. 3719.41) aimed at preventing the diversion of pseudoephedrine. The primary purpose of the record-keeping is regulatory compliance and immediate public safety monitoring, not the creation of evidence for a future prosecution. While the Defense correctly notes that *Clark* involved an emergency situation, the broader principle regarding statements made for administrative purposes rather than evidentiary ones applies. The records are non-testimonial business records, and their admission did not violate Ms. Kittle’s Confrontation Clause rights.

**III. AUTHENTICATION**

The Defense challenges the authentication of the records, arguing that Officer Lemonier lacked personal knowledge of the record-keeping system, relying on *State v. Brown*, 2014-Ohio-496, 2014 WL 555380 (10th Dist.). *Brown* involved private pharmacy records where the testifying officer had no connection to the pharmacy or its record-keeping practices.

However, *Strothers* directly addresses the authentication of NPLEX records. The *Strothers* court found that an officer’s testimony that he obtained the records from the NPLEX database maintained by BCI was sufficient to lay a proper foundation. *Strothers*, 2003-Ohio-3752, at ¶ 21. Because NPLEX is a centralized, state-maintained system, the officer’s ability to retrieve the records from that system serves as verification of their authenticity. The rule does not require the witness to be the custodian of the records or to have personal knowledge of the specific transaction, only that the record is identified as one kept in the regular course of business. Officer Lemonier’s testimony met this standard.

**IV. HARMLESS ERROR**

Finally, the Defense argues that even if the records were admissible, the error was not harmless because Ms. Kittle’s admission was insufficient to support the conviction. The Defense contends that the admission—"bought pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine"—does not prove she possessed the chemicals or assembled them for manufacturing.

This argument misinterprets the statute and the evidence. Ohio Revised Code Section 2925.041(A) prohibits the illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. Ms. Kittle’s admission that she purchased pseudoephedrine (a chemical used in manufacture) in exchange for methamphetamine establishes that she possessed the chemical and did so with the knowledge and intent that it would be used to manufacture drugs. This admission alone is sufficient to establish the elements of the offense.

Under *State v. Perry*, 101 Ohio St. 3d 205, 2004-Ohio-455, 803 N.E.2d 835 (2004), the prosecution must show that the error did not contribute to the conviction. Given Ms. Kittle’s direct admission to the conduct constituting the offense, the exclusion of the NPLEX records would not have changed the outcome of the trial. The admission provided independent evidence of the crime, rendering any error in admitting the records harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

The trial court properly admitted the NPLEX records under the business records exception, and their admission did not violate the Confrontation Clause. The records were properly authenticated by Officer Lemonier pursuant to *State v. Strothers*. Furthermore, any potential error was harmless given Ms. Kittle’s admission to the elements of the offense. The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
