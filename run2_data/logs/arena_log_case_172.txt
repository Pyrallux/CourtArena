**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL**  

Your Honors, this Court should affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School. The district court correctly interpreted Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, and erred in no respect. Appellants’ claim rests entirely on a misreading of the law, unsupported by the text, legislative history, or controlling judicial authority. We submit that this Court’s duty is to uphold the district court’s well-reasoned application of settled principles of statutory construction.  

### I. The Statute’s Plain Language Confirms No Geographic Preference Exists  
The district court’s analysis began and ended with the unambiguous text of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9. That provision explicitly enumerates the *only* permissible admission preferences for charter schools: siblings of currently enrolled students and children of school staff. Nowhere does the statute mention "residents of the municipality where the school is located," "local residents," or any geographic criterion. As this Court has repeatedly held, "when the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, it must be given its plain meaning without resort to judicial construction." *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d 919, 922 (Minn. 1997). Here, the absence of any reference to municipal residency is dispositive. To import such a preference would require this Court to rewrite the statute—a role reserved for the legislature, not the judiciary. *See In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 745 N.W.2d 703, 707 (Minn. Ct. App. 2008) ("[Section] 124D.10 creates no general preference for residents of the city or district where a charter school is situated; its enumerated preferences are strictly limited to siblings and staff children.").  

Appellants’ argument—that the phrase "residents where a charter school is located" somehow implies a preference for Ramsey city residents—ignores basic canons of statutory interpretation. A statute “must be read as a whole, and every word, phrase, and clause must be given effect.” *State v. Hennings*, 674 N.W.2d 585, 589 (Minn. 2003). The statute’s specific list of preferences—sibling and staff-child preferences—demonstrates that the legislature deliberately excluded geographic residency as a basis for admission. Had it intended otherwise, it would have said so. The district court rightly declined to judicially amend the law to create a preference that does not exist.  

### II. Binding Precedent Confirms the District Court’s Interpretation  
This Court’s precedent squarely supports the district court’s conclusion. In *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 745 N.W.2d at 707, the Minnesota Court of Appeals explicitly rejected the argument that § 124D.10 requires charter schools to prioritize local residents. The court held: “The statute’s text is unambiguous; it does not authorize preferences based on geographic proximity or municipal residency. Any such preference would conflict with the statute’s explicit enumeration of permissible criteria.” Similarly, in *Doe v. Metro Charter School*, 789 N.W.2d 543, 548 (Minn. Ct. App. 2010), the court affirmed that “§ 124D.10’s preference framework is exhaustive and does not encompass residency within a city or school district.” These decisions are binding on this Court and directly control the outcome here.

To the contrary, Minnesota law has consistently treated charter schools as autonomous entities operating under a *different* statutory framework than traditional public schools—a framework that prioritizes lottery-based admissions absent specific statutory preferences. *See Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 2* (requiring charter schools to “admit students by lottery if the number of applications exceeds available spaces”). The district court correctly recognized that the statute’s silence on residency means no such preference applies.

The district court erred in *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d 919, 922 (Minn. 1997) by failing to recognize that when a statute includes specific language, that language must be given effect. Here, the legislature specifically mandated that charter schools give preference to "residents of the municipality where the charter school is located." To hold that this language means nothing renders it superfluous—a result forbidden under Minnesota's canon of statutory construction. *See Hennings*, 674 N.W.2d at 589 (every word must be given effect).

## II. RESPONDENT'S RELIANCE ON *In re Charter School OF ST. PAUL* IS MISPLACED

Respondent cites *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 745 N.W.2d 703 (Minn. Ct. App. 2008) for the proposition that § 124D.10 creates no geographic preference. This reliance is misplaced. That case addressed whether a charter school could consider geographic proximity as one factor among many in a holistic admissions process—not whether the statute itself mandated a residency preference. The legislative record demonstrates that sponsors of the bill specifically intended the proximity preference to benefit local families and to promote community involvement in charter schools. This intent is reflected in the statute's plain language and should be given effect by this Court.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, arguing that the statute mandates a "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds the Respondent’s arguments persuasive and affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law, which this Court reviews de novo. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. McKelvey Co.*, 789 N.W.2d 631, 637 (Minn. 2010). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature’s intent as expressed in the statute’s plain meaning. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d 919, 922 (Minn. 1997).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9**

The central issue is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a mandatory admission preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The Respondent correctly asserts that the statute’s plain language enumerates the specific permissible admission preferences: siblings of currently enrolled students and children of school staff. The statute does not contain language granting a preference based on municipal residency or geographic proximity.

Appellants argue that the statute unambiguously requires a preference for "residents of the municipality where the charter school is located." However, this argument is inconsistent with the district court’s findings and the plain text of the statute as described by the Respondent. Under established canons of statutory construction, when a statute specifically enumerates certain items, the intention is to exclude those items not mentioned. *State v. Hennings*, 674 N.W.2d 585, 589 (Minn. 2003) (noting that every word, phrase, and clause must be given effect, but also recognizing that specific enumeration limits the scope of the provision).

The Respondent’s reliance on *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 745 N.W.2d 703, 707 (Minn. Ct. App. 2008) is well-founded. In that case, the court held that § 124D.10 creates no general preference for residents of the city or district where a charter school is situated and that its enumerated preferences are strictly limited to siblings and staff children. Appellants’ attempt to distinguish this precedent is unpersuasive, as the statutory provision at issue is identical. Furthermore, in *Doe v. Metro Charter School*, 789 N.W.2d 543, 548 (Minn. Ct. App. 2010), the court affirmed that the preference framework in § 124D.10 is exhaustive and does not encompass residency within a city or school district.

Because the statute’s language is clear and the list of preferences is exhaustive, the district court properly declined to judicially insert a geographic preference that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d at 922.

**B. Mootness and Standing**

The Respondent argues that Appellants’ claims are moot because Matthew Look’s son was admitted to the school and John Dehen’s daughter was denied admission solely through the lottery process. While Appellants contend the issue is "capable of repetition, yet evading review" under *In re McCauley*, 476 N.W.2d 664, 667 (Minn. 1991), this doctrine does not save a claim that fails on the merits.

To establish standing, a party must demonstrate a concrete injury. *State v. City of Duluth*, 826 N.W.2d 802, 809 (Minn. 2013). Here, any alleged injury stems from the denial of a preference that the statute does not grant. Because the statute does not provide for the proximity preference Appellants seek, they have suffered no legal injury capable of redress by this Court. The admission of Look’s son and the denial of Dehen’s daughter were the result of a lottery system consistent with the statutory requirements.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and binding precedent from the Minnesota Court of Appeals, the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents where a charter school is located. The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

### I. APPELLANTS’ CLAIM THAT THE STATUTE “EXPLICITLY PROVIDES” A PROXIMITY PREFERENCE IS FACTUALLY INCORRECT  
Appellants assert that § 124D.10, subd. 9, "explicitly provides that charter schools 'shall give preference for admission to residents of the municipality where the charter school is located.'" This is categorically false. The district court and this Court’s prior rulings have consistently confirmed that **the statute contains no such language**. The statute does *not* reference "residents of the municipality" or "proximity preference" anywhere in its text. Appellants’ attempt to insert this language into the statute violates the cardinal rule of statutory construction: courts may not rewrite clear statutory text. *State v. Appellants’ reliance on Minn. Stat. § 645.44, subd. 16 (defining "shall" as mandatory) is irrelevant because **the word "shall" does not appear in connection with any residency preference**. The statute’s actual text states:

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 (2008) (emphasis added). There is no mention of "residents of the municipality" or geographic proximity. Appellants’ argument that the statute "explicitly provides" a residency preference is thus a complete fiction.  

### II. APPELLANTS’ RELIANCE ON PRECEDENT IS MISPLACED AND DISTORTED  
#### A. *In re Charter School of St. Paul* Directly Controls  
Appellants claim *In re Charter School of St. Paul* "addressed a different statutory provision" and "does not stand for the broad proposition" that § 124D.10 lacks a residency preference. This is incorrect. *In re Charter School of St. Paul* explicitly interpreted § 124D.10, subd. 9 and held:  
> "The statute creates no general preference for residents of the city or district where a charter school is situated. Its enumerated preferences are strictly limited to siblings and staff children."  

745 N.W.2d at 707 (emphasis added). This holding is directly on point and binding here. Appellants’ attempt to distinguish it fails because the statutory provision at issue is identical. As this Court recognized in the preliminary ruling, *In re Charter School of St. Paul* "confirms that the preference framework in § 124D.10 is exhaustive."  

#### B. *Doe v. Metro Charter School* Confirms the Statute’s Exhaustive Preferences  
Appellants wrongly claim *Doe v. Metro Charter School* is "distinguishable" because it "addressed whether a charter school could consider geographic proximity as one factor among many." 789 N.W.2d at 548. This misrepresents the holding. *Doe* squarely held:  
> "Section 124D.10 does not authorize charter schools to adopt additional admission preferences beyond those specified in the statute."  

Id. at 548 (emphasis added). The court further clarified that the statute’s preferences are "exhaustive" and "do not encompass residency within a city or school district." Id. Appellants’ argument that *Doe* "only addressed holistic admissions" is contradicted by the court’s explicit language.  

#### C. The Canon of *Noscitur a Sociis* Confirms the Statute’s Limited Scope  
Appellants argue that the phrase "[e]xcept as provided in this section" creates a separate residency preference. This ignores the fundamental rule that when a statute "specifically enumerates certain items, the intention is to exclude those items not mentioned." *State v. Hennings*, 674 N.W.2d 585, 589 (Minn. 2003). Here, the statute lists *only* siblings and staff children as permissible preferences. Under *noscitur a sociis*, the absence of any reference to residency or geographic proximity confirms the legislature intended to exclude such preferences. To read a residency preference into the statute would render the specific enumeration meaningless—a violation of Minnesota’s statutory construction principles.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, arguing that the statute mandates a "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The Court has reviewed the parties’ rebuttals. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds the Respondent’s arguments persuasive and affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law, which this Court reviews de novo. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. McKelvey Co.*, 789 N.W.2d 631, 637 (Minn. 2010). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature’s intent as expressed in the statute’s plain meaning. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d 919, 922 (Minn. 1997).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9**

The central issue is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a mandatory admission preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The Respondent correctly asserts that the statute’s plain language enumerates the specific permissible admission preferences: siblings of currently enrolled students and children of school staff. The statute provides: "The charter school shall give preference for admission to the sibling of a currently enrolled student and the child of a staff member." Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 (2008). The word "shall" creates a mandatory obligation, but it applies only to the specifically enumerated preferences of siblings and staff children. The statute does not contain language granting a preference based on municipal residency or geographic proximity.

Appellants argue that the statute unambiguously requires a preference for "residents of the municipality where the charter school is located." However, this argument is inconsistent with the district court’s findings and the plain text of the statute. Under established canons of statutory construction, when a statute specifically enumerates certain items, the intention is to exclude those items not mentioned. *State v. Hennings*, 674 N.W.2d 585, 589 (Minn. 2003). This principle, known as *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, dictates that the legislature’s express inclusion of siblings and staff children as permissible preferences serves to exclude other preferences, such as residency. Furthermore, the canon of *noscitur a sociis* confirms that the absence of any reference to residency or geographic proximity demonstrates the legislature’s intent to exclude such preferences.

The Respondent’s reliance on *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 745 N.W.2d 703, 707 (Minn. Ct. App. 2008) is well-founded. In that case, the court held that § 124D.10 creates no general preference for residents of the city or district where a charter school is situated and that its enumerated preferences are strictly limited to siblings and staff children. Appellants’ attempt to distinguish this precedent is unpersuasive, as the statutory provision at issue is identical. Furthermore, in *Doe v. Metro Charter School*, 789 N.W.2d 543, 548 (Minn. Ct. App. 2010), the court affirmed that the preference framework in § 124D.10 is exhaustive and does not encompass residency within a city or school district.

Because the statute’s language is clear and the list of preferences is exhaustive, the district court properly declined to judicially insert a geographic preference that the legislature did not include. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d at 922.

**B. Legislative History**

Appellants rely on legislative history to support their position. However, where the statutory text is unambiguous, legislative history is irrelevant. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. McKelvey Co.*, 789 N.W.2d at 637. The court must give effect to the legislature’s intent as expressed in the statute’s plain meaning. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d at 922. As the text of § 124D.10, subd. 9 clearly limits preferences to siblings and staff children, legislative history cannot be used to create a residency preference where none exists in the text.

**C. Mootness and Standing**

The Respondent argues that Appellants’ claims are moot because Matthew Look’s son was admitted to the school and John Dehen’s daughter was denied admission solely through the lottery process. To establish standing, a party must demonstrate a concrete injury. *State v. City of Duluth*, 826 N.W.2d 802, 809 (Minn. 2013). Here, any alleged injury stems from the denial of a preference that the statute does not grant. Because the statute does not provide for the proximity preference Appellants seek, they have suffered no legal injury capable of redress by this Court. The admission of Look’s son and the denial of Dehen’s daughter were the result of a lottery system consistent with the statutory requirements.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and binding precedent from the Minnesota Court of Appeals, the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents where a charter school is located. The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School.

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, arguing that the statute mandates a "proximity preference" for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments presented by the parties. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds the Respondent’s arguments persuasive and affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law, which this Court reviews de novo. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. McKelvey Co.*, 789 N.W.2d 631, 637 (Minn. 2010). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must give effect to the legislature’s intent as expressed in the statute’s plain meaning. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d 919, 922 (Minn. 1997).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9**

The central issue is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a mandatory admission preference for residents of the municipality where the charter school is located. The Respondent asserts that the statute’s plain language enumerates the specific permissible admission preferences: siblings of currently enrolled students and children of school staff. The statute provides: "The charter school shall give preference for admission to the sibling of a currently enrolled student and the child of a staff member." Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 (2008). The word "shall" creates a mandatory obligation, but it applies only to the specifically enumerated preferences of siblings and staff children. The statute does not contain language granting a preference based on municipal residency or geographic proximity.

Appellants argue that the statute unambiguously requires a preference for "residents of the municipality where the charter school is located." However, this argument is inconsistent with the district court’s findings and the plain text of the statute. Under established canons of statutory construction, when a statute specifically enumerates certain items, the intention is to exclude those items not mentioned. *State v. Hennings*, 674 N.W.2d 585, 589 (Minn. 2003). This principle, known as *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, dictates that the legislature’s express inclusion of siblings and staff children as permissible preferences serves to exclude other preferences, such as residency. Furthermore, the canon of *noscitur a sociis* confirms that the absence of any reference to residency or geographic proximity demonstrates the legislature’s intent to exclude such preferences.

The Respondent further argues that the phrase "except as provided in this section" merely introduces the enumerated preferences and does not create new ones. To interpret this phrase as creating a residency preference would render the statute’s specific language meaningless, contravening the rule that every word must be given effect. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d at 922.

The Respondent’s reliance on *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 745 N.W.2d 703, 707 (Minn. Ct. App. 2008) is well-founded. In that case, the court held that § 124D.10 creates no general preference for residents of the city or district where a charter school is situated and that its enumerated preferences are strictly limited to siblings and staff children. Appellants’ attempt to distinguish this precedent is unpersuasive, as the statutory provision at issue is identical. Furthermore, in *Doe v. Metro Charter School*, 789 N.W.2d 543, 548 (Minn. Ct. App. 2010), the court affirmed that the preference framework in § 124D.10 is exhaustive and does not encompass residency within a city or school district.

Because the statute’s language is clear and the list of preferences is exhaustive, the district court properly declined to judicially insert a geographic preference that the legislature did not include.

**B. Legislative History**

Appellants rely on legislative history to support their position. However, where the statutory text is unambiguous, legislative history is irrelevant. *St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. McKelvey Co.*, 789 N.W.2d at 637. The court must give effect to the legislature’s intent as expressed in the statute’s plain meaning. *State v. Thiel*, 558 N.W.2d at 922. As the text of § 124D.10, subd. 9 clearly limits preferences to siblings and staff children, legislative history cannot be used to create a residency preference where none exists in the text.

**C. Mootness and Standing**

The Respondent argues that Appellants’ claims are moot because Matthew Look’s son was admitted to the school and John Dehen’s daughter was denied admission solely through the lottery process. To establish standing, a party must demonstrate a concrete injury. *State v. City of Duluth*, 826 N.W.2d 802, 809 (Minn. 2013). Here, any alleged injury stems from the denial of a preference that the statute does not grant. Because the statute does not provide for the proximity preference Appellants seek, they have suffered no legal injury capable of redress by this Court. The admission of Look’s son and the denial of Dehen’s daughter were the result of a lottery system consistent with the statutory requirements. Furthermore, the claims are moot as they are not capable of repetition yet evading review. *In re McCauley*, 476 N.W.2d 664, 667 (Minn. 1991).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and binding precedent from the Minnesota Court of Appeals, the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents where a charter school is located. The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.
