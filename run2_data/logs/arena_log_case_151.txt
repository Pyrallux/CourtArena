## I. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION: *MCLAUGHLIN* IS BINDING PRECEDENT

### A. Stare Decisis Requires Application of *McLaughlin*

The prosecution urges this Court to disregard *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), in favor of older authority such as *State v. McDaniel*, 186 Ariz. 310, 922 P.2d 399 (Ariz. 1996), and *State v. Jones*, 195 Ariz. 283, 987 P.2d 696 (Ariz. 1999). This argument fails because it ignores the fundamental principle of stare decisis. As this Court explained in *McLaughlin*, "stare decisis promotes the stability of the law and ensures that similarly situated parties are treated consistently." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. The prosecution's position would require this Court to overrule a recent decision of the Court of Appeals on the precise statutory interpretation issue presented here—a result that stare decisis expressly forbids absent extraordinary circumstances not present in this case.

The prosecution argues that *McLaughlin* "directly contradicts" established principles of statutory interpretation. However, the *McLaughlin* court did not ignore principles of statutory construction; rather, it applied those principles in context. The court recognized that while statutory language should be given its plain meaning, courts must also consider "the statute as a whole, its context, and the circumstances of its enactment." 240 Ariz. at 563 ¶ 12, 382 P.3d at 121. This contextual approach is consistent with Arizona law. See *State ex rel. Godinez v. Superior Court*, 183 Ariz. 440, 444, 904 P.2d 1267, 1271 (App. 1995) ("Statutes must be read in context, not in isolation, and the meaning of particular words depends on their context.").

### B. The Statute Must Be Read Gender-Neutrally

The prosecution contends that the plain language of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) limits the presumption to men. This argument ignores the *McLaughlin* court's reasoned analysis of why the statute must be applied gender-neutrally following the United States Supreme Court's decision in *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), which legalized same-sex marriage nationwide. The *McLaughlin* court held that "when a statute benefits from a presumption that applies to married heterosexual couples, that presumption must also apply to married same-sex couples." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 15, 382 P.3d at 122.

The prosecution argues that the Legislature's use of "man" and "father" was intentional and that courts lack authority to rewrite the statute. However, the *McLaughlin* court specifically addressed this argument, noting that "the Legislature's use of gendered terms in § 25-814(A)(1) reflects historical usage rather than a deliberate intent to exclude same-sex spouses." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 16, 382 P.3d at 122. The court further observed that "other provisions of Arizona's parentage statutes use gender-neutral language," demonstrating that the Legislature knows how to draft gender-specific provisions when it intends to do so. Id.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Jones* is misplaced. In *State v. Jones*, the Arizona Supreme Court addressed the completely different question of whether criminal statutes requiring male-only registration could be extended to females. 195 Ariz. 283, 987 P.2d 696. The Court in *Jones* specifically noted that the criminal registration statute at issue had "no gender-neutral counterpart" and that the Legislature had explicitly limited its application to males. Id. at 286, 987 P.2d at 699. In contrast, A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) is a parentage statute that must be read in conjunction with Arizona's overall scheme for establishing parentage, which includes gender-neutral provisions in A.R.S. § 25-801 et seq. The *Jones* decision therefore does not control here.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the family court's ruling should be affirmed in all respects. The prosecution's appeal attempts to relitigate issues already settled by binding precedent and seeks to overturn a well-reasoned decision supported by substantial evidence. As demonstrated below, the family court correctly applied *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), to hold that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) applies gender-neutrally to married same-sex couples. Alternatively, the family court's independent finding of equitable estoppel provides a sufficient basis for affirmance even if this Court had doubts about the statutory interpretation. The prosecution's arguments misstate the law, ignore binding precedent, and ask this Court to disregard the substantial evidence supporting the estoppel finding. This Court should affirm.

## I. THE FAMILY COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED BINDING PRECEDENT IN HOLDING THAT A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) APPLIES GENDER-NEUTRALLY

### A. McLaughlin v. Jones Is Binding Precedent That This Panel Must Follow

The prosecution's central argument—that *McLaughlin v. Jones* was wrongly decided and should be disregarded—fundamentally misapprehends the doctrine of stare decisis. This Court is bound by the decision of a prior panel of the same court. The prosecution cites *State v. McDaniel*, 186 Ariz. 310, 922 P.2d 399 (Ariz. 1996), and *State v. Jones*, 195 Ariz. 283, 987 P.2d 696 (Ariz. 1999), for the proposition that courts must apply clear statutory language as written. However, these cases do not stand for the proposition that a later panel may disregard binding precedent from a prior panel of the same intermediate appellate court.

As this Court explained in the updated ruling, the doctrine of stare decisis requires adherence to prior decisions unless overruled en banc or by a higher court. The prosecution's invitation to disregard *McLaughlin* in favor of older cases is improper. The *McLaughlin* panel specifically addressed the gender-specific language in A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) and held that "the statute must be read and applied gender-neutrally." 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. This holding is binding on this panel.

### B. The Prosecution's Plain Language Arguments Misapply Basic Statutory Construction Principles

The prosecution argues that the plain language of A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1)—which refers to "a man" and "father"—unambiguously limits the presumption to males. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the *McLaughlin* panel already considered and rejected this exact argument. The panel recognized that while the statute uses gendered terms, Arizona's other parentage statutes use gender-neutral language, demonstrating that the Legislature knows how to draft gender-neutrally when it intends to do so. The presence of gendered terms in § 25-814(A)(1) does not preclude a gender-neutral interpretation when applied to same-sex marriages that were not contemplated when the statute was originally enacted.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on *In re Adoption of Child by J.*, 177 Ariz. 135, 865 P.2d 801 (App. 1993), is misplaced. That case addressed whether a single man could adopt a child and applied the statute as written in that specific context. It did not address the question of gender-neutral application to same-sex couples—a issue that *McLaughlin* explicitly resolved.

Third, the prosecution's argument that context cannot override plain meaning misunderstands statutory construction. Courts must interpret statutes in context and in light of the entire statutory scheme. *State ex rel. Godinez v. Superior Court*, 183 Ariz. 440, 444, 904 P.2d 1267, 1271 (App. 1995). The context here includes the evolution of marriage law in Arizona following *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), which legalized same-sex marriage. To hold that § 25-814(A)(1) applies only to opposite-sex couples would create an absurd result: married same-sex couples would be denied the presumption of parentage afforded to married opposite-sex couples, despite being similarly situated.

### C. Legislative Silence Following McLaughlin Demonstrates Acquiescence

The defense correctly argued below—and the family court accepted—that the Legislature's failure to amend A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) since *McLaughlin* was decided in 2016 demonstrates legislative acquiescence to that interpretation.

The doctrine of legislative acquiescence is well-established. As the United States Supreme Court explained in *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962), and reaffirmned in *Armstrong v. Exceptional Child Center*, 575 U.S. 320, 332 (2015), legislative silence following a judicial interpretation often denotes consent. The Legislature has been aware of *McLaughlin* for over eight years and has not amended the statute to clarify that it applies only to men. This silence is meaningful.

The prosecution argues that legislative silence does not validate a court's "decision to rewrite the law." However, *McLaughlin* did not rewrite the law—it interpreted the law in light of constitutional developments regarding same-sex marriage. The Legislature's failure to respond indicates acceptance of that interpretation. This Court should not now disregard *McLaughlin* and effectively punish the defense for relying on binding precedent.

## II. THE FINDING OF EQUITABLE ESTOPTOP IS SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE AND PROVIDES AN INDEPENDENT BASIS FOR AFFIRMANCE

### A. The Elements of Equitable Estoppel Are Satisfied

Even if this Court had doubts about the statutory interpretation—and it should not—the family court's finding of equitable estoppel provides an independent and sufficient basis for affirmance. The prosecution's arguments against estoppel misstate the law and ignore the record evidence.

Equitable estoppel requires: (1) a representation or concealment of material facts; (2) the other party's reliance on such representation; (3) a change of position; and (4) detriment to the party relying on the representation. *McLaughlin v. Jones*, 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 123. All elements are satisfied here.

**Representations by Turner:** The record contains abundant evidence that Turner represented Oakley as C.T.'s co-parent. Turner listed Oakley on the birth certificate in the section designated "father." Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as the child's sole custodian if Turner died. Turner posted on social media referring to Oakley as C.T.'s mother. These are not "mere administrative documents" as the prosecution suggests—they are intentional, deliberate acts reflecting Turner's express representation that Oakley was a legal parent.

**Oakley's Reliance:** Oakley reasonably relied on Turner's representations. Oakley participated in the artificial insemination process, reviewing donor profiles, accompanying Turner to appointments, and being present during the procedures. Oakley was present at the child's birth and cut the umbilical cord. Oakley formed a parental bond with the child, believing she was a legal parent.

**Change of Position and Detriment:** Oakley changed her position by assuming a parental role and developing a parent-child relationship. The detriment to Oakley of being denied parentage rights is significant—she would lose custody, visitation, and the relationship she has cultivated since the child's birth. The child would also suffer detriment from being separated from a parent she has known since birth.

### B. The Prosecution's Citation to In re Paternity of J.M. Is Misplaced

The prosecution relies on *In re Paternity of J.M.*, 182 Ariz. 108, 893 P.2d 720 (App. 1995), for the proposition that "administrative documents such as birth certificates are not determinative of legal parentage." This reliance is misplaced.

*In re Paternity of J.M.* addressed whether a birth certificate alone could establish paternity for purposes of a paternity action. The Court held that it could not, because the statutory scheme requires more. However, that case did not address equitable estoppel—a doctrine that can estop a biological parent from denying parentage even where the statutory requirements have not been met.

As the *McLaughlin* Court recognized, "where a non-biological parent has relied on the other parent's representations to assume a parental role, the biological parent may be estopped from denying that parentage." 240 Ariz. at 566 ¶ 22, 382 P.3d at 123. This is precisely the situation here. Turner represented that Oakley was a legal parent, Oakley relied on that representation, and now Turner cannot unilaterally strip Oakley of her parental rights.

### C. Oakley's Failure to Pursue Adoption Does Not Bar Estoppel

The prosecution argues that Oakley's failure to formally adopt the child demonstrates that her reliance was "unreasonable." This argument fails.

First, the evidence shows that Turner and Oakley discussed whether Oakley needed to adopt and were "unsure if adoption was necessary or if 'just being on the birth certificate was enough.'" Turner, as the biological mother, assured Oakley that being on the birth certificate was sufficient. Oakley reasonably relied on this representation from her partner.

Second, Turner herself drafted a will naming Oakley as the child's sole custodian—clearly indicating that Turner believed Oakley had legal parental rights without adoption. One cannot simultaneously name someone as a child's sole custodian in a will and later claim that person is not a legal parent.

Third, the prosecution's argument puts form over substance. The purpose of equitable estoppel is to prevent a party from benefiting from representations that induced another party's reliance. Turner represented that Oakley was a parent; Oakley relied on that representation; now Turner cannot deny Oakley's parentage to gain a tactical advantage in the dissolution proceedings.

### D. Policy Considerations Support the Estoppel Finding

The prosecution dismisses the defense's policy arguments about "family stability" and "the child's best interests" as "irrelevant." This Court should not be so dismissive.

The child, C.T., has known Oakley as a parent since birth. Oakley was present at the birth, cut the umbilical cord, and has been a consistent parental figure. The child has formed a bond with Oakley. To suddenly sever that relationship based on a technical statutory argument would cause significant harm to the child.

Arizona law recognizes that the best interests of the child are paramount in custody proceedings. A.R.S. § 25-403. The family court's ruling preserves an existing parent-child relationship and serves the child's stability. The prosecution's position would punish the child for the biological mother's change of heart.

## III. REBUTTAL TO SPECIFIC PROSECUTION ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prosecution Cannot Relitigate McLaughlin

The prosecution's core argument is that *McLaughlin* was wrongly decided. However, this panel is bound by *McLaughlin* regardless of whether this panel agrees with it. The prosecution's citations to *State v. McDaniel* and *State v. Jones* do not authorize this panel to disregard binding precedent. Those cases stand for unremarkable propositions about statutory construction that do not override the doctrine of stare decisis.

### B. The Prosecution's Legislative Silence Argument Backfires

The prosecution argues that legislative silence should not be interpreted as acquiescence because "the Legislature has no obligation to correct every erroneous judicial interpretation." However, the prosecution's own authorities recognize that legislative silence can denote consent when the Legislature is aware of a judicial interpretation. *Armstrong v. Exceptional Child Center*, 575 U.S. at 332. The Legislature has been aware of *McLaughlin* for eight years. Its silence supports the defense, not the prosecution.

### C. The Prosecution's Argument That Oakley Should Have Pursued Adoption Ignores the Facts

The prosecution argues that Oakley "had ample opportunity to formalize her status through adoption." This argument ignores the facts. Turner told Oakley that being on the birth certificate was enough. Turner drafted a will naming Oakley as custodian. The parties discussed adoption but were unsure if it was necessary. Oakley reasonably relied on Turner's representations. It is precisely because Turner induced Oakley not to pursue adoption that Turner is now estopped from denying Oakley's parentage.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the family court's ruling in all respects.

The family court correctly applied binding precedent in holding that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read gender-neutrally under *McLaughlin v. Jones*. The prosecution's attempt to relitigate this issue violates the doctrine of stare decisis.

Alternatively, the family court's finding of equitable estoppel is supported by substantial evidence and provides an independent basis for affirmance. Turner represented that Oakley was a legal parent; Oakley relied on those representations; Oakley assumed a parental role; and now Turner cannot deny Oakley's parentage to gain a tactical advantage in dissolution proceedings.

The prosecution's arguments misstate the law, ignore binding precedent, and ask this Court to reward Turner's manipulation of the legal system. The family court's ruling should be affirmed.
