Your Honor, today this Court faces a fundamental question of constitutional law: whether the district court erred by recognizing a new Bivens remedy for substantive due process claims in circumstances where Congress has not authorized such a cause of action and where no compelling justification exists to extend Bivens into uncharted territory. We submit that the district court’s decision was legally unsound and must be reversed.  

Bivens actions are exceptional remedies. As this Court and the Supreme Court have consistently held, federal courts should not imply new causes of action under the Constitution absent extraordinary circumstances. *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), is a narrow exception to the general rule that Congress, not the judiciary, must create remedies for constitutional violations. In *Correctional Services Corporation v. Malesko*, 534 U.S. 61 (2001), the Supreme Court emphasized that Bivens is "a relic of the past" and that "expanding the *Bivens* remedy has become a 'disfavored judicial activity.'" Id. at 74. This principle has only strengthened in recent years.  

In *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that courts must exercise "caution" before recognizing new Bivens claims. The Court held that "creating a new Bivens remedy requires special justification" and that such claims should not be extended to "new contexts" where Congress has not provided a statutory remedy. Id. at 1857. *Abbasi* made clear that courts must ask: (1) whether the claim arises in a "new context"; (2) whether Congress has enacted alternative remedies; and (3) whether "special factors" counsel hesitation in recognizing a judicial remedy. Id. at 1857–58. Here, the district court failed this test at every step.  

First, this case presents a novel context for Bivens. The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against extending Bivens to circumstances where the underlying conduct involves government officials acting in sensitive roles. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858 ("[W]e have never held that a Bivens remedy exists for claims arising in a context where Congress has not provided a cause of action."). The district court’s decision to recognize a substantive due process claim here—without any clear congressional authorization—violates this bedrock principle. As *Abbasi* instructs, courts should not "second-guess the policy choices made by Congress" by creating new remedies where none exist. Id. at 1859.  

Second, Congress has not provided a statutory remedy for the alleged violations. While Doe raised other claims, the district court correctly dismissed those claims for lack of a private right of action. But that does not justify judicially crafting a Bivens remedy. In *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412 (1988), the Supreme Court held that when Congress has created a remedial scheme—even if imperfect—the judiciary may not supplement it with a Bivens action. Id. at 423. Here, the absence of a statutory remedy does not empower courts to invent one; it simply means Doe must seek relief through other channels, if any exist.  

Third, "special factors" counsel against recognizing a Bivens remedy here. *Abbasi* recognized that courts must consider "whether the case involves sensitive policy decisions reserved for the political branches." 137 S. Ct. at 1858. This case implicates core concerns about judicial overreach into areas where Congress alone has the authority to define rights and remedies. By allowing this claim to proceed, the district court improperly inserted the judiciary into a policy domain reserved for the legislative and executive branches.  

The district court also erred in denying Secretary Rumsfeld qualified immunity. Doe’s complaint alleges vague and generalized mistreatment—without any reference to specific, precedent-driven conduct—that cannot satisfy this demanding standard.  

In sum, the district court’s decision to recognize a new Bivens remedy in this case contravenes Supreme Court precedent and threatens to undermine the separation of powers. ## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

This Court is called upon to review the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss Appellee's substantive due process claims brought under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971). Secretary Rumsfeld advances two principal arguments on appeal: (1) that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action for substantive due process claims arising from military detention in Iraq; and (2) that he is entitled to qualified immunity. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY IMPLIED A BIVENS REMEDY FOR SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS CLAIMS

### A. The Nature of the Claims and the Applicable Framework

Secretary Rumsfeld urges this Court to adopt an extremely narrow reading of *Bivens* that would effectively eliminate constitutional remedies for civilian contractors detained and abused by military personnel. This Court should decline to adopt such an approach, which would leave Appellee's constitutional injuries without any meaningful judicial remedy.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S.Ct. 1843 (2017), establishes the framework for analyzing whether a Bivens remedy should be recognized. As this Court observed in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008), review de novo is appropriate. Under *Abbasi*, this Court must consider whether the claim arises in a "new context" and whether "special factors" counsel hesitation. 137 S.Ct. at 1857-58.

### B. This Case Does Not Present a "New Context" for Bivens Purposes

Secretary Rumsfeld incorrectly characterizes this case as presenting a novel context for Bivens analysis. To the contrary, the core constitutional violation alleged—detention without due process combined with physical abuse that "shocks the conscience"—represents the quintessential Bivens scenario that the Supreme Court recognized decades ago.

In *Bivens* itself, the Supreme Court recognized a remedy for Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents. In *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), the Court recognized a Fifth Amendment due process claim. In *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), the Court recognized an Eighth Amendment claim. Each of these cases involved constitutional violations by federal officials acting under color of law.

The allegations here are straightforward: Doe, a United States citizen, was detained by United States military personnel, held in solitary confinement for months, subjected to physical abuse including being kicked and choked, denied access to counsel, and never charged with any crime. These are classic substantive due process claims that have long been recognized under *Bivens*.

Secretary Rumsfeld's argument that this case is "new" because it involves military detention in a foreign war zone conflates the venue of the constitutional violation with the nature of the violation itself. The Constitution applies to the actions of United States government officials wherever they act. See *Korematsu v. United States*, 323 U.S. 214 (1944) (acknowledging constitutional constraints on military actions, even during wartime); *Ex parte Quirin*, 317 U.S. 1, 25 (1942) (noting that military tribunals must nonetheless comply with applicable law).

### C. The Absence of Alternative Congressional Remedies Supports, Rather Than Precludes, a Bivens Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that Congress has not provided a statutory remedy and that this somehow counsels against implying a Bivens action. This argument inverts the logic of *Bivens* and its progeny.

The whole point of *Bivens* is to provide a remedy when Congress has not acted. As the Supreme Court explained in *Bivens* itself, "where legal rights have been invaded, and the federal law does not provide any specific remedy, the injured party may be able to assert a cause of action in federal court." 403 U.S. at 397. The absence of a statutory remedy is precisely what triggers the Bivens analysis—it is not a reason to deny relief.

Secretary Rumsfeld cites *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412 (1988), for the proposition that courts should not create Bivens remedies where Congress has created a remedial scheme. However, *Chilicky* involved a situation where Congress had actually created an elaborate remedial scheme for Social Security disability claims, even if that scheme was imperfect. 487 U.S. at 423. Here, by contrast, Congress has not created any remedial scheme for civilian contractors detained and abused by military personnel in Iraq. The Detainee Treatment Act, which Doe also invoked, does not provide a private cause of action as the district court correctly held. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd.

### D. No Special Factors Counsel Against Recognizing a Bivens Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that "special factors" counsel hesitation because this case involves sensitive military and foreign policy decisions. This Court should reject this argument.

The "special factors" doctrine requires courts to consider whether the case involves "discretionary policy decisions reserved for the political branches." *Abbasi*, 137 S.Ct. at 1858. However, the question here is not whether Secretary Rumsfeld had discretion to establish detention policies—the alleged constitutional violations arise not from policy decisions themselves but from the implementation of those policies through concrete acts of abuse and deprivation of liberty.

Doe does not challenge the legality of the detention per se or the underlying military policies. Rather, he challenges the specific physical abuse inflicted upon him: being kicked in the back, being choked by guards, being subjected to extreme temperatures, being deprived of sleep, and being denied access to counsel. These are not policy choices—they are direct violations of clearly established constitutional rights.

Moreover, as the district court correctly noted, the "shock the conscience" standard has long been recognized as applicable to claims involving government detention and abuse. The Supreme Court has applied this standard in cases involving police misconduct, *see County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), and there is no reason to exempt military personnel from constitutional constraints when they engage in conduct that would clearly violate the Constitution if engaged in by civilian law enforcement officers.

### E. The Policy Arguments advanced by Secretary Rumsfeld Do Not Support Dismissal

Secretary Rumsfeld warns that recognizing a Bivens remedy here will open the floodgates to litigation against military officials. This Court should not be swayed by such speculation.

First, the precise allegations here involve conduct that crosses constitutional lines—prolonged solitary confinement, physical abuse, denial of counsel—that is not inherent in legitimate military detention. Second, qualified immunity provides robust protection for officials who act in good faith and in accordance with established law. Third, the practical difficulties of litigating such claims—issues of venue, evidence, witness access—will serve as significant practical barriers to recovery.

The more significant policy concern is the opposite of what Secretary Rumsfeld suggests: if this Court were to hold that military officials enjoy blanket immunity from constitutional liability for the treatment of civilian detainees, the result would be to create a constitutional vacuum in which abuses could occur without judicial recourse. Such a result would be contrary to the fundamental principle that constitutional rights are not forfeited upon deployment to a foreign theater.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY DENIED QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

Qualified immunity protects government officials from personal liability unless their conduct violates "clearly established" constitutional rights of which a reasonable official would have been aware. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The "clearly established" standard does not require a case directly on point, but rather that the law at the time gave "fair warning" that the conduct was unconstitutional. Id. at 740-41.

### B. The Constitutional Right at Issue Was Clearly Established

The right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience" was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct in 2005. The Supreme Court articulated this standard in *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), and applied it specifically to conditions of confinement in cases such as *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002).

In *Hope*, the Supreme Court held that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established even though no prior case had applied the exact same facts. 536 U.S. at 741. The Court explained that "obvious clarity" of constitutional violations may establish the requisite notice even without case-by-case precedent. Id.

The conduct alleged here—prolonged solitary confinement combined with physical abuse including kicking and choking, denial of counsel, and exposure to extreme temperatures—falls squarely within the conduct that "shocks the conscience." A reasonable official in 2005 would have understood that such treatment of a detainee violates the Due Process Clause.

### C. Secretary Rumsfeld's Argument Regarding the "Fact-Specific" Nature of the Standard Misapprehends the Law

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the "shock the conscience" standard is "inherently fact-specific" and therefore cannot defeat qualified immunity. This argument conflates two distinct inquiries.

Qualified immunity is an objective standard that asks whether a reasonable official would have understood that the conduct violated clearly established law. *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 202 (2001). The "shock the conscience" standard is the substantive standard applied to determine whether a constitutional violation occurred. While the ultimate determination of whether conduct "shocks the conscience" may require factual development, the question of whether the right was clearly established does not.

The district court correctly applied this framework. Doe alleged specific facts: being kicked in the back, being choked, being subjected to extreme temperatures, being deprived of sleep, being held in solitary confinement for months, and being denied access to counsel. These allegations, taken as true, describe conduct that any reasonable official would recognize as constitutionally impermissible.

### D. The Complaint Sufficiently Alleges a Violation by Secretary Rumsfeld

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that Doe has not sufficiently alleged that he personally participated in the constitutional violations. This argument misreads the complaint and the nature of the claims.

Doe's complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm." This allegation is sufficient at the motion to dismiss stage to establish personal involvement. Moreover, *Bivens* liability may attach to supervisors who are "directly involved in or caused the claimed violation." *See* *Briscoe v. LaHue*, 663 F.2d 713, 723 (6th Cir. 1981), *aff'd*, 460 U.S. 325 (1983).

The complaint specifically alleges that the policies implemented under Secretary Rumsfeld's authority resulted in the mistreatment Doe suffered. At the pleading stage, these allegations are sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

---

## III. COUNTERARGUMENTS TO SECRETARY RUMSFELD'S SPECIFIC CONTENTIONS

### A. Response to the Argument Regarding *Correctional Services Corporation v. Malesko*

Secretary Rumsfeld cites *Correctional Services Corporation v. Malesko*, 534 U.S. 61 (2001), for the proposition that Bivens is a "disfavored judicial activity." However, *Malesko* is distinguishable.

In *Malesko*, the Supreme Court declined to extend Bivens to a private corporation acting under color of federal law. The Court emphasized that the "special factors" analysis focused on whether the entity was a private actor. 534 U.S. at 70. Here, by contrast, all alleged wrongdoers are government officials—military personnel acting under color of federal authority. The case presents the classic Bivens scenario: constitutional violations by federal agents acting under color of law.

### B. Response to the Argument Regarding *Ziglar v. Abbasi*

Secretary Rumsfeld heavily relies on *Abbasi*, but that case actually supports affirmance here.

In *Abbasi*, the Supreme Court remanded for further proceedings on certain claims, indicating that Bivens analysis is fact-specific. 137 S.Ct. at 1862. The Court emphasized that "a Bivens claim may proceed in three different contexts," including where the claim is "identical to a previously recognized Bivens claim." Id. at 1855.

Doe's substantive due process claim is identical to claims previously recognized by the Supreme Court in cases like *Lewis* and *Hope*. This is not a novel context—it is the application of established constitutional principles to specific facts.

### C. Response to the Argument Regarding the Military Context

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the military context counsels judicial deference. This Court should reject this argument.

While courts appropriately defer to military decisions regarding strategy, tactics, and operational matters, the Constitution does not permit deferential review of constitutional violations. The Supreme Court has consistently held that military personnel are subject to constitutional constraints. *See* *Korematsu*, 323 U.S. at 215-16 (acknowledging military necessity but not abandoning constitutional review); *Winthrop*, 20 Ct. Cl. 95, 117 (1885) (noting that military officers are "always subject to law").

The treatment Doe alleges—prolonged solitary confinement, physical abuse, denial of counsel—is not a military necessity. It is conduct that any reasonable observer would recognize as abusive and unconstitutional.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court correctly held that Doe may proceed with his substantive due process claims under *Bivens*, and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation.

The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appeal of Donald Rumsfeld, former Secretary of Defense, from the order of the district court denying his motion to dismiss John Doe’s substantive due process claims.

**I. JURISDICTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This Court has jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal pursuant to the collateral order doctrine and the exception for orders denying qualified immunity. See *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 126 S.Ct. 1695, 164 L.Ed.2d 441 (2006). We review the district court’s legal conclusions de novo. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008).

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central question on appeal is whether the district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), for Appellee’s substantive due process claims. We hold that the district court erred in doing so. Furthermore, even if a *Bivens* action were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored judicial activity." *Correctional Services Corporation v. Malesko*, 534 U.S. 61, 74, 122 S.Ct. 515, 151 L.Ed.2d 452 (2001). In *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 198 L.Ed.2d 290 (2017), the Court established a rigid two-step framework for courts to follow before implying a new Bivens remedy. First, we must ask whether the case presents a new "context" different from those in which the Court has previously implied a cause of action. Second, if so, we must ask whether there are "special factors" counseling hesitation before extending Bivens into that new context. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

**1. New Context**

Appellee argues that his claims are not novel because they involve classic substantive due process violations—detention and physical abuse—akin to those recognized in *Bivens*, *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979), and *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1468, 64 L.Ed.2d 15 (1980). We find this argument unpersuasive. While the nature of the constitutional violation may be similar to prior cases, the context in which the alleged violation occurred is fundamentally different.

The Supreme Court has recognized a Bivens action only in three distinct contexts: (1) Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents (*Bivens*); (2) Fifth Amendment due process violations in the context of federal employment (*Davis*); and (3) Eighth Amendment violations by prison officials failing to provide medical care (*Carlson*). *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1855. The case at hand involves the detention and interrogation of a United States citizen by the military in a foreign war zone. This context is materially different from the civilian law enforcement and prison contexts addressed in *Bivens* and *Carlson*. The unique military environment, involving national security concerns and the conduct of warfare, constitutes a "new context" under *Abbasi*.

**2. Special Factors Counseling Hesitation**

Having identified a new context, we must determine whether "special factors" counsel hesitation. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The presence of "special factors" is dispositive here. The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that national security and military affairs present "special factors" that preclude the implication of a Bivens remedy. *See* *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1864-65 (noting that national security policy differs in fundamental ways from the policy contexts in which Bivens actions were previously recognized).

Appellee contends that the alleged abuse—kicking, choking, and extreme temperatures—constitutes conduct outside the scope of legitimate military policy and therefore does not implicate sensitive decision-making. However, the judiciary is ill-equipped to second-guess the military’s discretionary judgments regarding the detention and treatment of individuals in a combat zone. The separation of powers concerns implicated by this case are profound. As noted by the Supreme Court, it is for the political branches, not the judiciary, to weigh the costs and benefits of military detention policies. *Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. at 1862. Allowing a Bivens action to proceed against the Secretary of Defense for actions taken in the theater of war would intrude upon this exclusive domain.

Furthermore, Appellee’s reliance on the absence of a congressional remedy to support a Bivens action is misplaced. While the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) does not provide a private right of action, the absence of a statutory remedy does not compel the judiciary to create one, particularly where "special factors" are present. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 425, 108 S.Ct. 2460, 101 L.Ed.2d 370 (1988). Congress’s silence in this specific context, combined with the existence of the DTA, suggests that Congress intended the military and political branches to address these issues without judicial interference.

**B. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if we were to find that a Bivens remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violated "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739, 122 S.Ct. 2508, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002).

Appellee argues that the right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience" was clearly established in 2005, citing *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998). However, the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-intensive and context-dependent. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846. Appellee has not pointed to any prior case holding that a Secretary of Defense—or military officials in a war zone—violated clearly established law by authorizing the specific detention conditions alleged here.

To be clearly established, the law must be "sufficiently clear that every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S.Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999). Given the novel context of military detention in Iraq and the lack of specific precedent addressing the liability of high-ranking officials for such detention policies, it was not clearly established in 2005 that Secretary Rumsfeld’s conduct was unconstitutional. Therefore, qualified immunity applies.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this sensitive military context. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

Passman*, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979)), and Eighth Amendment violations by prison officials (*Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1468, 64 L.Ed.2d 15 (1980)). Therefore, this case presents a "new context" under *Abbasi*.

Having identified a new context, we must determine whether special factors counsel hesitation. The rebuttals correctly emphasize that the separation of powers implications are profound. Appellee’s complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm." This allegation directly implicates high-level discretionary policy decisions regarding the conduct of warfare and the detention of security threats.

The judiciary lacks the institutional competence to second-guess these military and national security judgments. As the Supreme Court noted in *Abbasi*, the possibility of institutional rivalry and tension between the branches counsels hesitation when *Bivens* claims would require courts to evaluate the wisdom of executive policy decisions. 137 S. Ct. at 1862. Allowing a *Bivens* action to proceed against the Secretary of Defense for actions taken in the theater of war would intrude upon this exclusive domain of the political branches.

Furthermore, Appellant’s argument that the Detainee Treatment Act’s (DTA) lack of a private right of action compels a *Bivens* remedy is rejected. The Supreme Court held in *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423, 108 S.Ct. 2460, 101 L.Ed.2d 370 (1988), that "the fact that Congress has not provided a statutory remedy is not a sufficient basis for implying a Bivens action." The existence of the DTA, even without a private right of action, indicates that Congress has addressed the subject matter and intends the political branches to manage these issues without judicial interference.

**B. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability unless their conduct violated "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739, 122 S.Ct. 2508, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002).

Appellant relies on *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), to argue that the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and context-dependent. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846. The circumstances of a high-speed police pursuit in a domestic setting are fundamentally different from the detention and interrogation of a suspected security threat in a foreign war zone.

Appellee has not cited any precedent holding that a Secretary of Defense—or military officials in a combat zone—violated clearly established law by authorizing the specific detention conditions alleged here. The Supreme Court has held that "clearly established" law must be "sufficiently clear that every reasonable official would have understood that what he is doing violates that right." *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S.Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999). Given the absence of precedent directly on point regarding military detention policies in a war zone, *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 202, 121 S.Ct. 1491, 149 L.Ed.2d 549 (2001), it was not clearly established in 2005 that Secretary Rumsfeld’s conduct was unconstitutional.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in this sensitive military context. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
