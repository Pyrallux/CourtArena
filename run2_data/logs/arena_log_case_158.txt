**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

May it please the Court:  

Today, we confront a fundamental legal error that deprived Mr. Van Ness of his rightful workers’ compensation benefits. The Director’s denial of his claim rests on a misapplication of settled law—specifically, the Director incorrectly applied the *Flor* test to a proximate cause question, ignoring the controlling standard that governs whether employment aggravated a pre-existing condition. This error demands reversal.  

As this Court knows, *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 320, 104 P.3d 547 (2004), establishes a narrow rule: the "resulting from the nature of employment" test applies *only* to determine whether a disease is inherently part of the job itself—such as when the occupation *creates* the disease. It does *not* govern questions of proximate cause for aggravated pre-existing conditions. That distinction is critical. For proximate cause—whether employment substantially contributed to the aggravation of a pre-existing condition—Hawaii law requires application of the unitary test. As recognized in Hawaii workers’ compensation jurisprudence, the unitary test asks whether the employment was a *substantial contributing factor* to the injury, regardless of whether the hazard existed outside the workplace. See *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005) (explaining that proximate cause under the unitary test focuses on whether employment "materially contributed" to the harm, even if the condition was present elsewhere).  

Here, the Director erred by focusing solely on whether vog was "present on the entire island of Maui"—a fact irrelevant to the unitary test. The Director’s reasoning conflates the *Flor* test’s limited scope with proximate cause. But under the unitary test, the mere presence of vog island-wide does not negate that Mr. Van Ness’s employment *substantially contributed* to the aggravation of his asthma. The record is unequivocal: Mr. Van Ness’s job required him to spend the vast majority of his workday in non-air-conditioned environments with open windows, exposing him directly to volcanic pollutants. He was tasked with maintaining computer equipment in these conditions, which necessitated frequent cleaning of dust-clogged filters and movement between campus buildings. Medical evidence confirms this exposure was a substantial factor in his deterioration. Dr. Mathison, who reviewed Mr. Van Ness’s complete medical history—including records from his hospitalization at Queen’s Medical Center—explicitly linked his symptoms to "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." Dr. Mathison further noted that "risk for serious and potentially fatal asthma exacerbation with exposure to volcanic products" is a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients," and that Mr. Van Ness’s condition "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu" after removal from the volcanic exposure environment.  

The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s report is misplaced. Dr. Arora admitted his analysis was incomplete—he stated the "issues are quite complex" and could not address the referral questions without access to medical records. Yet the Director credited this provisional opinion while ignoring Dr. Mathison’s comprehensive review, which included all relevant records and directly tied the workplace exposure to the exacerbation. Moreover, Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "unusually severe vog-polluted days" occurred at Lahainaluna during the relevant period, and Mr. Van Ness had discussed his respiratory struggles with her well before his December 2005 medical visit.  

This is not a case where the hazard was incidental to the job. It is a case where employment *required* Mr. Van Ness to work in environments where volcanic pollutants entered freely, directly triggering his asthma. Under the unitary test, that is sufficient. *Flor* does not apply here—this is not a question of whether vog "resulted from" the nature of employment as an inherent hazard, but whether employment *proximately caused* the aggravation. The Director’s legal error demands reversal, and we ask this Court to remand for an award of benefits consistent with the unitary test.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

May it please the Court:

The Defendant-Appellee, State of Hawai'i, Department of Education ("DOE"), respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the Director's decision denying Workers' Compensation benefits to Claimant-Appellant Van Ness. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the Director's decision, as it was supported by substantial evidence and reflected proper application of Hawaii workers' compensation law.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE

The Plaintiff asks this Court to overturn the Director's factual determination that Mr. Van Ness's respiratory condition was not work-related. This Court, however, should accord appropriate deference to the Director's factual findings and credibility determinations. The record supports the Director's conclusion that while Mr. Van Ness's asthma was indeed aggravated by vog exposure, that exposure was not peculiar to his employment at Lahainaluna High School but rather affected the entire island of Maui. Under established Hawaii workers' compensation principles, the mere presence of a worsening condition caused by general environmental factors occurring island-wide does not constitute a compensable work-related injury.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS PERTINENT TO DEFENSE

The Defense submits the following additional facts, drawn from the record, which are essential to the proper analysis of this matter:

Mr. Van Ness had a long-standing history of "mild persistent asthma" that predated his employment at Lahainaluna. **Van Ness v. State, Dept. of Education**, No. WC 2007-0192, at * (Haw. Dir. April 21, 2008). He had previously experienced respiratory difficulties in 1989 while teaching in Kona, where he was also exposed to vog and required systemic corticosteroid treatment. **Id.** His allergic condition had been confirmed through testing in 1991, and he received desensitizing immunotherapy for several years. **Id.** During his residence in Idaho from 1995 to 2001, he "had few symptoms of allergic-respiratory disease," demonstrating that his condition was sensitive to environmental factors regardless of employment. **Id.**

Critically, while Mr. Van Ness worked at Lahainaluna, he lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei with multiple HEPA filters, including a filter in his car. **Id.** at *. He testified that he spent "little time outside" his home and work, as he was "not much of an outdoors person." **Id.** This fact is significant because it demonstrates that Mr. Van Ness's exposure to vog was not limited to his workplace—he was exposed to volcanic pollutants throughout the island during his commutes, his time outdoors in Kihei, and at his residence.

Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Mr. Van Ness lived, than at the school. **Id.** at *. This admission directly undermines the Plaintiff's argument that the workplace exposure was uniquely hazardous or substantially greater than his exposure elsewhere on Maui.

Dr. Arora, the Independent Medical Examiner, specifically noted in his report that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." **Id.** at *. This medical opinion, which the Director credited, reflects the uncontroversial reality that volcanic pollution affected the entire island, not merely the school campus.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the Director's findings of fact under the substantial evidence standard. Under Hawaii Revised Statutes Section 91-14(g), the Director's decision shall be reversed only if the court finds that the decision is "clearly erroneous" in view of the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence on the whole record. The party challenging the Director's decision bears the burden of demonstrating that the decision was not supported by substantial evidence. **See** **Abong v. State**, 83 Haw. 143, 148, 925 P.2d 324, 329 (1996).

### B. The "Arising Out of Employment" Requirement

Under Hawaii workers' compensation law, a claimant must establish that the injury "arose out of and in the course of employment." HRS § 386-3. The "arising out of" requirement demands a causal connection between the employment and the injury. **See** **Flor v. Holguin**, 106 Haw. 320, 329, 104 P.3d 547, 556 (2004).

The Plaintiff correctly notes that **Flor** distinguishes between diseases inherent to the nature of employment versus pre-existing conditions aggravated by employment. However, the Plaintiff misapplies the "unitary test" by ignoring its fundamental requirement: there must still be a nexus between the employment and the injury. The unitary test does not create a presumption of compensability whenever a pre-existing condition worsens while an employee is at work.

In **Leong v. State**, 108 Haw. 1, 8, 116 P.3d 298, 305 (2005), this Court explained that under the unitary test, the question is whether employment "materially contributed" to the harm. This requires proof that the employment exposed the worker to a hazard distinct from or greater than that to which the general public is exposed. **See** **id.** at 9, 116 P.3d at 306 (holding that the unitary test "focuses on whether the employment materially contributed to the condition—generally, whether the employment brought about the resulting condition").

### C. Application to This Case

The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment." **Van Ness**, No. WC 2007-0192, at *. This finding is supported by substantial evidence in the record, including Mr. Van Ness's own testimony about the pervasive nature of vog on Maui, Vice Principal Dennis's confirmation that vog was "even worse" in Kihei where Mr. Van Ness lived, and Dr. Arora's observation that vog is not unique to schools.

The Director properly applied the law by requiring a showing that the workplace exposure was distinct from general island-wide exposure. Under **Leong**, this is a proper consideration—the unitary test does not award benefits for general environmental exposure that happens to occur while someone is employed. The test requires that employment "materially contribute" to the condition by exposing the worker to a heightened risk.

Here, the evidence demonstrates that Mr. Van Ness's exposure to vog was not materially greater at Lahainaluna than elsewhere on Maui. His home was equipped with air conditioning and HEPA filters, yet he still experienced symptoms. He voluntarily spent little time outdoors, yet his condition persisted. The fact that his symptoms improved when he left Maui entirely undermines any claim that his employment at Lahainaluna was a substantial contributing factor—it suggests that the island-wide environmental conditions, not the specific workplace, were the cause.

### D. The Director's Credibility Determinations Were Proper

The Director was entitled to credit Dr. Arora's report over Dr. Mathison's opinion. While the Plaintiff characterizes Dr. Arora's report as "incomplete," the Director was not required to accord it less weight simply because Dr. Arora lacked access to all medical records at the time of his examination. Dr. Arora's observation that vog is not unique to schools is a valid medical opinion that supports the Director's conclusion.

Dr. Mathison's report, while thorough, does not establish that the workplace exposure was distinct from general island exposure. Dr. Mathison noted that Mr. Van Ness's symptoms improved when he moved to Oahu, but this merely confirms that the volcanic pollutants on Maui generally affected his condition—it does not demonstrate that his employment at Lahainaluna specifically caused the aggravation beyond what he would have experienced living anywhere on Maui.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Plaintiff Misconstrues the Scope of the Unitary Test

The Plaintiff argues that the unitary test requires only that employment "substantially contribute" to the aggravation, without regard to whether the hazard exists elsewhere. This is incorrect. The unitary test, as explained in **Leong**, still requires that employment "materially contribute" to the condition. **Leong**, 108 Haw. at 9, 116 P.3d at 306. Material contribution implies that the employment created or exacerbated the risk beyond general background exposure.

The Director properly found that Mr. Van Ness failed to establish that his exposure at Lahainaluna was "so overwhelming" that his injury resulted from his place of employment rather than general island conditions. This finding is supported by the evidence that vog affected the entire island, including Kihei where Mr. Van Ness resided.

### B. The Workplace Conditions Do Not Establish a Unique Hazard

The Plaintiff emphasizes that Mr. Van Ness worked in non-air-conditioned environments with open windows. However, the record shows that many classrooms and offices were equipped with air conditioners—they were simply not in use. This does not establish that Mr. Van Ness was subjected to a unique hazard. The decision to not use air conditioning was a general condition of the campus, not a work-related exposure distinct from the ambient environment.

Similarly, while Mr. Van Ness cleaned computer filters clogged with dust, this exposure to dust is not unique to his employment and does not establish a compensable aggravation of his pre-existing asthma. Dust exposure occurs in numerous environments, and Mr. Van Ness has not demonstrated that his workplace dust exposure was substantially greater than what he would encounter in any non-air-conditioned building on Maui.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Compel a Different Result

Dr. Mathison's opinion that Mr. Van Ness's condition was exacerbated by "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" does not establish work-relatedness. Dr. Mathison acknowledged that volcanic pollutants affected the entire island, and his opinion does not distinguish between exposure at work and exposure elsewhere. The Director was within her authority to find this evidence insufficient to establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor.

Dr. Mathison's recommendation that Mr. Van Ness be assigned to areas without volcanic exposure reflects a prudent medical practice, but it does not establish that his employment at Lahainaluna caused his condition. The recommendation applies equally to any asthmatic employee on Maui, regardless of their specific job duties.

### D. The Timing of Symptoms Does Not Establish Causation

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s claim for workers’ compensation benefits. The Director found that Van Ness’s respiratory aggravation did not arise out of his employment because the volcanic smog (vog) affecting him was present island-wide and not unique to his workplace.

The standard of review for the Director’s findings of fact is the "substantial evidence" standard. Under Hawaii Revised Statutes § 91-14(g) and case law such as *Abong v. State*, 83 Haw. 143, 925 P.2d 324 (1996), this Court may not reverse the Director’s decision unless it is clearly erroneous in view of the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence on the whole record. However, questions of law, including the application of the correct legal standard to the facts, are reviewed *de novo*.

**II. LEGAL FRAMEWORK**

To recover workers’ compensation benefits, a claimant must prove that the injury "arose out of and in the course of employment." Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-3. The central legal dispute in this case is which test governs the "arising out of" inquiry when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by environmental factors present both inside and outside the workplace.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 320, 104 P.3d 547 (2004), the Hawaii Supreme Court clarified that the "resulting from the nature of employment" test applies only to determine if a disease is inherent to the occupation itself. This test is not applicable to claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

For aggravation claims, Hawaii law utilizes the "unitary test." As established in *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005), the unitary test focuses on whether the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. The critical inquiry is whether the employment "materially contributed" to the harm, not whether the hazard was unique to the workplace. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 9, 116 P.3d at 306.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Director denied Van Ness’s claim, reasoning that because vog was "present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment," there was insufficient evidence to establish a nexus between the employment and the injury. The Director further stated that Van Ness failed to prove his exposure at Lahainaluna was "so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only."

The Defense argues that this finding is supported by substantial evidence, citing the fact that vog was worse in Kihei (where Van Ness lived) and that Dr. Arora noted vog is not unique to schools. While the factual finding that vog was island-wide may be supported by the record, the Director’s legal conclusion that this precludes compensability constitutes reversible error.

The Director effectively applied the "resulting from the nature of employment" standard from *Flor*—which looks for risks unique to the job—rather than the "substantial contributing factor" standard from *Leong*. Under *Leong*, the presence of a hazard outside the workplace does not automatically bar recovery. The relevant question is whether the conditions of employment *materially contributed* to the aggravation of the condition.

The record contains substantial evidence that the employment conditions were a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s respiratory decline. Van Ness testified that he spent 95% of his workday in non-air conditioned buildings with open windows, directly exposed to volcanic pollutants and dust, unlike his home environment which was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters. The physical demands of his job, including walking uphill and cleaning dust-clogged computer filters, exacerbated his symptoms in a way that his protected home environment did not. Dr. Mathison’s report corroborates this, linking the exacerbation to "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noting improvement upon removal from the Maui environment.

The Director’s requirement that Van Ness prove exposure occurred "only" at work is an incorrect application of the law. The unitary test does not require exclusivity; it requires material contribution. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 8, 116 P.3d at 305. Because the Director applied the wrong legal standard—requiring the exposure to be unique to the workplace rather than a substantial contributing factor—the decision must be vacated.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Director committed legal error by applying the "resulting from the nature of employment" test to an aggravation claim. The Director failed to apply the correct "unitary test" set forth in *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005), which asks whether employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury. Based on the record, there is a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether the specific environmental conditions and physical demands of Van Ness’s employment at Lahainaluna High School materially contributed to the aggravation of his asthma.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying the claim is **VACATED**, and the matter is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s claim for workers’ compensation benefits. The Director denied the claim, finding that Van Ness’s respiratory aggravation did not arise out of his employment because the volcanic smog (vog) affecting him was present island-wide and not unique to his workplace.

The standard of review for the Director’s findings of fact is the "substantial evidence" standard. Under *Abong v. State*, 83 Haw. 143, 925 P.2d 324 (1996), this Court may not reverse the Director’s decision unless it is clearly erroneous in view of the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence on the whole record. However, questions of law, including the application of the correct legal standard to the facts, are reviewed *de novo*.

**II. LEGAL FRAMEWORK**

To recover workers’ compensation benefits, a claimant must prove that the injury "arose out of and in the course of employment." Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-3. The central legal dispute is whether the Director applied the correct legal standard to determine if Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 320, 104 P.3d 547 (2004), the Hawaii Supreme Court clarified that the "resulting from the nature of employment" test applies only to determine if a disease is inherent to the occupation itself. This test is not applicable to claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

For aggravation claims, Hawaii law utilizes the "unitary test." As established in *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005), the unitary test focuses on whether the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. The critical inquiry is whether the employment "materially contributed" to the harm, not whether the hazard was unique to the workplace. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 9, 116 P.3d at 306.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Defense argues that the Director’s decision should be reinstated because, even under the *Leong* unitary test, the evidence does not support a finding that employment materially contributed to Van Ness’s injury. The Defense contends that vog exposure was worse in Kihei (where Van Ness lived) than at Lahainaluna High School, and that Van Ness’s improvement only occurred after leaving Maui entirely, not merely after changing jobs.

While the Defense raises factual disputes regarding the relative severity of vog in Kihei versus Lahainaluna, these arguments do not cure the legal error inherent in the Director’s decision. The Director explicitly denied the claim based on a finding that there was "insufficient evidence" to show that Van Ness’s exposure to vog occurred "at this place of employment only." The Director further stated that the claim failed because vog was "present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment."

This reasoning demonstrates that the Director applied the "resulting from the nature of employment" standard from *Flor*—which requires a risk unique to the workplace—rather than the "substantial contributing factor" standard from *Leong*. Under *Leong*, the presence of a hazard outside the workplace does not automatically bar recovery. The relevant question is whether the conditions of employment *materially contributed* to the aggravation of the condition. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 8, 116 P.3d at 305.

The Defense’s reliance on the fact that vog was "even worse" in Kihei is a factual argument that goes to the weight of the evidence under the unitary test, but it does not validate the Director’s application of the wrong legal standard. The Director did not weigh the evidence of "material contribution"; instead, the Director required proof of exclusivity ("only at this place of employment"). This is a legal error that cannot be upheld on the basis of substantial evidence.

Furthermore, the record contains evidence that, if viewed under the correct legal standard, could support a finding of material contribution. Van Ness testified that he spent 95% of his workday in non-air conditioned buildings with open windows, directly exposed to volcanic pollutants and dust, while his home was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters. The physical demands of his job, including walking uphill and cleaning dust-clogged computer filters, exacerbated his symptoms in a way that his protected home environment did not. Dr. Mathison’s report corroborates this, linking the exacerbation to "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noting improvement upon removal from the Maui environment.

The Defense argues that the home environment was not "protected" because ash accumulated on Van Ness’s car in Kihei. However, the Director made no specific findings regarding the efficacy of the HEPA filters or the relative air quality inside the home versus the workplace. The Director’s decision was based solely on the island-wide presence of vog. Because the Director failed to apply the correct legal standard to evaluate the "material contribution" of the workplace environment—specifically the lack of filtration and physical exertion compared to the home—the decision must be vacated.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Director committed legal error by applying the "resulting from the nature of employment" test to an aggravation claim. The Director failed to apply the correct "unitary test" set forth in *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005), which asks whether employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury. The Defense’s arguments regarding the severity of vog in Kihei are factual issues that must be weighed by the Director under the correct legal standard on remand.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying the claim is **VACATED**, and the matter is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s claim for workers’ compensation benefits. The Director denied the claim, concluding that Van Ness’s injury did not arise out of his employment because the volcanic smog (vog) affecting him was present island-wide and not unique to his workplace.

The standard of review for the Director’s findings of fact is the "substantial evidence" standard. Under *Abong v. State*, 83 Haw. 143, 925 P.2d 324 (1996), this Court may not reverse the Director’s decision unless it is clearly erroneous in view of the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence on the whole record. However, questions of law, including the application of the correct legal standard to the facts, are reviewed *de novo*.

**II. LEGAL FRAMEWORK**

To recover workers’ compensation benefits, a claimant must prove that the injury "arose out of and in the course of employment." Hawaii Revised Statutes § 386-3. The central legal issue is whether the Director applied the correct legal standard to determine if Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 320, 104 P.3d 547 (2004), the Hawaii Supreme Court clarified that the "resulting from the nature of employment" test applies only to determine if a disease is inherent to the occupation itself. This test is not applicable to claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

For aggravation claims, Hawaii law utilizes the "unitary test." As established in *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005), the unitary test focuses on whether the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. The critical inquiry is whether the employment "materially contributed" to the harm, not whether the hazard was unique to the workplace. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 9, 116 P.3d at 306.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The Defense requests that this Court reverse the preliminary ruling, reinstate the Director’s decision, and deny benefits. The Defense argues that even under the correct legal standard—the *Leong* unitary test—the evidence does not support a finding that Van Ness’s employment materially contributed to his injury. Specifically, the Defense contends that (1) vog was an island-wide phenomenon that was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived; (2) Van Ness’s symptoms improved only after leaving Maui entirely, not merely after changing employment; and (3) the medical evidence does not establish causation.

While the Defense raises factual arguments that may be relevant to the *Leong* analysis, these arguments cannot save the Director’s decision from reversal. The Director’s decision was explicitly predicated on the application of the wrong legal standard. The Director found that Van Ness failed to prove "that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only." The Director further concluded that because vog was "present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment," there was "insufficient evidence" of a nexus between employment and injury.

This reasoning is a direct application of the *Flor* "resulting from the nature of employment" test, which requires a risk unique to the workplace. As held in *Leong*, this standard is inapplicable to aggravation claims. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 8, 116 P.3d at 305. The Director’s legal error is not harmless; it fundamentally distorted the factual analysis. By requiring proof of exclusivity ("only at this place of employment"), the Director failed to consider whether the specific conditions of Van Ness’s employment—such as the lack of air conditioning, the accumulation of dust in computer filters, and the physical exertion required to traverse the campus—materially contributed to his condition relative to his non-work environment.

The Defense argues that the evidence shows Van Ness’s home in Kihei was not "protected" because vog was "even worse" there. However, the Director made no findings regarding the comparative air quality inside Van Ness’s home (which was equipped with HEPA filters and air conditioning) versus the air quality inside the non-air conditioned classrooms at Lahainaluna. The Director’s finding that vog was "even worse" in Kihei was used solely to negate exclusivity, not to weigh material contribution under *Leong*.

Similarly, the Defense argues that Van Ness’s improvement only after leaving Maui proves that general environmental exposure, not employment, was the cause. Under *Leong*, however, the fact that a hazard exists outside the workplace does not automatically bar recovery. The relevant question is whether the employment conditions materially contributed to the injury. *Leong*, 108 Haw. at 8, 116 P.3d at 305. The record contains evidence from Van Ness and Dr. Mathison suggesting that the specific exposure at work—unfiltered air, dust, and physical exertion—was a material factor in his exacerbation. This is a factual dispute that the Director must weigh under the correct legal standard.

The Defense asks this Court to re-weigh the evidence and conclude that no reasonable fact-finder could find for Van Ness. However, where the Director has applied the wrong legal standard, the appropriate remedy is to vacate the decision and remand for further proceedings. This Court cannot affirm the Director’s decision by substituting its own factual findings for those of the Director, nor can it uphold a decision based on a legal theory the Director never considered. See *Abong*, 83 Haw. at 147-48, 925 P.2d at 327-28 (holding that this court may not make findings of fact).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Director committed legal error by applying the "resulting from the nature of employment" test to an aggravation claim. The Director failed to apply the correct "unitary test" set forth in *Leong v. State*, 108 Haw. 1, 116 P.3d 298 (2005). The Defense’s arguments regarding the severity of vog in Kihei and the timing of Van Ness’s improvement are factual issues that must be evaluated by the Director under the correct legal standard on remand.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying the claim is **VACATED**, and the matter is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. The Defense’s request to reinstate the Director’s decision is denied.
