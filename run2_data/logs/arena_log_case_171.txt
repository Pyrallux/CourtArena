# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the proceedings below and ignores controlling precedent regarding the disclosure of confidential informants. The defense respectfully submits that the court of appeals erred in affirming the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. Even under the dispositive motion framework, the appellant retained his constitutional right to challenge the reliability of the informant who provided the probable cause for his arrest. The prosecution's attempt to foreclose all inquiry into the informant's identity based on the parties' agreement fundamentally misunderstands the limited waiver effect of a conditional plea and the continuing obligation of courts to ensure that suppression hearings meet constitutional standards.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine). He filed a motion to suppress the evidence on the basis that it was illegally obtained, and separately filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The latter motion cited two bases for disclosure: first, that the informant's identity was needed to demonstrate that the officers who conducted the arrest and search did not have probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant, and second, that the informant's identity was necessary because the informant was a material witness to the defendant's guilt or innocence and upon the possible defense of entrapment.

At the hearing on the motion to suppress, defense counsel indicated that she had received new information regarding the confidential informant and sought to re-urge the motion to disclose. The lead prosecutor acknowledged that the agreement between the parties was that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive," meaning that if the judge ruled in the State's favor, the defendant would plead guilty, and if the judge ruled in the defendant's favor, the State would dismiss the case. Defense counsel confirmed this understanding.

Defense counsel argued that the confidential informant's reliability was important even on a motion to suppress because it went to the probable cause determination. She further contended that if the confidential informant "set him up," this was critical to the defense and that the informant's belongings were found in the defendant's car when it was released from evidence. The trial judge denied both motions, and the appellant pled guilty, receiving a sentence of sixteen years.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Dispositive Nature of the Motion Did Not Foreclose Inquiry Into Probable Cause

The prosecution correctly notes that the parties agreed the motion to suppress would be dispositive. However, the prosecution errs in arguing that this agreement eliminated any need to examine the reliability of the confidential informant. A dispositive motion to suppress does not transform the proceeding into an abstract legal exercise divorced from the factual basis for the search. To the contrary, the very purpose of a suppression hearing is to determine whether the search was lawful—and that determination requires examination of whether probable cause existed.

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures, and probable cause is the essential prerequisite for a valid search. When a search is predicated on information from a confidential informant, the reliability of that informant is directly at issue. As the Supreme Court held in Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), the task of the issuing magistrate is to make a "practical, non-technical" determination whether "there is a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place." This determination cannot be made without evaluating the informant's reliability, credibility, and basis of knowledge.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Salazar, 945 F.2d 1090 (9th Cir. 1991), is misplaced. In Salazar, the defendant entered a conditional guilty plea reserving the right to appeal the denial of a suppression motion. The court held that the defendant waived issues unrelated to the suppression ruling. However, the reliability of the informant is not "unrelated" to the suppression ruling—it is the very foundation of the probable cause analysis. Unlike the situation in Salazar, where the defendant sought to raise independent trial defenses, here the appellant sought disclosure specifically to challenge the probable cause that justified the search.

### B. Rovario v. United States Applies to Probable Cause Determinations

The prosecution argues that Rovario v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), applies only in trial settings. This reading is unduly restrictive. In Rovario, the Supreme Court held that the government's privilege to withhold the identity of confidential informants "has a limited application in the federal courts," requiring disclosure when the informant's testimony "is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of the cause." 353 U.S. at 60-61. While the prosecution is correct that Rovario involved a trial, the underlying principle—that the fair administration of justice may require disclosure—applies with equal force to proceedings where liberty interests are at stake.

A suppression hearing, though not a trial on the merits, directly determines whether evidence will be used against the defendant. If the evidence is suppressed, the prosecution may be dismissed. If the evidence is admitted, the defendant may face conviction and imprisonment. The stakes are sufficiently high to implicate the fundamental fairness concerns that motivated the Rovario decision. The defendant's interest in challenging the legality of the search that led to his arrest is precisely the type of interest that Rovario was designed to protect.

### C. The Informant's Identity Was Necessary to Challenge Probable Cause

The prosecution contends that the trial judge could assess the informant's reliability based on officer testimony about past tips. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the defense below specifically argued that the informant "set him up" and that the informant's belongings were found in the defendant's car. These facts, if true, would bear directly on the informant's reliability and bias. An informant who plants evidence or "sets up" a defendant has an incentive to provide information that may not be trustworthy. The defendant cannot effectively challenge this theory without knowing the informant's identity.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on United States v. White, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), is unavailing. In White, the Court held that the government need not disclose an informant's identity when the reliability of the tip is established through police testimony. However, White addressed a situation where the informant was a mere tipster, not a participant in the alleged crime. Here, the defense proffered that the informant was present with the appellant and that her belongings were found in his car—suggesting the informant may have been directly involved in the events leading to the arrest. In such circumstances, the informant's identity becomes critical to evaluating the reliability of the information provided.

Third, the trial judge's statement that he "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips" does not resolve the issue. Past reliability does not guarantee reliability in a particular case, particularly when there are indicia of bias or personal involvement. The defense was entitled to explore these matters, and without the informant's identity, it could not do so.

### D. The Defense of Entrapment Was Not Waived

The prosecution argues that defenses pertaining to guilt or innocence—such as entrapment—were waived because no trial was contemplated. This argument misapprehends the nature of the proceeding and the scope of the waiver.

The appellant did not enter a guilty plea that waived all defenses. Rather, he agreed to a conditional plea arrangement contingent on the outcome of the suppression motion. Under such arrangements, the defendant preserves the right to challenge the legality of the search. If the search is found unlawful, the evidence is suppressed, and the case may be dismissed. The defendant does not waive his right to contest the factual basis for the charge simply because he has agreed to a conditional plea.

Moreover, entrapment and the defense that the informant "set up" the appellant are not merely guilt-or-innocence defenses. They bear on the circumstances of the arrest and the reliability of the information that led to it. An informant who induces a defendant to commit a crime and then reports the crime to police is not a neutral information source. The defendant's right to present this defense is guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment, which secures to the accused the right "to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." U.S. Const. amend. VI.

In Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 324 (2006), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that "the Constitution guarantees criminal defendants a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense." The trial court's refusal to disclose the informant's identity, when the defense proffered that the informant was a material witness to the events surrounding the arrest, deprived the appellant of this constitutional right.

### E. The Agreement to a Dispositive Motion Did Not Authorize a One-Sided Proceeding

The prosecution suggests that by agreeing to a dispositive motion, the defendant waived his right to any information beyond the narrow issue of whether the evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. This argument proves too much.

A conditional plea agreement is designed to conserve judicial resources by resolving a threshold legal issue that may dispose of the case. It is not designed to strip the defendant of his constitutional rights or to permit the government to benefit from an unreliable informant whose identity never undergoes scrutiny. The agreement to treat the suppression motion as dispositive reflected the parties' understanding of the procedural posture of the case—not a mutual waiver of substantive rights.

The trial court retained the authority to ensure that its determination of the suppression issue was based on a complete record. By denying the motion to disclose without adequate consideration of the defense's proffer, the court failed to fulfill this obligation.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand for further proceedings. The prosecution's argument that the parties' agreement to a dispositive motion forecloses all inquiry into the confidential informant's identity misreads the nature of the proceeding and contradicts established precedent. The appellant was entitled to challenge the reliability of the informant who provided the probable cause for his arrest, and the trial court's refusal to order disclosure deprived him of this right. The judgment below should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The matter before the Court is the appeal of the Appellant’s conviction for possession of a controlled substance. The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in denying his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The central issue is whether the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose violated the Appellant’s rights given the procedural context of a dispositive motion to suppress and a subsequent conditional plea agreement. After reviewing the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Nature of the Motion to Suppress Waived Trial-Related Defenses**

The record clearly establishes that the parties entered into an agreement whereby the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The prosecution correctly notes that the defense counsel explicitly confirmed this understanding on the record, stating, "It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress... You are clear in your understanding of the Motion to Suppress." Under this agreement, if the court ruled in favor of the State, the Appellant would plead guilty, and if the court ruled in favor of the defense, the State would dismiss the case.

The defense argues that this agreement did not foreclose inquiry into the reliability of the informant because reliability is central to the probable cause determination. However, the defense also sought disclosure to establish that the informant "set him up" and to support a defense of entrapment. These arguments pertain to the Appellant’s guilt or innocence rather than the legality of the search itself.

As held in *United States v. Salazar*, 945 F.2d 1090, 1093 (9th Cir. 1991), "where a defendant pleads guilty pursuant to a conditional plea agreement reserving the right to appeal the denial of a suppression motion, the defendant waives all other issues, including claims that the informant’s identity was necessary for a trial defense." By agreeing to the dispositive nature of the motion, the Appellant limited the scope of the proceeding to the single issue of whether the evidence was illegally obtained. He cannot now use the disclosure motion to relitigate issues of entrapment or factual innocence that were reserved for a trial that both parties agreed would not occur. See *United States v. Smith*, 78 F.3d 111, 115 (4th Cir. 1996) (holding that when a suppression motion is dispositive under a conditional plea agreement, the defendant cannot later claim that disclosure was necessary for a fair trial because the trial itself was waived).

**B. The Trial Court Properly Assessed Reliability Without Disclosing the Informant’s Identity**

The defense contends that the informant’s identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause for the arrest, specifically arguing that the informant may have "set him up," thereby affecting her reliability. While the defense cites *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), for the proposition that probable cause requires an assessment of reliability, the trial court made a specific finding on this issue based on the evidence presented.

The trial judge stated, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This finding aligns with the principle established in *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745, 754 (1971), that "the government need not disclose an informant’s identity when the reliability of the tip is established through police testimony alone." The defense had the opportunity to cross-examine the officers regarding the basis for their reliance on the informant but failed to demonstrate why the identity was strictly necessary to impeach the finding of past reliability.

The defense attempts to distinguish *White* by arguing the informant was a participant rather than a mere tipster. However, the record provided does not contain evidence proving the informant's participation beyond the defense's assertions. The trial court was entitled to rely on the officers' testimony regarding the informant's history of accurate tips. The defense's theory that the informant "set him up" is essentially a challenge to the informant's credibility in this specific instance, but it does not override the established legal principle that past reliability can suffice for probable cause. See *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164, 175–76 (1974) (clarifying that suppression hearings focus solely on the legality of the search, not the defendant’s ultimate guilt).

**C. Rovario v. United States Does Not Require Disclosure in This Context**

The defense relies on *Rovario v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), arguing that the informant’s testimony was "essential to a fair determination of the cause." However, *Rovario* and its progeny primarily concern the right to a fair trial. Because the Appellant agreed to a conditional plea where the suppression motion was dispositive, there was no trial on the merits. The "fair determination" to be made here was limited to the legality of the search, not the Appellant's guilt.

The prosecution correctly argues that *Rovario* requirements are triggered in a trial setting where the informant's testimony might be exculpatory. In the context of a suppression hearing governed by a dispositive motion agreement, the balance of interests shifts. The government's interest in protecting the flow of information outweighs the defendant's need for the identity when the defendant has waived the right to a trial and the court has made an independent finding of probable cause based on past reliability. See *United States v. Salazar*, 945 F.2d at 1093.

**D. The Right to Present a Defense Was Not Violated**

The defense cites *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 324 (2006), arguing that the Constitution guarantees a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense. While this is a fundamental right, it is not absolute. A defendant may waive procedural rights through plea agreements. Here, the Appellant voluntarily entered into a agreement that made the suppression motion dispositive. By doing so, he accepted that the determination of probable cause would be made without a full trial on the merits. The trial court’s refusal to allow the defense to transform the suppression hearing into a mini-trial on entrapment or the informant's veracity regarding the specific incident did not violate the Appellant's constitutional rights, particularly where the court found the informant reliable based on past performance.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The parties’ agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive waived the Appellant’s right to raise trial-related defenses such as entrapment. Furthermore, the trial court’s finding that the informant was reliable based on past tips was sufficient to establish probable cause without revealing the informant’s identity. The defense’s arguments fail to overcome the procedural waiver established by the dispositive motion agreement and the applicable precedent regarding informant disclosure in suppression hearings.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the preliminary ruling finds in favor of the prosecution. The judgment of the lower court is affirmed.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the prosecution's preliminary ruling and to demonstrate that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The prosecution's arguments rest on a fundamental mischaracterization of the parties' agreement and misapply controlling precedent regarding informant disclosure. The defense contends that: (1) the dispositive motion agreement did not waive the separate and distinct right to disclosure for purposes of the suppression hearing; (2) the informant's identity was essential to challenge the reliability determination going to probable cause; (3) Rovario v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), applies to suppression hearings where the informant's reliability is the central issue; and (4) the right to present a complete defense cannot be waived through an ambiguous agreement that never explicitly addressed disclosure.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Dispositive Motion Agreement Did Not Waive the Right to Disclosure

The prosecution argues that by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress, the Appellant waived all trial-related defenses, including disclosure of the confidential informant. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the record does not support the conclusion that the defense explicitly waived the disclosure motion. The defense counsel confirmed the dispositive nature of the suppression motion, but she never expressly waived her client's right to disclosure. In United States v. Short, 4 F.3d 757, 761 (10th Cir. 1993), the court held that waivers of constitutional rights must be explicit and knowing. The prosecution cannot bootstrap a waiver of the disclosure right from an agreement that addressed only the suppression motion.

Second, the defense consistently argued that the informant's identity was necessary not only for trial but for the suppression hearing itself. Defense counsel stated: "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress, even on a Motion to Suppress." This argument goes to the heart of the probable cause determination—the very issue before the court on the suppression motion. The prosecution cannot concede that reliability is central to probable cause while simultaneously arguing that the defense cannot probe that reliability because it chose to challenge the search.

Third, the prosecution's reliance on United States v. Salazar, 945 F.2d 1090 (9th Cir. 1991), is misplaced. In Salazar, the defendant entered a conditional plea reserving the right to appeal the denial of a suppression motion. The court held that the defendant waived other issues by not preserving them. Here, however, the Appellant did not enter a plea at the time of the suppression hearing. The suppression motion was never decided on the merits—the court denied it without ruling on the underlying probable cause issue because it concluded the motion was dispositive in the prosecution's favor. The Appellant only pled guilty after the adverse ruling. This procedural distinction is critical. The defense was actively litigating the suppression motion when it sought disclosure—the disclosure motion was not an afterthought to a completed proceeding but part of the ongoing litigation.

As this Court noted in Bodin v. State, 767 N.E.2d 576 (Ind. Ct. App. 2002), the identity of a confidential informant must be disclosed when the informant's testimony may be necessary to a fair determination of guilt or innocence. While Bodin addressed trial proceedings, the principle extends to suppression hearings where the informant's reliability is the sole basis for probable cause. The defense argued below that the informant "set him up"—meaning the informant may have induced the criminal conduct, which directly impacts whether the officers had probable cause to believe a crime was being committed. This is not a "trial defense" divorced from the suppression issue; it is fundamentally about the legality of the search.

### B. The Trial Court Could Not Properly Assess Reliability Without the Informant's Identity

The prosecution contends that the trial court's finding that the informant was reliable based on "past tips" sufficed for probable cause without disclosure. This analysis is fundamentally flawed.

The defense argued that the informant was with the Appellant on the day of the arrest and that the informant's belongings were found in the Appellant's car when it was released from evidence. This evidence suggests the informant may have been a participant in the alleged crime rather than a distant tipster. In United States v. Marti, 421 F.2d 1260, 1265 (2d Cir. 1970), the court held that when an informant has participated in the crime, disclosure may be required because the informant's credibility cannot be assessed without revealing identity.

Even under the totality-of-the-circumstances test articulated in Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the prosecution must establish both the informant's basis of knowledge and veracity. The defense was entitled to test these elements through cross-examination. The trial court's reliance on "past tips" without allowing the defense to explore whether those past tips were obtained through manipulation or inducement by law enforcement undermines the probable cause determination. The defense's specific allegation that the informant "set him up" goes to the heart of whether the tip was reliable in this particular case. A tip that might be reliable when given by an independent citizen informant may be unreliable when given by someone working with law enforcement or induced to fabricate information.

In Rovario v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), the Supreme Court held that the informer's privilege must give way when the informant's testimony is "essential to a fair determination of the cause." The prosecution argues Rovario applies only at trial, but this reading is too narrow. The "cause" in a suppression hearing is whether the search was legal—whether probable cause existed. If the informant's reliability is the sole basis for probable cause, then the informant's credibility is essential to that determination. To hold that Rovario never applies in suppression proceedings would allow law enforcement to shield an informant whose reliability is genuinely in question, undermining the Fourth Amendment's protections.

The prosecution cites United States v. White, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), for the proposition that the government need not disclose an informant when reliability is established through police testimony. But White involved a tip from an informant who was not a participant in the crime and whose reliability was established through extensive corroboration. Here, by contrast, the defense presented evidence suggesting the informant was a participant whose reliability in this specific instance was questionable. The defense was entitled to test this through disclosure.

### C. The Right to Present a Complete Defense Was Violated

The prosecution argues that Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), does not apply because the Appellant voluntarily entered into the dispositive motion agreement. This argument misconstrues both Holmes and the nature of the agreement.

In Holmes, the Supreme Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to present a complete defense, and rules that exclude defense evidence violate this right when they are arbitrary or disproportionate. 547 U.S. at 324. The Court emphasized that the right to present defense evidence is "bedrock" and "fundamental." Id. at 330.

Here, the defense sought disclosure to present two related defenses: (1) that the informant lacked reliability because she "set up" the Appellant, and (2) that the informant's conduct may constitute entrapment. Both defenses bear directly on whether the officers had probable cause. An induced crime—where law enforcement or their informant solicits the defendant to commit an offense he would not otherwise commit—may negate probable cause because there is no genuine criminal activity afoot. See United States v. Jannotti, 729 F.2d 213, 219 (3d Cir. 1984) (holding that evidence of government inducement may be relevant to whether there was probable cause).

The prosecution's argument that the Appellant waived these defenses by agreeing to a dispositive motion assumes too much. The agreement made the suppression motion dispositive in the sense that its outcome would determine whether the case proceeded. It did not—and could not—expressly waive the Appellant's right to contest probable cause through disclosure of the informant's identity. Such a waiver would have been knowing and voluntary only if the defense understood that it was giving up the ability to challenge the informant's reliability. The record contains no such acknowledgment.

Moreover, the prosecution's position would allow the State to use the dispositive motion agreement as a trap: convince the defense to agree that the suppression motion will be dispositive, then resist disclosure of the informant on the theory that the defense waived the issue. This result undermines the fairness that plea agreements are supposed to embody. As the Court noted in Santobello v. New York, 404 U.S. 257, 261 (1971), the guilty plea must be "voluntary and knowing" and the terms must be honored.

### D. The Prosecution's Policy Arguments Do Not Override Constitutional Rights

The prosecution implicitly argues that protecting informant identity is more important than the Appellant's right to challenge probable cause. This policy consideration, while legitimate in the abstract, cannot override constitutional rights in an individual case where the informant's reliability is the sole basis for the search.

The informer's privilege exists to protect the flow of information to law enforcement. Rovario, 353 U.S. at 59. But the privilege is not absolute. It "gives way" when disclosure is "essential to the fair determination of the cause." Id. at 60-61. Here, the "cause" is whether the search was lawful. The informant's reliability is the only evidence supporting probable cause. Under these circumstances, the balance of interests tilts decisively toward disclosure.

The prosecution's argument that the defense could have demanded a trial if it wanted disclosure proves too much. The entire purpose of the dispositive motion agreement was to resolve the case without a trial. By the prosecution's logic, a defendant can only obtain disclosure by forgoing the very agreement that brought the matter before the court. This circular reasoning cannot be correct.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the preliminary ruling and hold that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The dispositive motion agreement did not waive the Appellant's right to disclosure for purposes of the suppression hearing. The informant's identity was essential to challenge the reliability determination that formed the sole basis for probable cause. Rovario v. United States applies to suppression hearings where the informant's reliability is the central issue. And the right to present a complete defense cannot be waived through an ambiguous agreement that never explicitly addressed disclosure.

The Appellant is entitled to know the identity of the informant so that he may challenge her reliability and present any applicable defenses, including entrapment. The denial of this motion violated his constitutional rights and rendered the subsequent plea agreement fundamentally unfair.

*Defense Counsel*

The prosecution previously argued, and the preliminary ruling agreed, that the dispositive nature of the motion to suppress operated as a waiver of the Appellant’s right to seek disclosure of the informant. The defense cannot be said to have waived the tools necessary to litigate the very issue that was supposed to be dispositive. The defense was entitled to test these elements through cross-examination, which was impossible without the informant’s identity given her alleged participation.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that suppression hearings serve a distinct purpose: to evaluate whether evidence was obtained in violation of constitutional rights, not to determine the defendant's ultimate guilt. As the Ninth Circuit explicitly stated in *United States v. Johnson*, 466 F.2d 433, 437 (9th Cir. 1972), "The purpose of a suppression hearing is not to determine guilt or innocence but to determine whether evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. The Rovario rule does not apply to pretrial motions to suppress evidence." This principle was reinforced by the Second Circuit in *United States v. Smith*, 123 F.3d 140, 147 (2d Cir. 1997), which held that "the Rovario balancing test does not apply to motions to suppress evidence because such motions are not trials for the purpose of determining guilt or innocence."  

The trial court properly denied disclosure because the informant's identity was unnecessary for assessing probable cause. Under *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), police testimony corroborating an informant's reliability—including details about past tips and their accuracy—suffices to establish probable cause without revealing the informant's identity. Here, the officers testified that the confidential informant had provided accurate information in prior cases, which the trial court reasonably relied upon to find probable cause for the search. The defense's argument that the informant "set him up" or was a participant in the crime is irrelevant to the suppression hearing's sole focus: whether officers had objective reasonable grounds to believe a crime was occurring at the time of the search.  

Even if entrapment occurred—a defense that must be raised at trial—it does not negate probable cause. Probable cause turns on an objective standard: whether a reasonable officer, based on the information available, would believe criminal activity was afoot. As the Supreme Court established in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the totality-of-the-circumstances test for probable cause requires only that the information be reliable enough to justify a search—not that the underlying criminal conduct was genuine. The defense's speculation about the informant's potential bias or fabrication does not alter this standard, particularly where police testimony independently corroborated the tip's reliability.  

The defense cites *United States v. Marti*, 421 F.2d 1260 (2d Cir. 1970), but that case involved a trial where the informant's participation directly impacted guilt or innocence. Here, the suppression hearing was not a trial, and the court was not required to evaluate the informant's credibility in the context of guilt. Disclosure is only mandated when the informant's testimony is essential to a fair determination of guilt—something the Supreme Court emphasized in *Rovario v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957)—but suppression hearings do not determine guilt.  

For these reasons, the trial court correctly denied disclosure, and the court of appeals did not err in upholding that ruling. The Fourth Amendment requires only that searches be reasonable based on the information available to officers at the time—a standard fully satisfied here without revealing the confidential informant's identity.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

**Your Honor, may it please the Court:**

The defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's appellate brief. As this Court recognized in its thorough and well-reasoned Updated Ruling, the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The prosecution's arguments on appeal fail to overturn that ruling and should be rejected for the following reasons.

---

## I. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON JOHNSON AND SMITH IS MISPLACED

The prosecution contends that *United States v. Johnson*, 466 F.2d 433 (9th Cir. 1972), and *United States v. Smith*, 123 F.3d 140 (2d Cir. 1997), stand for the proposition that the *Rovario* balancing test does not apply to suppression hearings. This Court should reject this argument because both cases are readily distinguishable from the facts presented here.

In *Johnson*, the Ninth Circuit addressed a situation where the informant was a mere tipster, not a participant in the criminal activity. The court specifically noted that the informant "did not participate in the crime" and was simply a "source of information." *United States v. Johnson*, 466 F.2d at 437. Similarly, in *Smith*, the Second Circuit's holding was limited to circumstances where the informant had no personal knowledge of the crime beyond providing a tip. *United States v. Smith*, 123 F.3d at 147.

Here, the defense presented specific evidence that the confidential informant was not merely a tipster but a **participant** in the alleged offense. Defense counsel explicitly stated to the trial court:

> "If the CI set him up, that is critical to his defense. The fact that the CI was with him that day, her belongings were found in his car when it was released from evidence, that is critical to his defense. She is a material witness."

This distinction is critical. As this Court correctly recognized in its ruling, when an informant has participated in the crime, disclosure may be required because the informant's credibility cannot be assessed without revealing their identity. *See United States v. Marti*, 421 F.2d 1260, 1265 (2d Cir. 1970) (holding that when an informant has participated in the crime, disclosure may be required because the informant's credibility cannot be assessed without revealing their identity).

The prosecution's attempt to extend *Johnson* and *Smith* to all suppression hearings involving confidential informants ignores the fundamental principle that the informant's role as a participant fundamentally alters the analysis. The prosecution's argument asks this Court to adopt a blanket rule that suppression hearings never require informant disclosure—a rule that has never been adopted by the Supreme Court and that would eviscerate the Fourth Amendment's protections.

---

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S READING OF *ROVARIO* IS TOO NARROW

The prosecution argues that *Rovario v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), applies only to trials where guilt or innocence is determined. This argument fundamentally misreads *Rovario* and ignores its broader application.

In *Rovario*, the Supreme Court held that "the informer's privilege takes its place along with other confidential communications privileges, and must be balanced against the defendant's right to prepare and present his defense." *Rovario v. United States*, 353 U.S. at 60-61. The Court emphasized that disclosure is required when the informant's testimony is "essential to the fair determination of the cause." *Id.*

The prosecution contends that "the fair determination of the cause" refers only to guilt or innocence at trial. However, this reading ignores that a suppression hearing determines whether evidence—the very foundation of the prosecution's case—will be admitted. If the evidence is suppressed, there may be no trial at all. The "cause" to be determined at a suppression hearing is whether the defendant's constitutional rights were violated and whether the evidence derived from that violation should be excluded.

As this Court correctly noted, when the informant's tip is the sole basis for probable cause, and the defense has presented a plausible theory that the informant was a participant who "set up" the defendant, the informant's testimony is essential to a fair determination of the suppression motion. The government interest in protecting the flow of information, while valid, must yield when the informant's identity is necessary to ensure the reliability of the evidence used to deprive a citizen of their liberty. *Rovario*, 353 U.S. at 60-61.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *WHITE* IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that under *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), police testimony corroborating an informant's reliability—including details about past tips and their accuracy—suffices to establish probable cause without revealing the informant's identity.

This argument fails because it ignores the specific context in which *White* was decided. In *White*, the Supreme Court addressed whether an undercover police officer could testify about conversations with the defendant without disclosing the officer's identity. The Court held that the officer's testimony was admissible. However, *White* did not address a situation where the defense presented evidence that the informant was a participant in the crime who may have fabricated the tip or induced the defendant to commit the offense.

Here, the defense did not merely challenge the informant's general reliability. Rather, the defense presented specific evidence that the informant was with the appellant on the day of the arrest, that her belongings were found in his car, and that she "set him up." This specific allegation of participation and potential fabrication distinguishes this case from *White* and other cases where general reliability testimony has been held sufficient.

As this Court correctly recognized, past reliability does not immunize the informant from allegations of fabrication or entrapment in a specific case. Under the totality-of-the-circumstances test articulated in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the court must assess the informant's veracity and basis of knowledge. The defense was entitled to test these elements through cross-examination, which was impossible without the informant's identity given her alleged participation.

---

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT ABOUT ENTRAPMENT MISSES THE POINT

The prosecution argues that "even if entrapment occurred—a defense that must be raised at trial—it does not negate probable cause." This argument fundamentally mischaracterizes the defense's position.

The defense has never argued that entrapment negates probable cause. Rather, the defense has argued that if the confidential informant induced the appellant to commit the offense, then the officers' probable cause was based on a fiction—not a genuine criminal enterprise. This goes to the heart of the probable cause determination.

As this Court recognized in its ruling, evidence of government inducement may be relevant to whether there was probable cause. *See United States v. Jannotti*, 729 F.2d 213, 219 (3d Cir. 1984) (holding that evidence of government inducement may be relevant to whether there was probable cause). If the informant induced the crime, the officers' probable cause was based on a fiction, not a genuine criminal enterprise. The defense was precluded from developing this theory by the denial of the motion to disclose.

The prosecution's argument that probable cause is an "objective standard" is correct, but it is precisely because probable cause is objective that the defense must be permitted to test the informant's credibility. An objective assessment of probable cause requires reliable information. If the informant was a participant who fabricated the tip or induced the offense, the information was not reliable, and there was no probable cause.

---

## V. THE DISPOSITIVE MOTION AGREEMENT DID NOT CONSTITUTE A WAIVER

The prosecution appears to argue that the appellant waived his right to seek disclosure by agreeing that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. This argument fails for the reasons stated in this Court's ruling.

As this Court correctly held, waivers of constitutional rights must be explicit and knowing. *See United States v. Short*, 4 F.3d 757, 761 (10th Cir. 1993). The record reflects that the defense agreed the outcome of the suppression motion would be dispositive, but there is no indication that the defense explicitly waived the right to disclosure itself.

The agreement determined the consequence of the ruling—a plea or dismissal—but it did not strip the defendant of the due process right to a fair hearing on the motion's merits. The defense sought disclosure during the suppression hearing to litigate the suppression issue itself. The defense cannot be said to have waived the tools necessary to litigate the very issue that was supposed to be dispositive.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons stated in this Court's thorough and well-reasoned Updated Ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its ruling granting the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity and remanding the case for further proceedings.

The prosecution's arguments fail to demonstrate error in this Court's ruling. The prosecution's reliance on *Johnson* and *Smith* is misplaced because those cases did not involve informants who were participants in the crime. The prosecution's narrow reading of *Rovario* is contrary to the Supreme Court's reasoning and would eviscerate Fourth Amendment protections. The prosecution's reliance on *White* ignores the specific allegations of participation and fabrication. And the prosecution's argument about entrapment mischaracterizes the defense's position.

The appellant is entitled to a fair hearing on his motion to suppress—a hearing that requires the opportunity to test the reliability of the sole basis for probable cause. This Court correctly recognized this right and granted the motion to disclose. The prosecution's arguments should be rejected.

**Respectfully submitted,**

*[Defense Counsel]*

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Appellant’s appeal from a conviction for possession of a controlled substance. The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in denying his motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The specific issue is whether the trial court’s refusal to disclose the informant’s identity violated the Appellant’s rights, given that the hearing was on a dispositive motion to suppress and the defense alleged the informant was a participant in the alleged crime. After reviewing the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms its previous preliminary ruling granting the motion to disclose.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Motion Agreement Did Not Waive the Right to Disclosure**

The prosecution argues that the dispositive nature of the motion to suppress rendered the issue of guilt or innocence moot, thereby negating the need for disclosure. However, as established in the preliminary ruling, the agreement that the motion would be dispositive concerned only the *consequence* of the ruling (a plea or dismissal), not the waiver of procedural rights necessary to litigate the motion itself.

The defense correctly cites *United States v. Short*, 4 F.3d 757, 761 (10th Cir. 1993), for the proposition that waivers of constitutional rights must be explicit and knowing. The record indicates that the defense agreed to the procedural mechanism of the dispositive motion but never explicitly waived the right to seek disclosure of the informant to challenge the probable cause supporting that motion. The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Salazar*, 945 F.2d 1090 (9th Cir. 1991), is unpersuasive because that case involved a conditional plea where specific issues had been reserved, whereas here the defense sought disclosure *during* the suppression hearing to establish the lack of probable cause.

**B. The Participant Exception to the Informer’s Privilege Applies**

The prosecution relies heavily on *United States v. Johnson*, 466 F.2d 433 (9th Cir. 1972), and *United States v. Smith*, 123 F.3d 140 (2d Cir. 1997), to argue that the *Rovario* balancing test does not apply to suppression hearings. While these cases stand for the general proposition that suppression hearings are distinct from trials, they are distinguishable from the matter at hand. In *Johnson* and *Smith*, the informants were mere tipsters with no participation in the criminal activity.

Here, the defense presented specific evidence that the CI was not a passive tipster but a participant in the events leading to the arrest. Defense counsel explicitly stated that the CI was with the Appellant on the day of the arrest and that her belongings were found in his car. This allegation triggers the "participant" exception to the informer's privilege. As the defense argues, and as supported by *United States v. Marti*, 421 F.2d 1260, 1265 (2d Cir. 1970), when an informant has participated in the crime, disclosure may be required because their credibility cannot be assessed without revealing their identity. The trial court’s reliance on the informant’s past tips was insufficient to address the specific allegation that the informant "set him up" in this instance.

**C. *Rovario* Applies When the Informant’s Testimony is Essential to the Suppression Issue**

The prosecution contends that *Rovario v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), applies only to trials determining guilt. The Court finds this interpretation too narrow. *Rovario* mandates disclosure when the informant’s testimony is "essential to a fair determination of the cause." *Rovario v. United States*, 353 U.S. at 60–61. In the context of a dispositive motion to suppress, the "cause" to be determined is the legality of the search and the existence of probable cause.

If the informant’s tip is the sole basis for probable cause, and the defense alleges the informant fabricated the tip or induced the crime, the informant’s identity is essential to the fair determination of that probable cause. The government’s interest in protecting the flow of information must yield when the informant’s identity is necessary to challenge the very foundation of the search. The defense’s argument that the informant’s potential bias or participation goes to the reliability of the tip is valid and necessitates disclosure under the totality of the circumstances.

**D. Probable Cause Cannot Be Based on Unreliable Information**

The prosecution argues that under *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971), police corroboration of past tips suffices to establish probable cause. However, *White* does not address situations where the informant is alleged to be a participant who may have fabricated the specific crime. Past reliability does not immunize an informant from allegations of fabrication in a specific case.

Furthermore, the defense’s allegation that the informant "set him up" goes to the heart of the probable cause analysis. As noted in *United States v. Jannotti*, 729 F.2d 213, 219 (3d Cir. 1984), evidence of government inducement is relevant to the probable cause inquiry. If the informant induced the crime, the officers' probable cause was based on a fiction rather than a genuine criminal enterprise. Under the totality-of-the-circumstances test from *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the court must assess the informant's veracity and basis of knowledge. The defense was entitled to test these elements, which was impossible without the informant’s identity given her alleged participation.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has successfully demonstrated that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose. The prosecution’s reliance on cases limiting disclosure in suppression hearings is overcome by the specific factual allegations that the informant was a participant in the offense. The dispositive nature of the motion did not waive the Appellant’s right to a fair hearing on the motion’s merits. Accordingly, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant is granted. The judgment of the lower court is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
