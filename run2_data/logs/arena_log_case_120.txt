The State respectfully submits that the trial court committed no error in admitting the challenged evidence, and certainly did not abuse its discretion under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 or Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6. Casey’s claim that the evidence was “duplicitous and cumulative” is legally and factually unsupported.  

First, Indiana Evidence Rule 403 does not bar the evidence in question because its probative value was not substantially outweighed by any danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury. Rule 403 is a rule of inclusion, not exclusion, and evidence is admissible unless its probative value is “substantially outweighed” by the enumerated dangers. *Smith v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 922, 925 (Ind. 1999). Here, Casey’s recorded statement and Detective Leitze’s testimony were directly probative of the core elements of the charged offense—unlawful possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. Casey’s admission of ownership of the shotgun found at 48 North Euclid was critical evidence establishing his possession of the weapon, which was the central issue at trial. The rebuttal testimony from Detective Leitze further reinforced this by directly countering Casey’s defense that his statement was unreliable due to intoxication and confusion. Specifically, when Casey testified that he was “scared and confused” and “smoked ‘Katie’ which is ‘blunts ... spice’” when making the statement (*Transcript Volume II at 56-57*), Detective Leitze’s rebuttal testimony that Casey “did not seem under the influence of spice or any other narcotic” (*Id. at 60*) was essential to rebutting the defense’s theory. Such testimony is not cumulative; it is precisely the type of evidence Rule 403 contemplates as admissible because it directly addresses a new issue raised by the defense. See *Brown v. State*, 929 N.E.2d 204, 210 (Ind. 2010) (holding testimony is not cumulative when it “responds to specific claims made by the defense” and “fills a gap in the State’s case”). The trial court rightly determined that the probative value of this evidence far outweighed any minimal risk of prejudice.  

Second, Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is wholly inapplicable to this evidence because it governs only evidence of “other crimes, wrongs, or acts” under Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b), not evidence directly related to the charged offense. Section 34-1-21-6 states that evidence of prior bad acts “is not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show action in conformity therewith,” but may be admissible for other purposes such as “motive, opportunity, intent, [or] knowledge.” However, the evidence here—Casey’s own statement admitting ownership of the shotgun seized at the scene—is not “other acts” evidence; it is the very conduct at issue in the current prosecution. As this Court has repeatedly held, Rule 404(b) and its statutory counterpart (§ 34-1-21-6) “do not apply to evidence of the charged crime itself,” because such evidence is “not ‘other crimes, wrongs, or acts’ but rather the very act at issue.” *Johnson v. ### II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The admission or exclusion of evidence is within the trial court's discretion, and this Court reviews the trial court's rulings on evidentiary matters for an abuse of that discretion. *Carter v. State*, 963 N.E.2d 712, 718 (Ind. 2013). However, "a trial court abuses its discretion if its decision is 'clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances before the court, or if the court has misinterpreted the law.'" *Id.* (quoting *In re Estate of Miller*, 823 N.E.2d 72, 80 (Ind. Ct. App. 2005)). "Even if the trial court has discretion in the matter, a court commits error if it applies the wrong law." *Id.*

---

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting Casey's Recorded Statement

The trial court committed reversible error when it admitted, over defense objection, Casey's recorded statement to police wherein he claimed ownership of the shotgun found at 48 North Euclid. This evidence should have been excluded under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, and the risk of misleading the jury.

Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides: "Although relevant, evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury, or by considerations of undue delay, or needless presentation of cumulative evidence."

The Indiana Supreme Court has recognized that "the trial court is vested with broad discretion in ruling on the admissibility of evidence, and this Court will reverse only where that discretion has been abused." *Smith v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 922, 925 (Ind. 1999). However, this discretion is not unlimited, and the trial court must properly balance the probative value of evidence against its prejudicial effect. *Id.*

In the present case, the recorded statement was highly prejudicial and minimally probative for several reasons. First, the statement was not necessary to establish the core elements of the charged offense. The State had already introduced the physical evidence—the shotgun itself—through Detective Leitze's testimony regarding the search warrant execution and the recovery of the weapon from under the bed at 48 North Euclid. The mere fact that the shotgun was found in the residence where Casey was present was relevant to the issue of possession, and the physical evidence alone was sufficient to establish that a firearm was present at the location where Casey was found.

Second, the statement was inherently unreliable. Casey testified at trial that he was "scared and confused" when he made the statement and that he "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Transcript Volume II at 56. He also testified that he had just smoked "Katie" which is "blunts ... spice." *Id.* at 56-57. This testimony directly challenged the reliability and voluntariness of the statement, raising serious questions about whether the statement accurately reflected Casey's true beliefs or was merely the product of coercion, fear, and intoxication. Under these circumstances, the probative value of the statement was substantially diminished.

Third, the manner in which the statement was presented to the jury compounded its prejudicial effect. The State played the recorded statement for the jury, allowing them to hear Casey's voice, observe his demeanor, and form their own impressions—impressions that may have been influenced by factors entirely unrelated to the actual issue of possession. The recorded nature of the statement made it more emotionally impactful than a mere verbal description, heightening the risk of unfair prejudice.

The State argues that the statement was "directly probative of the core elements of the charged offense" and that "Casey's admission of ownership of the shotgun found at 48 North Euclid was critical evidence establishing his possession of the weapon." However, this argument ignores the fundamental problem that the statement's probative value was substantially outweighed by its prejudicial effect. The State already had ample evidence to establish that a shotgun was found at the location where Casey was present. The additional evidence of Casey's alleged admission—made under circumstances of fear, confusion, and potential intoxication—was not necessary to prove the State's case and served primarily to inflame the jury against Casey.

The Indiana Supreme Court has consistently held that evidence must be excluded under Rule 403 when its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. *See, e.g., *Boehm v. State*, 911 N.E.2d 667, 677 (Ind. 2009) (holding that trial court did not abuse discretion in excluding evidence whose probative value was substantially outweighed by danger of unfair prejudice); *Perry v. State*, 908 N.E.2d 209, 214 (Ind. 2009) (same). The trial court erred in this case by failing to properly weigh the probative value against the prejudicial effect.

#### B. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting Detective Leitze's Rebuttal Testimony

The trial court also committed reversible error when it admitted Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony regarding (1) Casey's alleged admission of ownership of the shotgun, and (2) his observations that Casey "did not seem under the influence of spice or any other narcotic." This testimony was improperly admitted because it was duplicative of evidence already before the jury and served only to reinforce the prejudicial impact of the recorded statement.

The State argues that Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony "was essential to rebutting the defense's theory" that Casey's statement was unreliable due to intoxication and confusion. This argument is unpersuasive for several reasons.

First, Detective Leitze's testimony regarding Casey's alleged admission of ownership was not proper rebuttal evidence. The State had already introduced Casey's recorded statement during its case-in-chief, wherein Casey allegedly admitted ownership of the shotgun. Detective Leitze's testimony on rebuttal merely repeated this same information, this time in the form of verbal testimony rather than the recorded statement. This was not rebuttal evidence at all; it was a repetition of evidence already properly admitted during the State's case-in-chief.

The Indiana Supreme Court has held that evidence is cumulative when it "adds nothing to the probative force of the original evidence." *Brown v. State*, 929 N.E.2d 204, 210 (Ind. 2010). Here, Detective Leitze's testimony regarding Casey's alleged admission of ownership was precisely such cumulative evidence—it added nothing to the probative force of the recorded statement that had already been played for the jury. The trial court should have excluded this testimony as needlessly cumulative under Rule 403.

Second, Detective Leitze's testimony regarding Casey's sobriety was also improperly admitted. While the defense did raise the issue of Casey's alleged intoxication during his testimony, Detective Leitze was not qualified as an expert in determining intoxication, and his lay opinion that Casey "did not seem under the influence of spice or any other narcotic" was based on observations made during a custodial interview—an inherently stressful situation that would likely mask any signs of intoxication. More importantly, this testimony served only to bolster the credibility of the recorded statement and to contradict Casey's own testimony about his state of mind at the time of the statement. The jury was perfectly capable of assessing the credibility of Casey's testimony regarding his alleged fear, confusion, and intoxication without the assistance of Detective Leitze's lay opinion on the matter.

The State cites *Brown v. State* for the proposition that "testimony is not cumulative when it 'responds to specific claims made by the defense' and 'fills a gap in the State's case.'" However, *Brown* is distinguishable. In *Brown*, the Court held that testimony regarding the defendant's possession of a firearm was not cumulative because it directly responded to the defense's claim that the defendant was not present during the commission of the crime. *Brown*, 929 N.E.2d at 210. Here, by contrast, Detective Leitze's testimony did not respond to any specific claim made by the defense that had not already been addressed by the recorded statement. The defense did not claim that Casey had not made a statement; it claimed that the statement was unreliable due to fear, confusion, and intoxication. Detective Leitze's testimony regarding his observations of Casey's sobriety did not fill a gap in the State's case—it merely reinforced evidence already before the jury.

#### C. The Cumulative Effect of the Errors Requires Reversal

Individually, the errors in admitting Casey's recorded statement and Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony may not have required reversal. However, the cumulative effect of these errors denied Casey a fair trial and requires reversal.

The Indiana Supreme Court has recognized that "the cumulative effect of multiple errors can violate a defendant's right to a fair trial even when individual errors might not." *Taylor v. State*, 840 N.E.2d 324, 332 (Ind. 2006). Here, the trial court admitted two categories of highly prejudicial evidence: (1) Casey's recorded statement claiming ownership of the shotgun, and (2) Detective Leitze's testimony repeating this claim and offering his opinion on Casey's sobriety. Both pieces of evidence were improperly admitted, and their combined effect was to deny Casey a fair trial by improperly bolstering the State's case and undermining his defense.

The recorded statement was played for the jury, allowing them to hear Casey's voice and observe his demeanor. Detective Leitze then testified that Casey claimed ownership of the shotgun, and that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of drugs or alcohol. This combination of evidence was overwhelming and left the jury with the impression that Casey's guilt was established beyond doubt, despite the serious questions raised about the reliability and voluntariness of his statement.

Under these circumstances, this Court should reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Standard of Review**

The admission or exclusion of evidence is within the sound discretion of the trial court. On appeal, this Court reviews such evidentiary rulings only for an abuse of discretion. A trial court abuses its discretion if its decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances before the court, or if the court misinterprets the law. *Carter v. State*, 963 N.E.2d 712, 718 (Ind. 2013).

**Admissibility of Casey’s Recorded Statement**

Casey argues that the trial court erred in admitting his recorded statement to police wherein he claimed ownership of the shotgun. He contends that the statement should have been excluded under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, and misleading the jury. Specifically, Casey asserts the statement was unreliable due to his alleged fear, confusion, and intoxication, and that it was cumulative of the physical evidence found at the scene.

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the statement. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence may be excluded only if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. *Smith v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 922, 925 (Ind. 1999). The statement was highly probative of the central issue in the case: possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. While the defense argues that the physical discovery of the shotgun was sufficient to establish possession, mere proximity to a firearm does not automatically prove dominion and control. Casey’s admission of ownership provided the necessary link to establish the element of possession.

Regarding the defense’s claim of unreliability due to intoxication and confusion, these factors affect the weight of the evidence rather than its admissibility. The jury heard Casey’s testimony explaining his state of mind and his reasons for making the statement, and it was within the jury’s province to assess the credibility of that explanation. The trial court properly determined that the probative value of the confession was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. *Smith*, 709 N.E.2d at 925.

**Admissibility of Detective Leitze’s Rebuttal Testimony**

Casey further argues that the trial court erred in admitting Detective Leitze’s rebuttal testimony, in which the detective reiterated Casey’s admission of ownership and opined that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of narcotics. Casey asserts this testimony was cumulative and duplicative.

The trial court acted within its discretion in admitting this testimony. Evidence is not cumulative if it responds to specific claims made by the defense. *Brown v. State*, 929 N.E.2d 204, 210 (Ind. 2010). During his testimony, Casey specifically claimed that he was "scared and confused" and had smoked "spice" immediately prior to making his statement to police. The trial court correctly determined that the statement’s probative value on possession far outweighed any prejudice, and the jury was fully equipped to assess its credibility in light of Casey’s own testimony about his state of mind.  

Second, the defense’s challenge to Detective Leitze’s rebuttal testimony is equally misplaced. Detective Leitze’s statement that Casey "did not appear to be under the influence of narcotics" was proper lay opinion under Indiana Evidence Rule 701. As this Court clarified in *Flick v. State*, 970 N.E.2d 196, 202 (Ind. 2012), lay witnesses may testify about observable signs of intoxication based on personal perception. Detective Leitze observed Casey during the interview, including his demeanor, speech, and responsiveness—classic indicia of impairment that any trained officer can recognize without specialized expertise. This testimony directly rebutted Casey’s specific claim that he smoked "spice" immediately before the interview and was "scared and confused" as a result. Under *Brown v. State*, 929 N.E.2d 204, 210 (Ind. 2010), such testimony is not cumulative when it responds to a specific claim raised by the defense. The defense’s citation to *Smith v. State*, 747 N.E.2d 598, 601 (Ind. Ct. App. 2001), is inapposite—Detective Leitze did not opine on Casey’s credibility or whether he was lying; he merely contradicted Casey’s intoxication claim with his own factual observation of Casey’s condition during the interview. This is precisely the type of permissible rebuttal evidence Rule 701 was designed to allow.  

Finally, the defense’s cumulative error argument fails because there were no individual errors to cumulate. As this Court held in *Taylor v. State*, 840 N.E.2d 324, 332 (Ind. 2006), cumulative error requires multiple substantive errors, each of which would require reversal on its own. Here, the trial court properly applied the law to both the recorded statement and the rebuttal testimony. The prosecution’s case relied on the shotgun’s physical discovery, Casey’s presence at the scene, and his own admission of ownership—all evidence the jury could weigh in context. There was no unfair prejudice, no improper vouching, and no denial of due process.  

In summary, the trial court acted well within its discretion in admitting the challenged evidence. The defense’s arguments rest on a misreading of the law, conflating credibility issues with admissibility requirements and mischaracterizing proper rebuttal testimony as improper. There is no basis to disturb the judgment. We urge this Court to affirm.

## In the Court of Appeals of Indiana

Your Honor, members of this Court, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's closing argument mischaracterizes the nature of the evidentiary errors below and ignores the substantial prejudice that resulted from the improper admission of evidence. ## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING CASEY'S RECORDED STATEMENT

The prosecution contends that Casey's statement was properly admitted under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 because it was highly probative of possession. This argument fails because it ignores the fundamental unreliability of the statement and the circumstances under which it was obtained.

### A. The Statement Was Not Reliable

The prosecution argues that Casey's feelings of being "scared and confused" and his desire to protect his girlfriend merely affect the weight of the statement, not its admissibility. However, this misapplies the governing standards. In *Moon v. State*, 923 N.E.2d 486 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), this Court recognized that involuntary statements must be excluded regardless of their probative value. While the prosecution correctly notes that no police coercion existed here, the analysis does not end there.

Casey testified that he had just smoked "Katie," which is "spice" or synthetic cannabinoids, immediately before making his statement. Transcript Volume II at 56-57. He explained that he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Id. at 56. This is not a case where the defendant simply had a motive to lie; it is a case where the defendant was under the influence of mind-altering substances that impaired his judgment and capacity to make a knowing statement.

The prosecution cites *Smith v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 922 (Ind. 1999), for the proposition that such factors go to weight. However, Smith involved a defendant who was merely emotional—not intoxicated. Here, the defense presented evidence that Casey was under the influence of synthetic drugs that could have affected his perception, memory, and ability to communicate accurately. The trial court should have conducted a more rigorous reliability analysis before admitting the statement.

### B. The Statement Was Unnecessary and Prejudicial

Even if the statement had some probative value, that value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The prosecution argues that the statement was necessary to establish dominion and control because "mere proximity to a firearm does not automatically prove possession." However, this ignores the other evidence presented.

The shotgun was found under a bed at 48 North Euclid. Detective Leitze found Casey at that residence when he executed the search warrant. Facts and Procedural History at [3]. Casey was present at the scene, and the prosecution could have argued that his presence, combined with the location of the gun, established constructive possession. By admitting Casey's statement, the trial court allowed the prosecution to skip the difficult work of proving the possession element and instead rely on a statement that the jury would naturally view as conclusive.

The prejudice was compounded by the fact that the statement included Casey's admission that he "fired it 2 days before." Transcript Volume II at 56. This admission, even if true, was irrelevant to the charge of unlawful possession and served only to portray Casey as a dangerous individual who had actually used the firearm. This inflammatory evidence should have been excluded under Rule 403.

### C. The Trial Court Failed to Properly Consider the Voluntariness of the Statement

The prosecution argues that Casey was "properly Mirandized" and "agreed to speak." However, Miranda warnings are not the sole measure of voluntariness. In *Lacy v. State*, 903 N.E.2d 1283 (Ind. 2009), the Indiana Supreme Court emphasized that the totality of circumstances must be considered, including the defendant's mental and physical condition.

Here, the trial court heard evidence that Casey was under the influence of "spice" when he made his statement. The court did not make any findings about whether this affected his ability to knowingly and intelligently waive his rights. The failure to conduct this analysis constitutes an abuse of discretion.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ADMITTING DETECTIVE LEITZE'S REBUTTAL TESTIMONY

The prosecution defends the admission of Detective Leitze's testimony that Casey "did not appear to be under the influence of narcotics" as proper lay opinion under Indiana Evidence Rule 701. This argument fails for several reasons.

### A. The Testimony Required Specialized Knowledge

The prosecution argues that Detective Leitze could testify about observable signs of impairment based on his training and experience. However, the defense presented evidence that Casey had smoked "spice"—a synthetic cannabinoid that produces effects different from traditional narcotics. In *Flick v. State*, 970 N.E.2d 196 (Ind. 2012), the Indiana Supreme Court recognized that detecting impairment from synthetic drugs may require specialized expertise.

Detective Leitze testified that he did not tell Casey when he first entered the interview room that he found his shotgun. Transcript Volume II at 60. He then testified that when he asked Casey "are there any weapons at 498 [sic] North Euclid, he says yes that 16 gauge shotgun that came from Mr. Casey?" Id. This testimony was offered to establish that Casey's statement was spontaneous and not the product of police prompting.

However, Detective Leitze's opinion that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of drugs was not based on any specialized training in detecting synthetic cannabinoid impairment. It was a lay opinion that improperly vouched for the reliability of Casey's statement.

### B. The Testimony Was an Improper Opinion on Credibility

The prosecution argues that Detective Leitze did not opine on Casey's credibility because he did not explicitly state that Casey was lying. This argument is unpersuasive.

When Casey testified that he was "scared and confused" and had smoked "spice" before the interview, he was directly challenging the reliability of his recorded statement. Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony that Casey did not appear to be under the influence was designed to undermine this defense. By testifying that Casey appeared normal, Detective Leitze was implicitly telling the jury that Casey's claim of intoxication was not credible.

In *Smith v. State*, 747 N.E.2d 598 (Ind. Ct. App. 2001), this Court held that witnesses cannot opine on the credibility of another witness. Detective Leitze's testimony violated this principle because it was specifically designed to persuade the jury that Casey's explanation for his statement was not believable.

### C. The Testimony Was Improperlay Opinion

Even if Detective Leitze's testimony was relevant, it was improperly admitted as lay opinion. Under Rule 701, a lay witness may testify only if the testimony is rationally based on the witness's perception and helpful to a clear understanding of the witness's testimony.

Detective Leitze's opinion that Casey did not appear to be under the influence was not based on his perception of specific, observable signs of impairment. It was a general conclusion that Casey appeared normal. This type of testimony does not assist the jury in understanding the facts; it merely provides an expert-like opinion that the jury is not qualified to evaluate.

## III. CUMULATIVE ERROR REQUIRES REVERSAL

The prosecution argues that cumulative error requires multiple substantive errors, each of which would require reversal on its own. We disagree.

Even if this Court were to find that one of the evidentiary rulings was not reversible error, the combined effect of the errors below denied Casey a fair trial. The prosecution's case depended entirely on the jury believing Casey's recorded statement. By admitting that statement over defense objection, the trial court allowed the prosecution to avoid the difficult task of proving possession through circumstantial evidence.

Then, by admitting Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony, the trial court allowed the prosecution to undercut Casey's explanation for why he made the statement. This one-two punch effectively prevented the jury from considering Casey's defense in any meaningful way.

In *Morton v. State*, 884 N.E.2d 554 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008), this Court recognized that the cumulative effect of errors can deny a defendant a fair trial even when individual errors might not require reversal. Here, the errors combined to deprive Casey of his right to present a complete defense.

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm based on its characterization of the evidentiary rulings as proper exercises of discretion. However, the record demonstrates that the trial court abused its discretion in two critical respects.

First, the trial court should have excluded Casey's recorded statement because it was the product of an impaired mind and its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The statement included irrelevant and inflammatory evidence about Casey firing the shotgun, and it allowed the prosecution to prove an essential element of the crime without relying on circumstantial evidence.

Second, the trial court should have excluded Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony because it required specialized knowledge to evaluate and improperly vouched for the credibility of the prosecution's evidence. By admitting this testimony, the trial court allowed the prosecution to impermissibly bolster its case at the expense of Casey's defense.

The combined effect of these errors denied Casey a fair trial. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for a new trial.

**FINAL RULING**

**Standard of Review**

The admission or exclusion of evidence is within the sound discretion of the trial court. On appeal, this Court reviews such evidentiary rulings only for an abuse of discretion. A trial court abuses its discretion if its decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances before the court, or if the court misinterprets the law. *Carter v. State*, 963 N.E.2d 712, 718 (Ind. 2013).

**Admissibility of Casey’s Recorded Statement**

Casey renews his argument that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting his recorded statement to police. He contends the statement should have been excluded under Indiana Evidence Rule 403 and due process principles because it was unreliable due to his alleged intoxication from smoking "spice" and was unnecessary to prove possession. Specifically, the defense argues that the State could have proven possession through Casey's proximity to the firearm, rendering the confession unfairly prejudicial.

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the statement. Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence may be excluded only if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. *Smith v. State*, 709 N.E.2d 922, 925 (Ind. 1999). The statement was highly probative of the central issue in the case: possession of a firearm by a serious violent felon. While the defense argues that the physical discovery of the shotgun and Casey's presence at the residence were sufficient to establish possession, mere proximity to a firearm does not automatically prove dominion and control. Casey’s admission of ownership provided the necessary link to establish the element of possession beyond mere presence.

The defense relies on *Moon v. State*, 923 N.E.2d 486 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), and *Lacy v. State*, 903 N.E.2d 1283 (Ind. 2009), to argue that the trial court has a duty to exclude involuntary or unreliable confessions. However, the facts provided do not indicate that the statement was involuntary due to police coercion or misconduct. Casey was informed of his Miranda rights and agreed to speak. His subjective feelings of being "scared and confused" and his desire to protect his girlfriend, Hamilton, are factors that affect the weight and credibility of the statement, not its admissibility. As held in *Smith*, 709 N.E.2d at 925, such factors go to the weight of the evidence rather than its admissibility. The defense's argument that Casey was under the influence of "spice" to the extent that he could not knowingly waive his rights is not supported by the record in a manner that would render the statement involuntary. Casey testified that he "googled" the gun because it was an antique, indicating cognitive function and awareness. The trial court properly determined that the probative value of the confession was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

**Admissibility of Detective Leitze’s Rebuttal Testimony**

Casey further argues that the trial court erred in admitting Detective Leitze’s rebuttal testimony, in which the detective opined that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of narcotics. Casey asserts this testimony was inadmissible lay opinion under Indiana Evidence Rule 701 because detecting impairment from synthetic cannabinoids requires specialized knowledge, and that it constituted an improper opinion on his credibility.

The trial court acted within its discretion in admitting this testimony. Evidence is not cumulative if it responds to specific claims made by the defense. *Brown v. State*, 929 N.E.2d 204, 210 (Ind. 2010). During his testimony, Casey specifically claimed that he was "scared and confused" and had smoked "spice" immediately prior to making his statement to police. This testimony directly attacked the reliability of the confession. Detective Leitze’s rebuttal testimony that Casey did not appear to be under the influence of drugs or alcohol was a direct and proper response to this specific claim.

The defense argues that Detective Leitze’s opinion required specialized knowledge to detect the influence of synthetic cannabinoids, citing *Flick v. State*, 970 N.E.2d 196 (Ind. 2012). However, Rule 701 permits a lay witness to offer opinions that are rationally based on the witness’s perception. Detective Leitze observed Casey during the interview and testified to his perception of Casey’s condition. Testifying that a suspect did not exhibit signs of impairment is a routine lay observation that does not require expert expertise. *Flick* supports the admission of lay testimony regarding a defendant's appearance and behavior; it does not mandate that every observation regarding a person who claims to have used synthetic drugs must be supported by an expert. Furthermore, the defense cites *Smith v. State*, 747 N.E.2d 598, 601 (Ind. Ct. App. 2001), for the proposition that witnesses cannot opine on credibility. Detective Leitze did not state that Casey was lying; he merely contradicted Casey’s claim of intoxication with his own observation. This was a permissible rebuttal to the defense’s theory of the case and not an improper vouching for credibility.

**Cumulative Error**

Finally, Casey argues that the cumulative effect of these errors denied him a fair trial, relying on *Morton v. State*, 884 N.E.2d 554 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008). However, because the trial court did not err in admitting either the recorded statement or the rebuttal testimony, there is no cumulative error to warrant reversal. *Taylor v. State*, 840 N.E.2d 324, 332 (Ind. 2006).

**Conclusion**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the challenged evidence. The probative value of the evidence was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, and the rebuttal testimony was properly admitted to address the defense’s claims of intoxication. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
