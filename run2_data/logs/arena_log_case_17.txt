### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Trial Court Erred in Denying Casey's Motion to Suppress the Search Warrant Because It Was Not Supported by Probable Cause

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that "no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation." U.S. Const. amend. IV. Similarly, Article 1, Section 11 of the Indiana Constitution guarantees the right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures, and warrantless searches are per se unreasonable unless they fall within a recognized exception. *See Taylor v. State*, 842 N.E.2d 827, 830 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006).

A search warrant must be supported by probable cause, which requires "a fair probability that evidence of a crime will be found in the particular place to be searched." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). The probable cause determination must be based on "the totality of the circumstances," including the informant's veracity, reliability, and basis of knowledge. *Id.* at 230-33. An anonymous tip, standing alone, is generally insufficient to establish probable cause because anonymous informants are inherently unreliable and their tips lack indicia of reliability. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000); *see also* *Morgan v. State*, 934 N.E.2d 762, 767 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010) (holding that an anonymous tip, without more, does not establish probable cause).

In this case, Detective Leitze obtained a search warrant for 48 North Euclid Avenue based solely on an anonymous phone call. The anonymous tipster did not provide any basis for their knowledge, and there is no evidence that the tipster was a citizen informant or had any known reliability. Detective Leitze did not corroborate the tip through any independent investigation before seeking the warrant. Under the totality of the circumstances, the anonymous tip was insufficient to establish probable cause to search 48 North Euclid Avenue.

The defense submits that the trial court erred in denying Casey's motion to suppress the evidence obtained pursuant to the invalid search warrant. The shotgun, ammunition, and spent cartridges found at 48 North Euclid Avenue should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *See Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); *see also* *Johnson v. State*, 835 N.E.2d 491, 498 (Ind. Ct. App. 2005) (holding that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment must be suppressed).

#### B. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting Casey's Statement Without Conducting a *Jackson v. Denno* Hearing

Even if the search warrant was valid, the trial court erred in admitting Casey's recorded statement without conducting a hearing to determine its voluntariness. The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the use of coerced confessions. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157, 167 (1986). Similarly, Article 1, Section 14 of the Indiana Constitution provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal cause to give testimony against himself." Ind. Const. art. 1, § 14.

When a defendant challenges the voluntariness of a confession, the trial court must conduct a hearing outside the presence of the jury to determine whether the confession was voluntarily given. *Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368, 376 (1964); *Bedwell v. State*, 481 N.E.2d 1369, 1375 (Ind. 1985). The burden is on the State to prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the confession was voluntary. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 702 (1975); *Bedford v. State*, 589 N.E.2d 250, 253 (Ind. 1992).

In this case, Casey testified at trial that he was "scared and confused" when he made the statement, that he had just smoked "spice," and that he did not want Hamilton to go to jail. This testimony raised a bona fide issue regarding the voluntariness of Casey's statement. The trial court, however, did not conduct a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing to determine whether Casey's statement was voluntarily given. Instead, the trial court admitted the statement over defense counsel's objection.

The defense submits that the trial court's failure to conduct a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing violated Casey's constitutional rights. The admission of an involuntary confession is structural error that requires reversal without regard to the harmless error doctrine. *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991); *Bedford v. State*, 589 N.E.2d at 254.

#### C. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting Evidence Under Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6

The State's opening argument contends that Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is inapplicable because Casey's statement was "direct proof of his possession" and not "other acts" evidence. This argument misconstrues the nature of the evidence and the applicable law.

Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) provides that evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is not admissible to prove a person's character in order to show that the person acted in conformity therewith. However, such evidence may be admissible for other purposes, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident. Ind. Evidence Rule 404(b).

Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 codifies the limitations on evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts. The statute provides that evidence of a person's conduct is not admissible unless the proponent gives reasonable notice in advance of trial and the court finds that the evidence is relevant to a material issue in the case and that its probative value outweighs its prejudicial effect. Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6.

The State's argument that "Evidence of the crime charged is not 'other acts' evidence" is a misstatement of the law. The statement at issue—Casey's alleged admission that he possessed the shotgun—is direct evidence of the charged offense. However, the manner in which the statement was obtained, and the circumstances surrounding it, involve separate and distinct issues that may constitute "other acts."

Specifically, Casey's statement that he "fired the shotgun two days before" the charged offense suggests that Casey committed an additional crime (illegal use of a firearm) that was not charged in this case. This is precisely the type of "other acts" evidence that Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 were designed to exclude.

In *Cutter v. State*, 763 N.E.2d 495, 501 (Ind. Ct. App. 2002), this Court held that evidence of the defendant's prior bad acts was inadmissible under Rule 404(b) because it was offered to prove the defendant's propensity to commit the charged offense. Similarly, in *Neal v. State*, 855 N.E.2d 710, 718 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006), this Court held that the trial court erred in admitting evidence of the defendant's prior drug transactions because the evidence was not relevant to any material issue in the case and was offered solely to prove the defendant's character.

In this case, the prosecutor asked Detective Leitze: "And he keeps referencing a Stevens, is that the same shotgun we saw in court today?" Detective Leitze answered: "That is the shotgun right in front of me." This questioning was designed to elicit testimony that Casey had fired the shotgun two days before the charged offense—an act that constitutes an entirely separate crime. The admission of this evidence violated Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6.

Moreover, even if the evidence was technically admissible, its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The evidence that Casey fired the shotgun suggested that he was a dangerous individual willing to commit violent crimes, which inflamed the jury's passions and predisposed them to convict. Under Indiana Evidence Rule 403, such evidence should have been excluded.

#### D. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting Rebuttal Testimony That Contradicted Casey's *Miranda* Rights

The State's opening argument contends that Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony was proper to rebut Casey's claim that his statement was false due to fear and confusion. However, this argument ignores the fact that Casey's *Miranda* rights prohibited the State from using his statement against him unless the State proved that Casey voluntarily waived his rights.

In *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 479 (1966), the United States Supreme Court held that statements made by a defendant during custodial interrogation are inadmissible unless the State proves that the defendant was informed of his right to remain silent and to have an attorney present, and that the defendant voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently waived those rights. Once a defendant invokes his right to remain silent, the police must cease interrogation. *Id.* at 474.

In this case, Casey testified that he was "scared and confused" when he made the statement, that he had just smoked "spice," and that he did not want Hamilton to go to jail. This testimony did not constitute an invocation of Casey's *Miranda* rights, but it did raise questions about the voluntariness of the waiver and the statement itself.

Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony—that Casey mentioned the shotgun "unprompted" and did not seem under the influence of spice—was designed to bolster the credibility of Casey's statement and to undermine Casey's defense. However, this testimony improperly vouched for the statement's reliability and effectively shifted the burden to Casey to prove that his statement was involuntary.

The defense submits that the trial court erred in admitting Detective Leitze's rebuttal testimony because it improperly circumvented Casey's *Miranda* rights and allowed the State to benefit from a statement that may have been involuntarily given.

#### E. The Cumulative Effect of the Errors Denied Casey a Fair Trial

Even if this Court finds that any single error was harmless, the cumulative effect of the errors denied Casey a fair trial. Under the cumulative error doctrine, the cumulative effect of multiple errors can constitute reversible error even if each individual error would alone be considered harmless. *See Henderson v. State*, 935 N.E.2d 149, 159 (Ind. 2010); *see also* *Taylor v. State*, 840 N.E.2d 324, 337 (Ind. 2006).

In this case, the errors were numerous and prejudicial: (1) the admission of evidence obtained from an invalid search warrant; (2) the admission of a potentially involuntary confession without a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing; (3) the admission of "other acts" evidence suggesting that Casey fired the shotgun; and (4) the admission of improper rebuttal testimony.

These errors, individually and collectively, undermined the fundamental fairness of Casey's trial. The jury was presented with a shotgun that may have been illegally seized, a statement that may have been involuntarily given, and testimony that improperly suggested Casey had committed other crimes. Under these circumstances, the defense submits that Casey is entitled to a new trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the State and the Defense, as well as the provided facts and procedural history. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion in admitting Casey’s recorded statement to police and the evidence derived from the search of 48 North Euclid Avenue. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that the Defense has demonstrated reversible error regarding the admission of the evidence, necessitating a reversal of the conviction.

**I. Search Warrant Validity**

The Defense argues that the search warrant for 48 North Euclid Avenue was unsupported by probable cause because it was based solely on an anonymous tip lacking corroboration. The State’s opening argument does not address the validity of the search warrant, focusing instead on the admissibility of the statement under evidentiary rules.

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article 1, Section 11 of the Indiana Constitution require that a search warrant be supported by probable cause. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Probable cause exists when there is a "fair probability that evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place." *Id.* Under the totality of the circumstances test, an anonymous tip must possess indicia of reliability, which typically requires police corroboration of its details. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000); *Morgan v. State*, 934 N.E.2d 762, 767 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010).

The facts provided indicate that Detective Leitze received an anonymous phone call and subsequently obtained a search warrant. The Defense asserts, and the record provided does not contradict, that this tip was the sole basis for the warrant and that no independent police work corroborated the tipster’s information. As held in *Morgan v. State*, 934 N.E.2d at 767, an anonymous tip, without more, is insufficient to establish probable cause. Because the State has failed to argue that the tip was corroborated or that the informant had a known basis of knowledge, the Court must conclude that the trial court erred in denying the motion to suppress. Consequently, the shotgun and ammunition seized pursuant to this warrant should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963).

**II. Voluntariness of Confession and *Jackson v. Denno* Hearing**

The Defense contends that the trial court erred in admitting Casey’s statement without conducting a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing to determine its voluntariness. The State argues that the statement was admissible as direct evidence of the crime but does not address the procedural requirement for a voluntariness hearing.

Under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, a defendant’s confession must be voluntary to be admissible. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157, 167 (1986). When a defendant challenges the voluntariness of a confession, the trial court is required to conduct a hearing outside the presence of the jury to resolve the issue. *Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368, 376 (1964); *Bedwell v. State*, 481 N.E.2d 1369, 1375 (Ind. 1985). The burden rests on the State to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the confession was voluntary. *Bedford v. State*, 589 N.E.2d 250, 253 (Ind. 1992).

The facts show that Casey testified at trial that he was "scared and confused," had smoked "spice," and made the statement to protect his girlfriend. This testimony raised a bona fide dispute regarding the voluntariness of the waiver of his *Miranda* rights and the statement itself. The trial court admitted the statement over objection but did not hold a hearing to determine voluntariness. The State’s failure to address this procedural deficiency is fatal. The admission of the confession without the requisite *Jackson v. Denno* hearing constituted structural error. *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991).

**III. Admissibility Under Rule 404(b) and Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6**

The Defense argues that Casey’s statement regarding firing the shotgun constituted inadmissible "other acts" evidence under Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6. The State counters that the statement was direct evidence of the charged offense—unlawful possession—and therefore not subject to Rule 404(b).

Evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is not admissible to prove a person’s character in order to show action in conformity therewith. Ind. Evidence Rule 404(b). However, "Evidence of the crime charged is not 'other acts' evidence under Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b) and thus not subject to the limitations of that rule." *Jones v. State*, 886 N.E.2d 626, 630 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008).

The Court agrees with the State on this limited point. Casey’s statement that he possessed and fired the shotgun was direct evidence of the charged offense of unlawful possession of a firearm. While the mention of firing the weapon may reference an uncharged act, it is inextricably linked to the admission of possession, the crime for which he was charged. Therefore, the trial court did not err in determining that the statement was not excluded under Rule 404(b) or Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 on the basis of being "other acts" evidence.

**IV. Rebuttal Testimony and Cumulative Evidence**

The Defense argues that the State’s rebuttal testimony, in which Detective Leitze contradicted Casey’s claims of fear and intoxication, violated Casey’s *Miranda* rights and was cumulative. The State argues the rebuttal was proper to counter Casey’s credibility defense.

Evidence is not cumulative when it is offered to rebut a specific defense raised by the defendant. *Hensley v. State*, 795 N.E.2d 1053, 1059 (Ind. Ct. App. 2003). By testifying that he was scared, confused, and under the influence of spice, Casey attacked the voluntariness and reliability of his confession. The State was entitled to rebut this theory with Detective Leitze’s testimony that Casey appeared coherent and mentioned the shotgun unprompted. This testimony did not violate *Miranda* but rather addressed the credibility of the confession’s content. The trial court acted within its discretion in admitting this rebuttal testimony.

**CONCLUSION**

While the State correctly argues that the statement was admissible under Rule 404(b) and that the rebuttal testimony was proper, the Defense has demonstrated that the search warrant was invalid due to a lack of probable cause and that the trial court failed to conduct a mandatory *Jackson v. Denno* hearing regarding the voluntariness of the confession. These constitutional and procedural errors require reversal.

**PRELIMINARY ORDER**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court GRANTS the Defense’s request for relief. The judgment of the trial court is REVERSED, and the case is REMANDED for a new trial consistent with this ruling.

J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000). As held by the Indiana Court of Appeals in *Morgan v. State*, 934 N.E.2d 762, 767 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), an anonymous tip, without more, is insufficient to establish probable cause.

The record provided indicates that the tip was the sole basis for the warrant and that there was no independent police corroboration. Because the State has failed to rebut the Defense’s assertion that the warrant lacked probable cause, the Court adheres to its preliminary finding that the trial court erred in denying the motion to suppress. The shotgun and ammunition seized pursuant to this warrant should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963).

**II. Voluntariness of Confession and *Jackson v. Denno* Hearing**

The State’s rebuttal also fails to address the trial court’s failure to conduct a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing. The State argues that the statement was admissible as direct evidence but ignores the procedural prerequisite for admitting a confession when voluntariness is in dispute.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits the admission of involuntary confessions. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157, 167 (1986). When a defendant challenges the voluntariness of a confession, the trial court is required to conduct a hearing outside the presence of the jury to resolve the issue. *Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368, 376 (1964); *Bedwell v. State*, 481 N.E.2d 1369, 1375 (Ind. 1985). The burden rests on the State to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the confession was voluntary. *Bedford v. State*, 589 N.E.2d 250, 253 (Ind. 1992).

The facts show that Casey testified at trial that he was "scared and confused," had smoked "spice," and made the statement to protect his girlfriend. This testimony raised a bona fide dispute regarding the voluntariness of his waiver of *Miranda* rights and the statement itself. The trial court admitted the statement over objection but did not hold a hearing to determine voluntariness. The State’s silence on this issue in its rebuttal is fatal. The admission of the confession without the requisite *Jackson v. Denno* hearing constituted structural error. *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991).

**III. Admissibility Under Rule 404(b) and Rule 403**

The State argues that Casey’s statement was direct evidence of the charged offense and therefore not subject to Indiana Evidence Rule 404(b). The Defense counters that the portion of the statement referencing firing the weapon constituted inadmissible "other acts" evidence.

Evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is not admissible to prove a person’s character in order to show action in conformity therewith. Ind. Evidence Rule 404(b). However, evidence of the crime charged is not "other acts" evidence. *Jones v. State*, 886 N.E.2d 626, 630 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008).

The Court agrees with the State that the admission of ownership was direct evidence of the charged offense. However, the Defense correctly points out that the reference to firing the shotgun was not necessary to prove the element of possession. While the preliminary ruling found the statement admissible as direct evidence, the Defense’s argument regarding the prejudicial nature of the "firing" comment has merit. Nevertheless, because the statement must be excluded due to the failure to hold a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing, the Court need not decide whether the admission of the "firing" comment constituted reversible error under Rule 404(b) or Rule 403 standing alone. The statement is inadmissible on constitutional grounds regardless of its characterization under the rules of evidence.

**IV. Rebuttal Testimony**

The State argues that the rebuttal testimony was proper to counter Casey’s claims of fear and intoxication. The Defense argues that this testimony was improper because it relied on the tainted statement and constituted improper opinion testimony.

Evidence is not cumulative when it is offered to rebut a specific defense raised by the defendant. *Hensley v. State*, 795 N.E.2d 1053, 1059 (Ind. Ct. App. 2003). However, because Casey’s statement was improperly admitted due to the lack of a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing, the State’s reliance on that statement to justify rebuttal testimony is misplaced. Furthermore, the Defense is correct that the detective’s testimony regarding Casey’s state of mind bordered on improper vouching for the State's case. Given that the primary evidence (the confession) was erroneously admitted, the rebuttal testimony derived from it cannot cure the error.

**CONCLUSION**

The State’s rebuttal fails to address the two dispositive issues in this case: the invalidity of the search warrant and the failure to conduct a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing. The State’s arguments regarding the characterization of the evidence under Rule 404(b) and the propriety of the rebuttal testimony are insufficient to overcome these constitutional and procedural deficiencies.

Because the State has not contested these findings, and because these errors are not rendered harmless by the evidentiary rulings the State seeks to defend, the judgment of the trial court is reversed.

**I. The Search Warrant Was Invalid**

The State’s closing argument does not challenge the finding that the search warrant for 48 North Euclid was unsupported by probable cause. The record reflects that Detective Leitze obtained the warrant based solely on an anonymous tip. The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant be supported by probable cause based on the totality of the circumstances. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). An anonymous tip, without more, is insufficient to establish probable cause. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 270 (2000); *Morgan v. State*, 934 N.E.2d 762, 767 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010).

The State’s silence on this issue constitutes a concession that the warrant was invalid. Consequently, the physical evidence seized pursuant to the warrant—the shotgun and ammunition—should have been suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). The State’s arguments regarding the admissibility of Casey’s statement under Rules 404(b) and 403 cannot cure this initial Fourth Amendment violation.

**II. The Failure to Conduct a *Jackson v. Denno* Hearing Was Structural Error**

The State argues that Casey’s statement was admissible as direct evidence of the crime charged. While this may be true regarding the *content* of the statement, the State ignores the procedural defect in its *admission*. The Due Process Clause prohibits the admission of involuntary confessions. *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157, 167 (1986).

At trial, Casey testified that he was "scared and confused," had smoked "spice," and made the statement to protect his girlfriend. This testimony raised a bona fide dispute regarding the voluntariness of his statement. When a defendant challenges the voluntariness of a confession, the trial court is required to conduct a hearing outside the presence of the jury to resolve the issue before the statement is admitted. *Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368, 376 (1964); *Bedwell v. State*, 481 N.E.2d 1369, 1375 (Ind. 1985). The burden rests on the State to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the confession was voluntary. *Bedford v. State*, 589 N.E.2d 250, 253 (Ind. 1992).

The trial court admitted the statement over objection but did not hold a hearing to determine voluntariness. The State’s rebuttal does not dispute this procedural failure. The admission of a confession without the requisite *Jackson v. Denno* hearing constitutes structural error, which requires automatic reversal. *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991). Therefore, the State’s reliance on *Jones v. State*, 886 N.E.2d 626 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008), to characterize the statement as direct evidence, and *Williams v. State*, 905 N.E.2d 590 (Ind. Ct. App. 2009), to defend the Rule 403 balancing, is misplaced. The statement was inadmissible on constitutional grounds regardless of its probative value or characterization under the rules of evidence.

**III. Evidentiary Arguments Are Moot**

The State devotes the majority of its rebuttal to arguing that the trial court properly admitted the evidence under Rules 404(b) and 403, and that Detective Leitze’s rebuttal testimony was proper under *Hensley v. State*, 795 N.E.2d 1053, 1059 (Ind. Ct. App. 2003). The Court acknowledges that evidence of the crime charged is not "other acts" evidence under Rule 404(b), and that rebuttal testimony is permissible to counter a specific defense.

However, these evidentiary determinations are moot because the underlying evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment and the statement was admitted in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment. The State cannot bootstrap the admissibility of evidence that was constitutionally infirm. The Defense correctly notes that the State’s attempt to divorce the constitutional issues from the evidentiary rulings is "fundamentally misguided." The trial court’s discretion to admit evidence is bounded by the Constitution; when that discretion is exercised in violation of constitutional rights, reversal is required.

**CONCLUSION**

The State’s rebuttal fails to address the dispositive constitutional and procedural errors in this case. The search warrant was invalid, and the trial court’s failure to conduct a *Jackson v. Denno* hearing constituted structural error. The State’s arguments regarding the application of Rules 404(b) and 403 are insufficient to overcome these foundational defects.

**FINAL ORDER**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court AFFIRMS the preliminary ruling in favor of the Defense. The judgment of the trial court is REVERSED, and the case is REMANDED for a new trial consistent with this ruling.
