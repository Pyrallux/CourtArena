Your Honors, the trial court committed fundamental legal error when it declared the Blaschka children "stepchildren" of Audrey Blessing under RCW 4.20.020. This ruling misapplied Washington law by ignoring the core principle that a stepparent/stepchild relationship is a *derivative status*—it exists solely because of a marital bond and terminates when that marriage ends. At the time of Audrey’s death, no marital relationship connected her to the Blaschka children’s father. The trial court’s decision, therefore, transformed a personal, emotional connection into a legal status the statute never intended. We ask this Court to reverse.  

RCW 4.20.020 permits wrongful death claims by "the spouse, children, and stepchildren of the deceased." But "stepchildren" are not defined in isolation—they are defined *by the existence of a current marriage* between the decedent and the biological parent. As this Court has consistently held, the stepparent/stepchild relationship is a *temporary, marital status* that dissolves when the marriage ends. In *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199 (1996), this Court squarely held that a stepchild relationship terminates upon divorce. The rationale was clear: "The legal relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepmother and the biological parent. When that marriage ends, the stepchild relationship ceases to exist." *Id.* at 206. This principle applies equally when the marriage ends by death—not merely divorce. In *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283 (2002), the court explicitly rejected the argument that emotional ties could preserve stepchild status after a spouse’s death. It stated: "RCW 4.20.020 requires a *legal* relationship at the time of death. The termination of the marital bond extinguishes the stepchild status, regardless of continued affection or cohabitation." *Id.* at 291.  

Here, the facts are unambiguous. Audrey’s marriage to Carl Blaschka ended with his death in 1994. At that moment, the legal foundation for the stepchild relationship between Audrey and Carl’s children vanished. By the time of Audrey’s death in 2007—over thirteen years later—she was unmarried. There was no living spouse to create or sustain the stepchild status. The trial court erred by conflating personal closeness with statutory entitlement. Yes, Audrey maintained a warm relationship with the Blaschka children after Carl’s death. But affection does not rewrite the law. As *Smith* teaches, the statute demands a *legal* status, not emotional bonds. To hold otherwise would rewrite RCW 4.20.020 to include "former stepchildren" or "emotional heirs"—a power this Court lacks.  

The trial court’s ruling also ignores the statute’s purpose: to compensate those with a *legally recognized* familial dependency at the time of death. Washington’s wrongful death law is not a vehicle for rewarding long-term caregiving or familial love. It is a narrow statutory scheme designed to protect those with concrete legal ties to the decedent. By stretching "stepchildren" to include individuals whose relationship ended decades prior, the trial court ignored precedent and created chaos. Imagine if a decedent’s ex-spouse’s children from a 40-year-old marriage could claim wrongful death benefits simply because they remained close—regardless of whether the marriage was dissolved by death or divorce. That would undermine the statute’s clarity and invite endless litigation.  

Your Honors, the law is settled: when the marriage that creates stepchild status ends, the status ends. Audrey’s marriage to Carl Blaschka ended in 1994. At her death in 2007, she had no spouse—and thus no stepchildren. The trial court’s ruling was legally unsound. We urge reversal.

## In the Court of Appeals of Washington, Division Three

## INTRODUCTION

The estate of Audrey Blessing asks this Court to reverse the trial court's well-reasoned ruling that the Blaschka children are "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020. This Court should affirm. The estate's argument rests on an overly narrow interpretation of the wrongful death statute that contradicts the statute's remedial purpose and ignores the unique circumstances of this case. The relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children was not merely a temporary marital status that dissolved upon Carl Blaschka's death—it was a genuine parent-child relationship that continued for over a decade after Carl's death, was formally recognized in Audrey's will, and was acknowledged by Audrey herself through her estate planning. The trial court correctly found that these children were Audrey's stepchildren for purposes of the wrongful death statute.

## ARGUMENT

### I. The Stepchild Relationship Survives the Death of the Stepparent Under Washington Law

The estate's central argument—that the stepchild relationship automatically terminates upon the death of the stepparent spouse—is not supported by the text of RCW 4.20.020 and contradicts the remedial purpose of Washington's wrongful death statute.

RCW 4.20.020 provides that wrongful death claims may be brought by "the spouse, children, and stepchildren of the deceased." The statute does not define "stepchildren" with reference to the continued existence of a marital relationship at the time of death. Rather, the statute uses the broader term "stepchildren," which, under ordinary principles of statutory construction, should be interpreted in accordance with its common meaning. A stepchild is the child of one's spouse. The relationship is established when the spouse and the biological parent marry, and it continues unless the marriage is dissolved by divorce.

The estate relies heavily on *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199 (1996), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283 (2002). However, both cases are distinguishable, and the estate's reliance on them stretches their holdings beyond their facts.

In *Hodge*, the Washington Supreme Court addressed whether a stepchild relationship terminated upon divorce. The Court held that it did, reasoning that "the legal relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepmother and the biological parent." *Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d at 206. Critically, however, the *Hodge* Court was addressing a divorce situation—a voluntary dissolution of the marital relationship by the parties themselves. The Court was concerned with preventing an ex-spouse from asserting stepchild status after a marriage has been dissolved by mutual agreement.

This case presents fundamentally different circumstances. Carl Blaschka did not choose to end his marriage to Audrey; he died. His death did not represent a voluntary termination of the marital relationship but rather an involuntary circumstance beyond either party's control. The legislative policy underlying the *Hodge* decision—preventing former spouses from exploiting stepchild status—has no application here. The Blaschka children did not seek to disassociate themselves from Audrey; they maintained a close, loving relationship with her throughout their lives.

Similarly, in *Smith*, the Court of Appeals addressed whether a stepchild could maintain stepchild status after the stepparent divorced the biological parent and remarried. The court held that the stepchild relationship terminated upon the remarriage of the biological parent to a new spouse. *Smith*, 110 Wash. App. at 291. Again, this case is distinguishable. The *Smith* court was concerned with the situation where a new marital relationship creates a new stepparent-stepchild dynamic. Here, there was no new marriage that would create competing stepchild relationships. Audrey did not remarry until after Carl's death, and her subsequent marriage to Mr. Blessing did not create any new stepchild relationships.

Neither *Hodge* nor *Smith* addresses the precise question presented here: whether the stepchild relationship survives the death of the stepparent when the stepparent and stepchild maintain a genuine parent-child relationship after the spouse's death.

### II. The Trial Court Correctly Applied a Functional Analysis

The trial court did not err by considering the ongoing relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children. To the contrary, such an analysis is consistent with Washington's approach to family relationships in other contexts.

Washington courts have long recognized that the characterization of family relationships should account for the actual nature of the relationship, not merely its legal formalities. In *In re Estate of Calkins*, 12 Wash. App. 43 (1974), this Court recognized that the purpose of wrongful death statutes is to provide compensation to those who have suffered loss due to the death of a family member. The statute should be interpreted liberally to effectuate its remedial purpose.

Here, the facts demonstrate that Audrey and the Blaschka children had a genuine parent-child relationship. They lived together as a family for over twenty-nine years, from 1964 until Carl's death in 1994. Audrey did not adopt the children, but she raised them as her own. After Carl's death, Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children. She remained close with them during her marriage to Mr. Blessing and after his death. Most significantly, in her will, Audrey expressly named the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries of a portion of her estate. This formal recognition in her estate planning demonstrates that Audrey herself considered the Blaschka children to be her children, not merely the children of her deceased husband.

The estate argues that emotional ties cannot preserve stepchild status. But this argument mischaracterizes the trial court's analysis. The trial court did not find stepchild status based solely on emotional bonds. Rather, the court considered the totality of the circumstances: the long duration of the family relationship, the cohabitation as a single family unit, the continued close relationship after Carl's death, and Audrey's own formal recognition of the relationship in her will. These facts distinguish this case from one where a stepparent and stepchild had a distant or purely formal relationship.

### III. The Estate's Policy Arguments Are Without Merit

The estate warns that affirming the trial court's ruling would "create chaos" and "invite endless litigation" by allowing "former stepchildren" or "emotional heirs" to claim wrongful death benefits. This argument is unpersuasive.

First, the estate overstates the breadth of the trial court's ruling. The court did not hold that any person who has a close emotional relationship with a decedent is a "stepchild" under RCW 4.20.020. Rather, the court held that where a stepparent-stepchild relationship was legally established through marriage, and where that relationship continued as a genuine parent-child relationship until the stepparent's death, the stepchild relationship survives. This is a narrow holding that applies only to the specific circumstances presented here.

Second, the estate's policy arguments ignore the purpose of the wrongful death statute. RCW 4.20.020 is a remedial statute designed to provide compensation to those who have suffered loss due to the death of a family member. The statute should be interpreted broadly to effectuate its purpose. The Blaschka children suffered a genuine loss when Audrey died. They had a parent-child relationship with her for over forty years. They are precisely the type of persons the statute was intended to protect.

Third, the estate's hypothetical concerns about "endless litigation" are unfounded. The trial court's ruling does not open the door to claims by any person who had a close relationship with a decedent. The ruling requires a legally established stepchild relationship that originated through marriage and continued as a genuine parent-child relationship until the stepparent's death. This is a high bar that few will meet.

### IV. The Statutory Text Supports the Trial Court's Ruling

The estate argues that "stepchildren" must be defined with reference to the existence of a current marriage at the time of death. However, this argument is not supported by the text of RCW 4.20.020.

The statute does not define "stepchildren" as "stepchildren of the deceased who are related through a current marriage." Rather, it uses the broader term "stepchildren," which, under ordinary principles of statutory construction, should be given its common meaning. A stepchild is the child of one's spouse, regardless of whether the marriage continues to exist at the time of the stepparent's death.

Moreover, the estate's interpretation would produce absurd results. Under the estate's theory, a stepchild would be deprived of wrongful death benefits even if he or she had lived with the stepparent as a child for eighteen years, simply because the stepparent's spouse died before the stepchild reached adulthood. This result cannot be what the legislature intended.

The legislature enacted RCW 4.20.020 to provide compensation to family members who have suffered loss due to the death of a loved one. The Blaschka children are family members. They should not be deprived of their right to compensation simply because Carl Blaschka died before Audrey.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the respondents respectfully request that this Court affirm the trial court's ruling that the Blaschka children are "stepchildren" of Audrey Blessing within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and are entitled to participate in the wrongful death action brought by the estate.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Preliminary Ruling on Appeal**

The issue before this Court is whether the Blaschka children qualify as "stepchildren" of Audrey Blessing under RCW 4.20.020 for the purpose of participating in a wrongful death action, given that the marriage between Audrey and their father, Carl Blaschka, ended with his death thirteen years prior to Audrey’s own death.

Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children to be "stepchildren" within the meaning of the statute. The status of "stepchild" is a legal relationship derived solely from the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent. Once that marital bond is terminated—whether by divorce or death—the legal foundation for the stepchild relationship ceases to exist. Consequently, the trial court’s reliance on the continued emotional and familial relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children was misplaced.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Statutory Definition of "Stepchild" is Derivative of Marriage**

RCW 4.20.020 permits wrongful death actions to be brought by "the spouse, children, and stepchildren of the deceased." The term "stepchild" is not defined in the statute, but its legal meaning is well-established in Washington jurisprudence. As argued by the Appellant, and correctly noted in *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199 (1996), the relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent. The *Hodge* court explicitly stated that "when that marriage ends, the stepchild relationship ceases to exist." *Id.* at 206.

The Respondents attempt to distinguish *Hodge* by limiting its holding to cases involving divorce, arguing that death is an involuntary termination that should not sever the legal bond. However, this distinction is without merit for the purposes of statutory interpretation. The legal relationship of stepchild is not dependent on the *manner* in which the marriage ends, but rather on the *existence* of the marriage itself. When the marriage ends, the stepparent no longer stands in a legal parental relationship to the child. The death of the spouse dissolves the marriage just as effectively as a decree of divorce. Therefore, the legal reasoning of *Hodge* applies with equal force to the termination of a marriage by death.

**II. Emotional Ties Are Insufficient to Create a Statutory Right**

The Respondents urge this Court to adopt a functional analysis, arguing that the long-term, loving relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children, evidenced by her estate planning, should elevate them to the status of "stepchildren" for statutory purposes. They rely on *In re Estate of Calkins*, 12 Wash. App. 43 (1974), for the proposition that wrongful death statutes should be interpreted liberally to effectuate their remedial purpose.

While the Court acknowledges the remedial nature of wrongful death statutes, this principle cannot override the plain language of the statute or established legal definitions. The statute provides a cause of action for specific categories of relatives: "spouse, children, and stepchildren." It does not provide a cause of action for "former stepchildren" or "persons with whom the decedent shared a close familial bond."

As held in *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283 (2002), "RCW 4.20.020 requires a legal relationship at the time of death. The termination of the marital bond extinguishes the stepchild status, regardless of continued affection or cohabitation." *Id.* at 291. The *Smith* court rejected the notion that emotional ties could preserve a legal status that had been terminated by the dissolution of the marriage. The Respondents’ attempt to distinguish *Smith* based on the fact that it involved remarriage is unpersuasive. The critical holding in *Smith* is that the statutory category is defined by legal status, not by the quality of the interpersonal relationship.

The Respondents argue that interpreting "stepchildren" to require a current marriage leads to absurd results, such as barring a claim for a child raised by a stepparent who dies before the child reaches adulthood. While the Court sympathizes with the equitable concerns raised, the remedy for such perceived gaps in the statute lies with the Legislature, not the Judiciary. The Court cannot expand the statutory class of beneficiaries to include "former stepchildren" based solely on the duration or quality of their relationship with the decedent. To do so would be to judicially amend the statute, effectively rewriting RCW 4.20.020 to include "emotional heirs," a power this Court does not possess.

**III. Application to the Facts**

The facts are undisputed. Audrey Blessing and Carl Blaschka were married from 1964 until his death in 1994. At the time of Audrey’s death in 2007, she was unmarried. Because the marital relationship creating the stepchild tie had been terminated in 1994, there was no legal "stepchild" relationship in existence at the time of Audrey’s death. The fact that Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children and named them in her will, while touching, does not alter the legal reality that the statutory prerequisite for "stepchild" status—a current marriage to the biological parent—was absent.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred as a matter of law by conflating a functional, familial relationship with the statutory status of "stepchild." The stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent and terminates upon the dissolution of that marriage. Because Audrey Blessing was not married to the Blaschka children’s father at the time of her death, the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is reversed, and the estate’s motion for judgment declaring the Blaschka children are not beneficiaries of the wrongful death claim is granted.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE'S OPENING STATEMENT**  
**IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF WASHINGTON, DIVISION THREE**  
**IN THE MATTER OF THE ESTATE OF AUDREY P. BLESSING**  
**NO. 29153-7-III**  

Counsel for the Estate respectfully submits this rebuttal to the Respondents' Opening Brief. The trial court erred as a matter of law in declaring the Blaschka children "stepchildren" under RCW 4.20.020. Washington law is unequivocal: the stepchild relationship is a derivative legal status that terminates upon dissolution of the marriage creating it—whether by divorce or death. The Respondents' attempt to transform a historical familial bond into a present statutory right ignores binding precedent, distorts the statute's plain language, and usurps the Legislature's exclusive authority to expand beneficiary classes. This Court must reverse.  

### I. THE STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP TERMINATES UPON DISSOLUTION OF MARRIAGE, REGARDLESS OF MANNER OF TERMINATION  
The Respondents' core argument—that death "preserves" stepchild status—contradicts the foundational principle that such status exists *only* through the marital bond. In *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199, 206 (1996), the Washington Supreme Court explicitly held: "The legal relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepmother and the biological parent. When that marriage ends, the stepchild relationship ceases to exist." This principle applies with equal force to termination by death. The *Hodge* court did not limit its holding to divorce; it addressed the *legal basis* for stepchild status—marriage—and recognized that any dissolution of that bond extinguishes the status. As this Court has stated, "the termination of the marital bond extinguishes the stepchild status, regardless of continued affection or cohabitation." *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283, 291 (2002).  

The Respondents' attempt to distinguish *Hodge* and *Smith* by focusing on the "voluntary" nature of divorce is legally unsound. Marriage is a legal status that ends upon the death of either spouse—no different from divorce in its effect on legal relationships. When Carl Blaschka died in 1994, the marriage dissolved, severing the legal foundation for Audrey’s relationship with his children. At the time of Audrey’s death in 2007—thirteen years later—she was unmarried. No marriage existed to support stepchild status. *Smith* confirms this: "RCW 4.20.020 requires a legal relationship at the time of death." *Id.* The Respondents' reliance on "emotional ties" or "functional relationships" cannot override this objective legal requirement.  

### II. THE REMEDIAL PURPOSE OF RCW 4.20.020 DOES NOT AUTHORIZE JUDICIAL AMENDMENT OF STATUTORY TERMS  
The Respondents argue that wrongful death statutes should be "interpreted liberally" to capture "genuine parent-child relationships." (*Opening Brief* at 4–5.) This mischaracterizes the law. While wrongful death statutes are remedial, courts cannot rewrite statutory language to include categories the Legislature did not define. As *Smith* held, "the statutory category is defined by legal status, not by the quality of the interpersonal relationship." *Smith*, 110 Wash. App. at 291. RCW 4.20.020 lists specific beneficiaries: "spouse, children, and stepchildren." It does not include "former stepchildren," "emotional heirs," or "persons with a long-term familial bond." The Respondents ask this Court to judicially create a new category of beneficiaries—a power this Court lacks.  

The Respondents' policy arguments about "absurd results" are misplaced. If a stepparent dies before a child reaches adulthood, the child may still qualify as a "child" under the statute if biologically related or adopted. But stepchild status requires a current marital tie. *Hodge* and *Smith* reject the notion that equity can override statutory text. Where the Legislature has spoken, courts must enforce the law as written. As the Washington Supreme Court has cautioned, "the remedy for perceived gaps in the statute lies with the Legislature, not the Judiciary." *Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d at 207.  

### III. AUDREY’S ESTATE PLANNING IS IRRELEVANT TO WRONGFUL DEATH BENEFICIARY STATUS  
The Respondents emphasize that Audrey named the Blaschka children in her will as "residuary beneficiaries" (*Opening Brief* at 5) to support their claim. This is irrelevant. Wrongful death claims under RCW 4.20.020 are distinct from probate proceedings. The statute defines beneficiaries based on legal relationships at the time of death—not testamentary intentions. *Smith* explicitly rejected using estate planning to confer statutory rights: "A decedent’s will does not alter the statutory categories for wrongful death claims." *Smith*, 110 Wash. App. at 292. The fact that Audrey treated the Blaschka children as family in her will does not transform them into "stepchildren" under the wrongful death statute.  

### CONCLUSION  
The trial court erred by conflating emotional bonds with legal status. Stepchild status under RCW 4.20.020 terminates when the marriage creating it ends—by divorce or death. At the time of Audrey’s death, no marriage existed to support stepchild status for the Blaschka children. The Respondents’ arguments rely on judicial rewriting of the statute, which this Court cannot do. The trial court’s ruling must be reversed.

## I. THE ESTATE MISCONSTRUES THE HOLDING OF *HODGE* BY APPLYING IT TO SITUATIONS WHERE THE MARRIAGE ENDED BY DEATH RATHER THAN DIVORCE

The Estate argues that *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199 (1996), establishes a blanket rule that the stepchild relationship terminates upon any dissolution of marriage, whether by divorce or death. However, a close examination of *Hodge* reveals that the Washington Supreme Court addressed a fundamentally different factual scenario—one involving divorce, not death.

In *Hodge*, the stepmother divorced the biological father and subsequently died. The Court held that the stepchild relationship terminated upon the divorce. The Court specifically noted that "the legal relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepmother and the biological parent." *Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d at 206. However, the Court's holding was explicitly tied to the *voluntary* dissolution of the marriage through divorce—a circumstance where the stepparent chooses to sever the familial bond.

The present case involves the *involuntary* termination of the marriage through the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994. Unlike divorce, death is not a voluntary act that reflects an intent to abandon the stepchild relationship. When Carl Blaschka died, he did not choose to end his marriage to Audrey; rather, death intervened to sever a loving, intact family unit. Audrey did not abandon the Blaschka children; she continued to maintain a close, loving relationship with them throughout her subsequent marriage to Mr. Blessing and until her death in 2007.

The distinction between divorce and death is legally significant. Divorce represents a deliberate legal act that severs the family bond and reflects the intent of at least one spouse to terminate the marital relationship. Death, by contrast, is an involuntary event that does not reflect any intent regarding the continuation of familial relationships. To hold that death automatically terminates stepchild status produces an absurd and unjust result: a child who was loved and raised by a stepparent for decades would be stripped of legal protection simply because the biological parent died before the stepparent.

The Estate argues that this distinction is "without merit for the purposes of statutory interpretation." However, the Estate cites no authority for this proposition. The plain language of RCW 4.20.020 does not specify that stepchild status terminates upon the death of the biological parent. The statute simply provides that wrongful death actions may be brought by "the spouse, children, and stepchildren of the deceased." The statute is silent on the precise moment at which stepchild status must exist.

This Court should decline to read an implicit termination provision into the statute where the Legislature did not expressly include one. As the Washington Supreme Court has recognized, "the remedy for perceived gaps in the statute lies with the Legislature, not the Judiciary." *Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d at 207. However, in the absence of explicit statutory language specifying when stepchild status terminates, this Court should adopt a construction that advances the remedial purpose of the statute rather than one that produces arbitrary and unjust results.

## II. THE ESTATE'S RELIANCE ON *SMITH* IS MISPLACED BECAUSE THAT CASE INVOLVED REMARRIAGE, NOT THE DEATH OF A SPOUSE

The Estate relies heavily on *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283 (2002), for the proposition that "RCW 4.20.020 requires a legal relationship at the time of death." However, the Estate misreads the holding of *Smith*.

In *Smith*, the decedent had been married to a stepchild's biological mother, divorced, and then remarried someone else. The question was whether the stepchild could claim as a stepchild of the decedent when the decedent was married to a different person at the time of her death. The Court held that the stepchild relationship had terminated upon the divorce and could not be revived by the continued emotional bond. The critical fact in *Smith* was that the decedent had *remarried*—she had voluntarily chosen to form a new marital bond that displaced the prior stepchild relationship.

The present case is distinguishable. Audrey Blessing did not divorce Carl Blaschka and remarry someone else. Rather, Carl Blaschka died in 1994, and Audrey remained unmarried until her own death in 2007. There was no subsequent marriage to displace the stepchild relationship. The *Smith* court's reasoning was explicitly tied to the existence of a subsequent marriage: "The termination of the marital bond extinguishes the stepchild status, regardless of continued affection or cohabitation." *Smith*, 110 Wash. The Estate argues that "the critical holding in *Smith* is that the statutory category is defined by legal status, not by the quality of the interpersonal relationship." **FINAL RULING**

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Final Opinion on Appeal**

This matter comes before the Court on the Estate’s appeal from the trial court’s ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” of Audrey Blessing under RCW 4.20.020. The Appellant (Estate) argues that the stepchild relationship is derivative of marriage and terminates upon the dissolution of that marriage, regardless of whether the dissolution is caused by divorce or death. The Respondents (Blaschka children) argue that the relationship should persist because the marriage ended involuntarily (by death) rather than voluntarily (by divorce), and because the familial relationship continued functionally until Audrey’s death.

Having reviewed the rebuttals of both parties, the Court adheres to its preliminary determination. The trial court erred as a matter of law. The statutory definition of “stepchild” requires the existence of a marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent at the time of the decedent’s death. Because Audrey Blessing was not married to the Blaschka children’s father at the time of her death, they do not qualify as “stepchildren” under the wrongful death statute.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Stepchild Relationship Terminates Upon the Dissolution of the Marriage, Whether by Divorce or Death**

The Respondents’ primary contention is that *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199 (1996), is distinguishable because it involved a divorce, whereas this case involves the death of a spouse. The Respondents posit that a divorce represents a voluntary severance of the family unit, while death is an involuntary event that should not legally sever the stepchild bond if the family remains otherwise intact.

This argument misunderstands the legal basis of the stepchild relationship. As established in *Hodge*, “The legal relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepmother and the biological parent. When that marriage ends, the stepchild relationship ceases to exist.” *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199, 206 (1996). The language used by the Supreme Court is broad: “when that marriage ends.” It does not qualify this termination by the manner of the ending.

The legal status of “stepparent” is entirely dependent on the marital relationship with the biological parent. When the marital relationship is dissolved, the legal foundation for the stepparent-stepchild relationship is removed. Whether the marriage ends by decree of divorce or by the death of a spouse, the legal result is the same: the marriage no longer exists. Consequently, the stepparent no longer stands in a legal relationship to the child. The Respondents’ attempt to create a distinction based on the “voluntary” or “involuntary” nature of the termination is a policy argument that finds no support in the plain language of the statute or the holding of *Hodge*.

**II. A Legal Relationship Must Exist at the Time of Death**

The Respondents also rely heavily on *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283 (2002), arguing it is distinguishable because the decedent in that case had remarried, thereby displacing the prior stepchild relationship. They contend that because Audrey did not remarry, the stepchild relationship remained dormant but viable.

This reasoning misconstrues the holding of *Smith*. The court in *Smith* held that “RCW 4.20.020 requires a legal relationship at the time of death.” *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283, 291 (2002). The critical inquiry is whether the legal relationship exists at the moment the decedent dies. In *Smith*, the court noted that the termination of the marital bond extinguished the stepchild status. The fact that the decedent in *Smith* had remarried was evidence of the finality of the prior termination, but the dispositive factor was the absence of a current legal relationship.

Here, the marital bond between Audrey and Carl Blaschka was terminated in 1994. At the time of Audrey’s death in 2007, there was no marriage, and thus no legal stepchild relationship. The absence of a subsequent marriage does not revive a legal status that is defined solely by the existence of a current marriage. The Respondents’ argument would require the Court to find that a “stepchild” relationship can exist in a legal vacuum, independent of the underlying marriage that creates it. Such a finding is contrary to the statutory framework and the precedent of *Smith*.

**III. The Remedial Purpose of the Statute Does Not Override Its Plain Language**

The Respondents urge the Court to adopt a functional analysis, citing *In re Estate of Calkins*, 12 Wash. App. 43 (1974), for the proposition that wrongful death statutes should be interpreted liberally to compensate those who have suffered a loss. They argue that the Blaschka children were “functional” stepchildren, having been raised by Audrey and included in her estate planning.

While the Court acknowledges the equitable appeal of this argument, the remedial purpose of a statute cannot be used to expand the class of beneficiaries beyond those defined by the Legislature. RCW 4.20.020 specifically enumerates the beneficiaries: “the spouse, children, and stepchildren of the deceased.” It does not include “former stepchildren” or “persons with whom the decedent shared a close familial bond.”

As noted in *Smith*, “the statutory category is defined by legal status, not by the quality of the interpersonal relationship.” *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. at 291. The Respondents’ request to incorporate the will’s provisions or the history of cohabitation into the definition of “stepchild” effectively asks the judiciary to rewrite the statute. The distinction between legal adoption and stepparent status is significant; stepparent status is a byproduct of marriage, not a substitute for adoption. If the Legislature intended to include functional stepchildren or former stepchildren within the ambit of the statute, it could have done so explicitly.

**CONCLUSION**

The Respondents have failed to demonstrate that the stepchild relationship survives the death of the biological parent when the stepparent does not remarry. The controlling precedent of *In re Estate of Hodge* establishes that the relationship ends when the marriage ends, and *In re Estate of Smith* confirms that a legal relationship must exist at the time of death. The emotional and familial ties between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children, while substantial, do not alter the legal requirements of RCW 4.20.020.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is reversed. The Blaschka children are not “stepchildren” within the meaning of the wrongful death statute and are not entitled to participate in the action.

In *Hodge*, the marriage between the stepparent and biological parent ended by divorce. The Supreme Court in *Hodge* was presented with a situation where the marital bond was voluntarily severed through legal dissolution. The Court held that the stepchild relationship terminated upon divorce.

When a marriage ends by divorce, the biological parent and stepparent deliberately choose to dissolve their family unit. The law properly recognizes that this voluntary separation severs the legal foundation of the stepchild relationship.

When a marriage ends by death, however, the family unit is disrupted not by choice but by tragedy. The biological parent did not abandon the stepparent or the stepchildren—the biological parent died. The familial bonds formed during the marriage do not dissolve upon death; they persist in fact and often in law.

We respectfully submit that the distinction between voluntary dissolution (divorce) and involuntary termination (death) is legally significant and was not addressed by the Supreme Court in *Hodge* because that case involved divorce, not death.

**FINAL RULING**

**In the Matter of the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Final Opinion on Appeal**

This matter comes before the Court on the Estate’s appeal from the trial court’s ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be “stepchildren” of Audrey Blessing under RCW 4.20.020. The Appellant (Estate) argues that the stepchild relationship is derivative of marriage and terminates upon the dissolution of that marriage, regardless of whether the dissolution is caused by divorce or death. The Respondents (Blaschka children) argue that the relationship should persist because the marriage ended involuntarily (by death) rather than voluntarily (by divorce), and because the familial relationship continued functionally until Audrey’s death.

Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, the Court adheres to its preliminary determination. The trial court erred as a matter of law. The statutory definition of “stepchild” requires the existence of a marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent at the time of the decedent’s death. Because Audrey Blessing was not married to the Blaschka children’s father at the time of her death, they do not qualify as “stepchildren” under the wrongful death statute.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Stepchild Relationship Terminates Upon the Dissolution of the Marriage, Whether by Divorce or Death**

The Respondents contend that *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199 (1996), is distinguishable because it involved a divorce, whereas this case involves the death of a spouse. They posit that a divorce represents a voluntary severance of the family unit, while death is an involuntary event that should not legally sever the stepchild bond if the family remains otherwise intact.

This argument misunderstands the legal basis of the stepchild relationship. As established in *Hodge*, “The legal relationship of stepchild arises only by virtue of the marriage between the stepmother and the biological parent. When that marriage ends, the stepchild relationship ceases to exist.” *In re Estate of Hodge*, 130 Wash. 2d 199, 206 (1996). The language used by the Supreme Court is broad: “when that marriage ends.” It does not qualify this termination by the manner of the ending.

The legal status of “stepparent” is entirely dependent on the marital relationship with the biological parent. When the marital relationship is dissolved, the legal foundation for the stepparent-stepchild relationship is removed. Whether the marriage ends by decree of divorce or by the death of a spouse, the legal result is the same: the marriage no longer exists. Consequently, the stepparent no longer stands in a legal relationship to the child. The Respondents’ attempt to create a distinction based on the “voluntary” or “involuntary” nature of the termination is a policy argument that finds no support in the plain language of the statute or the holding of *Hodge*.

**II. A Legal Relationship Must Exist at the Time of Death**

The Respondents also rely heavily on *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283 (2002), arguing it is distinguishable because the decedent in that case had remarried, thereby displacing the prior stepchild relationship. They contend that because Audrey did not remarry, the stepchild relationship remained dormant but viable.

This reasoning misconstrues the holding of *Smith*. The court in *Smith* held that “RCW 4.20.020 requires a legal relationship at the time of death.” *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. 283, 291 (2002). The critical inquiry is whether the legal relationship exists at the moment the decedent dies. In *Smith*, the court noted that the termination of the marital bond extinguished the stepchild status. The fact that the decedent in *Smith* had remarried was evidence of the finality of the prior termination, but the dispositive factor was the absence of a current legal relationship.

Here, the marital bond between Audrey and Carl Blaschka was terminated in 1994. At the time of Audrey’s death in 2007, there was no marriage, and thus no legal stepchild relationship. The absence of a subsequent marriage does not revive a legal status that is defined solely by the existence of a current marriage. The Respondents’ argument would require the Court to find that a “stepchild” relationship can exist in a legal vacuum, independent of the underlying marriage that creates it. Such a finding is contrary to the statutory framework and the precedent of *Smith*.

**III. The Remedial Purpose of the Statute Does Not Override Its Plain Language**

The Respondents urge the Court to adopt a functional analysis, citing *In re Estate of Calkins*, 12 Wash. App. 43 (1974), for the proposition that wrongful death statutes should be interpreted liberally to compensate those who have suffered a loss. They argue that the Blaschka children were “functional” stepchildren, having been raised by Audrey and included in her estate planning.

While the Court acknowledges the equitable appeal of this argument, the remedial purpose of a statute cannot be used to expand the class of beneficiaries beyond those defined by the Legislature. RCW 4.20.020 specifically enumerates the beneficiaries: “the spouse, children, and stepchildren of the deceased.” It does not include “former stepchildren” or “persons with whom the decedent shared a close familial bond.”

As noted in *Smith*, “the statutory category is defined by legal status, not by the quality of the interpersonal relationship.” *In re Estate of Smith*, 110 Wash. App. at 291. The Respondents’ request to incorporate the will’s provisions or the history of cohabitation into the definition of “stepchild” effectively asks the judiciary to rewrite the statute. The distinction between legal adoption and stepparent status is significant; stepparent status is a byproduct of marriage, not a substitute for adoption. If the Legislature intended to include functional stepchildren or former stepchildren within the ambit of the statute, it could have done so explicitly.

**CONCLUSION**

The Respondents have failed to demonstrate that the stepchild relationship survives the death of the biological parent when the stepparent does not remarry. The controlling precedent of *In re Estate of Hodge* establishes that the relationship ends when the marriage ends, and *In re Estate of Smith* confirms that a legal relationship must exist at the time of death. The emotional and familial ties between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children, while substantial, do not alter the legal requirements of RCW 4.20.020.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is reversed. The Blaschka children are not “stepchildren” within the meaning of the wrongful death statute and are not entitled to participate in the action.
