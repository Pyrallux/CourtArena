As this Court has held, malice may be implied from conduct showing a "reckless disregard for human life." *State v. Lang*, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). However, this standard requires that the defendant have some level of conscious awareness of the risk being created by his conduct. The defendant must be capable of understanding the nature and consequences of his actions. Where a defendant is unconscious or lacks the capacity to appreciate the risk his conduct creates, malice cannot be inferred. *See State v. The mere fact of intoxication does not establish malice; rather, the inquiry focuses on whether the defendant, despite his intoxication, was capable of conscious disregard of the risks his conduct created.

He attempted to offer testimony showing that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by the State of North Carolina from a decision of the Court of Appeals reversing three convictions for second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed "plain error" because there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. After reviewing the record and the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, we find that the Court of Appeals erred in its determination. The evidence presented at trial was sufficient to allow a reasonable jury to find malice implied from the defendant’s reckless disregard for human life. Therefore, we grant the State’s request to reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand for further consideration.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Legal Standard for Second-Degree Murder**

Murder in the second degree is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a requisite element, the crime requires an intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately causes death. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983).

**B. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

The State argues that the evidence was unequivocally sufficient to establish malice. We agree. The record shows that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, indicating severe intoxication. Prior to the fatal incident, the defendant engaged in violent, rowdy behavior at Smokey’s Lounge. After being ejected and suffering a blow to the head, the defendant staggered to his car, drove onto Highway 311, and passed another vehicle in a "no passing" zone. He subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road.

Under the principles established in State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), the act of driving while highly intoxicated and committing a dangerous traffic violation—passing in a no-passing zone—constitutes an intentional act showing a reckless disregard for human life. Such conduct is sufficient to support an inference of malice necessary for a second-degree murder conviction. The State correctly asserts that the Court of Appeals ignored this established legal framework by focusing on the lack of intent to kill rather than the reckless nature of the defendant's conduct.

**C. The Defense of Unconsciousness**

The defense contends that the defendant lacked the capacity to form malice due to unconsciousness resulting from the blow to the head and extreme intoxication, citing State v. Prevette, 317 N.C. 148, 345 S.E. 2d 159 (1986). The defense further argues that the trial court erred in excluding evidence regarding this unconsciousness.

While the defense of unconsciousness is valid if supported by evidence, State v. Odom, 316 N.C. 516, 341 S.E. 2d 693 (1986), the issue before this Court regarding the "plain error" determination by the Court of Appeals is whether *any* evidence of malice existed. The defendant's ability to operate a vehicle, navigate onto a highway, execute a passing maneuver, and flee the scene creates a factual dispute regarding his level of consciousness. The jury observed the defendant's conduct and could reasonably infer that he was conscious enough to act with reckless disregard. The Court of Appeals’ finding of "plain error" based on a total lack of evidence of malice disregards the jury's opportunity to weigh the defendant's actions against his claimed memory loss.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals committed legal error by overturning the convictions on the grounds that there was no evidence of malice. The defendant's conduct—driving while severely intoxicated, passing illegally, and causing a fatal collision—provides a sufficient basis for a rational jury to infer malice under State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The State’s arguments are persuasive that the evidence meets the legal standard for second-degree murder.

Accordingly, the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded to that court for consideration of the issues properly raised by the defendant.

The prosecution argues that driving while intoxicated and passing in a no-passing zone constitutes sufficient evidence of malice. However, the prosecution overreads *Wilkerson*. That case does not hold that any intoxicated driver who causes a fatal accident is guilty of second-degree murder. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

The standard for second-degree murder requires an unlawful killing with malice, but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The central inquiry is whether the defendant performed an intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately caused death. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983).

The State argues, and we agree, that the evidence presented was sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. The record indicates that the defendant, with a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, operated a motor vehicle on Highway 311. He passed another vehicle in a "no passing" zone and subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. These actions constitute intentional acts. Under State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), driving while severely intoxicated and committing dangerous traffic violations is sufficient to support an inference of malice.

The defense contends that the evidence was insufficient because the defendant lacked conscious awareness due to a blow to the head and extreme intoxication, distinguishing this case from State v. The presence of a head injury does not automatically negate the possibility of conscious, reckless action. The jury was free to infer that the defendant was conscious enough to act with reckless disregard for human life, despite his impairment. The Court of Appeals’ finding of "plain error" based on a total lack of evidence of malice disregarded the jury's role in weighing this evidence.

**B. The Defense of Unconsciousness**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by excluding evidence of unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on this defense, citing State v. Prevette, 317 N.C. 148, 345 S.E. 2d 159 (1986). The defense asserts that the combination of the head injury and intoxication supported this defense.

The exclusion of the unconsciousness defense, if error, is a matter for the Court of Appeals to consider on remand in light of the properly preserved issues, but it does not justify a finding that the State's evidence was legally insufficient to support the verdict.

**C. Plain Error Review**

The defense argues that the Court of Appeals properly invoked plain error review because the absence of evidence of malice was fundamental and obvious. The State argues that plain error review was inappropriate because the issue of malice was fully litigated and the evidence was sufficient.

Plain error review is reserved for errors that are "obvious" and affect "substantial rights." State v. Odom, 316 N.C. 516, 341 S.E. 2d 693 (1986). Because we have determined that the evidence was sufficient to support a conviction for second-degree murder, there was no "obvious" error in the trial court's submission of the case to the jury. This argument misstates the nature of the unconsciousness defense and improperly substitutes this Court for the jury.

In *State v. Prevette*, 317 N.C. 148, 345 S.E.2d 159 (1986), this Court held that when there is sufficient evidence of unconsciousness, the defense must be submitted to the jury. The defense presented evidence that the defendant suffered a head injury, was extremely intoxicated, and could not remember any events following the bar fight. This evidence was sufficient to require the trial court to permit the jury to consider the unconsciousness defense.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Odom*, 316 N.C. 516, 341 S.E.2d 693 (1986), is misplaced. In *Odom*, this Court addressed whether evidence of unconsciousness was sufficient to require jury instructions. The prosecution argues that the defendant's operation of his vehicle demonstrates conscious control. However, the defense does not claim the defendant was in a comatose state. The defense claims, and presented evidence, that the defendant may have been operating his vehicle in an automatonic or semiconscious state—the very issue that should have been submitted to the jury.

The prosecution's argument that "complex motor functions" prove consciousness ignores medical evidence and the testimony of the defendant himself. The defendant testified that he could not remember anything after being struck. Walden Ball's testimony that the defendant passed his car in a no-passing zone does not establish consciousness—it establishes only that the vehicle was in motion. The manner in which the vehicle was operated is consistent with automatonic behavior, and the jury should have been permitted to consider this defense.

## III. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY INVOKED PLAIN ERROR

The prosecution argues that plain error review was inappropriate because the issue of malice was "fully litigated." This argument ignores the Court of Appeals' proper function. Appellate courts have a duty to ensure that convictions rest on sufficient evidence. When the State fails to prove every element of a crime, including malice, the convictions cannot stand regardless of whether the defendant raised the issue.

This Court has held that plain error review may be invoked when "obvious" errors affect "substantial rights." *State v. Odom*, 316 N.C. 516, 341 S.E.2d 693 (1986). The absence of evidence of malice is exactly such an obvious error. The Court of Appeals did not improperly intrude on the jury's role—it properly applied the legal standard requiring evidence of malice.

The prosecution argues that "it is for the jury—not an appellate court—to determine whether malice is implied." This is true when there is evidence from which malice may be inferred. But when there is no evidence of malice, the trial court commits error by submitting the case to the jury, and the appellate court commits error by affirming the conviction. The Court of Appeals correctly identified this fundamental deficiency.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *STATE V. WILKERSON* IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites *State v. Wilkerson*, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), for the proposition that "when evidence of intoxication and reckless driving exists, it is for the jury to determine whether malice is implied." However, *Wilkerson* is distinguishable.

In *Wilkerson*, the defendant was driving at an excessive rate of speed, ran a stop sign, and struck another vehicle, killing the driver. This Court found sufficient evidence of malice because the defendant's conduct demonstrated a "reckless disregard for human life." However, the defendant in *Wilkerson* was aware of his surroundings and made conscious choices to speed and run the stop sign.

Here, the defendant may have been unconscious due to a head injury and extreme intoxication. The evidence does not establish that the defendant made conscious choices to drive recklessly. Rather, the evidence suggests the defendant may have been operating his vehicle without awareness of his actions. This distinction is critical. The prosecution has not established that the defendant acted with malice; at most, it has established that the defendant was intoxicated and that his vehicle struck another. This is insufficient for second-degree murder.

The prosecution has failed to prove that the defendant acted with malice. The defendant suffered a head injury, was extremely intoxicated, and cannot remember the events in question. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon review of the decision by the Court of Appeals reversing three convictions for second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed "plain error" because there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. Having considered the closing arguments of both the State and the defendant, we find that the Court of Appeals erred in its determination. The evidence presented at trial was sufficient to allow a reasonable jury to infer malice from the defendant’s reckless disregard for human life. Therefore, we reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

The standard for second-degree murder requires an unlawful killing with malice, but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a "reckless disregard for human life." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The central inquiry is whether the defendant performed an intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately caused death. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983).

The State argues, and we agree, that the evidence presented was sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. The record indicates that the defendant, with a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, operated a motor vehicle on Highway 311. He passed another vehicle in a "no passing" zone and subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. These actions constitute intentional acts. Under State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), driving while severely intoxicated and committing dangerous traffic violations is sufficient to support an inference of malice.

The defense contends that the evidence was insufficient because the defendant lacked conscious awareness due to a blow to the head and extreme intoxication, distinguishing this case from State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). While the defense correctly notes that the defendant suffered a head injury prior to driving, the physical evidence of the defendant's conduct—navigating the highway, executing a passing maneuver, and fleeing the scene—creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding the defendant's consciousness and intent for the jury to resolve. The presence of a head injury does not automatically negate the possibility of conscious, reckless action. The jury was free to infer that the defendant was conscious enough to act with reckless disregard for human life, despite his impairment. The Court of Appeals’ finding of "plain error" based on a total lack of evidence of malice disregarded the jury's role in weighing this evidence.

**B. The Defense of Unconsciousness**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by excluding evidence of unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on this defense, citing State v. Prevette, 317 N.C. 148, 345 S.E. 2d 159 (1986). The defense asserts that the combination of the head injury and intoxication supported this defense.

The State counters, relying on State v. Odom, 316 N.C. 516, 341 S.E. 2d 693 (1986), that the defendant's ability to perform complex motor functions, such as operating a vehicle and passing another car, is inconsistent with a state of total unconsciousness. We find the State's argument persuasive on this point. While the defendant claimed amnesia regarding the events following the bar fight, the testimony of Walden Ball described the defendant’s vehicle approaching with squealing tires and actively passing in a no-passing zone. This conduct is indicative of conscious operation of the vehicle.

The issue of whether the trial court erred in excluding the unconsciousness evidence is a separate matter from the sufficiency of the evidence of malice. The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions solely on the ground that no evidence of malice existed. As established in State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), the defendant's reckless driving itself provides evidence of malice. Therefore, the Court of Appeals’ rationale for reversal was flawed. The exclusion of the unconsciousness defense, if error, is a matter for the Court of Appeals to consider on remand in light of the properly preserved issues, but it does not justify a finding that the State's evidence was legally insufficient to support the verdict.

**C. Plain Error Review**

The defense argues that the Court of Appeals properly invoked plain error review because the absence of evidence of malice was fundamental and obvious. The State argues that plain error review was inappropriate because the issue of malice was fully litigated and the evidence was sufficient.

Plain error review is reserved for errors that are "obvious" and affect "substantial rights." State v. Odom, 316 N.C. 516, 341 S.E. 2d 693 (1986). Because we have determined that the evidence was sufficient to support a conviction for second-degree murder, there was no "obvious" error in the trial court's submission of the case to the jury. The Court of Appeals’ sua sponte reversal on the grounds of insufficient evidence constituted an improper intrusion into the jury's fact-finding function. As held in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978), where evidence of intoxication and reckless conduct exists, it is for the jury to determine whether malice is implied. The Court of Appeals erred by substituting its judgment for that of the jury.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals committed legal error by overturning the convictions on the grounds that there was no evidence of malice. The defendant's conduct—driving while severely intoxicated, passing illegally, and causing a fatal collision—provides a sufficient basis for a rational jury to infer malice under State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The defense's arguments regarding the head injury and unconsciousness go to the weight of the evidence and the propriety of jury instructions, but they do not render the State's evidence of malice legally insufficient.

Accordingly, the decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed, and the case is remanded to that court for consideration of the issues properly raised by the defendant regarding the exclusion of evidence and jury instructions.
