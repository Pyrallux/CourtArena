**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court violated the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment by declaring a mistrial during the defendant's first trial without "manifest necessity" and subsequently retrying the defendant. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial. The State’s explicit offer to exclude the surprise evidence constituted a reasonable alternative to a mistrial, and the defense counsel’s actions did not constitute consent to the mistrial or a waiver of the double jeopardy claim. Therefore, the prosecution’s request to reverse the convictions and remand with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice is granted.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Manifest Necessity**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from repeated prosecutions for the same offense. A mistrial declared without the defendant's consent is permissible only if there is a "manifest necessity" for such an action. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). This standard requires a "high degree of necessity" and is satisfied only when there is no reasonable alternative to declaring a mistrial. *Id.* at 506; see also *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971).

The record demonstrates that the trial court failed to explore reasonable alternatives before declaring a mistrial. The surprise testimony regarding the note and serial numbers undoubtedly created a challenging situation. However, the prosecutor explicitly stated on the record that the State "can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." While the prosecutor noted this was not the State's first priority, the offer was clear and unequivocal. Under *United States v. Jorn*, when "the problem can be cured by excluding the testimony or giving a cautionary instruction," a mistrial is not justified. 400 U.S. at 485.

The trial court did not rule on the prosecution's offer to exclude the evidence, nor did it attempt to provide curative instructions to the jury to mitigate any prejudice from the testimony. Instead, the court declared a mistrial immediately. The defense counsel had also indicated a preference to go forward with the trial. By ignoring the specific alternative offered by the prosecution and failing to consider whether the prejudice could be cured by jury instructions, the trial court failed to exercise the "sound discretion" required by *Arizona v. Washington*. 434 U.S. at 505–06. Consequently, manifest necessity did not exist.

**B. Consent and Waiver**

The defense argues that Dickinson's counsel implicitly consented to the mistrial, thereby waiving any double jeopardy objection. However, the facts do not support a finding of consent. A defendant may waive double jeopardy protections by consenting to a mistrial, either explicitly or implicitly. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 608 (1976). Consent is determined by examining the totality of the circumstances. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 675 (1982).

Here, Dickinson’s counsel consistently expressed a preference to proceed with the trial. He stated that he would not move to strike the surprise testimony because doing so would emphasize it, which is a strategic trial decision, not an invitation for a mistrial. Furthermore, counsel’s statement that "if it deemed a mistrial necessary, he would seek a dismissal with prejudice" is a conditional objection, not consent. It indicates that counsel anticipated the court might err and intended to preserve the double jeopardy issue for appeal. This is distinct from the conduct described in *United States v. Wallace*, 848 F.2d 1464, 1472 (9th Cir. 1988), where the defense's conduct indicated acceptance of the mistrial. Because defense counsel wanted to proceed and only conditioned future motions on the court's decision, there was no implicit consent to the mistrial.

**C. Procedural Default**

Finally, the defense argues that Dickinson’s failure to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice below constitutes a procedural default that bars relief. This argument is unpersuasive. While the defense counsel stated he would seek such a motion if a mistrial were declared, he failed to file one. However, the Double Jeopardy Clause protects a fundamental constitutional right that can be raised for the first time on appeal. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 191 (1957); *United States v. Tateo*, 377 U.S. 463, 465 (1964).

The failure to file a motion does not waive the jurisdictional bar against double jeopardy. The Supreme Court has held that a defendant may challenge a retrial on double jeopardy grounds even if they did not object to the mistrial at trial, provided they did not consent to it. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 191–92. Dickinson’s failure to file the motion does not preclude this Court from reviewing the manifest necessity of the mistrial.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial without manifest necessity, as a reasonable alternative—excluding the evidence—was available and offered by the prosecution. The defendant did not consent to the mistrial, and the double jeopardy claim is not barred by procedural default. Accordingly, the convictions and sentences are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.

The Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits a second trial following a mistrial declared without the defendant's consent unless there was a "manifest necessity" for the mistrial. *Arizona v. The State argues that the defense implicitly consented to the mistrial through its conduct, specifically by failing to move to strike the surprise testimony and by conditioning future motions on the court's decision. This argument is unpersuasive.

Consent to a mistrial, either express or implied, constitutes a waiver of double jeopardy protections. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 608 (1976). However, the determination of consent is based on the totality of the circumstances. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 675 (1982).

As the appellant notes, this conduct is distinct from the acceptance of a mistrial described in *United States v. Wallace*, 848 F.2d 1464, 1472 (9th Cir. 1988). Because the defense wanted to proceed and only reacted to the court's unilateral decision to declare a mistrial, there was no implied consent.

**C. Procedural Default**

Finally, the State argues that Dickinson procedurally defaulted his double jeopardy claim by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial and by participating in the second trial. Tateo*, 377 U.S. 463, 465 (1964). The Double Jeopardy Clause is a jurisdictional bar that may be raised at any time. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 191–92. Dickinson's failure to file a motion to dismiss below does not preclude this Court from reviewing the manifest necessity of the mistrial.

The trial court declared a mistrial without manifest necessity. The defense did not consent to the mistrial, and the double jeopardy claim is not barred by procedural default. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal following the conviction of Dickinson for theft and related charges. The issue on appeal is whether the trial court violated the Double Jeopardy Clause by declaring a mistrial during the first trial without "manifest necessity" and subsequently retrying the defendant. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties. Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. The trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial because a reasonable alternative—excluding the surprise evidence—was available and explicitly offered by the prosecution. Furthermore, the defense did not consent to the mistrial, and the double jeopardy claim is not barred by procedural default.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Manifest Necessity and the Availability of Alternatives**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment protects a criminal defendant from repeated prosecutions for the same offense. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505 (1978). A defendant who successfully moves for a mistrial consents to a second trial, but if the mistrial is declared over the defendant's objection, the prosecution may retry the defendant only if there was a "manifest necessity" for the mistrial. *Id.* at 505. The "manifest necessity" standard requires a "high degree of necessity" and is satisfied only when the trial court determines that no reasonable alternative to declaring a mistrial exists. *Id.* at 506.

The record demonstrates that the trial court failed to identify a "manifest necessity" before aborting the trial. During the first trial, the prosecution introduced surprise testimony regarding a note containing serial numbers that contradicted the defense's theory of the case. However, when the trial court expressed concern about proceeding, the prosecutor explicitly stated on the record that the State "can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." Although the prosecutor noted this was not the State's "first priority," the offer was unequivocal that the State was willing to continue the trial without the damaging testimony.

The Supreme Court has held that "where the problem can be cured by excluding the testimony or giving a cautionary instruction, a mistrial is not justified." *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971). Here, the prosecution itself offered the cure identified in *Jorn*—exclusion of the testimony. The trial court did not rule on this offer, nor did it attempt to give curative instructions to mitigate the prejudice. By failing to utilize the specific alternative offered by the prosecution, the trial court failed to exercise the "sound discretion" required by *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 505–06. The existence of the prosecution's offer to proceed without the evidence demonstrates that a "manifest necessity" did not exist.

**B. Consent and Waiver**

The State argues that the defense implicitly consented to the mistrial through its conduct. Consent to a mistrial, either express or implied, constitutes a waiver of double jeopardy protections. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 608 (1976). However, the determination of consent is based on the totality of the circumstances. *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667, 675 (1982).

The record shows that defense counsel consistently expressed a preference to proceed with the trial. Counsel's strategic decision not to move to strike the surprise testimony—to avoid emphasizing it—does not equate to acceptance of a mistrial. To the contrary, it indicates a desire to continue the trial despite the error. Furthermore, counsel's statement that he would seek a dismissal with prejudice "if" a mistrial were declared was a conditional objection intended to preserve the double jeopardy issue for appeal, not an invitation to stop the trial. Because the defense wanted to proceed and only reacted to the court's unilateral decision to declare a mistrial, there was no implied consent.

**C. Procedural Default**

Finally, the State argues that Dickinson procedurally defaulted his double jeopardy claim by failing to file a motion to dismiss with prejudice after the mistrial and by participating in the second trial. This argument is rejected. The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a fundamental constitutional right that is not subject to procedural default rules in the same manner as other claims. The Supreme Court has held that a defendant may challenge a retrial on double jeopardy grounds for the first time on appeal. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 191 (1957); *United States v. Tateo*, 377 U.S. 463, 465 (1964). The Double Jeopardy Clause is a jurisdictional bar that may be raised at any time. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 191–92. Dickinson's failure to file a motion to dismiss below does not preclude this Court from reviewing the manifest necessity of the mistrial.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial without manifest necessity. The prosecution's explicit offer to exclude the surprise evidence constituted a reasonable alternative that the trial court failed to utilize. The defense did not consent to the mistrial, and the double jeopardy claim is not barred by procedural default. Accordingly, the convictions and sentences are reversed, and the case is remanded with instructions to dismiss the charges with prejudice.
