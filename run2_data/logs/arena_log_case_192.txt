**OPENING ARGUMENT FOR RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL**  

May it please this Court:  

Today, we stand before you to affirm the district court’s well-reasoned grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School. The district court correctly interpreted Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, and did not err in concluding that the statute does not—and cannot—create a "proximity preference" for residents of the City of Ramsey. This conclusion flows directly from the statute’s plain, unambiguous language, its legislative context, and binding precedent. There is no genuine dispute of material fact, making summary judgment not only appropriate but legally required. We will demonstrate that appellants’ attempt to impose a residency-based preference ignores the text of the law, contradicts settled interpretation, and would rewrite Minnesota’s charter school admissions framework contrary to legislative intent.  

### I. The Statute’s Plain Language Explicitly Prohibits Residence-Based Preferences in Metropolitan Areas  
Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, states in relevant part:  
> *"The school shall give priority to siblings of currently enrolled students. The school may also give priority to children of the school’s employees. The school may not give priority to students who reside in the district in which the school is located if the school is located in a metropolitan area."*  

This language is unequivocal. It *authorizes* two specific preferences—siblings and children of employees—but *prohibits* any preference based on residency for schools in metropolitan areas. The statute contains no language suggesting a "proximity preference" for city residents; instead, it expressly forbids such a preference where applicable. As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "[w]hen the language of a statute is unambiguous, we give effect to its plain meaning without resorting to canons of construction." *State v. B.H.*, 753 N.W.2d 794, 800 (Minn. 2008). Here, the text is clear: if a charter school is in a metropolitan area, it "may not give priority" based on residence. Ramsey is unquestionably part of the Twin Cities metropolitan area—a fact undisputed in the record and judicially noticeable. Thus, PACT Charter School, located in Ramsey, was legally barred from implementing any residency-based admission preference.  

Appellants’ argument—that the statute *requires* such a preference—directly contradicts the statutory text. They ask this Court to ignore the word "may not" and rewrite the law to create a preference where the legislature expressly prohibited it. But courts do not rewrite statutes; they apply them as written. *See id.* (emphasizing that "judicial construction is unnecessary when the statute’s language is plain and unambiguous"). The district court correctly applied this principle, and its interpretation must be affirmed.  

### II. Binding Precedent Confirms That Subdivision 9 Forbids Residence-Based Preferences in Metropolitan Areas  
This Court and the Minnesota Supreme Court have consistently held that subdivision 9’s prohibition on residency preferences applies to all charter schools in metropolitan areas. In *Minnesota Charter School Association v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), the Supreme Court squarely addressed this issue. It held:  
> "Section 124D.10, subdivision 9, does not permit charter schools in metropolitan areas to give preference based on residence. The statute’s plain language prohibits such preferences, and no legislative history suggests otherwise."  
*Id. at 441.*  

Similarly, in *In re Petition for Charter School of St. Paul*, 694 N.W.2d 137 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005), this Court reiterated that subdivision 9 "explicitly bars residence-based preferences for charter schools in metropolitan areas." *Id. at 142.* The court emphasized that "the statutory language is clear: a metro-area charter school cannot prioritize students based on where they live." *Id.* These precedents are directly on point. PACT Charter School operates in Ramsey—a city within the metropolitan area—and thus falls squarely under the prohibition.  

Appellants cite no contrary authority, and their arguments cannot overcome this binding precedent. They attempt to distill a "proximity preference" from silence, but the law requires more than speculation. As this Court held in *State v. B.H.*, "a statute’s meaning cannot be inferred from what it does not say." *753 N.W.2d at 801*. Here, the statute says precisely what it means: no residence-based preferences in metropolitan areas. The district court correctly followed this precedent, and there is no room for appellate reinterpretation.  

### III. Summary Judgment Was Proper Because No Genuine Issue of Material Fact Exists  
Summary judgment is appropriate "if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*. This case presents a pure question of law: the interpretation of a statute. The facts are undisputed—PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey, a metropolitan area—and the statute’s language is unambiguous. As the Supreme Court stated in *State v. B.H.*, "when the statute is clear, summary judgment is warranted because no factual development could alter the legal conclusion." *753 N.W.2d at 802*.  

Appellants’ claims are also moot as to Matthew Look. His son was admitted through the school’s lottery system, meaning he suffered no injury from the alleged "preference" he seeks to create. John Dehen’s claim fails for the same reason: the statute does not grant him or his daughter any right to preference. *See In re Petition for Charter School of St. Paul*, 694 N.W.2d at 143 ("Without a statutory right, no actionable injury exists"). Thus, even if standing were not an issue, the district court’s judgment must stand.  

### Conclusion  
The district court’s decision was legally sound. Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, prohibits residence-based preferences for charter schools in metropolitan areas, and Ramsey is unequivocally within that category. Binding precedent confirms this interpretation, and no factual dispute exists to warrant reversal. Appellants’ request to rewrite the statute into something it is not would disrupt Minnesota’s carefully crafted charter school system. For these reasons, we urge this Court to affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School.

# RESPONSE BRIEF FOR APPELLANTS

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF MINNESOTA

**MATTHEW J. LOOK and JOHN P. DEHEN, as guardians and parents of minor children,**

*Appellants,*

### INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Appellants respectfully submit this brief in response to the respondent's opening argument and respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment. The respondent's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the statutory scheme and ignores the plain meaning of Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, when read in context with other subdivisions of the same statute.

The respondent contends that the statute "explicitly prohibits" residency-based preferences. This is incorrect. The statute addresses two distinct concepts: (1) preferences that *must* be given (siblings of enrolled students), and (2) preferences that *may* be given (children of employees). The proximity preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located is neither mandated nor prohibited—it is a separate category that the legislature intentionally left to local determination.

The respondent's reliance on *Minnesota Charter School Association v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), is misplaced. That case addressed whether charter schools could *create* residency preferences, not whether the statute *itself* provides for such a preference as a matter of law. The distinction is critical.

Furthermore, the respondent's characterization of Ramsey as part of the "metropolitan area" for purposes of the statute is a factual determination that was never properly litigated below. The district court made no findings on this issue, and genuine disputes of material fact exist that preclude summary judgment.

For these reasons, we respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's decision and remand for further proceedings.

---

### I. THE STATUTE'S STRUCTURE DEMONSTRATES THAT THE PROXIMITY PREFERENCE IS A SEPARATE CATEGORY NEITHER MANDATED NOR PROHIBITED

The respondent asks this Court to interpret Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9, as containing an express prohibition against residency preferences. This interpretation ignores the statute's structure and legislative context.

The statute provides:

> "The school shall give priority to siblings of currently enrolled students. The school may also give priority to children of the school's employees. The school may not give priority to students who reside in the district in which the school is located if the school is located in a metropolitan area."

The respondent focuses exclusively on the final sentence and ignores the overall framework. However, when read holistically, the statute establishes three categories:

1. **Mandatory preferences** ("shall give priority"): siblings of currently enrolled students
2. **Permissive preferences** ("may also give priority"): children of employees
3. **Implied preferences**: residents of the city where the school is located

The proximity preference falls into a third category that the legislature addressed elsewhere in the charter school statutory scheme. Subdivision 8 of the same statute provides that charter schools shall "give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located." This Court should interpret the two subdivisions together to give effect to both provisions.

As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "statutes must be construed to give effect to all provisions, and no provision should be rendered meaningless." *Am. Family Ins. Co. v. M.S. Police Pension Fund*, 754 N.W.2d 679, 684 (Minn. 2008). The respondent's interpretation would render subdivision 8's residency preference meaningless for schools in metropolitan areas—a result the legislature clearly did not intend.

The district court erred in isolating subdivision 9 without considering its relationship to subdivision 8. This Court should reverse and remand for proper interpretation of the statutory scheme.

---

### II. THE RESPONDENT'S RELIANCE ON *MINNESOTA CHARTER SCHOOL ASSOCIATION* IS MISPLACED

The respondent cites *Minnesota Charter School Association v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), for the proposition that "subdivision 9's prohibition on residency preferences applies to all charter schools in metropolitan areas." This is a misreading of that decision.

In *Minnesota Charter School Association*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed whether charter schools could *voluntarily adopt* residency preferences. The Court held that the statute did not grant charter schools authority to create such preferences. *Id.* at 441.

However, this case presents a different question: whether the statute *itself* provides a proximity preference as a matter of law. The appellant is not arguing that PACT Charter School could create a preference that the statute does not provide. Rather, the appellant argues that the statute already provides for such a preference, and the school is bound to implement it.

These are legally distinct questions. The *Minnesota Charter School Association* Court specifically noted that its holding was limited to the question presented: "whether charter schools have statutory authority to adopt residency preferences." *Id.* at 438. The Court did not address whether the statute provides for such a preference as a mandatory component.

The respondent's attempt to extend *Minnesota Charter School Association* to this context exceeds the scope of that holding and should be rejected.

---

### III. GENUINE DISPUTES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST PRECLUDING SUMMARY JUDGMENT

Even if this Court were to accept the respondent's interpretation of the statute, summary judgment was nevertheless improper because genuine disputes of material fact exist.

The respondent claims that Ramsey is "unquestionably part of the Twin Cities metropolitan area." This assertion is contested. No evidence was presented below establishing that Ramsey qualifies as a "metropolitan area" under the definition applicable to Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10.

The term "metropolitan area" is not defined in the statute. Minnesota law contains multiple definitions of "metropolitan area" for different purposes. The statutory definition applicable to the Minnesota Charter School Act is found in Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 1, which references Minnesota Statutes § 473.121, subdivision 4. That definition relates specifically to the Twin Cities metropolitan area and encompasses only certain counties and cities.

Ramsey is a city in Anoka County. While Anoka County is included in the metropolitan area for some purposes, the specific question of whether Ramsey falls within the statutory definition for charter school purposes was never properly determined below. The district court assumed this fact without analysis, but factual disputes cannot be resolved on summary judgment.

As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "summary judgment is inappropriate if reasonable persons could draw different inferences from the facts." *Anderson v. Composite State*, 754 N.W.2d 677, 682 (Minn. 2008). Here, the factual question of whether Ramsey qualifies as a metropolitan area under the statute presents exactly such a situation.

---

### IV. THE APPELLANTS HAVE STANDING AND THE CLAIMS ARE NOT MOOT

The respondent argues that the claims are moot because Matthew Look's son was admitted through the lottery and because John Dehen's daughter has no statutory right to preference. This argument fails.

First, the declaratory judgment action seeks interpretation of the statute for future applications. As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "a declaratory judgment action is appropriate where the plaintiff seeks resolution of a legal controversy before a harm has actually occurred." *Lake Superior Ctr. v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 403, 410 (Minn. 2004). The appellant Look is challenging the school's admission practices going forward, not merely seeking relief for his individual son.

Second, even if individual claims were moot, the appellant Dehen's claim is not. His daughter was not selected for admission and continues to seek enrollment. The respondent's argument that "no actionable injury exists" assumes the very legal conclusion in dispute—namely, whether the statute provides for a proximity preference. If the statute does provide for such a preference, then the daughter was wrongfully denied admission priority, and an actionable injury exists.

The district court erred in failing to address the standing and mootness arguments on their merits. This Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the respondent's argument fails to establish that the district court correctly interpreted Minnesota Statutes § 124D.10, subdivision 9. The statute, when read in conjunction with subdivision 8, provides for a proximity preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located. The respondent's reliance on *Minnesota Charter School Association v. State* exceeds the scope of that holding. Genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding whether Ramsey qualifies as a metropolitan area under the statute. The claims are neither moot nor lacking in standing.

Appellants respectfully request that this Court reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.

**Respectfully submitted,**

*Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding the application of the statute, precluding summary judgment. Accordingly, the district court’s decision is reversed, and the matter is remanded.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

On appeal from a grant of summary judgment, this Court must determine whether the district court erred in its application of the law and whether there are any genuine issues of material fact. See *Dahlberg v. Ford Motor Co.*, 772 N.W.2d 833, 836 (Minn. 2009). Summary judgment is appropriate only if the pleadings, depositions, admissions, and affidavits show that there is no genuine issue of material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03. The Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party against whom judgment was granted. *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 882, 886 (Minn. Ct. App. 1993).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Genuine Dispute of Material Fact Exists Regarding "Metropolitan Area" Status**

The district court granted summary judgment based on its interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The Respondent argues that the statute’s plain language prohibits residence-based preferences for charter schools located in a "metropolitan area." However, the application of this prohibition hinges on a factual determination: whether the City of Ramsey, where PACT Charter School is located, qualifies as a "metropolitan area" under the statutory definition.

The Respondent asserts that Ramsey is "unquestionably part of the Twin Cities metropolitan area" and that this fact is judicially noticeable. However, the Appellants have contested this assertion, arguing that the term "metropolitan area" is defined elsewhere in statute—specifically Minn. Stat. § 473.121, subd. 4—and that no evidence was presented below to establish that Ramsey meets this specific definition. The Appellants correctly note that the district court made no findings on this issue and assumed the fact without analysis.

Under Minnesota law, summary judgment is inappropriate if reasonable persons could draw different inferences from the facts. *Anderson v. Composite State*, 754 N.W.2d 677, 682 (Minn. 2008). Because the Appellants have raised a genuine dispute regarding the applicability of the "metropolitan area" definition to Ramsey, the district court erred in resolving this issue on summary judgment. The status of Ramsey as a "metropolitan area" is a material fact that must be determined by the fact-finder before the statutory prohibition can be applied.

**B. Statutory Construction Requires Consideration of the Whole Act**

The Respondent relies heavily on the plain language of subdivision 9, arguing that the phrase "may not give priority" creates an absolute prohibition. While the Court acknowledges the principle that "when the language of a statute is unambiguous, we give effect to its plain meaning," *State v. B.H.*, 753 N.W.2d 794, 800 (Minn. 2008), the Appellants have raised a substantial argument that the statute cannot be read in isolation.

The Appellants argue that subdivision 9 must be read in conjunction with subdivision 8, which they assert provides a preference for residents of the city in which the school is located. They cite *Am. Family Ins. Co. v. M.S. Police Pension Fund*, 754 N.W.2d 679, 684 (Minn. 2008), for the proposition that "statutes must be construed to give effect to all provisions, and no provision should be rendered meaningless." If the Respondent’s interpretation of subdivision 9 were adopted, it could potentially render the residency preference in subdivision 8 meaningless for schools in metropolitan areas.

The Respondent relies on *Minnesota Charter School Association v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), and *In re Petition for Charter School of St. Paul*, 694 N.W.2d 137 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005), to support the prohibition. However, the Appellants distinguish *Minnesota Charter School Association* by noting that it addressed whether schools could *voluntarily adopt* residency preferences, not whether the statute *itself* mandates a preference. This distinction is legally significant. Given the conflict between the potential mandate in subdivision 8 and the prohibition in subdivision 9, the district court should have engaged in a more thorough analysis of the statutory scheme as a whole rather than granting summary judgment based solely on the text of subdivision 9.

**C. Standing and Mootness**

The Respondent argues that Appellant Look’s claim is moot because his son was admitted, and that Appellant Dehen lacks standing because the statute grants no right to preference. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive at this stage.

The Appellants seek a declaratory judgment regarding the interpretation of the statute for future applications. As held in *Lake Superior Ctr. v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 403, 410 (Minn. 2004), "a declaratory judgment action is appropriate where the plaintiff seeks resolution of a legal controversy before a harm has actually occurred." Because the school’s admission practices are ongoing, the controversy is capable of repetition yet evading review. Furthermore, Appellant Dehen’s daughter was not selected for admission and continues to seek enrollment. If the Appellants’ interpretation of the statute is correct, she has suffered an injury by being denied a priority to which she is entitled. Therefore, the Appellants have standing to challenge the district court’s interpretation.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was premature. A genuine dispute of material fact exists regarding whether Ramsey is a "metropolitan area" under the relevant statutory definitions. Furthermore, the interplay between subdivisions 8 and 9 of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 requires a more comprehensive statutory analysis than was conducted by the district court.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is reversed, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.**

### INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT  

The district court correctly granted summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School ("PACT") because Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, unambiguously prohibits charter schools in metropolitan areas from granting proximity preferences to residents of the city where the school is located. Ramsey, where PACT is situated, indisputably falls within the statutory definition of a "metropolitan area," rendering the district court’s interpretation of the statute both legally sound and factually unassailable. Appellants’ arguments to the contrary rely on a fundamental misreading of the statute’s plain language, a mischaracterization of binding precedent, and an improper attempt to inject factual disputes where none exist. This Court should reverse the appellate court’s erroneous ruling and reinstate the district court’s judgment.  

---

### I. THE STATUTE’S PLAIN LANGUAGE UNAMBIGUOUSLY PROHIBITS PROXIMITY PREFERENCES FOR SCHOOLS IN METROPOLITAN AREAS, AND NO CONFLICT EXISTS BETWEEN SUBDIVISIONS 8 AND 9  

Appellants incorrectly assert that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not prohibit proximity preferences because subdivision 8 "provides a preference for residents of the city." This argument ignores the statute’s clear, unambiguous text and violates the principle that courts must give effect to plain meaning when language is clear. *State v. B.H.*, 753 N.W.2d 794, 800 (Minn. 2008). Subdivision 9 states:  
> "The school may not give priority to students who reside in the district in which the school is located **if the school is located in a metropolitan area**."  

This language is unequivocal: it imposes a categorical prohibition on proximity preferences for schools in metropolitan areas. Subdivision 8’s instruction that charter schools "shall give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located" applies *only* in non-metropolitan contexts. When a school is located in a metropolitan area, subdivision 9’s prohibition directly controls, rendering subdivision 8 inapplicable. This reading gives effect to *both* provisions: subdivision 8 mandates city-resident preferences for schools *outside* metropolitan areas, while subdivision 9 prohibits such preferences for schools *inside* them.  

Appellants’ claim that subdivision 9 "renders subdivision 8 meaningless" is legally unsound. As this Court has repeatedly held, statutes are not rendered meaningless when contextual exceptions apply; rather, they are harmonized by their plain terms. *See Minnesota Charter School Ass’n v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436, 441 (Minn. 2004) ("The statute does not grant charter schools authority to adopt residency preferences for schools in metropolitan areas."). **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. After reviewing the rebuttals submitted by both parties, the Court finds that the district court correctly interpreted the statute and that no genuine dispute of material fact exists to preclude summary judgment. Accordingly, the district court’s decision is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

On appeal from a grant of summary judgment, this Court must determine whether the district court erred in its application of the law and whether there are any genuine issues of material fact. See *Dahlberg v. Ford Motor Co.*, 772 N.W.2d 833, 836 (Minn. 2009). Summary judgment is appropriate only if the pleadings, depositions, admissions, and affidavits show that there is no genuine issue of material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03. The Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party against whom judgment was granted. *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 882, 886 (Minn. Ct. App. 1993).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Ramsey’s Status as a "Metropolitan Area" is Not a Genuine Dispute of Material Fact**

The Appellants argue that a genuine dispute of material fact exists regarding whether the City of Ramsey qualifies as a "metropolitan area" under Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. They contend that the district court erred by taking judicial notice of this fact without analysis. However, the Respondent has correctly identified that Ramsey is located within Anoka County, which is undisputedly part of the seven-county metropolitan area defined by Minn. Stat. § 473.121, subd. 4.

Under Minnesota law, a court may take judicial notice of a fact that is not subject to reasonable dispute because it can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned. *See Minn. R. Evid. 201(b)(2)*. The geographic boundaries of the metropolitan area are established by statute and are a matter of public record. As held in *Anderson v. Composite State*, 754 N.W.2d 677, 682 (Minn. 2008), summary judgment is appropriate where no reasonable dispute exists over facts. The Appellants have not provided evidence suggesting Ramsey is outside the statutory definition; rather, they argue for a different legal interpretation of the term "metropolitan area" in this context. This presents a question of law, not a genuine dispute of material fact. Therefore, the district court properly concluded that Ramsey is located in a metropolitan area.

**B. Statutory Construction: Subdivision 9 Prohibits the Preference in Metropolitan Areas**

The Appellants argue that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 8, which states that a charter school "shall give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located," creates a mandatory preference that applies regardless of location. They assert that reading subdivision 9 as a prohibition in metropolitan areas would render subdivision 8 meaningless for those schools, violating the canon of construction that no provision should be rendered meaningless. *See Am. Family Ins. Co. v. M.S. Police Pension Fund*, 754 N.W.2d 679, 684 (Minn. 2008).

The Court is not persuaded by this argument. The proper method of statutory construction is to harmonize the provisions to give effect to both. Subdivision 8 provides a general rule granting a preference to city residents. Subdivision 9 provides a specific exception, stating: "The school may not give priority to students who reside in the district in which the school is located if the school is located in a metropolitan area." When a general provision and a specific provision conflict, the specific provision controls. Thus, subdivision 8 applies to charter schools in non-metropolitan areas, while subdivision 9 prohibits such preferences for schools in metropolitan areas. This reading gives effect to both provisions and avoids the conflict alleged by the Appellants.

Furthermore, the Respondent’s reliance on *Minnesota Charter School Ass’n v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), is well-founded. In that case, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that the statute does not grant charter schools the authority to adopt residency preferences for schools located in the metropolitan area. *Id.* at 441. The Appellants attempt to distinguish this case by arguing it addressed only *voluntary* adoption of preferences, whereas subdivision 8 imposes a *statutory mandate*. This distinction is immaterial. Subdivision 9 contains an absolute prohibition ("may not give priority") that applies regardless of whether the school attempts to establish a preference voluntarily or under the mandate of subdivision 8. Because PACT Charter School is located in a metropolitan area, the prohibition in subdivision 9 controls, and the school lacks the authority to grant the proximity preference sought by the Appellants.

**C. Standing and Mootness**

The Respondent argues that Appellant Look’s claim is moot because his son was admitted to the school, and that Appellant Dehen lacks standing because the statute grants him no right to a preference. The Court agrees.

Regarding Look, the specific controversy regarding his son’s admission has ceased to exist. While Appellants cite *Lake Superior Ctr. v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 403, 410 (Minn. 2004), for the proposition that declaratory judgment is appropriate for future controversies, this principle does not save Look’s claim where the applicable statute is clear and prohibits the relief he seeks. Because the statute unambiguously prohibits proximity preferences for metropolitan-area schools, there is no ongoing controversy capable of repetition that would evade review; the legal standard is settled.

Regarding Dehen, he must show a concrete and particularized injury to establish standing. *See Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*. Dehen alleges his daughter was wrongfully denied admission priority. However, because subdivision 9 prohibits the school from granting such a priority, Dehen’s daughter has no legal right to the preference she claims was denied. An injury cannot be predicated on the denial of a benefit that the law does not grant. Therefore, Dehen lacks standing to challenge the school’s compliance with the statute.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was proper. Ramsey is indisputably located within a metropolitan area as defined by statute. Under Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, charter schools in metropolitan areas are prohibited from giving admission priority to residents of the school’s district. This prohibition overrides the general preference in subdivision 8. Appellant Look’s claim is moot, and Appellant Dehen lacks standing.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.**

But that is a question of law, not fact. *See Dahlberg v. Ford Motor Co.*, 772 N.W.2d 833, 836 (Minn. 2009) (summary judgment appropriate where "no genuine issue of material fact" exists). The Appellants’ attempt to recast a legal question as a factual dispute is precisely the error the district court corrected.  

### II. **THE STATUTORY CONSTRUCTION PRINCIPLES SUPPORT THE DISTRICT COURT’S INTERPRETATION**  
The Appellants argue that reading subdivision 9 as prohibiting preferences in metropolitan areas "renders subdivision 8 meaningless," but this misstates the law. Minnesota courts have long held that when a general provision conflicts with a specific exception, the specific provision controls. *See Am. Family Ins. Co. v. M.S. Police Pension Fund*, 754 N.W.2d 679, 684 (Minn. 2008). Here, subdivision 8 provides a *general rule*: charter schools "shall give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located." Subdivision 9 provides a *specific exception*: "The school may not give priority to students who reside in the district in which the school is located if the school is located in a metropolitan area." This is not a contradiction—it is a clear hierarchy. Subdivision 8 applies to schools in *non-metropolitan* areas, while subdivision 9 operates as an absolute bar for schools in metropolitan areas. To hold otherwise would violate the canon of statutory construction that requires courts to "harmonize provisions to give effect to both," not to ignore the specific language of subdivision 9. The Appellants’ assertion that subdivision 9 "nullifies" subdivision 8 ignores that the statute intentionally carves out an exception for metropolitan areas. The district court correctly applied this principle.  

### III. **MINNESOTA CHARTER SCHOOL ASSOCIATION V. STATE CONTROLS THIS CASE**  
The Appellants’ attempt to distinguish *Minnesota Charter School Ass’n v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), is without merit. They claim the case addressed only "voluntary" preferences, while subdivision 8 imposes a "statutory mandate." But this distinction is irrelevant. In *Minnesota Charter School Ass’n*, the Supreme Court unequivocally held: "The statute does not grant charter schools authority to adopt residency preferences for schools located in the metropolitan area." *Id.* at 441 (emphasis added). The Court’s reasoning turned on the statute’s plain language—specifically, subdivision 9’s prohibition that "the school may not give priority" in metropolitan areas. That language is absolute and unqualified. Whether a school attempts to adopt a preference "voluntarily" or claims it is "mandated" by subdivision 8 makes no difference; subdivision 9 forbids it entirely. As this Court noted in its preliminary ruling, the statute’s "absolute prohibition" controls. The Appellants’ effort to create a distinction where none exists is an exercise in futility.  

### IV. **APPELLANTS’ CLAIMS ARE MOOT AND DEHEN LACKS STANDING**  
The Appellants’ standing and mootness arguments collapse under scrutiny. First, Look’s claim is moot. His son was admitted through the lottery—there is no ongoing controversy regarding his admission. While Appellants cite *Lake Superior Ctr. v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 403, 410 (Minn. 2004), for the proposition that declaratory judgments may address "future controversies," that principle applies only when a "controversy capable of repetition yet evading review" exists. Here, the statute is unambiguous: schools in metropolitan areas *cannot* grant proximity preferences. There is no "repetition" to address because the legal standard is settled. As this Court rightly held, "no ongoing controversy exists for future applications" where the statute’s meaning is clear.  

Second, Dehen lacks standing. To establish standing, a plaintiff must show a "concrete and particularized injury." *Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03*. Dehen alleges his daughter was "wrongfully denied admission priority," but no such right exists under the law. Subdivision 9 explicitly prohibits the preference he claims was denied. An injury cannot arise from the denial of a benefit the statute does not confer. *See Anderson v. Composite State*, 754 N.W.2d at 682 (summary judgment appropriate where "no reasonable dispute exists over facts"). Dehen’s standing argument is circular: he assumes the very right he seeks to vindicate. But the district court correctly held that subdivision 9 eliminates any statutory right to proximity preference for Ramsey-area schools.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honor, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was correct as a matter of law. Ramsey’s location in a statutory metropolitan area is beyond dispute. Subdivision 9’s absolute prohibition overrides subdivision 8’s general preference. *Minnesota Charter School Ass’n* squarely supports this interpretation. And Appellants lack standing and a live controversy. The Appellants’ rebuttal relies on legal missteps, not facts or binding precedent. We urge this Court to affirm the district court’s judgment. The law is clear, the facts are undisputed, and justice demands that summary judgment stand.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLANTS

## In Support of Reversing the District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. This Court should reverse because genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding Ramsey's status as a metropolitan area, because the district court incorrectly construed Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivisions 8 and 9, and because the court improperly dismissed Appellants' claims on standing and mootness grounds. The Respondent's closing argument masks fundamental legal errors with confident assertions. We demonstrate below why reversal is warranted.

---

### II. THE METROPOLITAN AREA DETERMINATION PRESENTS A GENUINE DISPUTE OF MATERIAL FACT

The Respondent contends that Ramsey's status within the seven-county metropolitan area is beyond dispute. This is incorrect. The question of whether Ramsey qualifies as a "metropolitan area" under Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is not merely a geographic fact—it is a legal determination that requires analysis of the statute's purpose and context.

The district court took judicial notice of Ramsey's location within Anoka County without adequate analysis. However, the mere fact that Ramsey is geographically within the seven-county metropolitan region does not automatically resolve whether the statutory term "metropolitan area" was intended to include all municipalities within those counties or only those that meet certain criteria of urban density or character.

The Respondent cites *Anderson v. Composite State*, 754 N.W.2d 677, 682 (Minn. 2008), for the proposition that summary judgment is appropriate where no reasonable dispute exists over facts. But this case actually supports reversal here: the Anderson court emphasized that summary judgment is inappropriate when reasonable minds could differ on the facts. Here, reasonable minds could differ on whether Ramsey, as a specific municipality, should be treated as a "metropolitan area" for purposes of the charter school preference prohibition.

The Respondent's reliance on Minn. R. Evid. 201(b)(2) is misplaced. While courts may take judicial notice of indisputable facts, the application of a statutory term to a particular municipality involves interpretation, not mere notice. The district court conflated geographic location with statutory classification—a legal error warranting reversal.

---

### III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN STATUTORY CONSTRUCTION

The district court's construction of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subdivisions 8 and 9 violates fundamental principles of statutory interpretation.

**A. Subdivision 8 Creates a Mandatory Preference**

Subdivision 8 provides that a charter school "shall give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located." The word "shall" indicates a mandatory duty, not mere authorization. The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently held that "shall" imposes a nondiscretionary obligation. See *Shell Oil Co. v. City of Minneapolis*, 741 N.W.2d 392, 399 (Minn. 2007).

The district court acknowledged this mandatory language but attempted to reconcile it with subdivision 9 by treating subdivision 9 as a specific exception that overrides the general rule. This reading, however, creates an absurd result: the legislature would have imposed a mandatory duty in subdivision 8 only to immediately nullify it in subdivision 9 for certain schools.

**B. The Specific-General Canon Does Not Apply Here**

The Respondent argues that subdivision 9 is a "specific exception" that controls over the "general rule" in subdivision 8. This misapplies the canon of construction. The specific-general canon applies when both provisions can be harmonized but appear to conflict. Here, they cannot be harmonized under the district court's reading.

If subdivision 9 absolutely prohibits what subdivision 8 mandates, then subdivision 8 is rendered mere surplusage for metropolitan-area schools—a result the Respondent acknowledges violates the canon against rendering provisions meaningless. See *Am. Family Ins. Co. v. M.S. Police Pension Fund*, 754 N.W.2d 679, 684 (Minn. 2008).

**C. The Correct Interpretation Preserves Both Provisions**

The proper interpretation gives effect to both subdivisions by recognizing that subdivision 9 addresses a different subject than subdivision 8. Subdivision 8 refers to "residents of the city," while subdivision 9 refers to "students who reside in the district." These are distinct populations. A student may reside in the school district without residing in the city itself.

Thus, subdivision 8 requires preference for city residents, while subdivision 9 prohibits preference for district residents when the school is in a metropolitan area. These provisions address different categories and can be harmonized.

**D. Minnesota Charter School Association v. State Is Distinguishable**

The Respondent heavily relies on *Minnesota Charter School Ass'n v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004). However, that case is distinguishable. In *Minnesota Charter School Association*, the Court addressed whether charter schools had *voluntary* authority to adopt residency preferences. The Court held they did not have such authority under the statute.

Here, however, the Appellants do not argue the school has voluntary authority. Rather, they argue that subdivision 8 *mandates* a preference that cannot be overridden by subdivision 9. The distinction is critical: the question in *Minnesota Charter School Association* was whether schools could *choose* to implement preferences; the question here is whether the legislature *required* such preferences.

The Court in *Minnesota Charter School Association* did not address whether the mandatory language in subdivision 8 could be overridden by the prohibition in subdivision 9. That specific question is novel and was not resolved by that case.

---

### IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING MOOTNESS

The district court found Appellant Look's claim moot because his son was admitted through the lottery. This was error.

The doctrine of mootness requires that an actual controversy exist throughout the litigation. However, the Supreme Court has recognized an exception for cases "capable of repetition, yet evading review." See *Lake Superior Ctr. v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 403, 410 (Minn. 2004). This exception applies when: (1) the challenged action is too short in duration to be fully litigated before it ends, and (2) there is a reasonable expectation that the same complaining party will be subject to the same action again.

Both prongs are satisfied here. Look's son was admitted mid-litigation, but the underlying statutory interpretation question remains live. The school continues to operate under the interpretation that no city resident preference applies. Future applicants, including Look's children in future years, will continue to be affected by this interpretation.

The Respondent argues that "the legal standard is settled" because the statute is unambiguous. But this argument assumes the very question in dispute. If the statute is unambiguous in the Respondent's favor, then there is no reason to avoid review on mootness grounds—the Court could simply hold the statute unambiguous and dismiss. But the Appellant's position is that the statute is ambiguous and susceptible to multiple interpretations, making the controversy ripe for adjudication.

---

### V. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING LACK OF STANDING

The district court found that Appellant Dehen lacks standing because the statute grants him no right to a preference. This reasoning is circular and erroneous.

**A. The Injury-in-Fact Requirement Is Satisfied**

To establish standing, a plaintiff must show: (1) injury in fact, (2) a causal connection between the injury and the conduct complained of, and (3) likelihood that the injury would be redressed by a favorable decision. See *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs.*, 528 U.S. 167, 180-81 (2000).

Dehen's daughter applied for admission to PACT Charter School and was not selected through the lottery. This is a concrete injury: she was denied admission. The causal connection is direct: she was not admitted because the school did not apply a city-resident preference that the Appellant contends is required by statute. If the Court rules in the Appellant's favor, the school would be required to implement such a preference, and Dehen's daughter would have a better chance of admission in future lotteries.

**B. The Standing Argument Assumes the Merits**

The Respondent's standing argument—that Dehen has no right to a preference because subdivision 9 prohibits it—assumes the very question this case presents: whether subdivision 9 prohibits what subdivision 8 mandates. The Court cannot assume the merits of the case in determining standing.

In *Sprint Commc'ns Co. v. APCC Servs.*, 554 U.S. 269, 289 (2008), the Supreme Court warned against "confusing standing with the merits." The district court committed this error by essentially holding that because the statute does not grant the claimed right, the plaintiff lacks standing to argue that it does.

---

### VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment. Genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding Ramsey's status as a metropolitan area. The district court incorrectly construed the statute by rendering subdivision 8 meaningless for metropolitan-area schools. *Minnesota Charter School Association v. State* is distinguishable because it addressed voluntary rather than mandatory preferences. The mootness and standing determinations were erroneous.

The Appellants are entitled to a declaratory judgment that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 8 requires charter schools to give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located, and that this requirement applies to PACT Charter School in Ramsey.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. After reviewing the closing arguments from both parties, the Court finds that the district court erred in granting summary judgment. Genuine disputes regarding the proper construction of the statute exist, specifically concerning the distinction between "city" and "district" residents, and the applicability of precedent regarding mandatory versus voluntary preferences. Accordingly, the district court’s decision is reversed and remanded.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

On appeal from a grant of summary judgment, this Court must determine whether the district court erred in its application of the law and whether there are any genuine issues of material fact. See *Dahlberg v. Ford Motor Co.*, 772 N.W.2d 833, 836 (Minn. 2009). Summary judgment is appropriate only if the pleadings, depositions, admissions, and affidavits show that there is no genuine issue of material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Minn. R. Civ. P. 56.03. The Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party against whom judgment was granted. *Fabio v. Bellomo*, 504 N.W.2d 882, 886 (Minn. Ct. App. 1993).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Construction: "City" vs. "District" Creates Ambiguity**

The district court held that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 acts as a specific prohibition that overrides the general preference found in subdivision 8. The Respondent argues that subdivision 9’s prohibition on giving priority to "students who reside in the district" necessarily encompasses the "residents of the city" mentioned in subdivision 8. However, the Appellants have raised a compelling textual argument distinguishing these two populations.

Subdivision 8 mandates that a charter school "shall give preference to residents of the city in which the school is located." The word "shall" indicates a mandatory duty. See *Shell Oil Co. v. City of Minneapolis*, 741 N.W.2d 392, 399 (Minn. 2007). In contrast, subdivision 9 states: "The school may not give priority to students who reside in the district in which the school is located if the school is located in a metropolitan area."

The Appellants correctly argue that "city" and "district" are distinct legal and geographic terms. A resident of the city is necessarily a resident of the district, but the inverse is not true. By prohibiting priority for "district" residents, the legislature may have intended to prevent schools from favoring the broader district population over the wider metropolitan area, while still preserving the mandate to favor the specific host city. The Respondent’s closing argument failed to address this specific textual distinction, instead relying on the general assertion that subdivision 9 is a specific exception to subdivision 8.

When a statute is susceptible to multiple reasonable interpretations, summary judgment is inappropriate. See *Am. Family Ins. Co. v. M.S. Police Pension Fund*, 754 N.W.2d 679, 684 (Minn. 2008) (courts must harmonize provisions to give effect to both). Because the Appellants have offered a harmonious reading where subdivision 8 mandates city preference and subdivision 9 prohibits district preference, a genuine issue of statutory interpretation remains. The district court erred by resolving this ambiguity in favor of the Respondent at the summary judgment stage.

**B. Precedent Regarding Mandatory Preferences**

The Respondent relies heavily on *Minnesota Charter School Ass’n v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 436 (Minn. 2004), where the Supreme Court held that the statute does not grant charter schools the authority to adopt residency preferences in metropolitan areas. The Appellants persuasively distinguish this case by noting that it addressed *voluntary* adoption of preferences. Here, the Appellants seek enforcement of a *statutory mandate* ("shall") found in subdivision 8. The question of whether a mandatory statutory duty can be nullified by a subsequent subdivision is a distinct legal issue not resolved by *Minnesota Charter School Ass’n*. Therefore, that case does not control the outcome of this specific dispute.

**C. Mootness and Standing**

The district court dismissed Appellant Look’s claim as moot and Appellant Dehen’s claim for lack of standing. The Court finds these determinations were premature.

Regarding Look, the "capable of repetition, yet evading review" exception applies. The school’s admission lottery is an annual event. Although Look’s son was admitted, Look could face the same issue with other children or in future years where the legal controversy might evade review due to the short duration of the admission process. See *Lake Superior Ctr. v. State*, 684 N.W.2d 403, 410 (Minn. 2004).

Regarding Dehen, the district court erred by conflating standing with the merits. Dehen alleges a concrete injury—his daughter’s denial of admission. The Respondent argues Dehen lacks standing because the statute grants no right to a preference, but this argument assumes the very interpretation of the statute that is in dispute. As held in *Sprint Commc'ns Co. v. APCC Servs.*, 554 U.S. 269, 289 (2008), courts must not confuse standing with the merits. If the Appellants’ interpretation of subdivision 8 is correct, Dehen has suffered a redressable injury. Therefore, he has standing to challenge the school’s interpretation.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. The text of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, referring to "district" residents, creates ambiguity regarding its application to the mandatory preference for "city" residents in subdivision 8. This ambiguity precludes summary judgment. Furthermore, the claims are not moot, and Appellants have standing to pursue this action.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is reversed and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.**
