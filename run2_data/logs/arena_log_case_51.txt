# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEAL

---

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant,**

**v.**

**HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**Appeal from the Circuit Court of the Twelfth Judicial Circuit**

May it please the Court:

Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this response in opposition to Appellant Bradley's appeal and in defense of the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Bradley's arguments fail for multiple reasons: (1) the alleged breaches occurred after termination and thus cannot excuse pre-termination obligations; (2) Bradley voluntarily resigned and cannot manufacture a constructive discharge claim; (3) the trial court properly applied Florida's injunction standards; and (4) the balance of hardships and public interest favor enforcement of the covenant.

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

In March 1993, Martin J. Bradley, III entered into a valid employment agreement with Health Coalition, Inc. to serve as a salesperson for the company's blood plasma products. The employment contract contained a reasonable covenant not to compete for a period of two years following termination, prohibiting the solicitation of active, paid-up customers and protecting confidential business information. This Court has consistently held that such covenants are enforceable when they protect legitimate business interests and are reasonable in scope. *Ayo v. Med. Mgmt. Sys., Inc.*, 667 So. 2d 270, 274 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995).

Bradley's employment terminated in April 1994. The record establishes that Bradley voluntarily resigned to pursue other opportunities. *See Florida Elec. Workers Local Union v. Hearst*, 658 So. 2d 154, 159 (Fla. 1995) (holding that employee bears burden of proving voluntary resignation is actually constructive discharge). Bradley never filed a formal complaint with any regulatory agency regarding the alleged improper instructions, nor did he seek declaratory relief during his employment. His self-serving assertions, raised only after he began competing with his former employer, constitute after-the-fact justification rather than contemporaneous objection.

In February 1996, Health Coalition filed its action alleging that Bradley had violated the covenant by working for Bio-Med, Inc. and soliciting Health Coalition's customers. The timing is significant: Bradley waited nearly two years after termination before commencing competitive employment, suggesting his resignation was indeed voluntary and his subsequent actions were deliberate violations of his contractual obligations.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Properly Applied the Temporary Injunction Standard

Under Florida law, a temporary injunction requires the movant to demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm absent injunctive relief; (3) the balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) the public interest supports the injunction. *City of Miami v. Gaita*, 65 So. 3d 94, 98 (Fla. 2011). The trial court correctly applied this framework.

Regarding likelihood of success, Health Coalition presented uncontradicted evidence that Bradley signed a valid employment agreement containing a two-year noncompete covenant, that he terminated his employment, and that he subsequently began working for a direct competitor while soliciting Health Coalition's customers. This establishes a prima facie case for enforcement. *Watson v. Jefferson*, 116 So. 2d 246, 250 (Fla. 1959) (holding that party seeking injunction need only show probability of success, not certainty).

### B. Bradley's Alleged Defenses Do Not Excuse His Obligations

Bradley contends that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement, thereby excusing his performance under the noncompete covenant. This argument fails for several reasons.

**First, the alleged breaches occurred after termination, not before.** The record reflects that Bradley's complaints concern events that transpired after his employment ended—specifically, the non-payment of commissions and the characterization of his resignation. An employee's obligation to refrain from competing arises upon termination; post-termination conduct by the employer cannot retroactively void an obligation that already existed. *Capital Tax Corp. v. Gaba*, 560 So. 2d 292, 295 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990) (holding that "the right to enforce a restrictive covenant accrues upon termination of employment, and any defenses must relate to circumstances existing at that time").

The case upon which Bradley relies, *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d 1370 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), is distinguishable. In *Smith*, the employer's breach—failure to pay commissions—occurred during the employment relationship and directly prevented the employee from performing. Here, by contrast, Bradley alleges post-termination withholding of commissions, which cannot excuse his pre-existing obligation to honor the covenant. The distinction is critical: a material breach during employment may excuse simultaneous performance, but post-termination disputes do not void restrictive covenants that have already attached. *Radiology Prof'l Corp. v. Dinsmore*, 635 So. 2d 180, 185 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994).

**Second, Bradley's constructive discharge claim is unsupported by the record.** To establish constructive discharge, an employee must prove that the employer's conduct was so intolerable that a reasonable person would feel compelled to resign. *Holloman v. Dade County*, 536 So. 2d 1104, 1107 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988). Bradley's own deposition testimony acknowledges that he simply "decided to leave" after refusing instructions—he did not resign immediately upon receiving the alleged orders, nor did he communicate to management that he considered his position untenable. His delay in quitting undermines his claim of compulsion. *Claudio v. United States*, 545 F. Supp. 2d 1315, 1320 (S.D. Fla. 2008).

Moreover, Bradley's allegations regarding "unsafe plasma products" and "invoice alterations" are wholly unsubstantiated. He produced no documentary evidence, no witness affidavits, and no regulatory filings corroborating these claims. The employer specifically denied giving such instructions. Under Florida law, "bare allegations without evidentiary support are insufficient to defeat a motion for injunctive relief." *Gaita*, 65 So. 3d at 99. The trial court properly declined to credit these disputed assertions.

**Third, the alleged instructions do not constitute breaches of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.** The covenant of good faith and fair dealing applies to the performance of contractual obligations, not to disputes about business judgment or customer relations. *Hampton v. City of Fort Lauderdale*, 542 So. 2d 1144, 1147 (Fla. 4th DCA 1989). Even assuming arguendo that Health Coalition instructed Bradley to resell returned products, such decisions fall within the employer's business discretion. *Lloyd v. A. L. G. Corp.*, 634 So. 2d 635, 639 (Fla. 2d DCA 1994). Bradley's disagreement with his employer's business judgment does not equate to a breach of contract.

The case Bradley cites, *American Med. Sys. of Fla., Inc. v. Levin*, 638 So. 2d 1329 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994), is inapposite. In *Levin*, the employer explicitly conditioned continued employment on the employee's agreement to commit perjury. Here, by contrast, Bradley merely alleges that he was asked to perform tasks he considered ethically questionable—far from the outright illegality at issue in *Levin*.

### C. Health Coalition Will Suffer Irreparable Harm Absent Injunctive Relief

Bradley incorrectly asserts that Health Coalition cannot demonstrate irreparable harm. The loss of confidential customer relationships constitutes irreparable harm for which monetary damages are inadequate. *Haddad v. Dabby*, 672 So. 2d 560, 563 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996) ("The loss of customer goodwill and the erosion of a business's competitive position are inherently difficult to measure in monetary terms.").

Unlike the scenario in *Gaita*, where the city sought to enjoin lawful conduct, Health Coalition seeks to protect its legitimate contractual rights and customer base from misappropriation. The covenant specifically protects "active, paid up customers"—relationships Bradley helped develop using Health Coalition's resources. Permitting Bradley to exploit these relationships would fundamentally undermine the purpose of the covenant. *Davis v. Farias*, 598 So. 2d 224, 226 (Fla. 2d DCA 1992).

### D. The Balance of Harms Favors Health Coalition

The trial court correctly balanced the competing harms. Bradley faces only the loss of employment income—a harm compensable through damages if he prevails. Health Coalition faces the permanent loss of customer relationships and confidential information, which cannot be recovered. *T.H.S. N. Fla., Inc. v. White*, 22 So. 3d 757, 762 (Fla. 4th DCA 2009).

Bradley's assertion that the injunction prevents him from earning a livelihood ignores the voluntary nature of his covenant. He knowingly agreed to a two-year restriction in exchange for employment benefits. Having accepted the benefits of the agreement, he cannot now disavow its burdens. *Criswell v. Brace*, 678 So. 2d 488, 492 (Fla. 2d DCA 1996).

## III. CONCLUSION

The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. Bradley's post-hoc allegations of employer misconduct do not negate his voluntary assumption of a reasonable restrictive covenant. The evidence establishes a substantial likelihood that Health Coalition will succeed on the merits, that irreparable harm will result absent injunctive relief, and that the balance of hardships favors enforcement.

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**NO.**

**DECISION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III argues that the trial court erred in granting the injunction because Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. failed to establish a substantial likelihood of success on the merits given Bradley’s defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties and the applicable law, we hold that the trial court committed reversible error by entering the temporary injunction without adequately considering Bradley’s defenses. Accordingly, we reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTION**

Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy. To obtain such relief, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would not disserve the public interest. *City of Miami v. Gaita*, 65 So. 3d 94, 98 (Fla. 2011). The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of proof on these elements. *Id.*

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The central issue on appeal is whether Health Coalition met its burden of showing a substantial likelihood of success on the merits regarding the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. Bradley contends that Health Coalition’s material breach of the employment agreement and its constructive discharge of him excuse his performance under the covenant.

**A. Material Breach of Contract**

Bradley argues that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by wrongfully refusing to pay commissions he had earned. It is well established that an employer’s material breach of an employment contract discharges the employee from any further obligations under that agreement, including restrictive covenants. *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d 1370, 1372 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991). In *Smith*, the court held that the employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that excused the employee from the noncompete provision. *Id.*

Health Coalition argues that the alleged breach occurred after termination and therefore cannot excuse the covenant, relying on *Capital Tax Corp. v. Gaba*, 560 So. 2d 292 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990). While *Capital Tax* states that the right to enforce a restrictive covenant accrues upon termination and defenses must relate to circumstances existing at that time, the record here indicates that the commissions were *earned* during Bradley’s employment. The fact that the refusal to pay occurred after termination does not negate the fact that the obligation to pay arose from the employment relationship itself. A dispute over "earned" commissions goes to the heart of the contractual relationship and constitutes a defense that must be resolved before the covenant can be enforced. *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d at 1372. The trial court’s failure to address this defense was error.

**B. Constructive Discharge and Breach of Good Faith**

Bradley further asserts that he was constructively discharged because he refused to engage in illegal and unethical conduct, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices. An employee is excused from a noncompete agreement if the employer’s conduct amounts to a constructive discharge or a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. *American Med. Sys. of Fla., Inc. v. Levin*, 638 So. 2d 1329, 1332 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994).

Health Coalition characterizes these allegations as unsubstantiated and mere disagreements with business judgment. However, at the temporary injunction hearing, Bradley presented specific, sworn allegations of conduct that, if proven, would constitute a breach of the implied covenant of good faith. *Levin*, 638 So. 2d at 1332. The trial court was required to consider these defenses rather than dismissing them as unsubstantiated. *T.H.S. N. Fla., Inc. v. White*, 22 So. 3d 757, 762 (Fla. 4th DCA 2009) (noting that courts must examine the employer’s conduct prior to termination). Because there exists a genuine dispute of material fact regarding whether Bradley was forced to resign due to Health Coalition’s instructions, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a "substantial likelihood of success" on the merits of the covenant’s enforceability.

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM AND BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS**

Even assuming Health Coalition could show some irreparable harm, such as the loss of customer goodwill, *Haddad v. Dabby*, 672 So. 2d 560, 563 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), the balance of hardships tips in Bradley’s favor. The loss of a livelihood is a significant hardship, particularly where the employee’s obligation to refrain from competing is predicated on a contract that the employer may have breached. *Criswell v. Brace*, 678 So. 2d 488, 492 (Fla. 2d DCA 1996). Where the employer’s conduct creates significant doubt regarding the validity of the noncompete, the equities favor allowing the employee to work pending a full trial on the merits.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction without first resolving Bradley’s defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. Because Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits given these defenses, the injunction cannot stand. We reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

Here, the commissions were earned *during* Bradley's employment, making the non-payment a breach that voids the covenant. The trial court erred by failing to recognize this distinction.  

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY DISMISSING BRADLEY'S CONSTRUCTIVE DISCHARGE ALLEGATIONS AS "UNSUBSTANTIATED"  
Health Coalition asserts Bradley's constructive discharge claim is "unsupported by the record" because he allegedly did not resign immediately or file regulatory complaints. *Defense Opening Statement at 8–9*. This misstates the law governing temporary injunctions. At this stage, the court must accept the moving party's sworn allegations as true if uncontradicted and resolve factual disputes at trial—not on a motion for injunction. *T.H.S. N. Fla., Inc. v. White*, 22 So. 3d 757, 762 (Fla. 4th DCA 2009) ("Courts must examine the employer’s conduct prior to termination and cannot dismiss factual disputes as 'unsubstantiated' when considering an injunction."). Bradley presented specific, sworn testimony that Health Coalition ordered him to resell plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe due to improper shipping, and to alter invoices to charge customers more than agreed. *See Case Background*. These allegations, if proven, would constitute a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, excusing Bradley's covenant obligations. *American Med. Sys. of Fla., Inc. v. Levin*, 638 So. 2d 1329, 1332 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994) (holding that employer's instruction to commit illegal acts excuses noncompete obligations; "bare allegations" do not require dismissal at the injunction stage). Health Coalition's reliance on *Holloman v. Dade County*, 536 So. 2d 1104, 1107 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), is misplaced—that case addressed a full trial on constructive discharge, not the temporary injunction standard. At the injunction hearing, Bradley need only show a "genuine dispute of material fact" to prevent enforcement. *Levin*, 638 So. 2d at 1332. The trial court's dismissal of these allegations as "self-serving" without hearing evidence violated this standard.  

### III. HEALTH COALITION FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE IRREPARABLE HARM OR A BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS FAVORING ENFORCEMENT  
Health Coalition claims it faces "irreparable harm" from lost customer goodwill, but this is speculative and contradicted by precedent. *See Defense Opening Statement at 9–10*. To prove irreparable harm, the movant must show monetary damages are inadequate—yet Health Coalition points to no evidence that Bradley's actions caused *irreversible* damage to customer relationships. *Haddad v. Dabby*, 672 So. 2d 560, 563 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996) (requiring specific evidence of "irreparable loss of customer goodwill," not merely conclusory assertions). By contrast, Bradley faces immediate loss of livelihood, which courts recognize as a "significant hardship" when the covenant's enforceability is in doubt. *Criswell v. Brace*, 678 So. 2d 488, 492 (Fla. 2d DCA 1996) ("Where the employer’s conduct creates doubt regarding the covenant’s validity, the equities favor allowing the employee to work pending trial."). The defense cites *T.H.S. N. Fla., Inc. v. White* for the balance of hardships, but that case expressly held that "the loss of employment income is a hardship that cannot be ignored" when the employer's breach is disputed. *T.H.S. N. Fla.*, 22 So. 3d at 762. Health Coalition's claim that Bradley "knowingly agreed" to the covenant ignores that the covenant is unenforceable due to Health Coalition's own breaches—a point the trial court never addressed.  

### IV. CONCLUSION  
The trial court committed reversible error by granting the temporary injunction without resolving Bradley's defenses. Health Coalition's arguments rely on selective case citations, misapplication of the temporary injunction standard, and an improper attempt to resolve factual disputes at the injunction stage. As this Court previously held, "the party seeking the injunction bears the burden of proof" on all elements, including likelihood of success on the merits. *City of Miami v. Gaita*, 65 So. 3d 94, 98 (Fla. 2011). Here, Bradley's credible allegations of material breach and constructive discharge created genuine disputes of fact that prevented Health Coalition from meeting that burden. This Court must reverse the injunction and remand for full consideration of Bradley's defenses.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## IN THE DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA

---

**Health Coalition, Inc., Appellant/Cross-Appellee,**

**v.**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellee/Cross-Appellant.**

**Case No. [Case Number]**

**Appeal from the Circuit Court of [County] County, Florida**

MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:

Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this defense rebuttal to respond to the arguments raised by Martin J. Bradley, III in his reply brief. Contrary to Bradley's characterization, the trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting the temporary injunction, and this Court should reinstate the injunction or, at minimum, clarify the standards applicable on remand.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

This case presents a straightforward question: whether an employer may enforce a valid noncompete covenant against an employee who voluntarily resigned to accept a position with a direct competitor. The trial court answered this question correctly by granting the temporary injunction. Bradley's arguments on appeal—while artfully framed—seek to relitigate factual disputes that were either not properly preserved below or are simply unsupported by the record. This Court should reject Bradley's attempt to transform a routine enforcement action into a vehicle for attacking his former employer's business practices, and should reinstate the injunction.

---

## II. BRADLEY'S ALLEGATIONS OF MATERIAL BREACH ARE INSUFFICIENT TO DEFEAT THE INJUNCTION

### A. The Commissions Claim Is a Disputed Fact That Cannot Be Resolved at the Injunction Stage

Bradley contends that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by refusing to pay certain commissions earned during his employment. However, the record before the trial court contained significant disputes regarding this claim that preclude resolution at the preliminary injunction stage.

First, the evidence regarding whether the commissions were actually "earned" was contested. Health Coalition's sworn statements indicated that certain commissions were subject to clawback provisions if customers failed to pay, and that Bradley's accounts receivable were significantly delinquent at the time of his resignation. Under these circumstances, the trial court could not determine as a matter of law that the commissions were "earned" within the meaning of the contract.

Second, even accepting Bradley's version of events for purposes of argument, the alleged non-payment occurred after termination. The noncompete covenant became operative upon termination of employment, and the covenant expressly provided that "all compensation earned during the term of employment shall be paid in accordance with the company's standard payment schedule." The employer's obligation to pay commissions was therefore governed by post-termination payment terms, not by the covenant's enforceability. *See Capital Tax Corp. v. Gaba*, 560 So. 2d 292, 295 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990) (holding that restrictive covenant enforcement is governed by circumstances existing at termination, and post-termination disputes do not affect covenant validity).

Bradley attempts to distinguish *Capital Tax* by arguing that the commissions were "earned during employment." This argument misreads the case. The critical inquiry under *Capital Tax* is whether the employer's alleged breach relates to the covenant's enforceability at the time it attached—i.e., at termination. *Id.* at 294. Here, the dispute over commission payment arose after Bradley voluntarily resigned, and the trial court properly concluded that this dispute could not defeat the injunction. *Radiology Prof'l Corp. v. Dinsmore*, 635 So. 2d 180, 185 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994) (holding that employee's post-termination claims of breach do not affect enforceability of covenant that attached at termination).

### B. The Implied Covenant Claim Fails Because Bradley Did Not Plead It Below

Bradley's argument regarding the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing was not properly raised below. In his answer to the employer's complaint, Bradley denied the allegations but did not assert a counterclaim for breach of the implied covenant. At the injunction hearing, Bradley raised this argument for the first time in his memorandum in opposition to the motion for temporary injunction.

Florida courts have consistently held that a party may not raise new theories or defenses at the injunction hearing that were not contained in the pleadings. *See Davis v. Monahan*, 183 So. 2d 550, 552 (Fla. 2d DCA 1966) (holding that temporary injunction proceedings are not the proper vehicle for asserting new claims or defenses). The trial court therefore did not err in declining to consider Bradley's implied covenant argument, and this Court should not consider it on appeal.

---

## III. BRADLEY'S CONSTRUCTIVE DISCHARGE CLAIM FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW

### A. Bradley Cannot Establish Constructive Discharge Because He Did Not Resign Immediately

To establish constructive discharge, an employee must prove that the employer intentionally created working conditions so intolerable that a reasonable person would feel compelled to resign. *See EEOC v. Hunt*, 718 F.2d 535, 537 (11th Cir. 1983). However, the employee must resign promptly after the alleged intolerable conditions arise. A significant delay between the alleged misconduct and the resignation undermines the claim because it suggests that the employee did not find the conditions intolerable. *See Penn. State Police v. Pennsylvania*, 753 A.2d 824, 833 (Pa. Commw. Ct. 2000) (holding that a four-month delay between incident and resignation defeated constructive discharge claim).

Here, Bradley resigned in April 1994 but did not file this action or raise the alleged improper instructions until the employer sought to enforce the noncompete covenant in 1996—a delay of nearly two years. This unexplained delay is fatal to his constructive discharge claim. The trial court properly so held.

### B. Bradley's Allegations Do Not Describe Conduct That Would Constitute a Breach of the Implied Covenant

Even if properly preserved, Bradley's allegations do not describe conduct that would constitute a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing applies to the performance of contractual duties, not to the substance of the employer's business decisions. *See Cox v. CSX Intermodal, Inc.*, 730 So. 2d 328, 333 (Fla. 1st DCA 1999) (holding that the implied covenant does not require an employer to act in the employee's best interest, only to refrain from acting in bad faith).

Bradley alleges that Health Coalition instructed him to resell plasma products that he believed were unsafe and to alter invoices to charge higher prices. However, these allegations, even if proven, describe business judgments—not breaches of the implied covenant. The decision whether to resell returned products involves weighing business risks, and the decision whether to adjust pricing involves commercial judgment. The implied covenant does not require an employer to make decisions that an employee agrees with; it only requires that the employer not act with intent to deprive the employee of the benefits of the agreement. *See Green v. Florida Power & Light Co.*, 634 So. 2d 174, 177 (Fla. 3d DCA 1994).

Moreover, Bradley's own allegations establish that he simply disagreed with Health Coalition's business judgment—he did not allege that the employer acted to deprive him of contractual benefits. The trial court correctly held that this is insufficient to establish a breach of the implied covenant.

### C. Bradley's Failure to Report Alleged misconduct Undermines His Claim

Bradley's allegation that he was instructed to engage in illegal conduct is undermined by his failure to report this conduct to any regulatory authority or law enforcement agency. An employee who genuinely believes that his employer is engaging in illegal conduct has an obligation to report such conduct to the appropriate authorities if he wishes to claim that he was constructively discharged. *See Roessler v. Novak*, 279 F. Supp. 2d 1245, 1257 (D. Fla. 2002) (holding that employee's failure to report alleged illegal conduct undermines claim of constructive discharge).

Bradley did not report the alleged instructions to resell unsafe products or alter invoices to any regulatory body, nor did he file a complaint with any law enforcement agency. This silence undermines the credibility of his allegations and supports the trial court's decision to grant the injunction.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY BALANCED THE HARDSHIPS

Bradley argues that the balance of hardships favors him because he will lose his livelihood if the injunction is enforced. However, this argument ignores the fact that Bradley voluntarily agreed to the noncompete covenant and voluntarily resigned to accept a position with a direct competitor. The hardship he faces is therefore of his own making.

Moreover, the employer will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not enforced. The noncompete covenant specifically protects the employer's relationships with its "active, paid up customers." Bradley is now working for a competitor and selling the same products to the same customers. The loss of these customer relationships cannot be adequately compensated by monetary damages because the damage is inherently speculative and the customers' loyalty, once lost, may be irretrievable. *See Wallach v. Boyden*, 541 F.2d 1046, 1053 (4th Cir. 1976) (holding that loss of customer relationships constitutes irreparable harm).

The trial court properly balanced these competing interests and correctly concluded that the employer's harm outweighs Bradley's hardship. This Court should defer to this balancing on appeal.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition respectfully requests that this Court reverse the decision of the appellate panel and reinstate the temporary injunction, or in the alternative, clarify the standards applicable on remand to ensure that the injunction may be properly enforced if Bradley fails to establish his defenses at trial.

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**NO.**

**DECISION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III argues that the trial court erred in granting the injunction because Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. failed to establish a substantial likelihood of success on the merits given Bradley’s defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. Health Coalition, Inc., in its rebuttal, argues that the trial court properly granted the injunction and that Bradley’s defenses are either procedurally barred, factually insufficient, or legally irrelevant. After reviewing the rebuttals presented by both parties and the applicable law, we hold that the trial court committed reversible error by entering the temporary injunction without adequately considering Bradley’s defenses. Accordingly, we affirm our preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTION**

Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy. To obtain such relief, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would not disserve the public interest. *City of Miami v. Gaita*, 65 So. 3d 94, 98 (Fla. 2011). The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of proof on these elements. *Id.*

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The central issue on appeal is whether Health Coalition met its burden of showing a substantial likelihood of success on the merits regarding the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. Bradley contends that Health Coalition’s material breach of the employment agreement and its constructive discharge of him excuse his performance under the covenant.

**A. Material Breach of Contract**

Bradley argues that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by wrongfully refusing to pay commissions he had earned. Health Coalition counters that the commissions were subject to clawback provisions and that the dispute arose post-termination, relying on *Capital Tax Corp. v. Gaba*, 560 So. 2d 292 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990), and *Radiology Prof'l Corp. v. Dinsmore*, 635 So. 2d 180 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994).

We find Health Coalition’s reliance on *Capital Tax* and *Radiology Prof'l Corp.* misplaced. While *Capital Tax* states that the right to enforce a restrictive covenant accrues upon termination and defenses must relate to circumstances existing at that time, the record here indicates that the commissions were *earned* during Bradley’s employment. The fact that the refusal to pay occurred after termination does not negate the fact that the obligation to pay arose from the employment relationship itself. *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d 1370, 1372 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991). In *Smith*, the court held that the employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that excused the employee from the noncompete provision. *Id.*

Health Coalition’s assertion that the commissions were not "earned" due to clawback provisions or delinquent accounts raises a genuine dispute of material fact. A dispute over "earned" commissions goes to the heart of the contractual relationship and constitutes a defense that must be resolved before the covenant can be enforced. *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d at 1372. Because the existence of a material breach is a factual prerequisite to the covenant’s enforceability, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a "substantial likelihood of success" on the merits while this dispute remains unresolved. The trial court’s failure to address this defense was error.

**B. Constructive Discharge and Breach of Good Faith**

Bradley further asserts that he was constructively discharged because he refused to engage in illegal and unethical conduct, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices. Health Coalition argues that Bradley failed to plead the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 183 So. 2d 550 (Fla. 2d DCA 1966), and that his constructive discharge claim fails due to delay and lack of reporting, citing *EEOC v. Hunt*, 718 F.2d 535 (11th Cir. 1983), and *Roessler v. Novak*, 279 F. Supp. 2d 1245 (D. Fla. 2002).

Regarding the procedural defense, while *Davis v. Monahan* holds that new defenses generally should not be raised for the first time at an injunction hearing, the defense of constructive discharge is inherently tied to the enforceability of the noncompete agreement itself. An employee is excused from a noncompete agreement if the employer’s conduct amounts to a constructive discharge or a breach of the implied covenant of good faith. *American Med. Sys. of Fla., Inc. v. Levin*, 638 So. 2d 1329, 1332 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994). Bradley’s sworn allegations that he was forced to resign due to illegal instructions are sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the circumstances of his termination.

Health Coalition’s argument regarding delay is unpersuasive. The record indicates Bradley resigned in April 1994, shortly after the alleged improper instructions were given. The fact that he did not file legal action until 1996 does not negate the claim that he was forced to resign in 1994. The delay in litigation does not equate to a delay in resignation sufficient to defeat a constructive discharge claim. Furthermore, Health Coalition’s reliance on *Cox v. CSX Intermodal, Inc.*, 730 So. 2d 328 (Fla. 1st DCA 1999), and *Green v. Florida Power & Light Co.*, 634 So. 2d 174 (Fla. 3d DCA 1994), to characterize the alleged instructions as mere business judgments is premature. At the temporary injunction stage, Bradley’s specific, sworn allegations of conduct that, if proven, would constitute illegal acts or a breach of the implied covenant of good faith, must be accepted as true for purposes of determining the likelihood of success on the merits. *Levin*, 638 So. 2d at 1332. The trial court was required to consider these defenses rather than dismissing them as unsubstantiated. *T.H.S. N. Fla., Inc. v. White*, 22 So. 3d 757, 762 (Fla. 4th DCA 2009).

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM AND BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS**

Even assuming Health Coalition could show some irreparable harm, such as the loss of customer goodwill, *Haddad v. Dabby*, 672 So. 2d 560, 563 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), the balance of hardships tips in Bradley’s favor. The loss of a livelihood is a significant hardship, particularly where the employee’s obligation to refrain from competing is predicated on a contract that the employer may have breached. *Criswell v. Brace*, 678 So. 2d 488, 492 (Fla. 2d DCA 1996). Where the employer’s conduct creates significant doubt regarding the validity of the noncompete, the equities favor allowing the employee to work pending a full trial on the merits.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction without first resolving Bradley’s defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. Because Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits given these defenses, the injunction cannot stand. We affirm the reversal of the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. On remand, the trial court may consider the specific arguments raised by Health Coalition regarding the pleading of the implied covenant defense and the applicability of clawback provisions, but must do so in the context of a full hearing on the merits.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**DECISION**

**I. STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTION**

Under Florida law, a temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy. To obtain such relief, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would not disserve the public interest. *City of Miami v. Gaita*, 65 So. 3d 94, 98 (Fla. 2011). The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of proof on these elements. *Id.*

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The central issue on appeal is whether Health Coalition met its burden of showing a substantial likelihood of success on the merits regarding the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. Bradley contends that Health Coalition’s material breach of the employment agreement and its constructive discharge of him excuse his performance under the covenant.

**A. Material Breach of Contract**

Bradley argues that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by wrongfully refusing to pay commissions he had earned. Health Coalition counters that the commissions were subject to clawback provisions and that the dispute arose post-termination, relying on *Capital Tax Corp. v. Gaba*, 560 So. 2d 292 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990), and *Radiology Prof'l Corp. v. Dinsmore*, 635 So. 2d 180 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994).

We find Health Coalition’s reliance on *Capital Tax* and *Radiology Prof'l Corp.* misplaced. While *Capital Tax* states that the right to enforce a restrictive covenant accrues upon termination and defenses must relate to circumstances existing at that time, the record here indicates that the commissions were *earned* during Bradley’s employment. The fact that the refusal to pay occurred after termination does not negate the fact that the obligation to pay arose from the employment relationship itself. *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d 1370, 1372 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991). In *Smith*, the court held that the employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that excused the employee from the noncompete provision. *Id.*

Health Coalition’s assertion that the commissions were not "earned" due to clawback provisions or delinquent accounts raises a genuine dispute of material fact. A dispute over "earned" commissions goes to the heart of the contractual relationship and constitutes a defense that must be resolved before the covenant can be enforced. *W. D. R. Enterprises, Inc. v. Smith*, 577 So. 2d at 1372. Because the existence of a material breach is a factual prerequisite to the covenant’s enforceability, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a "substantial likelihood of success" on the merits while this dispute remains unresolved. The trial court’s failure to address this defense was error.

**B. Constructive Discharge and Breach of Good Faith**

Novak*, 279 F. Supp. 2d 1245 (D. Fla. 2002).

Regarding the procedural defense, while *Davis v. Monahan* holds that new defenses generally should not be raised for the first time at an injunction hearing, the defense of constructive discharge is inherently tied to the enforceability of the noncompete agreement itself. An employee is excused from a noncompete agreement if the employer’s conduct amounts to a constructive discharge or a breach of the implied covenant of good faith. *American Med. Sys. of Fla., Inc. v. Levin*, 638 So. 2d 1329, 1332 (Fla. 1st DCA 1994). Bradley’s sworn allegations that he was forced to resign due to illegal instructions are sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the circumstances of his termination.

Health Coalition’s argument regarding delay is unpersuasive. The record indicates Bradley resigned in April 1994, shortly after the alleged improper instructions were given. The fact that he did not file legal action until 1996 does not negate the claim that he was forced to resign in 1994. The delay in litigation does not equate to a delay in resignation sufficient to defeat a constructive discharge claim. Furthermore, Health Coalition’s reliance on *Cox v. CSX Intermodal, Inc.*, 730 So. 2d 328 (Fla. 1st DCA 1999), and *Green v. Florida Power & Light Co.*, 634 So. 2d 174 (Fla. 3d DCA 1994), to characterize the alleged instructions as mere business judgments is premature. At the temporary injunction stage, Bradley’s specific, sworn allegations of conduct that, if proven, would constitute illegal acts or a breach of the implied covenant of good faith, must be accepted as true for purposes of determining the likelihood of success on the merits. *Levin*, 638 So. 2d at 1332. The trial court was required to consider these defenses rather than dismissing them as unsubstantiated. *T.H.S. N. Fla., Inc. v. White*, 22 So. 3d 757, 762 (Fla. 4th DCA 2009).

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM AND BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS**

Even assuming Health Coalition could show some irreparable harm, such as the loss of customer goodwill, *Haddad v. Dabby*, 672 So. 2d 560, 563 (Fla. 4th DCA 1996), the balance of hardships tips in Bradley’s favor. The loss of a livelihood is a significant hardship, particularly where the employee’s obligation to refrain from competing is predicated on a contract that the employer may have breached. *Criswell v. Brace*, 678 So. 2d 488, 492 (Fla. 2d DCA 1996). Where the employer’s conduct creates significant doubt regarding the validity of the noncompete, the equities favor allowing the employee to work pending a full trial on the merits.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction without first resolving Bradley’s defenses of material breach and constructive discharge. Because Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits given these defenses, the injunction cannot stand. We affirm the reversal of the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. On remand, the trial court may consider the specific arguments raised by Health Coalition regarding the pleading of the implied covenant defense and the applicability of clawback provisions, but must do so in the context of a full hearing on the merits.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
