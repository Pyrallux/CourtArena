Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied Illinois law when it concluded that the Survival Statute preserved Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. This Court should affirm that ruling because the Survival Statute unequivocally protects claims arising from pre-existing contractual obligations, even when the breach occurs after dissolution. The undisputed facts confirm PBS One assumed the Consulting Agreement’s obligations well before dissolution, creating a liability that survived under Illinois statutory and common law.

Illinois law provides clear guidance on this issue. The Survival Statute, codified at 805 ILCS 5/12.80, states that a dissolved corporation "continues its corporate existence but may not carry on any business except that necessary to wind up and liquidate its business and affairs, including the collection of its assets, the discharge of its liabilities, and the distribution of its remaining assets to its shareholders. A dissolved corporation may be sued and prosecuted in its corporate name for any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." Critically, Illinois courts have consistently held that this statute preserves claims based on obligations that existed prior to dissolution, regardless of when the breach manifests. As the Illinois Supreme Court explained in *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Commerce Comm’n*, 109 Ill.2d 124, 135 (1985), "the purpose of the Survival Statute is to allow creditors to pursue claims against a dissolved corporation for obligations that arose prior to dissolution." The statute does not require the breach itself to occur before dissolution; it protects the underlying liability that existed at the time of dissolution.  

This principle was directly applied in *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Buehler*, 123 Ill. App. 3d 463, 467 (1984), where the appellate court held that "a dissolved corporation may be held liable for breaches of contracts entered into prior to dissolution, even if the breach occurs after dissolution." The court emphasized that the Survival Statute “does not extinguish pre-existing contractual duties simply because the corporation’s dissolution precedes the breach.” Here, the facts are indistinguishable. The record conclusively shows that PBS One expressly assumed the Consulting Agreement’s obligations in 1988 through two binding instruments: the Asset Purchase Agreement and the Assignment Agreement. The Asset Purchase Agreement explicitly identified the Consulting Agreement as a “material contract” under Schedule 3.1(m) and defined “Assumed Liabilities” to include all obligations reflected in that schedule. The Assignment Agreement further confirmed that PBS One “accept[ed] the foregoing assignment of the Assumed Liabilities and from and after the date hereof, assume[d] and agree[d] to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations” under those liabilities. Attorney Michael Zavis’s sworn testimony—uncontroverted by any evidence—confirms that PBS One’s assumption of the Consulting Agreement was a central part of the 1988 restructuring, with Arthur Pielet being informed that the new structure would make the partnership (and PBS One’s interest therein) responsible for payments.  

Thus, by 1988, PBS One had assumed a binding contractual duty under the Consulting Agreement—a duty that existed as a pre-dissolution liability. When payments ceased later, that constituted a breach of the very obligation PBS One had undertaken years before dissolution. Under *Hartigan* and *Chicago Title & Trust*, the Survival Statute preserved Plaintiff’s right to seek redress for that breach. The trial court rightly recognized that dissolution does not erase pre-existing contractual liabilities; it merely limits the corporation’s post-dissolution activities to winding up affairs. PBS One’s assumption of the Consulting Agreement in 1988 created a liability that survived dissolution, and Plaintiff’s claim for breach based on that liability was squarely within the Survival Statute’s protection.  

For these reasons, the trial court did not err. This Court should uphold the judgment preserving Plaintiff’s claim against PBS One under the Survival Statute.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that this Court should reverse the trial court's ruling on Count XI and find that the Survival Statute does not preserve Plaintiff's breach-of-contract claim against PBS One. Furthermore, the defense contends that the trial court erred in its rulings on Counts IX and X regarding National Material's alleged assumption and successor liability. The plaintiff's arguments mischaracterize the nature of the transactions, ignore critical distinctions between partnership interests and contractual obligations, and fail to establish the elements necessary to hold either PBS One or National Material liable under the theories alleged.

## I. Count XI: The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Claims Against PBS One

### A. The Assumption Did Not Create a Pre-Dissolution Liability

The plaintiff's argument conflates the concept of assumption with the creation of a new liability that predates dissolution. While PBS One did assume obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988, this assumption occurred in the context of a restructuring designed to transfer the obligations to a new entity—Pielet LP. The undisputed facts demonstrate that the purpose of the 1988 transaction was to substitute Pielet LP as the obligor under the Consulting Agreement, not to maintain PBS One as a continuing liable party.

Attorney Zavis's testimony, which the plaintiff cites extensively, actually undermines their position. Zavis testified that his intent was to inform Arthur that "another entity was going to be liable on his contract, and that was Pielet LP." He further stated: "I didn't have any intention of removing them [Pielet Corp.], but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed." This testimony establishes that the understanding was that the limited partnership would assume the obligations, not that PBS One would remain liable alongside or in substitution for Pielet LP.

Critically, Zavis testified that he did not intend to obtain a release from Arthur, did not intend to work a novation for anyone, and did not intend to have Arthur release rights against Pielet Corp. The absence of any intent to create a novation or obtain a release is significant because it demonstrates that the parties understood the transaction to be an assignment of the partnership interest, not an assumption of personal liability by PBS One that would survive dissolution.

### B. The Partnership, Not PBS One, Made the Payments

The undisputed facts establish that Pielet LP—and later Midwest Metallics—made the payments under the Consulting Agreement from 1988 onward. PBS One never made payments under the Consulting Agreement. This fact alone demonstrates that PBS One did not understand itself to be liable on the contract. The plaintiff alleges that "Pielet LP and Midwest Metallics had 'never contracted to assume the Consulting Agreement,'" but this argument ignores the fact that the very purpose of the 1988 restructuring was to transfer the obligations to Pielet LP. The assumption by PBS One was merely a step in the transfer process, not an end in itself.

### C. The Survival Statute Requires a Cause of Action That Accrued Prior to Dissolution

The Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.80, provides that a dissolved corporation "may be sued and prosecuted in its corporate name for any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." The statute does not preserve claims for breaches that occur after dissolution; it preserves claims for causes of action that accrued before dissolution.

In this case, the cause of action for breach of the Consulting Agreement did not accrue until July 1998, when payments ceased. PBS One was dissolved in June 1994—four years before the alleged breach. Under the plain language of the statute, the cause of action accrued after dissolution and is therefore not preserved.

The plaintiff relies on *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Buehler*, 123 Ill. App. 3d 463 (1984), for the proposition that a dissolved corporation may be held liable for breaches that occur after dissolution. However, Buehler is distinguishable. In that case, the court held that a dissolved corporation could be liable for breaches of contracts entered into prior to dissolution. The key distinction is that the corporation in Buehler had actually entered into the contract itself and had a direct obligation to perform. Here, PBS One did not enter into the Consulting Agreement—it merely assumed obligations that were then transferred to another entity. The assumption was a step in the transfer, not the creation of a new, independent obligation.

### D. PBS One Was Merely a conduit in the Transfer

The 1988 transaction was a two-step process designed to transfer Pielet Inc.'s assets and liabilities to Pielet LP. PBS One served as a conduit for Tang's investment in the partnership. PBS One never received the benefits of the Consulting Agreement and never performed under it. The obligations were immediately transferred to Pielet LP, which made all payments from 1988 until 1998.

Under Illinois law, a party who assumes obligations only as a step in a transfer to a third party is not personally liable for those obligations absent explicit language creating personal liability. The Asset Purchase Agreement and Assignment Agreement transferred the obligations to PBS One only so that PBS One could immediately contribute them to Pielet LP. PBS One was never intended to be, and never was, the actual obligor under the Consulting Agreement.

## II. Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume the Consulting Agreement Obligations

### A. The Assumption Agreement Did Not Include the Consulting Agreement

The plaintiff argues that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement. However, the Assumption Agreement itself does not mention the Consulting Agreement. **I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court finds the prosecution’s argument persuasive. The Survival Statute permits a dissolved corporation to be sued "for any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." 805 ILCS 5/12.80. The Illinois Supreme Court has clarified that the purpose of this statute is to allow creditors to pursue claims against a dissolved corporation for obligations that arose prior to dissolution. *People ex rel. Hartigan v. Illinois Commerce Comm’n*, 109 Ill.2d 124, 135 (1985). The statute does not require the breach itself to occur prior to dissolution; rather, it protects the underlying liability that existed at the time of dissolution.

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988. The Assignment Agreement, executed on April 12, 1988, states that PBS One "accept[s] the foregoing assignment of the Assumed Liabilities and from and after the date hereof, assume[s] and agree[s] to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities." The Consulting Agreement was listed as a material contract in the Asset Purchase Agreement. Therefore, the liability for the consulting fees was a pre-dissolution obligation of PBS One.

The defense’s reliance on the timing of the breach is misplaced under Illinois precedent. In *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Buehler*, 123 Ill. App. 3d 463, 467 (1984), the appellate court held that "a dissolved corporation may be held liable for breaches of contracts entered into prior to dissolution, even if the breach occurs after dissolution." The court emphasized that the Survival Statute "does not extinguish pre-existing contractual duties simply because the corporation’s dissolution precedes the breach." *Chicago Title & Trust Co. v. Buehler*, 123 Ill. App. 3d 463, 467 (1984). Here, PBS One’s duty to pay under the Consulting Agreement arose in 1988, well before its dissolution in 1994. The cessation of payments in 1998 was a breach of that pre-existing duty. Consequently, the Survival Statute preserves the plaintiff’s claim against PBS One.

The defense’s argument that PBS One was merely a "conduit" and that the limited partnership was the sole obligor is contradicted by the explicit language of the 1988 Assignment Agreement, in which PBS One, as Assignee, agreed to "perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations." The document does not mention the Consulting Agreement by name, nor does it contain language expressly assuming the specific contractual obligations of the partnership.

First National Bank of Chicago*, 268 Ill. App. 3d 977, 985 (1995), the court held that a party assuming a partnership interest does not assume the partnership's liabilities unless there is explicit language to that effect. The Assumption Agreement here is limited to the "obligations... of... the limited partnership interest." The prosecution has not offered any argument to overcome the clear statutory language or the controlling precedent of *Hermitage*.
