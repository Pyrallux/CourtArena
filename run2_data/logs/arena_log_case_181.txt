The Fourth Amendment requires that searches be supported by probable cause, which depends significantly on the reliability of an informant's tip. However, disclosure of an informant's identity is not required unless the defendant shows that the informant's testimony is essential to challenging the probable cause finding. As this Court has recognized, while *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), established that disclosure may be warranted when an informant's testimony is relevant and helpful to the defense, the standard for suppression hearings is more stringent. Suppression hearings address only the lawfulness of the search under the Fourth Amendment, not guilt or innocence. Thus, disclosure is justified only when the informant's identity directly affects the probable cause determination.  

Here, the trial court heard testimony that the informant had provided accurate tips in the past, establishing reliability through corroboration. In *United States v. Salinas*, 377 F.3d 541 (5th Cir. 2004), the Fifth Circuit held that "when the government has corroborated the informant's tip through independent police investigation, disclosure of the informant's identity is not required for a suppression hearing." This principle applies directly: the past accuracy of the informant's tips constituted sufficient corroboration to support probable cause, rendering disclosure unnecessary.  

The appellant's argument that disclosure was needed for an entrapment defense is irrelevant to the suppression issue. As the Supreme Court confirmed in *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973), entrapment is an affirmative defense concerning government inducement and predisposition, which goes solely to guilt or innocence and has no bearing on whether a search was lawful under the Fourth Amendment. ## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's opening argument mischaracterizes the applicable legal standard and ignores critical facts that establish the necessity of disclosing the confidential informant's identity. ## I. THE STANDARD FOR DISCLOSURE AT SUPPRESSION HEARINGS

The prosecution correctly acknowledges that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), governs the disclosure analysis. In *Rovaro*, the Supreme Court held that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity "has limited application" and must give way when the informant's testimony "may be important to a fair determination of the cause." The Court emphasized that the trial court's discretion must be exercised "in the light of the facts and circumstances of the particular case" and that "the informer's privilege may yield" when disclosure "is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957).

The prosecution argues that a "more stringent" standard applies at suppression hearings because such hearings address only the lawfulness of the search under the Fourth Amendment, not guilt or innocence. However, this argument conflates the purpose of the hearing with the nature of the inquiry. The suppression hearing required the trial court to determine whether probable cause existed to support the search. The reliability of the informant's tip was therefore central—not tangential—to the suppression analysis. As this Court has recognized, "the identity of an informant may be relevant and helpful to the defense when the issue is the existence of probable cause." *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911, 915 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989).

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Salinas*, 377 F.3d 541 (5th Cir. 2004), is misplaced. In *Salinas*, the court found disclosure unnecessary because the informant's tip was corroborated by independent police investigation—not merely by the informant's past record of accurate tips. Here, the trial court specifically stated it had "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This constitutes mere self-serving characterization of past tip accuracy, not the kind of independent corroboration contemplated by *Salinas*. The prosecution conflates the general reliability of an informant with corroboration of the specific tip at issue.

## II. THE DEFENSE MADE THE REQUISITE SHOWING FOR DISCLOSURE

The defense below established that disclosure was necessary for a fair determination of the suppression issue. Defense counsel specifically argued that the confidential informant "was with appellant that day" and that "her belongings were found in his car when it was released from evidence." This directly relates to the reliability of the informant's tip and the probable cause determination.

In *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989), this Court held that disclosure was required where the informant was a participant in the transaction that formed the basis of the search. The Court reasoned that "when the informant has participated in the crime, his identity is material to the defense because he may have knowledge of facts bearing on the question of guilt or innocence, and his relationship with the accused may be relevant to the issue of probable cause." *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d at 915. The facts here are indistinguishable: the confidential informant was with the appellant on the day in question, and her belongings were found in his car—facts that directly bear on whether the informant was a credible source or potentially fabricated the tip.

The prosecution argues that the informant's belongings in the car "does not challenge the officer's testimony about the tip's reliability." This is incorrect. If the informant was present at the transaction and her belongings were in the vehicle, this creates a compelling argument that the informant may have had motivation to fabricate the tip or that her information was not based on independent observation but on her own participation. This goes directly to the heart of probable cause.

The prosecution's argument regarding the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress is a red herring that should not bar this Court's review. The prosecution argued below that because the motion to suppress was "dispositive," the appellant could not raise the disclosure issue. Moreover, the prosecution's characterization of the "dispositive" motion misunderstands the nature of the proceedings. A plea agreement contingent on the outcome of a suppression motion does not strip a defendant of his constitutional right to challenge the lawfulness of the search. The appellant retained the right to litigate the suppression issue and, upon losing that motion, to challenge the denial on appeal. The "dispositive" nature of the motion affected only the plea outcome, not the substantive rights at issue.

The prosecution cannot cherry-pick one basis for disclosure while ignoring the other. The defense's argument that "if she set him up, it goes to her reliability" properly connects the informant's potential participation in the crime to the probable cause determination.

In *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that "the Constitution prohibits the exclusion of defense evidence under rules that serve no legitimate purpose or that are arbitrary." A rule that would categorically deny disclosure of an informant who may have participated in the crime, solely because the defendant also intends to raise an entrapment defense at trial, would be arbitrary and serve no legitimate purpose.

The trial court erred by requiring the defense to show that disclosure would "impact" the probable cause analysis, rather than applying the *Roviaro* standard of whether the informant's testimony would be "relevant and helpful" or "essential to a fair determination" of the cause. The correct inquiry is whether the informant's identity would shed light on the reliability of the tip—not whether disclosure would definitively change the outcome.

As this Court stated in *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911 (Okla. Crim. Here, the trial court failed to conduct any such balancing. The court simply found the informant reliable based on past tips without considering whether her potential participation in the crime undermined that reliability.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI) during a hearing on a motion to suppress evidence. The Appellant argues that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the probable cause determination. The Appellee argues that the CI’s reliability was sufficiently established through past corroboration and that the disclosure request was improperly tied to issues of guilt and innocence irrelevant to the suppression hearing. After reviewing the arguments and the provided factual record, the Court finds that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The privilege of the government to withhold the identity of an informant is not absolute. As established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), "the informer's privilege may yield where, in the opinion of the court, the disclosure of an informer's identity... is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957). This standard requires a balancing test where the court must weigh the public interest in protecting the flow of information against the individual's right to prepare a defense. *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911, 915 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion**

The prosecution argues that because the motion to suppress was "dispositive," the inquiry was strictly limited to the legality of the search under the Fourth Amendment, and therefore, defenses such as entrapment—which go to guilt or innocence—were irrelevant. The prosecution correctly cites *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973), for the proposition that entrapment is an affirmative defense concerning government inducement and does not negate the legality of the search.

However, the defense did not seek disclosure solely for an entrapment defense. Defense counsel explicitly argued that the CI’s reliability was "important even on a Motion to Suppress." The defense contended that if the CI "set him up," this fact goes directly to the CI's reliability, which is a foundational element of the probable cause analysis. The trial court’s characterization of these arguments as "defense matters for trial" ignored the critical link between the CI’s potential bias or participation and the validity of the tip provided to law enforcement. Therefore, the "dispositive" nature of the motion did not strip the Appellant of the right to challenge the credibility of the source of the probable cause.

**B. Reliability, Corroboration, and Participation**

The trial court denied the motion to disclose, finding that the CI’s reliability had been established through "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The prosecution relies on *United States v. Salinas*, 377 F.3d 541 (5th Cir. 2004), and *State v. Smith*, 585 S.E.2d 260 (S.C. 2003), to argue that corroboration of a tip eliminates the need for disclosure. While it is true that "when the government has corroborated the informant's tip through independent police investigation, disclosure of the informant's identity is not required," *United States v. Salinas*, 377 F.3d 541, 545 (5th Cir. 2004), the record here indicates that the corroboration relied upon was the CI's general history of accuracy, rather than independent police verification of the specific facts of the instant tip.

Furthermore, the defense presented "new information" that the CI was with the Appellant on the day of the arrest and that the CI's belongings were found in the Appellant's car. This evidence suggests that the CI was not merely a passive observer providing a tip, but a participant in the events. Under *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989), the Court held that "when the informant has participated in the crime, his identity is material to the defense because he may have knowledge of facts bearing on the question of guilt or innocence, and his relationship with the accused may be relevant to the issue of probable cause." *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911, 915 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989).

The presence of the CI’s belongings in the car creates a substantial question regarding whether the CI had a motive to fabricate the tip or frame the Appellant. This potential bias or participation is directly relevant to the reliability of the tip, which is the cornerstone of the probable cause determination. The trial court failed to conduct the balancing test mandated by *Roviaro*, instead relying solely on the CI’s past record. As noted in *People v. Allen*, 523 N.E.2d 575 (Ill. 1988), a defendant must show a "specific need for the informant's identity beyond mere general relevance." *People v. Allen*, 523 N.E.2d 575, 578 (Ill. 1988). The Appellant met this burden by presenting evidence that the CI was a participant whose belongings were found at the scene, thereby establishing a specific need to explore the CI's credibility and potential bias.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred by denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. While the prosecution correctly identified the limited scope of a suppression hearing, it failed to acknowledge that the informant's potential participation and bias are central to the probable cause inquiry. The defense established a specific need for the disclosure under *Roviaro* and *Bodin* by showing the CI was a material witness to the events. The general reliability established by past tips was insufficient to override the Appellant's right to a fair determination of the suppression issue.

Accordingly, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The trial court properly considered the informant's past reliability in establishing probable cause. An informant's history of providing accurate tips is sufficient to establish reliability without disclosure of identity. United States v. Harris, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971). State courts have consistently held that when an informant's reliability is established through past performance, disclosure is not required. State v. Smith, 585 S.E.2d 260, 263 (S.C. 2003). Here, the trial court heard testimony that the informant's past tips were "effective" and "accurate," which constitutes reliable corroboration of the tip's validity. The defense's assertion that this constitutes "mere self-serving characterization" ignores settled precedent.  

The defense's reliance on the informant's belongings found in the vehicle after the search is irrelevant to the probable cause analysis. Probable cause must be judged based on information available to law enforcement at the time of the search, not post hoc evidence. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 231 (1983). The presence of the informant's belongings was unknown to officers during the search and cannot retroactively affect the probable cause determination. The defense's speculative claims about potential bias or fabrication do not alter this analysis, as they are not tied to the officers' knowledge at the critical moment.  

The defense failed to meet the "specific need" standard required for disclosure. As held in People v. Allen, "a defendant must show a specific need for the informant's identity beyond mere general relevance." People v. Allen, 523 N.E.2d 575, 578 (Ill. 1988). The defense offered only vague allegations about the informant's presence and belongings, which do not demonstrate how disclosure would impact the Fourth Amendment inquiry. Roviaro does not require disclosure in every case where the informant is present; the Supreme Court emphasized that disclosure is required only when the informant's identity is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957). Here, the informant's identity was neither relevant nor helpful to the suppression issue, as it did not bear on whether officers had reasonable grounds for the search at the time it occurred.  

The defense's citation to Bodin v. State is inapposite. That case involved an informant who actively participated in the criminal transaction, not merely was present at the scene. Bodin v. State, 783 P.2d 911, 915 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989). Here, the defense presented no evidence that the informant actively participated in the crime—only that she was present and her belongings were later found in the vehicle. This distinction is critical; the defense's speculative claims about potential bias do not satisfy Bodin's participation requirement.  

The trial court correctly denied disclosure. The defense's arguments improperly sought to inject guilt/innocence issues into the suppression hearing and failed to demonstrate a specific need for disclosure. The court of appeals properly affirmed this ruling.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense regarding the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The issue remains whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion. The Appellant argues that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the probable cause determination, citing new evidence that the CI was present and her belongings were found in the Appellant’s car. The Appellee argues that the motion to suppress was dispositive and limited to the Fourth Amendment inquiry, and that the Appellant failed to establish a "specific need" for disclosure because the evidence of the CI's presence was unknown to the officers at the time of the search and therefore irrelevant to the probable cause analysis.

Upon careful consideration of the arguments and the provided factual record, this Court finds that the trial court did not abuse its discretion. The prosecution’s rebuttal correctly identifies that the Appellant’s request for disclosure is improperly tethered to a defense of entrapment or guilt, rather than the legality of the search, and that the "new information" regarding the CI’s belongings does not retroactively undermine the probable cause determination. Accordingly, the preliminary ruling in favor of the Appellant is withdrawn, and the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is affirmed.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The privilege of the government to withhold the identity of an informant is not absolute. As established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), "the informer's privilege may yield where, in the opinion of the court, the disclosure of an informer's identity... is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957). However, this privilege is recognized to protect the public interest in effective law enforcement. To overcome this privilege, a defendant must demonstrate a "specific need" for the informant's identity. *People v. Allen*, 523 N.E.2d 575, 578 (Ill. 1988).

In the context of a motion to suppress, the inquiry is limited to whether the law enforcement officers had probable cause to believe a crime was being committed. Probable cause is determined based on the totality of the circumstances known to the officers at the time of the search. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983). Furthermore, an informant’s past reliability can be sufficient to establish probable cause. *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion and the Probable Cause Inquiry**

The trial court proceeded on a "dispositive" motion to suppress, meaning the outcome of the suppression hearing would determine the fate of the case without a full trial on the merits. The prosecution correctly argues that this limits the scope of the inquiry to the legality of the search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment. Defenses such as entrapment, which go to the defendant's culpability rather than the legality of the officer's actions, are irrelevant to this specific inquiry. *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423, 429 (1973).

The defense attempts to frame its request for the CI's identity as a challenge to the "reliability" of the informant, asserting that if the CI "set him up," the tip was unreliable. While the reliability of an informant is indeed a component of the probable cause analysis, the defense’s argument conflates the *factual* determination of guilt with the *legal* determination of probable cause. The defense’s assertion that the CI "set him up" is fundamentally an entrapment or innocence argument, not a Fourth Amendment argument. As the prosecution notes, the fact that a CI may have induced a defendant or was present during the crime does not negate the officer's probable cause to arrest or search at the time, provided the officer acted in good faith reliance on the information provided.

**B. The Relevance of Post-Search Evidence to Probable Cause**

The crux of the defense’s argument rests on "new information" that the CI was with the Appellant and that her belongings were found in his car. The defense contends this evidence suggests the CI was a participant and therefore biased, necessitating disclosure to test her reliability under *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989).

However, the prosecution’s rebuttal correctly applies the standard set forth in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). Probable cause must be judged based on the information available to law enforcement at the time of the search, not on post hoc evidence discovered later. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231 (1983). The presence of the CI’s belongings in the car was unknown to the officers at the time they conducted the search. Therefore, this fact cannot be used to retroactively invalidate the probable cause that existed based on the officers' knowledge of the CI's past reliability.

The defense argues that the CI’s potential bias is relevant to the "reliability" factor of the probable cause analysis regardless of when it is discovered. This misapprehends the law. The "reliability" assessed in a probable cause inquiry is the reliability *as perceived by the officers* based on the informant's track record and the corroboration available at the time. *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971). The trial court heard testimony that the CI had provided effective and accurate tips in the past. This established the CI's reliability for the purposes of the probable cause determination. The fact that the CI may have had a private motive to fabricate the tip in this specific instance—a motive unknown to the officers at the time—does not negate the objective reasonableness of the officers' reliance on her past history.

**C. The "Specific Need" Requirement and Participation**

The defense relies heavily on *Bodin v. State*, arguing that the CI’s presence and belongings in the car constitute "participation" in the crime, triggering a requirement for disclosure. The prosecution distinguishes *Bodin* by noting that it involved an informant who actively participated in the criminal transaction (e.g., a buy-bust operation), whereas here the evidence only suggests the CI was present.

This Court finds the prosecution’s distinction persuasive. Even assuming the CI was present, the defense has not demonstrated how disclosure of her identity is "relevant and helpful" to the *suppression* issue, as opposed to the *guilt* issue. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957). The defense’s need for the CI stems from a desire to prove that the CI "set him up" or that the drugs belonged to the CI. These are defenses to the charge of possession, not challenges to the legality of the search. Because the motion to suppress was dispositive, the trial court was within its discretion to exclude evidence relating to guilt or innocence.

Furthermore, the defense failed to meet the "specific need" standard articulated in *People v. Allen*, 523 N.E.2d 575 (Ill. 1988). The defense offered only speculative allegations that the CI’s belongings in the car implied a motive to fabricate. This does not constitute a specific need for disclosure that would overcome the informer's privilege in the context of a suppression hearing, where the officers had independent grounds to rely on the CI’s tip based on her past performance.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The defense’s arguments regarding the CI’s reliability and participation are predicated on evidence that was unknown to the officers at the time of the search and therefore irrelevant to the probable cause determination. The "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress properly limited the inquiry to the Fourth Amendment issues, excluding the defense’s theories of entrapment or actual innocence which are properly reserved for trial. The prosecution’s rebuttal successfully demonstrates that the trial court’s ruling was consistent with the requirements of *Roviaro*, *Gates*, and *Harris*.

Accordingly, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to order disclosure, arguing that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the probable cause determination. Specifically, the Appellant relies on "new information" discovered after the search—that the CI was present and her belongings were found in the Appellant’s car—to assert that the CI may have "set him up," thereby rendering her tip unreliable. The Appellee counters that the motion to suppress was dispositive and limited to the legality of the search under the Fourth Amendment, and that the Appellant’s arguments regarding the CI’s potential bias or participation relate solely to the issue of guilt, which was not the subject of the hearing.

After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the factual record provided, this Court finds that the trial court did not abuse its discretion. The Appellant’s request for disclosure is predicated on evidence that was unknown to the officers at the time of the search and therefore cannot retroactively undermine the probable cause determination. Furthermore, the "dispositive" nature of the proceeding properly confined the inquiry to the Fourth Amendment issue, excluding the Appellant’s entrapment and innocence theories. Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The government’s privilege to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is well-established but not absolute. As held by the Supreme Court in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957), "the informer's privilege may yield where, in the opinion of the court, the disclosure of an informer's identity... is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." To overcome this privilege, a defendant must demonstrate a "specific need" for the informant's identity. *People v. Allen*, 523 N.E.2d 575, 578 (Ill. 1988).

In the context of a motion to suppress, the reviewing court must determine whether the law enforcement officers had probable cause to conduct the search. Probable cause exists when the facts and circumstances within the officers' knowledge are sufficient to warrant a prudent person in believing that the suspect has committed or is committing an offense. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983). This assessment is based on the "totality of the circumstances" known to the officers at the time of the search, not on hindsight. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231 (1983). Additionally, an informant’s track record of providing reliable information is a significant factor in establishing probable cause. *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion and the Probable Cause Inquiry**

The trial court proceeded on a "dispositive" motion to suppress, pursuant to an agreement between the State and the defense. The prosecution correctly argues that this agreement limited the scope of the hearing to the single issue of whether the search and seizure were lawful under the Fourth Amendment. The Appellant’s attempt to introduce evidence that the CI "set him up" or that the drugs belonged to the CI constitutes a defense to the charge of possession (e.g., entrapment or lack of knowledge), rather than a challenge to the legality of the officer’s actions.

As the prosecution noted in its rebuttal, if the Appellant wished to litigate issues of guilt or innocence, the proper forum was a trial, not a suppression hearing. The defense counsel’s acknowledgment that the motion was "dispositive" and that the understanding was that the court would decide the sentencing range based on the ruling confirms that the parties agreed to forgo a trial on the merits. Therefore, the trial court acted within its discretion in refusing to expand the scope of the hearing to consider the Appellant’s entrapment defense.

**B. The Relevance of Post-Search Evidence to Probable Cause**

The Appellant’s primary argument for disclosure rests on "new information"—specifically, that the CI’s belongings were found in the Appellant’s car and that she was present on the day of the arrest. The Appellant contends this evidence suggests the CI was a participant and therefore biased, necessitating disclosure to test her reliability under *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989).

This argument fails because it improperly relies on hindsight to assess probable cause. The standard for probable cause is objective; it looks to the information available to the officers at the time of the search. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231 (1983). The record indicates that the officers relied on the CI’s "effectiveness of her past tips" and the "accuracy of them" to establish probable cause. *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971). The fact that the CI’s belongings were later found in the car was unknown to the officers at the time they initiated the search. Consequently, this fact could not have factored into their calculation of probable cause.

The defense argues that the CI’s potential bias is relevant to the "reliability" factor regardless of when it is discovered. However, the "reliability" inquiry in a probable cause analysis focuses on the reliability *as perceived by the officers* at the time. *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971). The officers’ reliance on the CI’s established history of accurate tips was reasonable. The Appellant’s speculation that the CI had a private motive to fabricate the tip in this specific instance—a motive unknown to the officers at the time—does not negate the objective reasonableness of the officers’ reliance on her past performance.

**C. The "Specific Need" Requirement and Participation**

The Appellant asserts a "specific need" for the CI’s identity under *Roviaro* and *People v. Allen*, claiming the CI was a material witness to the alleged crime. While *Bodin v. State*, 783 P.2d 911 (Okla. Crim. App. 1989), supports disclosure when an informant participates in the crime, the Appellant has not demonstrated how disclosure is "relevant and helpful" to the *suppression* issue, as opposed to the *guilt* issue. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957).

The defense’s need for the CI stems from a desire to prove that the CI "set him up" or that the drugs belonged to the CI. These are defenses to the charge of possession, not challenges to the legality of the search. Because the motion to suppress was dispositive, the trial court was within its discretion to exclude evidence relating to guilt or innocence. The defense failed to show that the CI’s identity was necessary to challenge the officers' probable cause, given that the officers had independent grounds to rely on the CI’s tip based on her past reliability.

The defense’s argument that it faces a "Catch-22"—unable to prove unreliability without disclosure—is unpersuasive in this context. The law does not require the State to disclose an informant’s identity simply to allow the defendant to conduct a fishing expedition for potential bias that was unknown to the officers at the time of the search. The "specific need" standard requires a showing that the informant’s testimony is essential to a fair determination of the issue before the court. Here, the issue was the legality of the search, which the Appellant failed to undermine with the evidence presented.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The Appellant’s arguments regarding the CI’s reliability and participation are based on evidence that was unknown to the officers at the time of the search and therefore irrelevant to the probable cause determination. The "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress properly limited the inquiry to the Fourth Amendment issues, excluding the Appellant’s theories of entrapment or actual innocence. The prosecution’s rebuttal successfully demonstrates that the trial court’s ruling was consistent with the requirements of *Roviaro*, *Gates*, and *Harris*.

Accordingly, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is affirmed.
