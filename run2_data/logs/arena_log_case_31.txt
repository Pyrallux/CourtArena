FACTS:
Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. Judgment of conviction was entered upon his plea of guilty after the charge against him was reduced from aggravated battery, as origi*380nally alleged. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years in custody of the Board of Correction. He later filed a motion under I.C.R. 35, seeking reduction of the sentence so he could be released for psychotherapy that he claimed was unavailable in confinement. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

 However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

Mr. Preston Galbraith, through counsel, respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the State's argument and in support of his appeal from the district court's order denying his Rule 35 motion to reduce sentence. ---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN DENYING THE RULE 35 MOTION

### A. The Legal Standard for Rule 35 Relief

The defense acknowledges that Idaho Criminal Rule 35 provides that a motion to reduce a sentence is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). However, this discretion is not unlimited and must be exercised based upon consideration of the proper factors. *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984). When the district court exercises its discretion, it must weigh all relevant considerations, including the defendant's rehabilitative potential, the availability of treatment programs, and the constitutional prohibitions against cruel and unusual punishment.

### B. The Indeterminate Sentence Framework and Parole Considerations

The defense notes that when an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement for appellate review purposes is generally treated as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982). Here, Mr. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years, which the State characterizes as providing an opportunity for parole. However, the defense submits that this characterization obscures the reality that the actual time served—approximately twenty months before parole eligibility—represents a significant period of incarceration during which rehabilitative treatment is unavailable.

The defense contends that the very purpose of an indeterminate sentence with parole eligibility is to allow for rehabilitation and reintegration into society. When the treatment necessary for rehabilitation is unavailable within the correctional system, the indeterminate sentence loses its rehabilitative purpose and becomes, in effect, a fixed term of incarceration without the corresponding benefit of treatment.

### C. The District Court Failed to Adequately Consider Rehabilitation

The defense submits that the district court erred in its weighing of the competing interests. While the court expressed concern about the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Mr. Galbraith remained in confinement, it ultimately concluded that the risk to society if released early was greater. However, the defense argues that this analysis failed to properly consider several critical factors.

First, the defense notes that the second psychologist who testified at the Rule 35 hearing specifically recommended that Mr. Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. While the psychologist acknowledged that delaying treatment until parole would not substantially diminish the probability of success, this admission must be read in context. The psychologist's opinion was that therapy should commence as soon as possible, and the defense contends that the twenty-month period of incarceration before parole eligibility represents an unnecessary delay in treatment that could facilitate rehabilitation.

Second, the defense submits that the district court gave excessive weight to the psychological evaluation conducted prior to sentencing, which characterized Mr. Galbraith as a "typically poor" candidate for therapy. However, the defense notes that the subsequent psychological testimony at the Rule 35 hearing offered a more optimistic assessment. The defense argues that the passage of time, combined with Mr. Galbraith's demonstrated willingness to engage in therapy, warranted reconsideration of his rehabilitative potential.

Third, the defense contends that the district court failed to adequately consider the availability of supervision and treatment upon early release. The State has not demonstrated that Mr. Galbraith could not be released to a structured environment with mandatory therapy and supervision, which would address the court's expressed concerns about public safety.

## II. THE SENTENCE AS APPLIED CONSTITUTES CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT

### A. The Constitutional Prohibition

The defense acknowledges that the appellate court found Mr. Galbraith's constitutional attack on the original sentence to be untimely because he did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984); *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983). However, the defense submits that the constitutional claim as presented in the Rule 35 motion is properly before this Court.

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 9 of the Idaho Constitution prohibit cruel and unusual punishments. While the defense acknowledges that sentences within statutory limits are generally not subject to constitutional challenge, a sentence may be unconstitutional if it is grossly disproportionate to the offense or if it serves no legitimate penal purpose.

### B. Application to Mr. Galbraith's Case

The defense submits that the denial of Rule 35 relief, when coupled with the unavailability of necessary treatment in confinement, raises serious constitutional concerns. The primary purpose of an indeterminate sentence is rehabilitation, with parole serving as the mechanism for early release when rehabilitation is achieved. When the treatment necessary for rehabilitation is unavailable, and the defendant is denied the opportunity to seek that treatment through the parole process, the sentence becomes punitive rather than rehabilitative.

The defense notes that Mr. Galbraith's offense, while serious, involved a single incident of violence against his infant daughter. He has no prior criminal record of consequence. The defense further notes that the psychological evaluation conducted prior to sentencing concluded that Mr. Galbraith was not a danger to the general public, though he posed a risk to his child. The defense argues that this distinction is critical—society can be protected through appropriate supervision and treatment conditions without requiring the defendant to serve the full indeterminate term without access to necessary rehabilitation.

### C. The Proportionality Analysis

Applying the proportionality analysis required under *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. The primary objectives of protecting society, deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution must be balanced. The defense notes that the presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a "viable alternative." However, the defense argues that this recommendation was made in the context of sentencing, not in the context of the Rule 35 motion, where additional evidence regarding treatment availability was presented.

### C. The Indeterminate Sentence Loses Its Purpose Without Treatment Access

The defense submits that the State's characterization of the indeterminate sentence as providing "leniency" is contrary to the purpose of such sentences. An indeterminate sentence is designed to allow for parole based upon demonstrated rehabilitation. When the treatment necessary for rehabilitation is unavailable in confinement, and the defendant is denied the opportunity to seek that treatment through the parole process, the indeterminate sentence becomes functionally equivalent to a fixed term of incarceration. The defense contends that this result frustrates the purpose of the indeterminate sentencing scheme and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** Docket No. [Redacted]
**Appeal from the District Court of the Fourth Judicial District, State of Idaho.**

**Preliminary Ruling**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion to reduce his sentence. Galbraith was convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter and received an indeterminate sentence of five years. He appeals the denial of his motion, arguing that the court abused its discretion and that the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. Having reviewed the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable law, we affirm the district court’s order.

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984). On appeal, the sentence will not be disturbed absent a clear abuse of that discretion. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984). When reviewing a sentence, or a refusal to reduce it, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982).

**II. Timeliness of Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the state and federal constitutions. However, he did not appeal the judgment containing the original sentence. A constitutional attack on the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely if not raised on direct appeal. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983). Because we conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion, Galbraith has failed to demonstrate that relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment. Accordingly, his constitutional challenge is without merit.

**III. Denial of Rule 35 Motion**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for sentence reduction. The court properly exercised its discretion by weighing the risk to society against the potential for Galbraith’s rehabilitation.

The defense contends that the court failed to give adequate consideration to the rehabilitative purpose of the sentence. The record contradicts this assertion. The district court explicitly observed that it was required to weigh "the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence." This demonstrates that the court considered the relevant factors mandated by State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982), and State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984).

The court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the benefit of immediate release was supported by the evidence. The presentence report and psychological evaluation indicated that Galbraith had a "poor social conscience," "communication problems," and a "pronounced lack of ability to empathize." The psychologist further opined that Galbraith represented a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child." While the defense correctly notes that Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public, the court specifically found that he presented a continuing risk to the victim and "other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry." This finding is a rational inference based on the nature of the offense—striking an infant with a fist causing a complex skull fracture and subdural hematoma.

Regarding the availability of treatment, the defense argues that the indeterminate sentence loses its rehabilitative purpose without access to immediate psychotherapy. However, the clinical psychologist who testified in support of the Rule 35 motion admitted that delaying treatment until Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the hearing—would not result in a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." This admission undercuts the defense’s claim that the twenty-month period of incarceration before parole eligibility frustrates the rehabilitative goals of the sentence. The court was entitled to rely on this testimony in finding that the risk to society posed by early release was greater than the risk of delayed treatment.

Finally, the sentence itself is not excessive. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence of five years for a serious assault resulting in permanent injury to an infant. Under State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982), the duration of confinement is generally treated as one-third of the facial length, or approximately twenty months. This period is not disproportionate to the severity of the crime and is consistent with the legislative intent to protect society.

**IV. Conclusion**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. The court balanced the need for societal protection against the defendant's rehabilitative needs and did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion. The order denying relief is affirmed.

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984). On appeal, the sentence will not be disturbed absent a clear abuse of that discretion. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984). When reviewing a sentence, or a refusal to reduce it, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982).

**II. Timeliness of Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the state and federal constitutions. The defense contends that this challenge is not procedurally barred because it is an "as applied" claim based on post-conviction developments—specifically, the lack of available psychotherapy in confinement—which could not have been adjudicated at the time of the original sentencing.

We are not persuaded by this distinction. A constitutional attack on the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely if not raised on direct appeal. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983). While a Rule 35 motion allows for consideration of an expanded record, the motion itself is a plea for leniency addressed to the trial court's discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984). Because we conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion, Galbraith has failed to demonstrate that relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment. Accordingly, his constitutional challenge fails on the merits.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Mr. Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for conduct that, while serious, resulted from a moment of poor judgment by a young man who himself was a victim of childhood abuse. ## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO ADEQUATELY WEIGH THE REHABILITATIVE FACTORS

### A. The Court's Analysis Under *Toohill* Was Incomplete

The prosecution correctly cites *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982), for the proposition that the primary objective of sentencing is protecting society, with related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. However, the prosecution's argument obscures a critical distinction: while protection of society is indeed the primary objective, this does not mean that rehabilitation must be subordinated in all cases, particularly where the defendant has demonstrated genuine potential for reform.

The psychologist was acknowledging the theoretical possibility of later treatment, not endorsing delay as ideal. The defense submits that the court's reliance on this admission to justify a denial of relief represents an abuse of discretion.

### B. The Indeterminate Sentence Structure Does Not Adequately Serve Rehabilitative Goals

The prosecution emphasizes that Mr. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence rather than a fixed term, suggesting this demonstrates leniency. However, this argument conflates structural leniency with actual rehabilitative opportunity. An indeterminate sentence that provides no meaningful access to rehabilitation programming fails to serve the rehabilitative goals that justify its structure. The prosecution suggests that intermediate sanctions would not adequately mitigate the risk. However, the defense submits that the court failed to consider properly structured probation with mandatory therapy, supervised visitation, and other conditions that could protect society while allowing Mr. Galbraith to access necessary treatment. The court's rejection of "probation and retained jurisdiction" does not reflect consideration of all alternative sentencing structures.

### B. The Psychological Profile Supports Treatment, Not Merely Incarceration

However, this finding actually supports the argument for structured treatment rather than pure incarceration. This distinction is critical because it suggests that supervised treatment with appropriate safeguards could address the identified risk while avoiding the pure warehousing of a defendant whose primary need is therapeutic intervention.

## IV. THE MITIGATING FACTORS REQUIRES GREATER WEIGHT

### A. Youth and Background

The prosecution acknowledges the mitigating factors present in this case—Mr. Galbraith was eighteen years old at the time of the offense, had no prior criminal record, was a member of the Marine Corps Reserve, and was himself a victim of childhood abuse. *See State v. Araiza*, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct. App. 1985) (requiring individualized consideration of mitigating factors).

However, the prosecution argues that these factors were "outweighed" by the severity of the offense. The defense submits that this analysis is incomplete. The very factors that the prosecution dismisses as insufficient—youth, lack of criminal history, status as abuse victim—are the factors that make rehabilitation not merely possible but likely. A young first offender with a documented history of being abused, who has demonstrated the capacity for military service and structured activity, presents a different rehabilitative profile than one without such characteristics.

### B. The Sentence Is Disproportionate to the Offense

The prosecution argues that the sentence is not disproportionate because Mr. Galbraith could have received a fixed five-year term. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the *Toohill* framework requires proportionality analysis based on the sentence actually imposed, not the theoretical maximum. The approximately twenty months of actual incarceration before parole eligibility must be proportionate to the offense of aggravated assault, not merely less than the maximum possible.

Second, the prosecution's argument proves too much. By this logic, any sentence less than the maximum could never be disproportionate. This Court has held that proportionality review requires consideration of all circumstances, including the defendant's personal history and characteristics. *State v. Araiza*, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct. App. 1985). The defense submits that a sentence that denies access to necessary treatment, for a young first offender whose crime arose from unaddressed childhood trauma, fails proportionality review.

---

## V. THE CONSTITUTIONAL CLAIM IS PROPERLY BEFORE THIS COURT

### A. The "As Applied" Challenge Is Timely

The prosecution argues that the constitutional challenge is untimely because it was not raised on direct appeal. However, the defense submits that this case presents an "as applied" challenge that could not have been adjudicated at the time of original sentencing.

The constitutional claim is not that the sentence as originally imposed was unconstitutional—it is that the continued execution of the sentence, under circumstances where necessary treatment is unavailable, violates the prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. This is a distinct claim that arises from post-conviction developments. The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984), and *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. ### B. The Sentence Violates the Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment

Even if this Court were to find the constitutional claim timely, the defense submits that the sentence, as executed, violates the Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 6 of the Idaho Constitution.

The Supreme Court has recognized that the Eighth Amendment "proscribes torture and the like" and "draws its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). In determining whether punishment is cruel and unusual, courts consider: (1) the gravity of the offense compared to the harshness of the penalty; (2) the sentences imposed on other criminals in the same jurisdiction; and (3) the sentences imposed for the same crime in other jurisdictions. *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277, 290 (1983).

Here, the gravity of the offense—while serious—must be balanced against the harshness of denying necessary treatment. A sentence that serves only punitive purposes without any meaningful rehabilitative component, particularly where the defendant's underlying pathology makes rehabilitation essential to public safety, fails this analysis.

Mr. Galbraith is not a dangerous criminal who requires lengthy incarceration for public safety. He is a young man who committed a serious offense arising from unaddressed childhood trauma. The sentence imposed—denying him access to necessary treatment—serves neither the rehabilitative goals of the criminal justice system nor the ultimate protection of society. As this Court recognized in *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982), rehabilitation is a legitimate goal of sentencing. A sentence that affirmatively prevents rehabilitation fails to serve this goal.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse and remand for further proceedings consistent with this analysis.

**DECISION ON APPEAL**

This matter comes before the Court following the submission of closing arguments by both the prosecution and the defense. Preston Galbraith appeals the district court’s order denying his Rule 35 motion to reduce his indeterminate sentence of five years for aggravated assault. Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in denying the motion and that the sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. After reviewing the parties’ arguments, the record, and the applicable law, we affirm the district court’s order.

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984). On appeal, we will not disturb the decision absent a clear abuse of that discretion. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct. App. 1984). In reviewing the sentence, or the refusal to reduce it, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982).

**II. Timeliness of the Constitutional Challenge**

Galbraith argues that his sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the state and federal constitutions. The defense characterizes this as an "as applied" challenge, relying on Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), and Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277 (1983), asserting that the unavailability of psychotherapy in confinement renders the sentence’s execution unconstitutional. The defense contends this claim could not have been raised on direct appeal because it relies on post-conviction developments.

We are not persuaded. A constitutional attack on the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely if not raised on direct appeal. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct. App. 1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct. App. 1983). While a Rule 35 motion allows for consideration of an expanded record, the motion itself is a plea for leniency addressed to the trial court's discretion. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct. App. 1984). The defense’s attempt to frame the claim as an "as applied" challenge to the execution of the sentence does not overcome the procedural bar. Because we conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the Rule 35 motion, Galbraith has failed to demonstrate that relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment. Accordingly, his constitutional challenge fails.

**III. Denial of the Rule 35 Motion**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for sentence reduction. The court properly exercised its discretion by weighing the risk to society against the potential for Galbraith’s rehabilitation.

**A. Balancing of Societal Protection and Rehabilitation**

The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to rehabilitative factors, asserting that the court subordinated rehabilitation to societal protection in violation of the framework established in State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct. App. 1982). The defense emphasizes the testimony of its expert, who recommended psychotherapy "as soon as possible."

The record, however, supports the district court’s balancing analysis. The court explicitly observed that it was required to weigh "the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence." Under Toohill, the primary objective of sentencing is the protection of society. The court’s prioritization of this goal was legally sound.

Crucially, the defense’s expert admitted that delaying treatment until Galbraith earned parole—which might occur approximately one year after the hearing—would not result in a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." This admission undercuts the defense’s claim that the twenty-month period of incarceration before parole eligibility frustrates the rehabilitative goals of the sentence. The court’s determination that the risk to society posed by early release was greater than the risk of delayed treatment was supported by the evidence and was within its discretion.

**B. Risk to Society**

The defense argues that the prosecution overstated the risk to society, noting that the psychologist limited the danger to situations where Galbraith had access to his child and that he was not considered a danger to the general public.
