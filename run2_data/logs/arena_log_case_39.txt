# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals

## INTRODUCTION

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the plaintiff's request to affirm the district court judgment. The plaintiff mischaracterizes the applicable legal framework and overstates the scope of tribal sovereignty as applied to the specific issue of motor vehicle registration reciprocity. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the district court's order and dismiss the plaintiff's claims.

---

## I. THE SCOPE OF TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY DOES NOT REQUIRE STATE RECIPROCITY

The plaintiff's reliance on *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), is misplaced. That case addressed tribal court jurisdiction over civil disputes between tribal members on reservation land—a matter of purely internal tribal governance. The Supreme Court has consistently held that tribal sovereignty is "inherent" but operates within boundaries established by federal law and is subject to congressional plenary power. *See United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978) ("Tribal sovereignty is limited by the superior and supreme authority of the United States.").

The critical distinction this Court must recognize is that the plaintiff asks not merely to exercise internal regulatory authority—the Band has done that—but to compel a sovereign state to recognize and honor that authority in the same manner it recognizes other jurisdictions. This is fundamentally different from protecting tribal self-governance from direct state interference. Minnesota is not seeking to regulate vehicles on the reservation; it is simply declining to extend a voluntary reciprocity benefit to tribal residents.

The Supreme Court has explicitly held that states are under no obligation to recognize tribal governmental acts beyond reservation boundaries absent congressional mandate. *See Strength v. United States*, 500 U.S. 1, 9 (1991) ("[A]s a matter of federal law, a State may, in its discretion, recognize or not recognize the tribal court judgment."). The same principle applies here: Minnesota's decision to extend reciprocity to certain jurisdictions is a matter of comity and policy discretion, not a constitutional requirement that must be extended to tribes.

---

## II. THE McCLANAHAN DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY TO THIS SITUATION

The plaintiff misapplies *McClanahan v. State Tax Commission of Arizona*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973). That case addressed direct state taxation of income earned on the reservation by tribal members—the Court held that states lack authority to tax such income without congressional authorization. *Id.* at 180.

This case involves neither direct taxation nor an attempt by Minnesota to assert jurisdiction over the reservation. Minnesota's motor vehicle registration statutes apply to vehicles operated on state highways, not to vehicles on tribal land. The Band's residents remain free to register their vehicles with the Band and operate them on the reservation. The only consequence is that if they wish to operate on Minnesota state highways, they must comply with Minnesota's registration requirements—just as residents of any other jurisdiction must do when using Minnesota roads.

The distinction between direct burdens and incidental effects is well-established in Indian law. *See Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 759, 768 (1985) (holding that states may impose nondiscriminatory fees on non-Indian hunters and fishermen off the reservation). Minnesota's registration fee applies equally to all vehicles using state highways regardless of owner; the Band's residents are not singled out for discriminatory treatment. The fact that the Band's residents may face additional costs compared to residents of reciprocal jurisdictions does not transform this into a tribal sovereignty issue.

---

## III. MINNESOTA HAS SUBSTANTIAL CONSIDERATIONS JUSTIFYING DIFFERENTIAL TREATMENT

The district court erred in finding that no substantial considerations justified Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity. The record establishes the following considerations that fully support the State's position:

**A. The Red Lake Band Is Not a Comparable Jurisdiction**

The jurisdictions with which Minnesota has entered reciprocity agreements—the District of Columbia, the State of Wisconsin, and the Province of Ontario—are all entities whose motor vehicle registration systems are subject to federal or international oversight and meet established standards. The Band's ordinance, while valid under tribal law, has not been subject to the same regulatory scrutiny and does not carry the same governmental guarantees.

**B. Revenue and Safety Considerations**

Minnesota has a legitimate interest in ensuring that vehicles operated on its highways are properly registered and that registration fees are paid. The district court's finding that reciprocity would further Minnesota's "economic and social development" does not address the legitimate revenue and safety concerns that underlie the State's registration system. The State is not required to sacrifice these interests simply because the Band has adopted a registration ordinance.

**C. Federal Policy Favoring Tribal Economic Development Does Not Require Reciprocity**

While federal policy may encourage tribal economic development, *see* Indian Financing Act of 1974, 25 U.S.C. § 1451 *et seq.*, this policy does not mandate that states extend every benefit to tribes that they extend to other governmental entities. The decision to grant reciprocity is a policy choice, not an obligation.

---

## IV. THE TRIBAL ORDINANCE DOES NOT BIND MINNESOTA

The plaintiff argues that denying reciprocity "dilutes" the effectiveness of the Band's governmental authority. This argument proves too much. By this logic, any state refusal to recognize a tribal governmental act would constitute improper dilution of sovereignty. This is not and has never been the law.

The Band's ordinance is valid under tribal law and is an appropriate exercise of tribal self-governance. But its validity under tribal law does not require other sovereigns to recognize it. The Band remains free to enforce its ordinance against tribal members on the reservation; Minnesota's refusal to extend reciprocity in no way interferes with that authority.

The cases cited by the plaintiff do not support a contrary result. In *Williams v. Lee*, the Court prevented Arizona state courts from exercising jurisdiction over a contract dispute between tribal members on the reservation. *Id.* at 220. This case involves no assertion of state jurisdiction over the reservation or tribal members. The plaintiff's attempt to extend *Williams* to this context exceeds any reasonable reading of that precedent.

---

## V. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ITS APPLICATION OF EQUAL PROTECTION PRINCIPLES

The district court held that Minnesota was obligated to afford the Band "the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities." This holding is legally erroneous. The Equal Protection Clause does not require states to extend benefits granted to some jurisdictions to all other jurisdictions that request them. *See Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 334 U.S. 507, 538 (1949) (holding that legislative classification is valid if rationally related to a legitimate state interest).

Minnesota's reciprocity agreements with other jurisdictions are matters of interstate and international comity, not constitutional obligations. The State may extend such agreements to some jurisdictions and not others without violating the Equal Protection Clause, provided the classification is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. Here, Minnesota's distinction between the Band and other reciprocal jurisdictions is rationally related to its interests in revenue collection, highway safety, and administrative convenience.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction, and dismiss the plaintiff's claims with prejudice.

The Band's motor vehicle registration ordinance is a valid exercise of tribal self-governance, and this Court does not doubt the district court's finding that the Band acted within its authority in adopting the ordinance. However, the Constitution and federal law do not require Minnesota to extend reciprocity to the Band, and the district court erred in holding otherwise. The plaintiff's attempt to transform a matter of state policy discretion into a federal constitutional right must be rejected.

**FINAL RULING**

**Analysis**

This Court has carefully considered the rebuttals presented by both the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the Band) and the State of Minnesota. The central issue remains whether the State of Minnesota’s refusal to extend motor vehicle registration reciprocity to the Band, while granting it to other jurisdictions, constitutes an impermissible interference with tribal self-government under the facts and precedent provided.

**I. State Authority vs. Tribal Sovereignty**

The State argues that its authority over highway regulation is broad and preemptive, citing *Alaska v. CustomAircraft*, 479 U.S. 361, 375 (1986), and *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 695 (1993). The State contends that because it possesses sovereign authority to regulate vehicles on its highways, its decision to deny reciprocity is a valid exercise of that power, not an interference with tribal governance. The State further relies on *County of Oneida v. Oneida Indian Nation*, 470 U.S. 226, 252 (1985), to assert that tribal and state sovereignty are concurrent and neither is inherently superior.

While this Court acknowledges the State’s broad police power over its highways, this authority is not absolute when it conflicts with the federal policy protecting tribal self-government. The State’s reliance on *Alaska* and *Bourland* is misplaced because those cases addressed the preemption of state law by federal statute or the regulation of non-Indian activities on non-Indian lands. Here, the issue is not whether the State can regulate highways, but whether it can apply its regulatory scheme in a manner that discriminates against a tribal government.

The Band correctly identifies *Williams v. The State argues that the Red Lake Band is not "comparable" to the District of Columbia, Wisconsin, or Ontario, and thus falls outside the scope of the reciprocity statutes. St. 168.181 and 168.187. The State’s assertion that the Band lacks comparable regulatory oversight is not supported by the record; indeed, the approval by the Secretary of the Interior provides a federal oversight mechanism analogous to the federal administration of the District of Columbia or the international treaty oversight of Ontario. The State of Minnesota has not demonstrated that its refusal to grant reciprocity to the Red Lake Band is justified by substantial considerations, nor has it shown that the Band is so dissimilar to other reciprocal jurisdictions as to merit exclusion. The State’s policy significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority in violation of the principles established in *Williams v. Lee*. Therefore, the district court’s judgment must be affirmed.

The State of Minnesota is obligated to afford the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians the same privileges regarding motor vehicle registration reciprocity that it grants to other comparable jurisdictions.

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

May it please the Court: The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this closing statement in defense of its sovereign authority to regulate motor vehicle registration on its public highways. The plaintiff, the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians, asks this Court to compel Minnesota to extend motor vehicle registration reciprocity to Band residents while simultaneously imposing a separate tribal registration requirement. This the State cannot do without abdicating its legitimate regulatory authority and without running afoul of fundamental principles of federalism that preserve the balance between state and tribal sovereignty.

The district court erred in its application of *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), and related precedent. This Court should reverse because: (1) the State's highway regulatory authority is broad and preeminent; (2) the Band is not a "comparable jurisdiction" under Minnesota's reciprocity statutes; and (3) even if comparability existed, the State has demonstrated substantial justifications for its regulatory classification.

## I. THE STATE'S SOVEREIGN AUTHORITY OVER HIGHWAY REGULATION IS BROAD AND PREEMPTIVE

The State of Minnesota possesses inherent sovereign authority to regulate the use of motor vehicles on its public highways. This authority flows from the State's police power and is fundamental to the administration of public roadways. As this Court recognized in *Alaska v. CustomAircraft*, 479 U.S. 361, 375 (1986), states retain substantial authority over activities conducted on state highways, including the authority to require registration and taxation of vehicles using those roads.

The Band's reliance on *Williams v. Lee* is misplaced. That case addressed a direct jurisdictional conflict between state and tribal courts over the adjudication of a contract dispute. The Supreme Court in *Williams* held that tribal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over internal tribal matters. However, the instant case presents no such jurisdictional conflict. The State is not attempting to adjudicate matters within the Band's internal governance or to exercise jurisdiction over the Band's tribal court system. Rather, the State is exercising its sovereign authority to regulate the use of its own highways—a authority that has never been delegated to tribal governments.

The Band's argument that *Williams* prohibits any state action that "significantly dilutes" tribal self-government reads that case far too broadly. In *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 695 (1993), the Supreme Court acknowledged that state sovereignty and tribal sovereignty are concurrent, and that neither is inherently superior. The State does not dispute that tribal governments possess substantial authority over internal matters. However, when a state regulates the use of its own public highways—highways that serve the general public and for which the State bears maintenance and safety responsibilities—it exercises authority that is fundamentally different from the type of interference *Williams* was designed to prevent.

The Band's registration ordinance applies to vehicles "using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." These are public roads that Minnesota helps fund and regulate. The State's requirement that vehicles using these roads be properly registered under state law is not an attack on tribal sovereignty; it is a legitimate exercise of the State's police power to ensure highway safety and proper funding of road maintenance.

## II. THE BAND IS NOT A "COMPARABLE JURISDICTION" UNDER MINNESOTA'S RECIPROCITY STATUTES

The district court erred in finding that the Red Lake Band is "comparable" to the District of Columbia, Wisconsin, and Ontario for purposes of Minn. St. 168.181 and 168.187. The District of Columbia is a federal territory administered directly by Congress. Wisconsin is a sovereign state of the Union. Ontario is a province of a foreign nation with which the United States maintains international treaties and agreements. Each of these entities operates under a fundamentally different governmental structure than the Red Lake Band.

The Band's unique government-to-government relationship with the federal distinguishes it from the entities enumerated in the reciprocity statutes. The Band is a federally recognized tribe whose relationship with the United States is governed by treaties, federal statutes, and administrative regulations—not by the same legal frameworks that govern states, territories, and foreign provinces. The district court found that the Band's ordinance performs "functions identical to those performed by the other jurisdictions" but this finding ignores critical differences. The District of Columbia's vehicle registration system operates under federal law and is subject to congressional oversight. These differences are not merely formalities—they reflect fundamentally different governmental structures and accountability mechanisms that justify differential treatment.

The State has not "arbitrarily excluded" the Band from reciprocity. ## III. THE STATE HAS DEMONSTRATED SUBSTANTIAL JUSTIFICATIONS FOR THE DISPARITY OF TREATMENT

The Band's argument that the State's revenue and safety concerns are "circular" because they would apply equally to Wisconsin and Ontario misapprehends the nature of the reciprocity analysis. The district court found that reciprocity "would further Minnesota's economic and social development," but this finding is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The State's judgment that revenue collection and highway safety considerations outweigh any potential benefit from reciprocity is a legitimate exercise of legislative discretion entitled to deference under *Railway Express Agency v. The Band's reliance on *Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 768, 770 (1985), is unavailing. **FINAL RULING**

**Analysis**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments presented by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the Band) and the State of Minnesota. The State asks this Court to reverse the district court’s judgment, arguing that its refusal to grant reciprocity is a valid exercise of sovereign authority, that the Band is not a "comparable jurisdiction," and that substantial justifications exist for the disparity. Upon careful consideration of the record and the applicable law, the State’s arguments are unpersuasive.

**I. The Scope of *Williams v. Lee* and Tribal Sovereignty**

The State contends that *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959), applies only to direct jurisdictional conflicts between state and tribal courts, and therefore does not prohibit the State’s highway registration scheme. This interpretation is overly narrow and inconsistent with the legal standard established in the record. The facts provided explicitly state that the State is obligated to afford the Band the same privileges granted to other entities if denial would "significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised." This standard aligns with the broader principle of *Williams*, which prohibits state actions that undermine the economic and political viability of tribal self-government.

The State’s reliance on *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 695 (1993), for the proposition that state sovereignty is concurrent and preeminent on highways, misses the mark. While the State has authority over its highways, that authority is not absolute when it results in the discriminatory treatment of a tribal government. By requiring Band residents to pay dual registration fees—effectively penalizing them for participating in the tribal regulatory system—while exempting residents of other jurisdictions, the State renders the Band’s registration ordinance "less worthy of recognition." This constitutes a significant dilution of the Band’s governmental authority that *Williams* seeks to prevent.

**II. Comparability of Jurisdictions**

The State argues that the Red Lake Band is not a "comparable jurisdiction" under Minn. St. 168.181 and 168.187 because it is a federally recognized tribe rather than a state, territory, or foreign province. The State asserts that the Band’s unique government-to-government relationship distinguishes it from entities like the District of Columbia, Wisconsin, or Ontario.

This argument ignores the functional reality of the Band’s ordinance as established in the record. The Band’s ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, imposes substantial fees, issues certificates of title, maintains records, establishes transfer procedures, and regulates plate display. These are the exact functions performed by the jurisdictions with which Minnesota grants reciprocity. The State’s insistence on formal distinctions over functional equivalence is arbitrary. The approval by the Secretary of the Interior provides federal oversight analogous to the federal administration of the District of Columbia or the international agreements governing Ontario. Therefore, for the specific purpose of motor vehicle registration reciprocity, the Band is a comparable jurisdiction.

**III. Substantial Justification for Disparity**

The State claims that revenue collection and highway safety constitute "substantial justifications" for the disparity in treatment. The State relies on *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 334 U.S. 507, 511 (1949), to argue for deference to legislative classifications.

However, the State cannot overcome the explicit factual finding made by the district court and reiterated in the record: "The record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require the disparity of treatment." The State’s generalized assertions of revenue and safety interests do not satisfy this burden. If these concerns were truly substantial, they would presumably apply equally to the residents of Wisconsin or Ontario, yet the State exempts those jurisdictions. The State has failed to explain why its interests are furthered by taxing Band residents but not the residents of these other comparable jurisdictions. Without such an explanation, the disparity remains unjustified.

Furthermore, the State’s reliance on *Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe of Indians*, 471 U.S. 768, 770 (1985), is misplaced. While the State distinguishes *Montana* as a tax case, the economic burden of dual registration fees operates similarly to a tax on tribal activity. Absent clear congressional authorization, the State cannot impose this burden on the Band’s self-government initiatives.

**Conclusion**

The State of Minnesota has not demonstrated that its refusal to grant reciprocity to the Red Lake Band is justified by substantial considerations, nor has it shown that the Band is functionally dissimilar to other reciprocal jurisdictions. The State’s policy significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority in violation of the principles established in *Williams v. Lee*. Therefore, the district court’s judgment must be affirmed.

**RULING**

The Court affirms the order of the district court granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction. The State of Minnesota is obligated to afford the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians the same privileges regarding motor vehicle registration reciprocity that it grants to other comparable jurisdictions.
