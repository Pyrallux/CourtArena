### I. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY DETERMINED THAT THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

#### A. The Legal Standard for Second-Degree Murder

The State correctly acknowledges that second-degree murder requires proof of "malice." *See* State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). What the State misstates is the nature and quantum of proof required to establish this element.

Malice for second-degree murder may be express or implied. *See* State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905. Implied malice requires proof that the defendant acted with "a wanton and willful disregard of the probable consequences to human life." State v. Hammonds, 290 N.C. 1, 224 S.E.2d 595 (1976). This is a demanding standard that requires more than mere negligence, recklessness, or poor judgment. The defendant must have acted with a level of culpability that demonstrates a "reckless indifference to human life." State v. Coker, 312 N.C. 432, 323 S.E.2d 342 (1984).

#### B. The Evidence at Trial Failed to Establish Malice

The trial record, fairly construed, establishes the following: The defendant was involved in an altercation at a tavern, was struck twice by the tavern owner, fell and struck his head on a door base, and has no memory of any events thereafter. He was subsequently found to have a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent—levels indicating severe intoxication approaching unconsciousness. He was unable to present evidence of his possible unconsciousness at the time of the accident because the trial court excluded such evidence.

The State asks this Court to infer malice from the bare facts that the defendant was driving 60-70 miles per hour and ran a red light. Respectfully, this inference does not follow as a matter of law. Driving through an intersection on a red light, even at excessive speed, is precisely the type of conduct that constitutes involuntary manslaughter or vehicular homicide—crimes involving culpable negligence—rather than second-degree murder, which requires the higher mental state of malice.

#### C. The State Misreads State v. Coker

The State relies heavily on *State v. Coker*, 312 N.C. 432, 323 S.E.2d 342 (1984), for the proposition that "driving at excessive speed through a red light demonstrates a reckless indifference to human life sufficient to establish malice for second-degree murder." This reading of *Coker* is overbroad and ignores the specific factual context of that decision.

In *Coker*, the defendant was observed driving at high speeds, passing other vehicles on the shoulder, running multiple red lights, and ignoring warnings from pursuing officers. The defendant's conduct in *Coker* was sustained, deliberate, and reflective of a pattern of dangerous driving. Here, by contrast, the defendant had no memory of the incident, had been struck on the head moments before, was severely intoxicated, and there is no evidence of any conduct prior to entering the intersection that would support an inference of malice.

The facts here more closely resemble those in *State v. Lang*, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), where this Court emphasized that second-degree murder requires "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." The defendant's conduct, as established by the record, was not "intentional" in any sense that would support a finding of malice. He did not choose to drive dangerously; the evidence suggests he may have been unconscious or near-unconscious from the combination of head trauma and alcohol.

#### D. The Unconsciousness Defense Was Improperly Excluded

The trial court's exclusion of the defendant's proffered unconsciousness evidence was error. The defendant sought to introduce testimony that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident—a defense that, if believed by the jury, would negate the element of malice entirely. An unconscious defendant cannot possess the "wanton and willful disregard" required for implied malice.

The trial court compounded this error by refusing to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness. When a defendant offers evidence tending to show unconsciousness, the jury must be permitted to consider that evidence in determining whether the defendant acted with the requisite mental state. The trial court's refusal deprived the defendant of a complete defense and undermined the integrity of the proceedings.

The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 19 of the North Carolina Constitution require that every element of a criminal offense be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *See* In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); State v. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF NORTH CAROLINA v. GERALD ALLEN MOORE**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE COURT OF APPEALS**

**NO. 84A85**

**FILED: 2025**

**OPINION**

The State appeals from the Court of Appeals’ decision reversing the defendant’s convictions for three counts of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, *sua sponte*, that the trial record contained no evidence of malice and thus constituted plain error. After careful consideration of the record, the arguments of counsel, and the controlling precedent, we conclude that the Court of Appeals erred in its determination. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

The central issue is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to withstand a motion for judgment of acquittal or to support a verdict of second-degree murder. Second-degree murder is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from conduct that shows a "wanton and willful disregard of the probable consequences to human life." State v. Hammonds, 290 N.C. 1, 224 S.E. 2d 595 (1976).

The Court of Appeals concluded there was "no evidence of malice." We disagree. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, reveals that the defendant, with a blood alcohol content between .24 and .32 percent, drove his vehicle at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour through a red light at a busy intersection. To avoid one vehicle, he swerved and crashed into another, killing three occupants. This testimony, provided by eyewitness Walden Ball, is sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with a wanton and willful disregard for human life.

The defendant argues that this evidence amounts only to negligence or recklessness insufficient for murder, distinguishing the facts from those in State v. Coker, 312 N.C. 432, 323 S.E. 2d 342 (1984). In *Coker*, this Court held that "driving at excessive speed through a red light demonstrates a reckless indifference to human life sufficient to establish malice for second-degree murder." The defendant contends *Coker* involved a sustained pattern of dangerous driving, whereas here he claims he was unconscious due to head trauma and intoxication.

While the defendant’s distinction regarding the duration of the conduct is noted, it does not negate the sufficiency of the evidence. The act of entering an intersection at 60 to 70 miles per hour against a red light is, in itself, an intentional act sufficient to show malice. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). The jury was presented with evidence of the defendant's extreme intoxication and the altercation preceding the crash. It was within the jury's province to weigh this evidence against the testimony regarding the defendant's driving and determine whether the State proved malice beyond a reasonable doubt. The existence of conflicting evidence or a defense of unconsciousness does not render the State's evidence nonexistent; it merely creates a question of fact for the jury. See State v. Earnhardt, 299 N.C. 345, 261 S.E. 2d 662 (1980). Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding there was no evidence of malice.

**II. The *Sua Sponte* Application of Plain Error Review**

The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions *sua sponte* under a theory of "plain error." It is well established that appellate courts generally should not raise issues not presented by the parties. State v. McNeil, 348 N.C. 131, 499 S.E. 2d 392 (1998). Plain error review is reserved for errors that are "so fundamental as to amount to a miscarriage of justice or which probably resulted in the verdict reaching a different result." State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E. 2d 375 (1983).

Because the trial record contained substantial evidence of malice, the Court of Appeals' finding of "plain error" based on a total lack of such evidence was misplaced. The appellate court's intervention circumvented the adversarial process to correct a defect that did not exist. The State correctly argues that the Court of Appeals created a procedural anomaly by reversing the conviction on a ground neither party argued—that the evidence was legally insufficient to support the charge.

**III. The Exclusion of Unconsciousness Evidence**

The defendant argues that the trial court committed error by excluding evidence of his potential unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on this defense. While the exclusion of a defense theory is a serious matter, this issue is distinct from the sufficiency of the evidence reviewed by the Court of Appeals. The Court of Appeals did not reverse based on an evidentiary ruling regarding unconsciousness; it reversed based on its finding that no evidence of malice existed in the record. As we have determined that evidence of malice was present, the rationale for the Court of Appeals' reversal fails. The propriety of the trial court's ruling on the unconsciousness evidence is a matter for the appellate court to consider on remand, should the defendant properly raise and preserve that issue.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. The evidence presented at trial, specifically the defendant's operation of a vehicle at high speed through a red light resulting in fatalities, was sufficient to support a jury finding of implied malice. The Court of Appeals erred in finding otherwise and in reversing the convictions *sua sponte*.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE COURT OF APPEALS**

**NO. 84A85**

**FILED: 2025**

**OPINION**

**BY THE COURT**

The State appeals from the Court of Appeals’ decision reversing the defendant’s convictions for three counts of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, *sua sponte*, that the trial record contained no evidence of malice and thus constituted plain error. After reviewing the rebuttals of the parties and the record provided, we conclude that the Court of Appeals erred in its determination. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

The central issue on appeal is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to withstand a motion for judgment of acquittal or to support a verdict of second-degree murder. Second-degree murder is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from conduct that shows a "wanton and willful disregard of the probable consequences to human life." State v. Hammonds, 290 N.C. 1, 224 S.E.2d 595 (1976).

The Court of Appeals concluded there was "no evidence of malice." We disagree. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, reveals that the defendant, with a blood alcohol content between .24 and .32 percent, drove his vehicle at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour through a red light at a busy intersection. To avoid one vehicle, he swerved and crashed into another, killing three occupants. This testimony, provided by eyewitness Walden Ball, is sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with a wanton and willful disregard for human life.

The Defense argues that the evidence was insufficient because the defendant lacked the requisite intent due to unconsciousness resulting from a head injury sustained during a fight prior to the accident. The Defense relies on State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), to assert that an "intentional act" requires a conscious intent to perform the act creating the lethal risk. While the Defense correctly identifies the necessity of an intentional act, the presence of evidence suggesting the defendant *may* have been unconscious does not negate the sufficiency of the State's evidence of the defendant's conduct.

The State presented direct evidence of the defendant's vehicle entering an intersection at a high rate of speed against a red light. This Court has previously held that "driving at excessive speed through a red light demonstrates a reckless indifference to human life sufficient to establish malice for second-degree murder." State v. Coker, 312 N.C. 432, 323 S.E.2d 342 (1984). The Defense attempts to distinguish *Coker* by noting the absence of a head injury in that case. However, the legal principle of *Coker* regarding the inference of malice from dangerous driving applies to the *act* observed. The existence of a potential defense of unconsciousness, which the trial court excluded from evidence, does not render the State's evidence of the driving nonexistent. See State v. Earnhardt, 299 N.C. 345, 261 S.E.2d 662 (1980) (stating that the existence of conflicting evidence does not render the State's evidence nonexistent; it merely creates a question of fact for the jury).

The Defense contends that because the defendant was unconscious, he could not possess the mental state of "wanton and willful disregard." This argument conflates the sufficiency of the evidence to support a conviction with the defendant's burden to establish a defense. The State's evidence, if believed, establishes that the defendant performed an intentional act—driving at high speed through a red light—that proximately caused the deaths. The fact that the defendant claims he was unconscious is a matter for the jury to consider, provided the evidence is admitted. The exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence is a separate evidentiary issue; it does not retroactively remove the State's evidence of malice from the record. Therefore, the Court of Appeals erred in concluding there was no evidence of malice.

**II. The *Sua Sponte* Application of Plain Error Review**

The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions *sua sponte* under a theory of "plain error." It is well established that appellate courts generally should not raise issues not presented by the parties. State v. McNeil, 348 N.C. 131, 499 S.E.2d 392 (1998). Plain error review is reserved for errors that are "so fundamental as to amount to a miscarriage of justice or which probably resulted in the verdict reaching a different result." State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983).

The Defense argues that the *sua sponte* review was proper because insufficiency of the evidence is a constitutional error implicating due process. While a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence is indeed a fundamental matter, the Court of Appeals' finding of insufficiency was based on an erroneous view of the record. As established above, the record contained substantial evidence of malice. Consequently, there was no "plain error" resulting from a lack of evidence. The appellate court's intervention circumvented the adversarial process to correct a defect that did not exist. The State correctly argues that the Court of Appeals created a procedural anomaly by reversing the conviction on a ground neither party argued—that the evidence was legally insufficient to support the charge.

**III. The Exclusion of Unconsciousness Evidence**

The defendant argues that the trial court committed error by excluding evidence of his potential unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on this defense. The Defense contends this issue is properly before this Court and warrants reversal because the exclusion of evidence negating malice is prejudicial.

While the exclusion of a defense theory is a serious matter, this issue is distinct from the sufficiency of the evidence reviewed by the Court of Appeals. The Court of Appeals did not reverse based on an evidentiary ruling regarding unconsciousness; it reversed based on its finding that no evidence of malice existed in the record. As we have determined that evidence of malice was present, the rationale for the Court of Appeals' reversal fails.

The propriety of the trial court's ruling on the unconsciousness evidence is a matter for the appellate court to consider on remand, should the defendant properly raise and preserve that issue. The Defense's request for this Court to affirm the reversal based on the exclusion of this evidence cannot be granted, as that was not the basis for the Court of Appeals' decision.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. The evidence presented at trial, specifically the defendant's operation of a vehicle at high speed through a red light resulting in fatalities, was sufficient to support a jury finding of implied malice. The Court of Appeals erred in finding otherwise and in reversing the convictions *sua sponte*.

**I. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

The dispositive issue is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to withstand a motion to judgment of acquittal or to support a verdict of second-degree murder. Second-degree murder is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). Malice may be implied from conduct that shows a "wanton and willful disregard of the probable consequences to human life." State v. The Court of Appeals concluded there was "no evidence of malice." We disagree. Viewed in the light most favorable to the State, the evidence reveals that the defendant, with a blood alcohol content between .24 and .32 percent, drove his vehicle at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour through a red light at a busy intersection. To avoid one vehicle, he swerved and crashed into another, killing three occupants. This testimony, provided by eyewitness Walden Ball, is sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with a wanton and willful disregard for human life.

The Defense argues that the evidence was insufficient because the defendant lacked the requisite intent due to unconsciousness resulting from a head injury sustained during a fight prior to the accident. The Defense relies on State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983), to assert that an "intentional act" requires a conscious intent to perform the act creating the lethal risk. While the Defense correctly identifies the necessity of an intentional act, the presence of evidence suggesting the defendant *may* have been unconscious does not negate the sufficiency of the State's evidence of the defendant's conduct.

The State presented direct evidence of the defendant's vehicle entering an intersection at a high rate of speed against a red light. This Court has previously held that "driving at excessive speed through a red light demonstrates a reckless indifference to human life sufficient to establish malice for second-degree murder." State v. Coker, 312 N.C. 432, 323 S.E.2d 342 (1984). The Defense attempts to distinguish *Coker* by noting the absence of a head injury in that case. However, the legal principle of *Coker* regarding the inference of malice from dangerous driving applies to the *act* observed. The existence of a potential defense of unconsciousness, which the trial court excluded from evidence, does not render the State's evidence of the driving nonexistent. See State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 655, 300 S.E.2d 375 (1983).

The Defense argues that the *sua sponte* review was proper because insufficiency of the evidence is a constitutional error implicating due process. The appellate court's intervention circumvented the adversarial process to correct a defect that did not exist. The State correctly argues that the Court of Appeals created a procedural anomaly by reversing the conviction on a ground neither party argued—that the evidence was legally insufficient to support the charge.

**III. The Exclusion of Unconsciousness Evidence**

The defendant argues that the trial court committed error by excluding evidence of his potential unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on this defense. The Defense contends this issue is properly before this Court and warrants reversal because the exclusion of evidence negating malice is prejudicial.

While the exclusion of a defense theory is a serious matter, this issue is distinct from the sufficiency of the evidence reviewed by the Court of Appeals. The Court of Appeals did not reverse based on an evidentiary ruling regarding unconsciousness; it reversed based on its finding that no evidence of malice existed in the record. As we have determined that evidence of malice was present, the rationale for the Court of Appeals' reversal fails.

The propriety of the trial court's ruling on the unconsciousness evidence is a matter for the appellate court to consider on remand, should the defendant properly raise and preserve that issue. The Defense's request for this Court to affirm the reversal based on the exclusion of this evidence cannot be granted, as that was not the basis for the Court of Appeals' decision.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the Court of Appeals is reversed. The evidence presented at trial, specifically the defendant's operation of a vehicle at high speed through a red light resulting in fatalities, was sufficient to support a jury finding of implied malice. The Court of Appeals erred in finding otherwise and in reversing the convictions *sua sponte*.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
