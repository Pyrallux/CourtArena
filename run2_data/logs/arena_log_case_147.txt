**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF WASHINGTON, Respondent, v. JOHN GEORGE and TOMMY GEORGE, Appellants.**

**NO. 57984-1-I**

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal regarding the sufficiency of evidence to support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The Appellants, John George and Tommy George, argue that the State failed to prove the "value" element of first-degree theft because it did not establish the actual market value of the 1974 Chevrolet pickup truck sold to undercover police officers. The Appellants further contend that the evidence was insufficient to prove deception and that the transaction was not completed. For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds the Appellants' arguments unpersuasive. The trial court properly denied the motion to dismiss, and the conviction is affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

When reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, the relevant inquiry is whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Green*, 94 Wash. 2d 216, 616 P.2d 628 (1980). The Court does not reweigh the evidence or assess the credibility of witnesses; rather, it accepts the State's evidence and all reasonable inferences drawn therefrom.

**B. The Value of Property in Theft by Deception**

The central issue on appeal is the legal definition of "value" under Washington's theft statute, RCW 9A.56.030, in the context of a theft by deception. The Appellants contend that the State was required to prove the fair market value of the truck exceeded $5,000. They argue that because the State presented no expert testimony or comparable sales data regarding the truck's worth, the evidence was insufficient to support the charge.

This argument is contrary to controlling Washington precedent. In cases of theft by deception, the "value" of the property is determined by the amount the victim parted with in reliance on the defendant's false representations, not the intrinsic market value of the item obtained. As held by the Washington Supreme Court in *State v. Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d 492, 495, 687 P.2d 1158 (1984), "the value of property taken by deception is the amount the victim parted with in reliance on the false representation." This principle ensures that the severity of the theft charge corresponds to the actual loss suffered by the victim as a result of the fraud.

The Court of Appeals reaffirmed this holding specifically in the context of vehicle fraud in *State v. Johnson*, 67 Wash. App. 722, 726, 840 P.2d 894 (1992), stating that "in cases involving odometer tampering, the value for theft purposes is the price paid by the victim, not the actual market value of the vehicle with correct mileage." The rationale is that the victim's loss is defined by the consideration paid based on the fraudulent premise.

Here, the evidence established that the Appellants represented the truck as having 70,000 miles, being in "perfect condition," having a single owner, and being "all stock and original." Based on these representations, the undercover detective agreed to pay $5,500. The transaction was completed, and the check was delivered. Under *State v. Brown* and *State v. Johnson*, the value of the property taken is therefore $5,500. This amount exceeds the $5,000 threshold for first-degree theft under RCW 9A.56.030(1)(a). The State was not required to present independent evidence of the truck's depreciated market value; the consideration paid by the victim is the statutory measure of value in a deception case. *State v. Smith*, 101 Wash. App. 300, 305, 3 P.3d 769 (2000) (reiterating that value is measured by consideration received, not market value).

**C. Sufficiency of Evidence Regarding Deception**

The Appellants argue that the evidence was insufficient to prove deception because the detectives initiated the contact and because the Appellants may have believed the representations they made. This argument lacks merit.

The record reflects that the Appellants made specific, material misrepresentations regarding the truck's history and condition. Tommy George stated that his father was the "original and sole owner," that the truck had "always been in the garage," and that it had 70,000 miles. John George confirmed these representations. The undisputed facts reveal that these statements were false: the truck had been purchased from Jerome Potter two years prior, had sat outside inoperable, and had a different engine installed. Furthermore, the odometer discrepancy (reading 70,000 while actually having 185,000 miles) was known to the Appellants or should have been known given the recent purchase history where Potter disclosed the mileage. The intent to deceive can be inferred from the act of making false representations regarding material facts to induce a sale. The State presented sufficient evidence for a jury to find that the Appellants knowingly deceived the victims.

**D. The Attempt Element**

The Appellants argue that the State failed to prove the "attempt" element because they did not actually "receive" the $5,500, noting that the cashier's check was not negotiated. This argument misinterprets the law of attempt.

A person is guilty of attempted theft if they take a substantial step toward commission of the crime with the intent to commit it. RCW 9A.28.030. Here, the Appellants completed the sale, transferred the title, and accepted the cashier's check as payment. The fact that the arrest occurred immediately thereafter, preventing the Appellants from cashing the check, does not negate the completion of the theft or the substantial step required for an attempt. The crime was essentially complete; the victims had parted with their funds (the check), and the Appellants had secured the payment instrument.

**E. Vagueness Challenge**

Finally, the Appellants' suggestion that the statute is unconstitutionally vague is foreclosed by precedent. In *State v. Bock*, 113 Wash. 2d 158, 163, 777 P.2d 14 (1989), the court explicitly rejected vagueness challenges to the theft statute, holding that "the statutory definition of value provides adequate notice and is not so vague as to permit arbitrary enforcement." The application of the "price paid" rule in this case provides clear notice to sellers of the potential consequences of misrepresenting their goods.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly denied the Appellants' motion for dismissal. The State presented sufficient evidence to support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. Under Washington law, the value of the property was the $5,500 paid by the victims, satisfying the statutory threshold. The evidence supported the jury's finding of deception and attempt. Accordingly, the decision of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.

### I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the defense's position and ignores critical flaws in the State's case. While the State correctly cites *State v. Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d 492, 687 P.2d 1158 (1984), and *State v. Johnson*, 67 Wash. The prosecution conflates these distinct elements. This Court should reverse the conviction.

### II. THE "PRICE PAID" RULE REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE VICTIM ACTUALLY PARTED WITH VALUE

The prosecution erroneously assumes that the mere tender of a cashier's check constitutes the victim having "parted with" value for purposes of first-degree theft. This assumption violates fundamental principles of property law and criminal law.

Under RCW 9A.56.030(1)(a), first-degree theft requires that the defendant "obtain" property "of a value exceeding five hundred dollars." The statute uses the word "obtain," not "agreed to accept" or "received as payment." A cashier's check, until negotiated, is merely a conditional payment instrument—not completed property transfer. *See* Black's Law Dictionary (defining "obtain" as "to gain possession of").

In *State v. Brown*, the court emphasized that value is measured by "the amount the victim parted with in reliance on the false representation." 102 Wash. 2d at 495, 687 P.2d 1158. But the Brown court contemplated situations where victims actually suffered loss—where funds were withdrawn, accounts debited, or property permanently transferred. Here, the record establishes that the cashier's check was never negotiated. The victims suffered no actual economic loss. The Georges were arrested immediately after receiving the check, before any funds could be drawn.

The prosecution argues that "the theft was functionally complete when the payment was delivered and accepted." This novel theory transforms attempted theft into a thought crime. Under this logic, a defendant could be convicted of completed first-degree theft merely by accepting payment—even if the payment is subsequently stopped, forged, or never honored. This cannot be the law.

The defense submits that *State v. Johnson* is distinguishable. In *Johnson*, the victim had actually paid $15,900 for a vehicle with tampered odometer. 67 Wash. App. at 724, 840 P.2d 894. The transaction was completed, and the victim had permanently lost $15,900. Here, by contrast, the State presented no evidence that the $5,500 was ever extracted from any account or that the cashier's check was honored. The prosecution's own evidence establishes that the check remained in the officers' names and was never negotiated.

This Court should hold that "parting with" value requires actual, completed transfer—not merely the tender of a conditional instrument that was never honored.

---

### III. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE THE GEORGES "OBTAINED" PROPERTY WORTH $5,500

Even accepting arguendo that the victims "parted with" a check for $5,500, the State must separately prove that the Georges "obtained" property of that value. The two elements are distinct under the statute.

The Georges sold a truck. Regardless of the misrepresentations, the Georges actually transferred ownership of a vehicle to the detectives. The State presented no evidence establishing the fair market value of the truck at the time of sale. The prosecution cannot satisfy the "obtain" element by pointing to the sale price alone when the defendants delivered equivalent consideration—a truck—to the victims.

This distinction matters because a theft conviction requires that the defendant benefit at the victim's expense. If the Georges sold a truck worth $4,000 for $5,500 through deception, the victim obtained a $4,000 truck and lost $5,500 in funds—but the Georges only "obtained" $5,500 in money if the transaction was completed. The Georges transferred $4,000 in value (the truck) to the victims. The net "obtainment" by the Georges is therefore not $5,500, but rather the difference between the money received and the value of the truck delivered.

The prosecution's theory would lead to absurd results: a seller who deceives a buyer into overpaying by $1 for a $5,000 item could be convicted of first-degree theft, even though the seller delivered full value and the buyer suffered no net loss. This cannot be the intent of the theft statute.

The defense acknowledges that *State v. Johnson* applied the "price paid" rule. But *Johnson* did not address the situation where the victim received equivalent consideration in the form of a vehicle. The *Johnson* court specifically noted that the victim "paid $15,900 for a vehicle that was actually worth far less." 67 Wash. App. at 726, 840 P.2d 894. The court presumed the victim received *something* of value—the vehicle—but the deception inflated the price. The key fact in *Johnson* was that the victim lost $15,900 and received a vehicle worth substantially less.

Here, the State presented *no evidence* regarding the truck's actual value. The prosecution cannot rely on the absence of defense evidence to establish an essential element of the crime. The burden of proof rests on the State, and the State failed to carry it.

### IV. THE EVIDENCE OF DECEPTION WAS INSUFFICIENT

The prosecution asserts that the Georges made material misrepresentations, but the record does not support this characterization.

First, regarding ownership: The prosecution claims the Georges falsely stated John George was the "original and sole owner." However, the facts establish that John George *was* the owner at the time of sale—he had purchased the truck from Jerome Potter and held title. The statement that John was the "original and sole owner" could reasonably be interpreted as referring to his ownership since acquiring the truck, particularly given that he had owned it for the two years since purchase. The prosecution's interpretation requires the Court to find that "original owner" meant "first owner ever," but the advertisement's language ("1 ownr") is ambiguous and could reasonably be understood as John being the only owner during his period of ownership.

Second, regarding the "always garaged" representation: The prosecution presented evidence that the truck had sat outside for two years prior to the Georges' purchase. But the Georges purchased the truck in 2001 and sold it in 2004. The truck could have been garaged during the Georges' ownership period. The detectives never asked when the truck was garaged or for how long. The prosecution asks this Court to infer that the statement was false during the entire lifespan of the truck, but the evidence only establishes that the truck was outside *before* the Georges acquired it.

Third, regarding the odometer: The prosecution claims the Georges knew or should have known the mileage was 185,000. But the odometer read 70,000. Jerome Potter told Tommy George the truck had "185,000 miles on it"—but Potter also said the five-digit odometer read 70,000. The defense is not required to prove the odometer was tampered; the State must prove the defendants *knew* the mileage was misrepresented. Potter's statement about total miles, combined with the odometer reading, could lead a reasonable person to believe the odometer had rolled over (as is common with five-digit odometers). The Georges' belief that the truck had 70,000 miles is at least plausible given Potter's representation about the odometer reading.

The prosecution argues that "the intent to deceive can be inferred from the act of making false representations." This reverses the burden of proof. The State must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt; it cannot merely infer it from the existence of false statements. Given the ambiguous nature of the representations and Potter's own statements about the odometer, the evidence was insufficient to prove the Georges knowingly deceived the detectives.

### V. THE ATTEMPT ELEMENT WAS NOT SATISFIED

The prosecution's argument that the "substantial step" element was satisfied because the sale was completed ignores the plain language of the attempt statute and the facts of this case.

RCW 9A.28.030 requires that the defendant take "a substantial step toward commission of the crime." The crime of theft requires that the defendant "obtain" property of value. The Georges completed the sale and accepted a check, but they never negotiated the check or obtained the funds. The check was made payable to them but remained in their possession unnegotiated at the time of arrest.

The prosecution argues that the "theft was functionally complete" when the check was delivered. But this Court should reject this expansive interpretation. If accepted, any seller who accepts payment for goods could be guilty of completed theft—even if the buyer stops payment the next day, the seller could be convicted of theft for the full amount. This criminalizes contract disputes, not theft.

The more reasonable interpretation is that "obtain" requires actual receipt of value—not merely acceptance of a conditional payment instrument. The Georges took a substantial step toward theft, but the theft was not completed because the funds were never actually received.

The "price paid" rule, as applied by the prosecution, provides no notice to sellers that they may be charged with first-degree theft merely for accepting payment from a buyer who claims to have been deceived. A reasonable seller would understand that overcharging a buyer might constitute fraud—but not theft, where the seller delivers full value.

The prosecution's theory subjects sellers to felony charges based on the victim's subjective assessment of value, not the seller's conduct. This permits arbitrary enforcement and fails to provide fair notice. The statute, as applied here, is unconstitutionally vague.

The prosecution failed to prove that the victims actually "parted with" $5,500, as the check was never negotiated. The prosecution failed to prove that the Georges "obtained" property worth $5,500, as they delivered a vehicle of unknown value. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case returns before the Court following the submission of rebuttal arguments by the State and the Appellants, John George and Tommy George. The Appellants challenge their conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception, specifically contesting the definition of "value" under the theft statute, the sufficiency of evidence regarding deception, and the constitutionality of the statute as applied. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

The standard for reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence remains well settled. When reviewing a motion to dismiss, the court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State and determine whether any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Green*, 94 Wash. 2d 216, 221, 616 P.2d 628 (1980). The Court does not weigh credibility or resolve conflicts in the evidence; those tasks are solely for the jury.

**B. The Value of Property in Theft by Deception**

The Appellants persist in their argument that the State failed to prove the "value" element of first-degree theft. They contend that because the cashier's check was not negotiated, the victims did not actually "part with" $5,500, and furthermore, that the State must prove the "net obtainment" by subtracting the value of the truck delivered from the price paid. These arguments are unpersuasive under controlling Washington law.

**1. The "Price Paid" Rule and the Cashier's Check**

The Appellants argue that *State v. Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d 492, 687 P.2d 1158 (1984), requires an actual loss of funds, which they claim did not occur because the check was not cashed. This misreads the statute and the precedent. In *Brown*, the Washington Supreme Court held that "the value of property taken by deception is the amount the victim parted with in reliance on the false representation." *Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d at 495. The victims here, undercover police officers, tendered a cashier's check for $5,500 to the Appellants. A cashier's check is a negotiable instrument representing the stated value. By delivering the check to the Appellants, the officers parted with value. The fact that the Appellants were arrested before they could negotiate the check does not negate the fact that the victims relinquished the instrument in reliance on the Appellants' misrepresentations. The "parting with" occurs at the moment the victim transfers the agreed-upon consideration based on the fraud.

**2. Rejection of the "Net Obtainment" Theory**

The Appellants urge this Court to adopt a "net obtainment" theory, arguing that because they delivered a truck to the officers, they did not "obtain" $5,500 in value, but rather the difference between the truck's worth and the price paid. This argument is directly contrary to the holding of *State v. Johnson*, 67 Wash. App. 722, 840 P.2d 894 (1992).

In *Johnson*, the defendant was convicted of theft for selling a vehicle with a tampered odometer. The victim paid $15,900 for the vehicle. The defendant in *Johnson* undoubtedly transferred a vehicle to the victim, yet the court held that "the value for theft purposes is the price paid by the victim, not the actual market value of the vehicle with correct mileage." *Johnson*, 67 Wash. App. at 726. The court did not reduce the theft value by the residual value of the car received by the victim. The rationale is that the victim's loss is defined by the consideration paid based on the fraudulent premise. To adopt the Appellants' "net obtainment" theory would require the State to prove the fair market value of the subject property in every deception case, a burden explicitly rejected by *Brown* and *Johnson*. The State proved the victims paid $5,500; that is the statutory value of the theft.

**C. Sufficiency of Evidence Regarding Deception**

The Appellants argue that the evidence of deception was insufficient because their statements were ambiguous or because they lacked knowledge of the true mileage. Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, these arguments fail.

The record establishes that Jerome Potter informed Tommy George that the truck had 185,000 miles, despite the odometer reading 70,000. Despite this knowledge, the Appellants advertised the truck as having "70 K mi" and represented to the detectives that the mileage was 70,000. This is direct evidence of a knowing misrepresentation regarding a material fact. The Appellants' claim that they might have believed the odometer accurately reflected the mileage is a question of credibility for the jury, which was resolved against them.

Similarly, the representation that John George was the "original and sole owner" and that the truck was "all stock and original" was contradicted by the evidence that they purchased the truck from Potter two years prior and that the engine had been swapped. The Appellants' attempt to parse "original owner" to mean "owner since I bought it" is unreasonable in the context of a 1974 vehicle advertised in a newspaper. The jury was entitled to find these statements were false and made with intent to defraud.

**D. The Attempt Element**

The Appellants' argument regarding the "attempt" element hinges on their assertion that the theft was not complete because the check was not cashed. As discussed above, the theft was complete when the victims parted with the $5,500 check in reliance on the Appellants' deception. However, even if the transaction were viewed as incomplete, the elements of attempted theft were satisfied. RCW 9A.28.030 defines an attempt as taking a "substantial step" toward the commission of the crime. The Appellants negotiated the sale, accepted the check, and transferred the title. These acts constitute a substantial step strongly corroborative of the criminal intent.

**E. Vagueness Challenge**

Finally, the Appellants' assertion that the statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied to sellers is foreclosed by precedent. In *State v. Bock*, 113 Wash. 2d 158, 163, 777 P.2d 14 (1989), the court held that the theft statute provides adequate notice and is not vague. The Appellants' novel theory that the statute fails to give notice that accepting payment for a fraudulent sale constitutes theft does not undermine the holding in *Bock*. The statute clearly prohibits obtaining property or services by deception; the "price paid" rule provides a clear metric for determining the degree of the offense.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly denied the Appellants' motion to dismiss. The State presented sufficient evidence to support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The value of the property taken was the $5,500 paid by the victims, satisfying the statutory threshold under *State v. Brown* and *State v. Johnson*. The evidence supported the jury's finding of deception, and the "attempt" element was met. The Appellants' arguments regarding the "net obtainment" of value and the negotiation of the cashier's check are rejected as contrary to established law. Accordingly, the decision of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.

The defense's claim that this transforms theft into a "thought crime" ignores that the officers parted with the check as consideration for the truck—this is not a mere agreement but an actual transfer of value in reliance on fraud.

Third, the evidence of deception was more than sufficient. The defense argues that statements about "original and sole owner" or "always garaged" were ambiguous, but the record proves otherwise. Jerome Potter disclosed to Tommy George that the truck had 185,000 miles despite the five-digit odometer reading 70,000. Despite this knowledge, the Georges advertised "70 K mi" and told detectives the mileage was 70,000. They also claimed John George was the "original and sole owner" when they purchased the truck from Potter just two years prior. They stated the truck was "all stock and original" and "always garaged" when it sat uncovered outside Potter's home for two years before their purchase. These were not ambiguous statements—they were deliberate misrepresentations of material facts. Under *State v. Green*, 94 Wash. 2d 216, 221, 616 P.2d 628 (1980), this Court does not reweigh credibility or resolve conflicts in the evidence; those are for the jury. Here, the jury reasonably found deception, and the evidence supports that finding.

Fourth, the "attempt" element was satisfied. RCW 9A.28.030 defines an attempt as taking a "substantial step" toward commission of the crime. The Georges negotiated the sale, accepted the cashier's check, and transferred the title. These acts constitute a substantial step strongly corroborative of criminal intent. The theft was complete upon the victims parting with the check in reliance on fraud—no further action was needed. The defense's suggestion that "obtain" requires actual cashing of the check is unsupported by law. *Brown* confirms that the value is measured by the consideration parted with, not by subsequent negotiation.

Finally, the defense's vagueness challenge is foreclosed by precedent. In *State v. Bock*, 113 Wash. 2d 158, 163, 777 P.2d 14 (1989), the Washington Supreme Court held that the theft statute provides adequate notice and is not unconstitutionally vague. The "price paid" rule under *Brown* and *Johnson* gives sellers clear notice that misrepresenting property value to obtain payment constitutes theft. Sellers are on notice that accepting payment based on false representations about mileage, ownership, or condition is illegal. The defense's claim that this theory subjects sellers to arbitrary enforcement ignores *Bock* and the statutory clarity.

The Court of Appeals correctly applied binding precedent. The value was $5,500—the amount parted with—under *Brown* and *Johnson*. The evidence of deception was sufficient. The attempt element was satisfied. And the statute is not vague under *Bock*. We urge the court to affirm.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Court of Appeals of the State of Washington

---

## JOHN GEORGE and TOMMY GEORGE, Appellants v. STATE OF WASHINGTON, Respondent

**No. 57984-1-I**

---

Your Honors, we respectfully submit that the Court of Appeals erred in affirming the conviction and that this Honorable Court should reverse. The prosecution has failed to establish the essential elements of attempted first-degree theft by deception beyond a reasonable doubt, and the conviction rests on a misapplication of controlling precedent.

---

## I. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE THE "VALUE" ELEMENT FOR FIRST-DEGREE THEFT

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm a conviction based on a cashier's check that was never negotiated, arguing that the mere tendering of an instrument constitutes "parting with" value under *State v. Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d 492, 687 P.2d 1158 (1984). This interpretation stretches the plain meaning of the statute beyond its intended scope and conflicts with fundamental principles of theft law.

### A. The Cashier's Check Was Never Negotiated, and No Actual Loss Occurred

The prosecution's reliance on *Brown* is misplaced. In *Brown*, the victim actually parted with funds—she wrote a check that was cashed, and the defendant received actual money. Here, the undercover officers tendered a cashier's check, but the Georges were arrested before they could negotiate it. No funds ever left the officers' control. The check remained in the possession of law enforcement, and the officers suffered no actual pecuniary loss.

The prosecution argues that "parting with" occurs at the moment of transfer based on fraud. However, this Court in *Brown* was concerned with actual losses suffered by victims, not hypothetical losses that might have occurred. A cashier's check, while a negotiable instrument, does not represent value transferred until it is honored. The officers retained the ability to stop payment, and the check was never cashed. To hold that mere tendering of a check constitutes "parting with" value would create liability for theft based on a fictional loss—a result the legislature could not have intended.

### B. The "Net Obtainment" Theory Should Apply

The defense respectfully maintains that the "net obtainment" theory articulated in our briefing is correct and consistent with fundamental fairness. When a defendant delivers property in exchange for payment, the defendant has obtained only the difference between the payment received and the value of the property delivered. This is not a novel theory—it is basic principles of value exchange applied to theft law.

In *State v. Johnson*, 67 Wash. App. 722, 840 P.2d 894 (1992), the defendant sold a vehicle with a tampered odometer. However, the defendant in *Johnson* actually delivered a vehicle to the victim—the victim drove away with the car. The victim suffered a loss because she paid $15,900 for a car worth far less. Here, the officers received the truck. They got what they paid for, even if the representations about the truck were false. The truck had value—it ran, had an engine, and was drivable after the differential was repaired.

The prosecution's position that the State need not prove the truck's market value effectively eliminates any requirement to show actual harm. Under this theory, a seller could be guilty of first-degree theft for selling a used item at a high price, even if the item had substantial value, simply because the buyer later regretted the purchase. This cannot be the law.

### C. The Statute Requires Actual "Obtainment" of Property or Services

RCW 9A.56.030 defines theft as obtaining "property or services" by deception. The statute uses the word "obtain," which implies actual receipt and retention of value. When the Georges delivered the truck to the officers, they did not "obtain" $5,500 in the sense contemplated by the statute—they received a check that was never honored, and they transferred ownership of a vehicle in return.

The prosecution confuses "parting with consideration" with "obtaining value." The victim must actually lose property or services for theft to occur. Here, the officers gave a check but retained the ability to stop payment. They gave up nothing of actual value until the check was honored. Because the check was never honored, no theft occurred.

---

## II. THE EVIDENCE OF DECEPTION WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT FIRST-DEGREE THEFT

The prosecution has overstated the misrepresentations made by the Georges and has failed to prove that the Georges knowingly deceived the officers.

### A. The Georges Did Not Knowingly Misrepresent the Mileage

The prosecution claims that because Jerome Potter told Tommy George the truck had 185,000 miles, the Georges must have known the odometer was inaccurate. This inference is illogical and ignores the facts.

Potter told Tommy George that the truck had 185,000 miles on it—but the odometer showed 70,000. Potter also told George that he had replaced the 350 engine with a 400. This information was conflicting and confusing. A reasonable person could have believed that the engine replacement affected the mileage reading, or that the odometer had been replaced along with the engine. The Georges had no independent knowledge of the truck's true mileage, and they reasonably relied on what the odometer showed.

The prosecution's theory requires the jury to infer that the Georges knew the mileage was falsified, but the evidence shows only that they had conflicting information. The prosecution has not proven beyond a reasonable doubt that the Georges knew their representations were false.

### B. The Representations About Ownership and Garaging Were Not Materially Deceptive

The prosecution makes much of the fact that John George claimed to be the "original and sole owner" when he had purchased the truck from Potter two years earlier. However, in the context of a used vehicle sale, "original owner" commonly refers to the person who has owned the vehicle since it was first purchased new. John George had owned the truck for two years—he was the owner since his purchase, even if not the first owner from the manufacturer.

Similarly, the representation that the truck was "all stock and original" referred to its condition at the time of the Georges' ownership. The engine had been swapped before they purchased the truck, and they honestly believed the truck was in original condition when they owned it. The statement "always garaged" was puffery—a common advertising term meaning the vehicle has been well-maintained, not a literal guarantee about storage.

These statements, while perhaps technically inaccurate, do not constitute the kind of knowing misrepresentation required for theft by deception. The prosecution has not proven that the Georges made these statements with knowledge of their falsity.

### C. The Officers Knew the Representations Were False

The prosecution overlooks a critical fact: the buyers were undercover police officers conducting an investigation. They knew the representations were false before they agreed to purchase the truck. Detectives Stokke, O'Donnell, and Duffy were searching for fraudulent advertisements—they were not innocent victims deceived by the Georges' lies.

The crime of theft by deception requires that the victim rely on the defendant's false representations. Here, the officers did not rely on the Georges' statements—they knew the statements were false and proceeded with the purchase to build a case. There can be no theft by deception when the "victim" knows the representations are false and proceeds anyway. *See State v. Riordan*, 89 Wash. 2d 105, 569 P.2d 1114 (1977) (holding that deception requires reliance on a misrepresentation).

---

## III. THE ATTEMPT ELEMENT WAS NOT SATISFIED

The prosecution argues that the Georges took a "substantial step" toward committing theft by negotiating the sale and accepting the check. However, even accepting the prosecution's theory of the case, the elements of attempt were not satisfied.

### A. The Georges Did Not Intend to Commit Theft

The prosecution has not proven that the Georges intended to defraud the officers. The Georges purchased the truck from Potter, made improvements, and attempted to sell it at a profit. This is the behavior of sellers seeking fair market value, not criminals seeking to defraud buyers.

The fact that the Georges made statements about the truck's condition does not establish criminal intent. Sellers routinely make puffery claims about their merchandise. The prosecution has not proven that the Georges knew their statements were false or that they intended to deceive the officers.

### B. The Sale Was Consensual and Negotiated

The officers approached the Georges, expressed interest, and negotiated the price. This is not the behavior of victims—it is the behavior of willing buyers. The Georges did not force the officers to buy the truck or trick them into paying more than they wanted to pay. The officers agreed to pay $5,500 freely and voluntarily.

For there to be an attempt, the defendant must take a substantial step toward completing the crime. Here, the Georges simply sold their truck to willing buyers. This is not a substantial step toward theft—it is the completion of a legitimate, if perhaps overly optimistic, sale.

---

## IV. THE STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AS APPLIED

The prosecution argues that *State v. Bock*, 113 Wash. 2d 158, 777 P.2d 14 (1989), forecloses the defense's vagueness challenge. However, *Bock* addressed a different issue—the clarity of the theft statute in general. The question here is whether the statute gives fair notice that selling a used vehicle with exaggerated claims constitutes first-degree theft.

First-degree theft requires that the property obtained be worth at least $5,000. Under the prosecution's theory, any seller who makes a false statement about a used item and receives payment could be guilty of first-degree theft, regardless of the item's actual value. This sweeps in conduct that ordinary citizens would not understand to be criminal.

A seller who advertises a used truck as having "70,000 miles" when it has 185,000 miles might be guilty of misrepresentation, but not first-degree theft. The seller expects to receive the purchase price in exchange for the item. The seller does not expect to be charged with theft for receiving payment.

The prosecution's theory creates a trap for sellers. A person who honestly believes their used item is worth the asking price could be convicted of first-degree theft if a buyer later claims they were deceived. This is not the kind of conduct the theft statute was designed to address.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Honorable Court reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and dismiss the charges against John George and Tommy George.

The prosecution has failed to prove that the Georges obtained property or services by deception, as required by RCW 9A.56.030. The cashier's check was never negotiated, and no actual loss occurred. The evidence of knowing deception was insufficient to support the conviction. The attempt element was not satisfied. And the statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied to sellers of used merchandise.

We ask this Court to apply the law fairly and to reverse a conviction that rests on an expansion of theft law beyond its intended scope.

**STATE OF WASHINGTON, Respondent, v. JOHN GEORGE and TOMMY GEORGE, Appellants.**

**NO. 57984-1-I**

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Appellants' challenge to their conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The Appellants, John George and Tommy George, contend that the State failed to prove the "value" element of the offense, arguing that the "net obtainment" theory should apply and that no actual loss occurred because the cashier's check was not negotiated. They further assert that the evidence of deception was insufficient and that the statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied. Having carefully reviewed the closing arguments of both parties and the record, the Court finds the Appellants' arguments unpersuasive. The trial court properly denied the motion to dismiss, and the conviction is affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review**

When reviewing a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, this Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State and determine whether any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Green*, 94 Wash. 2d 216, 221, 616 P.2d 628 (1980). The Court does not weigh credibility or resolve conflicts in the evidence; those tasks are solely for the jury. *Green*, 94 Wash. 2d at 221.

**B. The Value of Property and the "Price Paid" Rule**

The Appellants' primary contention is that the State failed to prove the "value" element of first-degree theft. They argue that because the cashier's check was not negotiated, the victims did not actually "part with" value, and furthermore, that the value should be calculated based on the "net obtainment"—the difference between the price paid and the truck's actual market value. Both arguments are rejected under controlling precedent.

**1. The "Parting With" of Value**

The Appellants argue that *State v. Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d 492, 687 P.2d 1158 (1984), requires an actual loss of funds, which they claim did not occur because the check was not cashed. This misinterprets the holding in *Brown*. In *Brown*, the Washington Supreme Court held that "the value of property taken by deception is the amount the victim parted with in reliance on the false representation." *Brown*, 102 Wash. 2d at 495.

Here, the victims, undercover police officers, tendered a cashier's check for $5,500 to the Appellants. A cashier's check is a negotiable instrument representing the stated value. By delivering the check to the Appellants, the officers relinquished control of that instrument in reliance on the Appellants' misrepresentations. The "parting with" occurs at the moment the victim transfers the agreed-upon consideration based on the fraud. The fact that the Appellants were arrested before they could negotiate the check does not negate the fact that the victims parted with the value represented by the check. The Appellants' argument that this creates liability for a "fictional loss" ignores the legal reality that a cashier's check is the equivalent of cash for purposes of the transfer of value.

**2. Rejection of the "Net Obtainment" Theory**

The Appellants urge this Court to adopt a "net obtainment" theory, arguing that because they delivered a truck to the officers, they did not "obtain" $5,500 in value, but rather the difference between the truck's worth and the price paid. This argument is directly contrary to the holding of *State v. Johnson*, 67 Wash. App. 722, 840 P.2d 894 (1992).

In *Johnson*, the defendant was convicted of theft for selling a vehicle with a tampered odometer. The victim paid $15,900 for the vehicle. The defendant in *Johnson* undoubtedly transferred a vehicle to the victim, yet the court held that "the value for theft purposes is the price paid by the victim, not the actual market value of the vehicle with correct mileage." *Johnson*, 67 Wash. App. at 726. The court explicitly rejected the notion that the State must prove the fair market value of the subject property or reduce the theft value by the residual value of the item received by the victim. The rationale is that the victim's loss is defined by the consideration paid based on the fraudulent premise. To adopt the Appellants' "net obtainment" theory would require the State to prove the fair market value of the subject property in every deception case, a burden explicitly rejected by *Brown* and *Johnson*. The State proved the victims paid $5,500; that is the statutory value of the theft.

**C. Sufficiency of Evidence Regarding Deception**

The Appellants argue that the evidence of deception was insufficient because their statements were ambiguous, because they lacked knowledge of the true mileage, or because the "victims" (police officers) did not actually rely on the misrepresentations. Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, these arguments fail.

**1. Misrepresentation of Material Facts**

The record establishes that Jerome Potter informed Tommy George that the truck had 185,000 miles, despite the odometer reading 70,000. Despite this knowledge, the Appellants advertised the truck as having "70 K mi" and represented to the detectives that the mileage was 70,000. This is direct evidence of a knowing misrepresentation regarding a material fact. The Appellants' claim that they might have believed the odometer accurately reflected the mileage, or that the engine replacement caused confusion, is a question of credibility for the jury, which was resolved against them. *State v. Green*, 94 Wash. 2d at 221.

Similarly, the representation that John George was the "original and sole owner" and that the truck was "all stock and original" was contradicted by the evidence that they purchased the truck from Potter two years prior and that the engine had been swapped. The Appellants' attempt to parse "original owner" to mean "owner since I bought it" is unreasonable in the context of a 1974 vehicle advertised in a newspaper. The jury was entitled to find these statements were false and made with intent to defraud.

**2. Reliance by the Victims**

The Appellants argue, citing *State v. Riordan*, 89 Wash. 2d 105, 569 P.2d 1114 (1977), that theft by deception requires reliance, and the police officers could not have relied on the statements because they knew they were false. This argument misconstrues the nature of the reliance element in a sting operation. The officers acted as willing buyers, negotiated the price, and tendered a cashier's check based on the Appellants' representations. Their outward conduct demonstrated reliance. The Appellants' intent to deceive a victim, and the completion of the transaction based on that deception, satisfies the statutory requirements. The subjective knowledge of the undercover officers does not negate the Appellants' criminal intent or the objective act of parting with value based on the fraud.

**D. The Attempt Element**

The Appellants' argument regarding the "attempt" element hinges on their assertion that the theft was not complete because the check was not cashed. As discussed above, the theft was complete when the victims parted with the $5,500 check in reliance on the Appellants' deception. However, even if the transaction were viewed as incomplete, the elements of attempted theft were satisfied. RCW 9A.28.030 defines an attempt as taking a "substantial step" toward the commission of the crime. The Appellants negotiated the sale, accepted the check, and transferred the title. These acts constitute a substantial step strongly corroborative of the criminal intent.

**E. Vagueness Challenge**

Finally, the Appellants' assertion that the statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied to sellers is foreclosed by precedent. In *State v. Bock*, 113 Wash. 2d 158, 163, 777 P.2d 14 (1989), the court held that the theft statute provides adequate notice and is not vague. The Appellants' novel theory that the statute fails to give notice that accepting payment for a fraudulent sale constitutes theft does not undermine the holding in *Bock*. The statute clearly prohibits obtaining property or services by deception; the "price paid" rule provides a clear metric for determining the degree of the offense.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly denied the Appellants' motion to dismiss. The State presented sufficient evidence to support a conviction for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The value of the property taken was the $5,500 paid by the victims, satisfying the statutory threshold under *State v. Brown* and *State v. Johnson*. The evidence supported the jury's finding of deception, and the "attempt" element was met. The Appellants' arguments regarding the "net obtainment" of value and the negotiation of the cashier's check are rejected as contrary to established law. Accordingly, the decision of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.
