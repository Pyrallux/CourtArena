## Legal Analysis

### I. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting the NPLEX Records Through Officer Lemonier

The prosecution contends that Officer Lemonier was qualified to lay foundation for the NPLEX records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6). However, the defense submits that this argument fails because Officer Lemonier lacked the personal knowledge necessary to authenticate these records as required by the rule.

Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) provides that records of an act, condition, or other event made in the regular course of business at or near the time of the act, condition, or event may be admitted if the custodian or another qualified witness testifies that the records were made in the regular course of business. The key requirement is that the witness authenticating the records must have personal knowledge of the record-keeping system and the manner in which the records were created.

In State v. Jackson, 109 Ohio St.3d 1, 2006-Ohio-1037, 845 N.E.2d 510, the Ohio Supreme Court addressed the requirements for authenticating business records. While the Court in Jackson affirmed that a non-custodian witness may authenticate business records when properly qualified, the Court emphasized that the witness must still demonstrate sufficient knowledge of the record-keeping system. The prosecution bears the burden of establishing this foundation through testimony.

Officer Lemonier's testimony in this case failed to establish that he possessed the requisite knowledge of the NPLEX record-keeping system. The record does not indicate that Officer Lemonier was involved in creating or maintaining the NPLEX records, nor that he had any familiarity with the procedures by which these records were generated. His testimony regarding the NPLEX records was based on review of the records themselves rather than personal knowledge of the system that produced them.

As the Ohio Supreme Court stated in State v. Davis, 116 Ohio St.3d 404, 2008-Ohio-2, 880 N.E.2d 31, the authentication requirement under Ohio Evidence Rule 901(A) requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what its proponent claims. This requirement is not satisfied merely by a witness identifying a document; rather, the witness must have personal knowledge sufficient to testify that the document is what it purports to be.

### II. The NPLEX Records Do Not Satisfy the Business Records Exception

Beyond the authentication deficiency, the defense submits that the prosecution failed to establish that the NPLEX records satisfy the foundational requirements of Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6). The rule requires proof that the records were: (1) made in the regular course of business; (2) at or near the time of the act, condition, or event recorded; and (3) made by a person with knowledge of the act or from information transmitted by a person with knowledge.

The prosecution's argument that the records were made in the regular course of business, while facially correct, fails to address whether the specific NPLEX records at issue were made in compliance with the regular practices of the NPLEX system. The defense submits that the trial court erred in admitting these records without requiring the prosecution to establish the regularity of the specific record-keeping process that produced them.

In United States v. Box, 530 F.2d 722 (6th Cir. 1976), the Sixth Circuit held that the business records exception requires a showing that the records were made in the regular course of business and that the circumstances of their creation indicate their trustworthiness. The prosecution in this case failed to make such a showing through Officer Lemonier's testimony.

### III. The Confrontation Clause Implications

While the prosecution correctly notes that the Confrontation Clause, as interpreted in Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S. Ct. 1354 (2004), applies only to testimonial statements, the defense submits that this does not resolve the foundational issues regarding authentication. Even assuming arguendo that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial business records, they must still satisfy the authentication requirements of Ohio Evidence Rule 901 and the foundational requirements of Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6).

Furthermore, in Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527 (2009), the Supreme Court emphasized the importance of proper foundation for documentary evidence. While the Court in that case distinguished between testimonial and non-testimonial business records, the Court did not eliminate the requirement that such records be properly authenticated and founded upon.

### IV. The Standard of Review Does Not Preclude Relief

The prosecution correctly notes that the standard of review for evidentiary rulings is abuse of discretion. However, this standard does not shield the trial court's ruling from meaningful review. An abuse of discretion exists when the trial court's ruling is unreasonable, arbitrary, or unconscionable. State v. Adams, 106 Ohio St.3d 231, 2005-Ohio-4788, 833 N.E.2d 1244.

The defense submits that the trial court's admission of the NPLEX records through Officer Lemonier, who lacked the requisite personal knowledge and qualification, was unreasonable and constituted an abuse of discretion. The prosecution failed to lay the proper foundation for these records, and their admission violated the rules of evidence.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling admitting the NPLEX records into evidence, and remand this case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. The defense submits that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting records that were not properly authenticated and that failed to satisfy the foundational requirements of the business records exception. The admission of these records without proper foundation constitutes reversible error that affected Ms. Kittle's substantial rights.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense regarding the admissibility of the NPLEX records. **ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard of review for the admission of evidence is abuse of discretion. A trial court abuses its discretion when its decision is unreasonable, arbitrary, or unconscionable. State v. Adams, 106 Ohio St.3d 231, 2005-Ohio-4788, 833 N.E.2d 1244. While appellate courts afford deference to a trial court's evidentiary rulings, this deference is not unlimited, and it does not preclude reversal where the admission of evidence violates established rules of evidence or constitutional rights.

**II. Confrontation Clause Considerations**

Both parties correctly identify the framework established by the United States Supreme Court regarding the Confrontation Clause. In Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S. Ct. 1354 (2004), the Court held that the Confrontation Clause applies only to "testimonial" statements. The prosecution argues, and the defense concedes, that the NPLEX records constitute business records created in the ordinary course of business and are therefore non-testimonial. This distinction was further clarified in Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, 557 U.S. 305, 129 S. Ct. 2527 (2009), where the Court confirmed that routine business records generally do not implicate the Confrontation Clause. However, as the defense correctly notes, the non-testimonial nature of the records does not absolve the prosecution of the burden to establish a proper foundation for their admission under the rules of evidence.

**III. Foundation and Authentication of Business Records**

The central dispute in this case is whether Officer Lemonier was a qualified witness to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) and authenticate them under Ohio Evidence Rule 901(A).

Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) permits the admission of records of regularly conducted activity if the custodian or "another qualified witness" testifies to the record's identity and the mode of its preparation. The Ohio Supreme Court has addressed the parameters of this rule in State v. Jackson, 109 Ohio St.3d 1, 2006-Ohio-1037, 845 N.E.2d 510. While *Jackson* affirms that a non-custodian witness may authenticate business records, it requires that the witness demonstrate sufficient knowledge of the record-keeping system to satisfy the rule's requirements. The witness must be able to testify that the records were made in the regular course of business at or near the time of the event recorded.

The defense argues that Officer Lemonier lacked the requisite personal knowledge of the NPLEX record-keeping system. Specifically, the defense contends that Officer Lemonier was not involved in the creation or maintenance of the records and that his testimony was based solely on his review of the documents themselves rather than an understanding of the system that produced them. This argument is supported by State v. Davis, 116 Ohio St.3d 404, 2008-Ohio-2, 880 N.E.2d 31, which holds that authentication under Rule 901(A) requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what its proponent claims it is. Mere identification of a document is insufficient; the witness must possess personal knowledge regarding the document's creation and reliability.

The prosecution's argument fails to rebut this specific deficiency. The prosecution asserts generally that Officer Lemonier was a "qualified witness" but does not provide facts demonstrating that Officer Lemonier had familiarity with the NPLEX system's procedures or that the records were made in the regular course of business. The prosecution relies on the broad proposition that non-custodians may authenticate records, but it does not address the defense's specific contention that this particular witness lacked the necessary knowledge of the system's operation.

Furthermore, the defense cites United States v. Box, 530 F.2d 722 (6th Cir. 1976), for the proposition that the business records exception requires a showing that the records were made in the regular course of business and that the circumstances of their creation indicate trustworthiness. The prosecution has not demonstrated through Officer Lemonier's testimony that the specific NPLEX records at issue met these criteria.

**IV. Conclusion**

The prosecution has not shown that Officer Lemonier possessed the personal knowledge of the NPLEX record-keeping system required by State v. Jackson, 109 Ohio St.3d 1, 2006-Ohio-1037, 845 N.E.2d 510, and State v. Davis, 116 Ohio St.3d 404, 2008-Ohio-2, 880 N.E.2d 31. Without a proper foundation establishing the regularity and trustworthiness of the records, their admission was erroneous. Therefore, the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the NPLEX records.

**RULING**

The defense's opening argument is sustained. The trial court's ruling admitting the NPLEX records is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

The Davis Court held that authentication under Rule 901(A) requires "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what its proponent claims it is." This is a minimal threshold. The prosecution cannot rely on Davis to satisfy the distinct and more demanding requirements of Rule 803(6). As this Court correctly noted in its preliminary ruling, Davis defines the substantive requirements that the trial court must apply before exercising its discretion. The prosecution cannot use the minimal authentication requirement of Davis to circumvent the foundational requirements for business records.

Third, the prosecution argues that United States v. Box, 530 F.2d 722 (6th Cir. 1976, is "equally misplaced." The defense disagrees. Box requires a showing that records were made in the regular course of business and that the circumstances of their creation indicate trustworthiness. The prosecution claims that "Officer Lemonier's testimony—rooted in his investigation of the meth lab, his review of the NPLEX records to identify pseudoephedrine purchases, and his use of those records to obtain Ms. Kittle's admission—directly established both elements." This argument fails because Officer Lemonier's testimony about his own investigative activities says nothing about how or when the NPLEX records were created. The fact that an investigator uses records in an investigation does not establish that the records were made in the regular course of business. The prosecution has it backwards: the witness must testify about the record-keeper's activities, not the investigator's activities.

Fourth, the prosecution argues that "Ms. Kittle's own confession" corroborates the records' trustworthiness. This argument is irrelevant to foundational requirements. The trustworthiness of records must be established through evidence about their creation, not through subsequent corroboration. Moreover, Ms. Kittle's confession—that she bought pseudoephedrine for J.S.—is consistent with the NPLEX records but does not establish that the records were created in the regular course of business. The prosecution cannot bootstrap the foundational requirements by pointing to corroborating evidence.

**V. The Confrontation Clause Analysis Is Separate From Foundation Requirements**

The defense acknowledges, as it did in its preliminary briefing, that the NPLEX records are non-testimonial business records not subject to the Confrontation Clause under Crawford v. However, the prosecution's concession on this point does not resolve the foundational issue.

The Confrontation Clause and the rules of evidence address different concerns. The Confrontation Clause protects defendants from testimonial statements of absent witnesses. Business records, because they are created for purposes unrelated to litigation, are not testimonial. However, even non-testimonial evidence must satisfy the rules of evidence, including foundational requirements. The prosecution cannot rely on the non-testimonial nature of the records to excuse the failure to establish proper foundation.

The prosecution's argument that "the defense's insistence that constitutional concerns 'cure' foundational deficiencies is legally incoherent" misrepresents the defense's position. The defense has never argued that constitutional concerns cure foundational deficiencies. The defense has argued, and continues to argue, that the foundational requirements for business records were not satisfied. The prosecution cannot distract from this failure by invoking the Confrontation Clause.

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. **ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

The standard of review for the admission of evidence is abuse of discretion. A trial court abuses its discretion when its decision is unreasonable, arbitrary, or unconscionable. State v. Adams, 106 Ohio St.3d 231, 2005-Ohio-4788, 833 N.E.2d 1244. While this Court affords deference to trial court evidentiary rulings, such deference is not a license to admit evidence that fails to meet the foundational requirements of the Ohio Rules of Evidence.

**II. Foundation for Business Records Under State v. Jackson**

The central issue is whether Officer Lemonier was a "qualified witness" capable of laying the proper foundation for the NPLEX records under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6). The Ohio Supreme Court in State v. Jackson, 109 Ohio St.3d 1, 2006-Ohio-1037, 845 N.E.2d 510, held that a non-custodian witness may authenticate business records only if they possess "sufficient knowledge of the record-keeping system" to testify that the records were made in the regular course of business at or near the time of the event recorded.

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier was qualified because he "personally retrieved and reviewed" the records during his investigation. However, mere retrieval and review of a document does not equate to knowledge of the record-keeping system that produced it. The facts provided establish that Officer Lemonier was a field officer involved in surveillance, the traffic stop, and the interview of Ms. Kittle. There is no indication in the record that he had personal knowledge of the administrative or operational aspects of the NPLEX database, such as how data is transmitted from pharmacies to the database or the quality control measures in place.

The prosecution's reliance on Officer Lemonier's role as the lead investigator is insufficient to satisfy *Jackson*. The "sufficient knowledge" required by *Jackson* refers to the witness's ability to testify to the method and mode of preparation of the records, ensuring their trustworthiness. Officer Lemonier's ability to read the records or use them to confront a suspect does not provide him with knowledge of how the records were created or maintained. Therefore, the prosecution failed to establish that Officer Lemonier was a qualified witness under *Jackson*.

**III. Authentication vs. Foundation: State v. Davis**

The prosecution relies heavily on State v. Davis, 116 Ohio St.3d 404, 2008-Ohio-2, 880 N.E.2d 31, arguing that Officer Lemonier's personal interaction with the records satisfied the authentication requirements of Rule 901(A). While *Davis* holds that authentication requires only "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what its proponent claims it is," this is merely the first step.

The defense correctly distinguishes between authentication under Rule 901(A) and the foundation required for the business records exception under Rule 803(6). *Davis* does not supplant the requirements of *Jackson*. Even if Officer Lemonier could authenticate that the documents were "NPLEX records," he still lacked the knowledge of the record-keeping system necessary to establish the foundational predicate for the business records exception—that the records were made in the regular course of business at or near the time of the events. The prosecution's conflation of these two distinct requirements is fatal to its argument.

**IV. Trustworthiness and Regularity: United States v. Box**

The admission of business records also requires a showing that the records were made in the regular course of business and that the circumstances of their creation indicate trustworthiness. United States v. Box, 530 F.2d 722 (6th Cir. 1976). The prosecution argues that the records' reliability was corroborated by Ms. Kittle's confession. However, trustworthiness under the business records exception must be derived from the circumstances of the record's creation, not from subsequent corroborating evidence. Because Officer Lemonier could not testify to the NPLEX system's procedures or the regularity of its entries, the prosecution failed to meet the trustworthiness requirement of *Box*.

**V. Conclusion**

The prosecution's arguments fail to demonstrate that Officer Lemonier possessed the requisite knowledge of the NPLEX record-keeping system. The trial court's decision to admit the records was not supported by a proper foundation under Ohio Evidence Rule 803(6) as defined by *State v. Jackson*. Consequently, the trial court abused its discretion.

**RULING**

The defense's closing argument is sustained. The preliminary ruling is affirmed. The trial court's ruling admitting the NPLEX records is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
