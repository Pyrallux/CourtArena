# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the Prosecution's arguments and in support of reversing the trial court's denial of the motions to suppress and to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The evidence obtained through the search of Appellant was the fruit of an unlawful search predicated on an unreliable confidential informant, and the trial court erred in denying disclosure of the informant's identity, which was critical to Appellant's defense of entrapment and to challenging the probable cause supporting the search.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

On the date in question, law enforcement officers conducted an arrest and search of Appellant based upon information provided by a confidential informant. The officers discovered cocaine in Appellant's possession. Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine).

Prior to trial, Appellant filed a motion to suppress the evidence, arguing that the cocaine was obtained through an illegal search. He also filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant on two grounds: (1) that the informant's identity was needed to demonstrate that the officers lacked probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant, and (2) that the informant was a material witness to Appellant's guilt or innocence, particularly in connection with the possible defense of entrapment.

The parties agreed that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive," meaning that if the court ruled in favor of the state, Appellant would plead guilty, and if the court ruled in favor of the defense, the state would dismiss the case. Defense counsel subsequently sought to supplement the motion to suppress with new evidence regarding the confidential informant's identity, arguing that this information was relevant to the reliability of the informant and thus to the validity of the search.

The trial court denied both motions. Appellant subsequently entered a guilty plea and was sentenced to sixteen years in prison.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Erred in Denying the Motion to Suppress

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. The exclusionary rule prohibits the admission of evidence obtained through unlawful searches. Weeks v. United States, 232 U.S. 383 (1914). The burden rests upon the prosecution to demonstrate that the search that yielded the evidence was lawful. Coolidge v. New Hampshire, 403 U.S. 443 (1971).

In the instant case, the search was predicated upon information provided by a confidential informant. For probable cause to exist, the informant must be reliable and credible, and the information must be corroborated. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). Without disclosure of the informant's identity, the Defense could not effectively challenge the informant's reliability or credibility, nor could it present evidence that the informant was unreliable or had provided false information in the past.

The trial court acknowledged that it had "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." However, without disclosure of the informant's identity, the Defense could not test this assertion through cross-examination or by presenting contrary evidence. The court's reliance upon the prosecution's representations regarding the informant's reliability, without allowing the Defense to examine the informant or challenge her reliability, violated Appellant's constitutional rights.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Denying the Motion to Disclose the Confidential Informant's Identity

#### 1. The Informant Privilege and Its Limitations

The state has a privilege to withhold the identity of a confidential informant. Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957). However, this privilege is not absolute and must yield to the defendant's constitutional right to present a complete defense when the informant's testimony is material to the defense. Id.

In Roviaro, the Supreme Court held that the trial court must balance the state's interest in protecting the informant's identity against the defendant's right to prepare and present his defense. The Court concluded that where the informant is a participant in the crime charged or a material witness to the transaction, disclosure may be required. The Court stated that "the privilege is limited by the fundamental requirements of fairness," and that "no fixed rule with respect to disclosure is justifiable." 353 U.S. at 60-61.

#### 2. The Informant Was a Material Witness to the Defense of Entrapment

The Defense argued below that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day in question and that her belongings were found in his car when it was released from evidence. Defense counsel argued that "if the CI set him up, that is critical to his defense" and that "she is a material witness."

Entrapment is an affirmative defense that requires the defendant to show that he was induced by a government agent to commit the crime. Sorrells v. United States, 287 U.S. 435 (1932); Sherman v. United States, 356 U.S. 369 (1958). If the confidential informant induced Appellant to possess the cocaine, this would constitute entrapment, a complete defense to the charge.

In United States v. Russell, 411 U.S. 423 (1973), the Supreme Court recognized that government involvement in crime creation can violate due process. More recently, in Jacobson v. United States, 535 U.S. 178 (2002), the Court emphasized the importance of examining whether the government induced the defendant to commit the crime.

Here, the Defense proffered that the confidential informant "set him up" and was "with him that day." This directly relates to the defense of entrapment. The informant's identity was therefore material to the defense, and the trial court erred in denying disclosure.

#### 3. The Informant's Identity Was Necessary to Challenge Probable Cause

The Defense also argued that the informant's identity was needed to show that the officers did not have probable cause. As discussed above, probable cause requires a reliable informant and corroborated information. Without knowing the informant's identity, the Defense could not investigate her background, challenge her reliability, or present evidence that she was not credible.

In Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266 (2000), the Supreme Court emphasized the importance of the informant's reliability in establishing probable cause. The Court held that an anonymous tip, without more, is insufficient to establish probable cause. Here, the Defense was entitled to test the informant's reliability by learning her identity.

#### 4. The Case Law Supports Disclosure

The Defense cited *Bodin v. State* and *Holmes v. South Carolina* in support of its motion. In *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), the Supreme Court held that a state rule excluding evidence of third-party guilt, when based solely on the forensic evidence, violated the defendant's constitutional right to present a complete defense. The Court emphasized that the defendant must be permitted to "put on a complete defense." 547 U.S. at 331.

Similarly, in *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), the Court held that the exclusion of critical defense evidence violated the defendant's rights. The Court stated that "the right of an accused in a criminal trial to due process is violated when evidence highly relevant to the defense is excluded." 410 U.S. at 294.

Here, the confidential informant's identity was critical to the defense. Without it, Appellant could not effectively challenge the search or present the defense of entrapment. The trial court's refusal to order disclosure violated Appellant's constitutional rights.

### C. The Plea Agreement Should Be Set Aside

The plea agreement was expressly contingent upon the resolution of the dispositive motion to suppress. The prosecution stated: "if he rules not in the State's favor, that we dismiss the case." The Defense entered the plea agreement based upon the understanding that the motion to suppress would be fully litigated and that, if denied, Appellant would plead guilty.

However, the motion to suppress could not be fully litigated without disclosure of the confidential informant's identity. The informant's reliability was central to the probable cause determination. By denying the motion to disclose, the trial court effectively prevented a full and fair hearing on the motion to suppress.

Under these circumstances, the plea agreement was voidable because it was entered into based upon an incomplete adjudication of the suppression issue. See Mabry v. Johnson, 467 U.S. 504 (1984) (a plea agreement is contractual in nature and must be fairly negotiated).

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motions to suppress and to disclose the confidential informant's identity, and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

Respectfully submitted,

**DEFENSE COUNSEL**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s denial of Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant and the subsequent denial of the motion to suppress. The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in refusing to order disclosure, as the informant’s identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause for the search and to support a defense of entrapment. The Prosecution contends that the hearing was limited to a "dispositive" motion to suppress, thereby precluding the consideration of guilt or innocence issues such as entrapment, and that the reliability of the informant had already been established.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The state’s privilege to withhold the identity of an informant is not absolute. As established in *Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957)*, this privilege must yield where the informant’s identity is relevant to the defense of the accused. The Court in *Roviaro* emphasized that "the fundamental requirements of fairness" mandate disclosure when the informant may be a material witness to the events leading to the charge. 353 U.S. at 60-61.

The Prosecution argues that the parties agreed the hearing would be a "dispositive Motion to Suppress," limiting the scope of inquiry to the legality of the search and excluding issues of guilt or innocence. While the Court acknowledges the procedural agreement between the parties, this agreement cannot override the Appellant’s constitutional right to a fair hearing on the motion to suppress. To effectively challenge the legality of the search, the Appellant was entitled to test the reliability and credibility of the informant, which are essential components of the probable cause analysis under *Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983)*.

The Defense has proffered specific facts suggesting the informant was not merely a tipster but a participant in the events. Counsel argued that the informant was "with [Appellant] that day," that her belongings were found in his car, and that she may have "set him up." These allegations, if true, would make the informant a material witness to the alleged crime and directly relevant to the defense of entrapment. Under *Roviaro*, when an informant is a participant in the crime, the need for disclosure is at its zenith. Furthermore, the inability to cross-examine the informant or investigate her background prevented the Appellant from effectively challenging the informant’s reliability, a critical factor in determining probable cause.

The trial court’s reliance on the State’s representation of the informant’s past reliability, without allowing the Defense an opportunity to rebut this evidence, violated the Appellant’s right to present a meaningful defense. As held in *Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319 (2006)*, a defendant must be permitted to put on a complete defense, and the exclusion of material evidence relevant to the defense undermines the fairness of the trial.

Finally, because the motion to suppress could not be fully and fairly litigated without the disclosure of the informant’s identity, the plea agreement entered into following the denial of that motion is suspect. A plea agreement must be entered into knowingly and voluntarily, and if the Appellant was deprived of the ability to challenge the evidence against him, the agreement may be voidable. See *Mabry v. Johnson, 467 U.S. 504 (1984)*.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The Defense established a reasonable probability that the informant was a material witness to the alleged entrapment and that her identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause supporting the search. The procedural constraints of the "dispositive" motion do not justify depriving the Appellant of the evidence needed to defend against the charge.

Accordingly, the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

May it please the Court, the prosecution's closing argument attempts to obscure a fundamental truth: no procedural agreement—whether characterized as "dispositive" or otherwise—can extinguish a defendant's constitutional right to challenge the legality of the evidence against him and to present a complete defense. The prosecution asks this Court to sanction a framework where a defendant voluntarily waives his right to trial while simultaneously being stripped of the ability to meaningfully challenge the probable cause that underlies the very search he seeks to suppress. Such a framework is antithetical to due process and finds no support in controlling precedent.

The prosecution's reliance on the parties' agreement fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the rights at stake. The defendant did not agree to a proceeding where his challenges would be heard but his ability to investigate those challenges would be foreclosed. The prosecution cannot have it both ways: if the motion to suppress was truly "dispositive," then the reliability of the informant went to the very heart of the matter—and the defendant was entitled to test that reliability through disclosure.

---

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts, as established in the record, demonstrate why disclosure was essential.

The appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance. The evidence against him—specifically, the cocaine—was obtained through a search that purportedly relied upon information from a confidential informant. The defense filed a motion to suppress this evidence, challenging the probable cause underlying the search.

Critically, the defense also filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. This motion was predicated on two grounds: first, that the informant's identity was needed to demonstrate whether the officers had probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant; and second, that the informant was a material witness to the defendant's guilt or innocence and necessary to the possible defense of entrapment.

During the hearing on the motion to suppress, defense counsel proffered specific facts: the confidential informant was "with [the Appellant] that day," her belongings were found in his car when it was released from evidence, and the informant may have "set him up." These allegations, if true, would place the informant at the scene of the alleged crime and suggest active participation in the events leading to the charges.

The trial court denied both motions. The appellant, in accordance with the plea agreement, entered a guilty plea and received a sentence of sixteen years in the second-degree felony range.

---

### III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

#### A. The Informant's Identity Was Required Under Roviaro

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957)* by arguing that it applies only at trial, not in suppression proceedings. This argument fails. The Supreme Court in *Roviaro* held that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity must yield when the informant "may be a material witness on the issue of guilt or innocence." However, the Court did not limit this principle to trial proceedings alone. Rather, it established a balancing test that considers the particular circumstances of each case, including the crime charged, the possible defenses, the relative importance of the informant's testimony, and the degree to which the informant's testimony would aid the defense.

Here, the facts proffered by the defense place the informant squarely within the *Roviaro* framework. The informant was allegedly present at the scene of the crime, may have "set up" the appellant, and her belongings were found in the appellant's vehicle. These are not mere allegations of peripheral involvement; they are assertions that the informant was a participant in the very events giving rise to the prosecution. Under *Roviaro*, the need for disclosure is at its zenith in such circumstances.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Williams, 667 F.3d 70 (2d Cir. 2011)* for the proposition that a defendant forfeits the right to demand informant disclosure when a motion to suppress is designated dispositive. However, this reading of *Williams* is overly broad and was not the holding of that case. In *Williams*, the Second Circuit addressed a different scenario where the defendant sought disclosure for purposes wholly unrelated to the suppression issue. Here, by contrast, the defense seeks disclosure precisely because it goes to the reliability of the informant—the very foundation of the probable cause argument. The prosecution cannot invoke a case about disclosure for unrelated purposes to justify denying disclosure when the purpose is directly relevant to the suppression issue.

#### B. The Dispositive Nature of the Motion Did Not Eliminate the Need to Test Probable Cause

The prosecution argues that by agreeing to a "dispositive" motion to suppress, the defendant waived his right to challenge anything beyond the narrow question of whether probable cause existed. This argument proves too much.

Even under the prosecution's own framework, the question of probable cause was squarely before the Court. And the reliability of the informant is a cornerstone of the probable cause analysis under *Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983)*. The prosecution cannot simultaneously argue that (1) the only issue before the Court was probable cause, and (2) the defendant cannot investigate the very factor—informant reliability—that is central to the probable cause inquiry.

The trial court stated that it had "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." But this finding was based solely on the State's unchallenged representations. The defense was denied any opportunity to investigate or rebut this evidence. This procedure fundamentally undermined the fairness of the proceeding. Due process requires more than the State's unilateral assertion of reliability; it requires an opportunity for the defense to test that assertion.

In *Gates*, the Supreme Court emphasized the totality-of-the-circumstances approach to evaluating informant credibility, which includes considerations of the informant's reliability, veracity, and basis of knowledge. Without the informant's identity, the defense cannot investigate her background, her potential bias, or the accuracy of her past tips. The prosecution's position would allow the State to establish probable cause through an informant whose reliability is asserted but never tested—a result incompatible with the Fourth Amendment's protections.

#### C. The Entrapment Defense Required Disclosure

The prosecution argues that entrapment is a guilt-or-innocence defense that was never part of the dispositive motion framework. This argumentignores the defendant's proffer.

Entrapment requires proof of government inducement and lack of predisposition. *Mathews v. United States, 485 U.S. 58 (1988)*. If, as the defense alleges, the confidential informant "set up" the appellant, her identity and testimony are critical to establishing this defense. The prosecution cannot plausibly argue that the defendant should have raised entrapment at a trial while simultaneously denying him the evidence necessary to investigate and present such a defense.

The prosecution's reliance on *Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319 (2006)* is misplaced, but not in the way the prosecution suggests. *Holmes* held that a defendant must be permitted to put on a complete defense. While the prosecution is correct that *Holmes* addressed trial proceedings, the principle underlying that holding—that defendants have a constitutional right to present their defense—applies with equal force to proceedings where the defendant's liberty interests are at stake. The trial court's refusal to order disclosure effectively precluded the appellant from investigating and presenting an entrapment defense, violating his due process rights under *Holmes*.

#### D. The Plea Was Not Knowing and Voluntary

The prosecution argues that the plea was valid because the defendant knowingly agreed to the dispositive framework. This argument inverts the analysis.

A plea must be entered knowingly and voluntarily. *Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969)*. The Supreme Court has held that a plea "is not more than an admission of guilt, and the defendant must understand the nature of the charge against him and the consequences of his plea." Here, the appellant could not fully understand the nature of the case against him because he was denied access to evidence bearing on the legality of the search and the possible defense of entrapment.

In *Mabry v. Johnson, 467 U.S. 504 (1984)*, the Supreme Court addressed the validity of plea agreements, emphasizing that the plea must be knowing and voluntary based on the information available at the time of the plea. The prosecution's argument—that the defendant cannot complain about the denial of disclosure because he agreed to the framework—assumes that the agreement itself was valid. But an agreement to forego a trial cannot be valid if it is premised on the denial of evidence necessary to make an informed decision.

The defendant agreed to plead guilty if the motion to suppress was denied. But he did not agree to a proceeding where his ability to challenge the suppression motion was fundamentally compromised. The prosecution cannot rely on an agreement that was itself tainted by the denial of due process.

---

### IV. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument rests on several mischaracterizations that this Court should reject.

**First**, the prosecution argues that the defense repeatedly affirmed the dispositive nature of the motion and never objected. This is true as a formal matter but ignores the substance of the defense's arguments. When defense counsel stated that "at the time when Judge Hill was here, we did not have the new information regarding the CI," she was specifically arguing that new evidence had emerged that warranted disclosure. The defense did not agree to a framework where disclosure would be denied; rather, the defense argued that disclosure was necessary even within that framework. The prosecution cannot convert a procedural agreement into a waiver of substantive rights.

**Second**, the prosecution cites *United States v. Martinez, 555 F.3d 318 (3d Cir. 2009)* and *State v. Johnson, 123 So. 3d 456 (Fla. 2013)* for the proposition that a dispositive motion waives all defenses beyond the suppression issue. Even accepting the holdings of these cases, they do not address the situation presented here. In both *Martinez* and *Johnson*, the defendants sought to litigate defenses that were wholly independent of the suppression issue. Here, by contrast, the defense seeks disclosure precisely because it relates to the suppression issue—the reliability of the informant. The prosecution's reliance on these cases conflates two distinct situations: (1) using a suppression hearing to litigate unrelated defenses, and (2) using a suppression hearing to investigate the very basis for the search.

**Third**, the prosecution argues that the defendant cannot "retroactively demand a trial-style proceeding after losing on the suppression issue." This argument mischaracterizes the defense's position. The defense is not seeking a trial; it is seeking disclosure of the informant's identity to investigate the probable cause basis for the search and to prepare potential defenses. This is not a request for a "trial-style proceeding"; it is a request for basic information necessary to challenge the State's case.

**Fourth**, the prosecution argues that the plea was valid because the defendant agreed to the terms. But the prosecution conflates agreement to a procedural framework with waiver of substantive rights. The defendant agreed to have the motion to suppress decided before sentencing; he did not agree to have that motion decided on a record where his ability to investigate the informant's reliability was foreclosed.

---

### V. CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to hold that a defendant can agree to a "dispositive" motion to suppress and thereby waive his right to investigate the very evidence that supports the search. This Court should decline that invitation.

The informant's identity was material to the probable cause inquiry, to the potential entrapment defense, and to the defendant's constitutional right to present a complete defense. The prosecution's reliance on the parties' agreement cannot override these rights. The agreement to treat the motion as dispositive addressed the sequencing of proceedings, not the substantive rights at stake.

The court of appeals correctly held that the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the informant's identity. This Court should affirm that holding.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon the review of the closing arguments submitted by the Prosecution and the Defense regarding the preliminary ruling, which reversed the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant. The Prosecution argues that the trial court’s ruling should be reinstated based on the parties’ agreement that the motion to suppress was "dispositive," thereby limiting the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search and waiving guilt-related defenses such as entrapment. The Defense contends that the "dispositive" nature of the agreement did not waive the right to challenge the reliability of the informant, which is central to the probable cause inquiry, nor did it extinguish the right to disclosure under *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), where the informant is a material witness. After careful consideration of the arguments and the provided record, the Court affirms the preliminary ruling in part and modifies the analysis.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Motion**

The Court agrees with the Prosecution that the parties entered into a binding agreement to treat the motion to suppress as dispositive. The record reflects that defense counsel explicitly confirmed this understanding, stating, "It was. It is clear... You are clear in your understanding of the Motion to Suppress." As the Prosecution argues, and as supported by precedent such as *United States v. Martinez*, 555 F.3d 318 (3d Cir. 2009), and *State v. Johnson*, 123 So. 3d 456 (Fla. 2013), a dispositive motion to suppress is a narrow procedural tool designed to resolve the legality of the evidence collection, not to serve as a surrogate trial on the merits. Consequently, the Defense was not entitled to litigate the defense of entrapment or other issues of guilt and innocence during this hearing. The trial court did not err in refusing to expand the scope of the hearing to encompass a full trial on the merits.

**B. Disclosure for Probable Cause and Reliability**

However, the Prosecution’s argument overreaches when it asserts that the "dispositive" agreement waived the Defense’s right to challenge the reliability of the informant. The sole issue before the court was probable cause. Under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), the reliability of an informant is a cornerstone of the probable cause analysis. The trial court found the informant reliable based solely on the State’s representation regarding the "effectiveness of her past tips." The Defense proffered specific facts—that the informant was "with [Appellant] that day," her belongings were found in his car, and she may have "set him up"—which directly contradict the State’s assertion of reliability and suggest bias or motive to fabricate.

The Prosecution relies on *United States v. Williams*, 667 F.3d 70 (2d Cir. 2011), for the proposition that a defendant forfeits the right to demand informant disclosure for purposes unrelated to probable cause in a dispositive motion. This authority is inapposite here. The Defense did not seek disclosure merely to pursue an entrapment defense; they sought disclosure to investigate the informant’s background and credibility to rebut the State’s probable cause showing. Because the informant’s alleged participation and potential bias go to the heart of the reliability inquiry, the request for disclosure was directly related to the suppression issue. The Defense could not effectively cross-examine the State’s witnesses regarding the informant’s veracity without knowing the informant’s identity. Therefore, the "dispositive" label did not strip the Appellant of his due process right to meaningfully challenge the evidence supporting the search warrant.

**C. Application of *Roviaro* and Materiality**

The Defense correctly asserts that the informant’s alleged status as a participant triggers the disclosure rule established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957). In *Roviaro*, the Supreme Court held that the government’s privilege to withhold an informant’s identity must yield when the informant’s testimony is material to the defense or when the informant is a participant in the crime charged. The Defense’s proffer that the informant "set him up" and was present at the scene places her within the zone of participation described in *Roviaro*. While the Prosecution argues that *Roviaro* applies only at trial, the principle that a defendant must have access to material evidence to challenge the State’s case applies with equal force to a critical suppression hearing where the outcome determines the defendant’s fate. The trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose prevented the Defense from investigating facts that could have exonerated the Appellant or negated probable cause.

**D. Validity of the Plea**

Finally, the Court must address the validity of the plea. A plea must be entered knowingly and voluntarily. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969). The Appellant agreed to plead guilty if the motion to suppress was denied. However, this agreement was premised on the understanding that the Appellant would receive a fair hearing on the motion. Because the Appellant was denied the opportunity to challenge the reliability of the informant—a critical element of the probable cause determination—the hearing on the motion to suppress was fundamentally unfair. As held in *Mabry v. Johnson*, 467 U.S. 504 (1984), a plea agreement cannot compel a defendant to waive constitutional rights without a full and fair opportunity to litigate the underlying issues. The Appellant cannot be said to have knowingly waived his right to challenge the search when he was deprived of the evidence necessary to do so.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution’s arguments regarding the waiver of entrapment defenses are well-taken, but they do not justify the denial of the motion to disclose. The "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress did not relieve the State of its burden to prove probable cause based on reliable information, nor did it permit the State to hide the identity of an alleged participant in the crime. The trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant’s identity.

Accordingly, the Court affirms the preliminary ruling in its result. The trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. On remand, the trial court shall order the disclosure of the confidential informant’s identity to allow the Defense to investigate the informant’s reliability and potential participation in the alleged offense.
