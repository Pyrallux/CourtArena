*6At an election held in June 1988, the County’s voters approved the tax ordinance by a bare (50.8 percent) majority vote. Prior to trial, the tax went into operation; tax revenues have been collected and accumulated pending final decision.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## IN THE MATTER OF THE CHALLENGE TO THE SAN DIEGO COUNTY REGIONAL JUSTICE FACILITY FINANCING AGENCY SALES TAX

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the Prosecution's arguments and in support of our position that the sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency should be upheld as valid.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the scope of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4—the provision requiring two-thirds voter approval for "special taxes" imposed by "cities, counties and special districts." The Defense does not dispute that section 4 imposes genuine requirements on certain governmental entities. The question before this Court is whether those requirements extend to an entity like the Agency—one created by the Legislature specifically as a limited funding mechanism, devoid of property tax authority, and possessing only a narrow sales tax power.

The Prosecution urges this Court to adopt an expansive interpretation of "special district" that would capture any entity the Legislature labels a "district" and empowers to impose any form of tax. Such an interpretation is contrary to the text of section 4, the intent of Proposition 13, and the limited guidance provided by the California Supreme Court in Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse its preliminary ruling and hold that the Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of section 4, and that the tax imposed is valid.

---

## II. THE AGENCY IS NOT A "SPECIAL DISTRICT" UNDER SECTION 4

### A. The Plain Meaning of Section 4 and the Property Tax Requirement

Section 4 of article XIII A provides that "cities, counties and special districts" may impose "special taxes" only upon approval by two-thirds of the voters. The text contains no definition of "special district," leaving this Court to interpret the term in context. The Defense submits that the term must be understood in light of Proposition 13's primary purpose and the historical understanding of what constitutes a "special district" in California law.

As the California Supreme Court recognized in Amador Valley Joint Union High School Dist. v. State Board of Equalization (1978) 22 Cal.3d 208, Proposition 13 was fundamentally a property tax relief measure. Its core provision, section 1, capped property tax rates at 1 percent of assessed value. The initiative was responsive to concerns about escalating property taxes and the threat of government seizure of family homes. The supermajority requirement in section 4 was designed to complement this property tax framework by preventing local governments from circumventing the property tax caps through alternative property tax mechanisms.

This Court should not interpret section 4 in isolation from its context. The provision must be read in harmony with Proposition 13's overall structure and purpose. To hold that section 4 reaches entities without property tax authority is to extend the provision beyond the problem it was designed to address.

### B. The Richmond Majority's Implied Guidance

The Prosecution places great weight on the Richmond dissent, but the majority opinion in Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 provides more instructive guidance. In Richmond, the California Supreme Court considered whether the Los Angeles County Transportation Commission was a "special district" subject to section 4. The majority concluded that the Commission possessed the power to levy property taxes and therefore fell within section 4's scope. Critically, the majority stated: "We need not decide whether the Commission is a 'special district' within the meaning of section 4 because it has the power to levy property taxes." Richmond, 31 Cal.3d at 208.

This statement implies that property tax authority is relevant—perhaps essential—to the section 4 inquiry. The majority found it unnecessary to decide the "special district" question because the property tax power resolved the case. While the Defense acknowledges that the majority did not explicitly hold that property tax power is a prerequisite, the logical structure of the opinion suggests that entities without such power fall outside section 4's reach.

The Prosecution argues that the Richmond majority's statement was merely dictum and that the dissent provides the proper analytical framework. However, the dissent's warning about a "loophole" was specifically tied to the majority's approach in that case—which involved an entity with property tax power. The dissent did not address whether an entity without any property tax authority would be covered. The Defense submits that the majority's reasoning provides the better interpretation: section 4 applies to entities that possess or could possess property tax authority, not to entities created by the Legislature with only a narrow sales tax power.

### C. The Agency's Limited Structure Distinguishes It from Traditional Special Districts

The Agency was created by the Legislature under Government Code sections 26250-26285 with a single, specific purpose: to impose a supplemental sales tax to finance justice facilities. The act creating the Agency explicitly provided that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax." Gov. Code § 26283. The Agency has no general budgetary discretion; it is bound to spend revenues on the specific purposes enumerated in the statute. It has no independent governance in the traditional sense; its powers are defined and limited by the enabling legislation.

Traditional special districts in California—such as fire protection districts, mosquito abatement districts, and hospital districts—are created to provide specialized governmental services and possess property tax authority to fund those services. These entities have independent boards, general budgetary discretion, and the power to adapt to changing circumstances. The Agency bears none of these characteristics. It is, in essence, a funding mechanism created by the Legislature to accomplish a specific, time-limited objective.

This Court expressed concern in its preliminary ruling that allowing the Agency to escape section 4 would permit the Legislature to create "thousands of funding mechanisms" for every conceivable purpose. This concern overstates the implications of our interpretation. The Defense does not argue that any entity with any taxing authority falls outside section 4. We argue only that an entity created without property tax authority—solely for the purpose of imposing a limited sales tax to fund specific capital projects—should not be deemed a "special district" within the meaning of the Constitution.

If the Legislature created an entity with general taxing authority, including the power to levy property taxes, section 4 would clearly apply. The Agency is different: it was deliberately structured without property tax power because the Legislature recognized that property tax increases would require two-thirds voter approval. The Legislature chose to impose a sales tax instead—a tax that had not been limited by Proposition 13—and sought approval through the democratic process. The voters approved. This is not evasion; it is the exercise of legislative discretion within constitutional bounds.

## III. THE TAX IS NOT A "SPECIAL TAX" REQUIRING SUPERMAJORITY APPROVAL

### A. The Distinction Between Special Taxes and General Taxes

Even if the Agency were considered a "special district," the tax it imposed might not constitute a "special tax" within the meaning of section 4. The Prosecution argues that any tax imposed by a special district for special purposes is a "special tax." This interpretation is too broad.

Section 4 refers to "special taxes" in the context of limiting the ability of local governments to impose taxes beyond the property tax framework. A "special tax" in the Proposition 13 context is properly understood as a tax that supplements or replaces property tax revenue—i.e., a tax that would effectively circumvent the property tax caps. A sales tax imposed on retail transactions is fundamentally different from a property tax. It is imposed on consumption, not on property ownership, and it does not directly implicate the concerns that motivated Proposition 13.

The Defense acknowledges that the term "special tax" has been interpreted broadly in some cases. However, this Court should be cautious before extending section 4 to taxes that have no connection to the property tax system. The sales tax imposed by the Agency is a general excise tax on retail transactions. It applies uniformly to all retail sales within the County, regardless of the nature of the property or the identity of the taxpayer. It is not a special tax in any traditional sense.

### B. The Voters Approved the Tax Through a Valid Election

The Agency's enabling legislation provided for a countywide election on the sales tax ordinance, to be conducted under the provisions of Government Code sections 26271 and 26273. The election was held in June 1988, and the County's voters approved the tax by a majority vote of 50.8 percent. This represents the democratic exercise of the voters' power to approve or reject local taxation.

The Prosecution argues that only a two-thirds majority could validly approve the tax. But if the Agency is not a "special district" or the tax is not a "special tax," then no supermajority requirement applies. The voters were fully informed about the tax and its purpose when they approved it. Their decision should be respected.

---

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S POLICY ARGUMENTS DO NOT SUPPORT INVALIDATION

### A. The "Loophole" Argument Is Overstated

The Prosecution and the Court's preliminary ruling emphasize the danger of a "loophole" that would allow the Legislature to evade section 4 by creating entities without property tax authority. This concern, while legitimate in the abstract, does not justify the broad interpretation adopted by the Court.

The Defense does not dispute that the Richmond dissent correctly identified a potential for evasion. However, the solution to that potential is not to interpret section 4 so broadly that it captures entities the Constitution was never intended to cover. The Legislature retains the power to create entities with general taxing authority, and those entities would be subject to section 4. The Agency is different: it was created with limited powers and a specific purpose. There is no evidence that the Legislature sought to evade section 4; rather, it exercised its discretion to structure the Agency in a manner consistent with the constitutional framework as the Defense understands it.

Moreover, the Prosecution's "loophole" argument proves too much. If accepted, it would mean that any entity created by the Legislature with any taxing authority—even a narrow sales tax—would be subject to section 4, regardless of its structure or purpose. This would render the Legislature's power to create special districts meaningless, as every such entity would face the supermajority requirement. Such an interpretation is inconsistent with the principle of legislative discretion in matters of local government.

### B. The Interpretation Creates Uncertainty

The Defense is concerned that the Court's interpretation will create uncertainty about the status of numerous entities created by the Legislature for various purposes. If the Agency is a "special district" despite its lack of property tax authority, what other entities might be similarly classified? The Court has suggested that the distinction is simply whether the Legislature labels an entity a "district" or "agency." But this formalistic approach ignores the substantive differences between entities like the Agency and traditional special districts.

The Defense submits that the proper approach is to consider whether the entity possesses property tax authority or the functional equivalent. If it does, section 4 applies. If it does not—as in the case of the Agency—section 4 does not apply. This approach provides clarity and predictability, while the Court's interpretation creates confusion.

---

## V. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse its preliminary ruling and hold that:

1. The San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4, because it lacks property tax authority and is a limited funding mechanism created for a specific purpose;

2. The sales tax imposed by the Agency is not a "special tax" requiring two-thirds voter approval, as it is a general excise tax on retail transactions unrelated to the property tax system that Proposition 13 was designed to limit;

3. The tax is valid because it was approved by a majority of the County's voters at a properly conducted election; and

4. The judgment of the Court of Appeal reversing the trial court should be affirmed.

The Defense acknowledges the significant interests at stake in this case. Proposition 13 represents a fundamental shift in California taxation, and its provisions must be interpreted carefully to preserve their intent. But the intent of Proposition 13 was to limit property taxes, not to impose impossible hurdles on all forms of local taxation. The Agency was created to address a genuine public need—improved courtrooms and jails—and the voters approved the tax to fund those facilities. To invalidate the tax now, based on an interpretation of section 4 that extends far beyond its text and purpose, would be to frustrate the democratic process and penalize the voters for exercising their constitutional rights.

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court rule in our favor and uphold the validity of the sales tax.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. Upon careful consideration of the statutory framework, the constitutional provisions at issue, and the precedent provided, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. The sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency) is invalid. The Agency constitutes a "special district" under California Constitution article XIII A, section 4, and the tax imposed is a "special tax" requiring approval by a two-thirds vote of the electorate.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Agency is a "Special District" Under Section 4**

The threshold question is whether the Agency falls within the constitutional definition of a "special district." The Defense contends that the Agency is merely a "funding mechanism" rather than a special district because it lacks property tax power and possesses only a narrow sales tax authority. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive as a matter of constitutional text and statutory structure.

Section 4 of article XIII A provides that "cities, counties and special districts" may impose "special taxes" only with a two-thirds vote. Cal. Const., art. XIII A, § 4. The text does not define "special district," nor does it qualify the term by requiring the entity to possess property tax authority. The Agency was created by the Legislature as a distinct political subdivision under Government Code sections 26250–26285, specifically for the specialized purpose of financing justice facilities. It is a district created for a special purpose. By definition and function, it is a "special district."

The Defense's attempt to characterize the Agency as a "funding mechanism" ignores its status as a separate legal entity endowed with the power to levy a sales tax. The fact that the Legislature chose to limit the Agency's powers to a specific sales tax does not strip it of its status as a special district; rather, it confirms the Agency's specialized nature. To hold otherwise would allow the Legislature to bypass constitutional restrictions simply by labeling a taxing entity a "funding mechanism" or "agency" rather than a "district."

**II. The "Loophole" Foreclosed by *Richmond* and the Dissent**

The Defense relies heavily on the majority opinion in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, arguing that because the majority found it unnecessary to decide if the commission was a "special district" due to its property tax power, this Court should infer that property tax power is a prerequisite for section 4 coverage. The Court declines to adopt this inference.

The majority in *Richmond* explicitly stated, "We need not decide whether the Commission is a 'special district' within the meaning of section 4 because it has the power to levy property taxes." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208. This statement leaves the question open. It does not establish a negative implication that entities *without* property tax power are excluded.

The Prosecution correctly directs the Court to the dissenting opinion in *Richmond*, which identified the precise danger presented by the Defense's interpretation. Justice Richardson warned:

"If the majority's view is correct, any entity could avoid the supermajority requirement by simply choosing to impose a sales tax rather than a property tax... This would create a significant loophole in Proposition 13." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 214 (Richardson, J., dissenting).

The Agency is the embodiment of this loophole. It was created specifically to impose a sales tax to finance capital projects traditionally within the county's purview, but structured without property tax power to avoid the two-thirds vote requirement. The Court cannot endorse an interpretation of the Constitution that permits such formalistic evasion of the voters' intent. The Constitution cannot be subverted by legislative maneuvering that creates a special district for the sole purpose of avoiding the supermajority requirement.

**III. The Scope of Proposition 13 and Section 4**

The Defense contends that Proposition 13 is primarily concerned with property tax relief, citing *Amador Valley Joint Union High School Dist. v. State Board of Equalization* (1978) 22 Cal.3d 208. While the Court acknowledges that the primary aim of Proposition 13 was to limit property taxes, this does not render section 4's specific language regarding "special taxes" moot.

Section 4 is a distinct provision within article XIII A. It explicitly governs the imposition of "special taxes" by "special districts." The term "special taxes" is not qualified by the text to include only property taxes. The voters who approved Proposition 13 understood that local governments might seek to replace lost property tax revenue with other forms of taxation. To prevent this circumvention, the voters imposed a supermajority requirement for "special taxes." The Defense's argument that section 4 applies only to property taxes ignores the plain language of the provision and undermines the structural integrity of the initiative.

Furthermore, the Defense's argument that the sales tax is a "general excise tax" and not a "special tax" is unavailing. The tax is levied for a specific purpose—the financing of justice facilities. It is not a tax raised for the general operations of government. Therefore, it fits the definition of a "special tax" for which the voters demanded a higher threshold of approval.

**IV. The Agency's Structure Does Not Exempt It from Constitutional Requirements**

The Defense argues that applying section 4 to the Agency would infringe on legislative discretion and create uncertainty. The Court disagrees. The Legislature has broad discretion to create special districts and authorize taxes, but that discretion is bounded by the Constitution.

The Legislature cannot create a special district, empower it to levy taxes, and then declare it exempt from the constitutional requirements applicable to all special districts. The Agency's lack of property tax power is a result of legislative choice, not a constitutional exemption. If the Legislature wishes to impose a sales tax for justice facilities without a two-thirds vote, it must do so through an entity that is not a "special district" or via a tax that is not a "special tax." Creating a special district with the sole power to levy a special tax and then claiming exemption from section 4 is inconsistent with the rule of law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Agency is a "special district" subject to the supermajority voting requirements of California Constitution article XIII A, section 4. The sales tax ordinance was approved by a simple majority, which is insufficient under the Constitution. Therefore, the tax is invalid. The judgment of the trial court in favor of the Plaintiffs is reinstated.
