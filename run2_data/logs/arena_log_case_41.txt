Discussion

[14] The issue is whether the trial court erred or abused its discretion in permitting Casey's statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. Generally, we review the trial court's ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. Roche v. State , 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We reverse only when the decision is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Joyner v. State , 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997), reh'g denied . We may affirm a trial court's decision regarding the admission of evidence if it is sustainable on any basis in the record. Barker v. State , 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998), reh'g denied . A trial court "has the duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted" before it, Garcia v. State , 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), and "is given wide latitude of discretion in carrying out [its] duties." Pitman v. State , 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

[15] Casey cites Ind. Evidence Rule 403 and asserts that the unsolicited presentation of the duplicitous and cumulative evidence deprived him of his right to a fair trial. He argues that Ind. Code § 34-1-21-6 outlines strict circumstances when a jury may be entitled to receive duplicitous or cumulative evidence and none of the circumstances were present in his case.

[16] The State argues the trial court properly exercised its discretion by replaying Casey's statement to police in response to jury questions. It asserts that the trial court reasonably exercised its discretion to replay the evidence based on the multiple jury questions asking about the content of the already played video and other concerns regarding audio problems and redaction. The State also contends that Casey has not shown substantial prejudice.

[17] Initially, we note that Casey did not specifically mention Ind. Evidence Rule 403 in his objection to the replaying of the video at trial. Ind. Evidence Rule 403 provides: "The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." To the extent the objection by Casey's counsel that the replaying was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over," falls under Rule 403, we will address the issue.3 Transcript Volume II at 45.

[18] The trial court replayed Casey's recorded statement due to multiple questions from the jury and a juror's inability to hear. We have previously held that, "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." Lewis v. State , 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), trans. denied . We cannot say that Casey has demonstrated that replaying his statement to police would not aid the jury.  Under these circumstances, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion. See Chambers v. State , 422 N.E.2d 1198, 1204 (Ind. 1981) (holding that, "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it); see also Harris v. State , 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995) (holding that the trial court did not abuse its discretion by sending exhibits and a tape player to the jury room when the jury began its deliberations where the audio taped confession served to aid the jury in its attempt to understand to what extent defendant participated in the charged crimes and how the crimes were committed, and there was little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court).

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Issue

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court abused its discretion when it replayed Casey's entire recorded statement to the jury a second time, over defense counsel's explicit objection. While the prosecution characterizes this as a reasonable response to juror confusion, the record demonstrates that the replay was unnecessary, cumulative, and designed to emphasize already-admitted evidence in a manner that prejudiced the defendant. The prosecution's reliance on *Lewis v. State*, *Chambers v. State*, and *Harris v. State* is misplaced, as those cases are distinguishable and do not support the blanket proposition that a trial court may replay entire witness statements whenever a juror claims difficulty hearing. This Court should reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

## Factual Background Emphasizing the Defense Position

The facts establish that the replay was not a measured response to genuine juror need, but rather an unnecessary amplification of evidence already before the jury. On May 17, 2017, Detective Leitze executed a search warrant at 48 North Euclid and found a 16-gauge shotgun manufactured by Stevens under a bed, along with ammunition and spent cartridges. Transcript Volume II at 17. Casey was present at the residence and was transported to the City County Building for an interview, where he made a recorded statement after receiving Miranda warnings.

The recorded statement was played for the jury during Detective Leitze's testimony. Transcript Volume II at 33. During examination, Detective Leitze testified that "Mr. Casey claimed ownership of this shotgun found at 48 North Euclid." Id. The prosecutor then asked: "And he keeps referencing a Stevens, is that the same shotgun we saw in court today?" Id. Detective Leitze answered: "That is the shotgun right in front of me." Id. The statement was admitted as State's Exhibit 20 over defense objection. Id.

After the State rested, Casey testified in his own defense, explaining that he lived at 8523 Lena Court, that Hamilton was his girlfriend, that the shotgun belonged to Hamilton, and that he did not fire the shotgun. Id. at 56. He testified that he "was scared and confused" when he made the statement to police "and didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Id. He also stated he had just smoked "Katie" which is "blunts ... spice." Id. at 56-57.

It was only after both parties had completed their examination of Detective Leitze—and after the State had rested—that the court received juror questions. Id. at 41. One question asked about "what was the question before the confrontation about finding the gun?" Id. Another asked "what time did you get the tip" and "how long does it take to get a warrant." Id. Most significantly, one juror claimed she "missed [Casey's] answer regarding whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid" and "clearly couldn't hear." Id. at 44.

Defense counsel specifically objected to replaying the video, arguing it was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Id. at 45. The court overruled this objection and replayed the **entire** video for the jury. Id.

## Legal Analysis and Rebuttal to Prosecution's Arguments

### I. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion by Replaying the Entire Statement

The prosecution attempts to frame this case as a straightforward application of judicial discretion to ensure jurors can hear testimony. This characterization ignores critical distinctions in the record. The trial court did not replay a brief portion of testimony that a juror missed; it replayed the **entire** recorded statement after both parties had completed their examination of Detective Leitze and after the State had rested its case.

Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence "may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." The defense explicitly raised this concern at trial, arguing the replay was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over." Transcript Volume II at 45.

The prosecution argues that *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), supports the replay. However, *Lewis* is readily distinguishable. In *Lewis*, the court addressed a situation where "the trial court did not allow the jury to hear portions of the testimony that were inaudible." 726 N.E.2d at 845. The instant case presents the opposite scenario: the entire statement had already been played once, and the court chose to replay it in full rather than addressing the specific portion a single juror claimed to have missed.

The prosecution also relies on *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981). In *Chambers*, the Indiana Supreme Court held that replaying a tape was permissible where "at most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence." 422 N.E.2d at 1204. However, the Court emphasized that the replay was justified because "the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it." Id. (emphasis added). Here, the court did not limit the replay to ensure all jurors could hear a previously inaudible portion; rather, it replayed the entire statement after the jury had already heard it in full.

Similarly, *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522 (Ind. 1995), is distinguishable. In *Harris*, the trial court allowed the jury to review audio tapes during deliberations "when the jury began its deliberations." 659 N.E.2d at 526-27. The Court noted there was "little risk that the jury would misuse or give undue weight to these exhibits because the audio tapes had already been played in open court." Id. at 527. Critically, the *Harris* Court was addressing the jury's request for evidence during deliberations—a context fundamentally different from the instant case, where the replay occurred during the presentation of evidence, before the State had even rested.

### II. The Prosecution's Characterization of the Juror Questions Is Exaggerated

The prosecution contends that "multiple jury questions" justified the replay. Transcript Volume II at 41. A closer examination of the record reveals that most questions concerned peripheral matters—timing of the tip, how long it takes to get a warrant—rather than the content of Casey's statement. Only one question directly related to the statement: a juror's claim that she "missed [Casey's] answer regarding whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid." Id. at 44.

The appropriate remedy for a juror's inability to hear a specific answer is to play that specific portion, not to replay the entire statement. The court's decision to replay the entire video was neither necessary nor proportional to the stated problem. This is precisely the type of unnecessary repetition that Indiana Evidence Rule 403 was designed to prevent.

### III. Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 Does Not Authorize the Replay

The prosecution argues that Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 is "entirely inapplicable" because it "governs jury requests for evidence during deliberations, not during trial testimony." This argument, however, cuts against the prosecution's position. If the statute governs requests during deliberations, and the replay occurred during trial testimony, then the replay was entirely outside any statutory framework authorizing jury review of evidence. The trial court had no written rule or statute authorizing it to replay evidence mid-trial simply because a juror claimed difficulty hearing. The court's inherent authority to manage proceedings does not extend to repeating evidence already admitted, over objection, in the absence of any demonstrated necessity.

### IV. The Replay Caused Substantial Prejudice

The prosecution claims Casey has not shown substantial prejudice. This ignores the obvious: the entire purpose of replaying Casey's statement was to emphasize his admission of ownership of the shotgun. Detective Leitze had already testified that "Mr. Casey claimed ownership of this shotgun found at 48 North Euclid." Transcript Volume II at 33. The prosecution then asked: "And he keeps referencing a Stevens, is that the same shotgun we saw in court today?" Id. The message was clear.

By replaying the statement a second time—after the defense had presented its case and after Casey had explained he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail"—the court underscored the prosecution's narrative. The replay was not neutral; it reinforced the State's theory that Casey owned and possessed the firearm. This is precisely the "undue prejudice" that Indiana Evidence Rule 403 requires courts to guard against.

Casey testified that he told police the shotgun was his because he was "scared and confused" and "didn't want [Hamilton] to go to jail." Transcript Volume II at 56. He further explained he had recently smoked "Katie" (spice), which affected his judgment. Id. at 56-57. Replaying his statement after this testimony effectively undermined his credibility defense by allowing the jury to hear, again, his incriminating statements while being unable to consider his explanation for why he made them in the same proceeding.

## Conclusion

The trial court abused its discretion by replaying Casey's recorded statement in its entirety. The replay was cumulative, unnecessary, and designed to emphasize evidence already before the jury. The prosecution has not demonstrated that the replay was justified by any legitimate need, and the cases it relies upon are readily distinguishable. The cumulative effect of playing the statement twice, combined with the timing of the replay (after the defense had presented its case), created substantial prejudice that violated Casey's right to a fair trial.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in permitting Casey’s statement to police to be played for the jury a second time. We review a trial court’s ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. *Roche v. State*, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). We will reverse the trial court’s decision only if it is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. *Joyner v. State*, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). A trial court is given wide latitude in carrying out its duty to manage and control the proceedings conducted before it. *Garcia v. State*, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988); *Pitman v. State*, 436 N.E.2d 74, 78 (Ind. 1982).

**II. Analysis**

Casey argues that the trial court abused its discretion by replaying his entire recorded statement, asserting that the evidence was duplicitous, cumulative, and prejudicial under Indiana Evidence Rule 403. Specifically, the defense contends that because only one juror indicated she could not hear a specific answer regarding "whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid," the court should have replayed only that specific portion rather than the entire video. The defense further argues that the replay was prejudicial because it occurred after the State had rested and after Casey had offered an explanation for his statement, thereby undermining his credibility defense.

The State counters that the replay was a proper exercise of the trial court’s discretion, necessitated by the jury’s need to hear and understand the testimony. The State points to the record, which shows that jurors submitted multiple questions regarding the content of the video and that one juror explicitly stated she could not hear portions of the testimony.

We agree with the State. It is well established that "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). The trial court was presented with a juror who was unable to hear critical testimony, as well as other juror questions suggesting confusion regarding the content of the statement. In these circumstances, the trial court acted within its wide latitude to manage the proceedings by ensuring the jury had access to the evidence in an audible and comprehensible form.

While the defense argues that *Lewis* and *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), are distinguishable because the court replayed the entire statement rather than a limited portion, we find this distinction does not rise to the level of an abuse of discretion. In *Chambers*, the court held that "[a]t most, the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" and that the trial court did not err in allowing the tape to be replayed so all of the jurors could hear it. *Chambers*, 422 N.E.2d at 1204. The trial court here was faced with the practical task of addressing a juror's hearing deficit and general confusion. Replaying the entire video was a reasonable method to ensure the juror understood the context of the missed answer, particularly where the questions related to the sequence of the interrogation and the identification of the firearm.

Furthermore, the defense’s reliance on *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522 (Ind. 1995), is misplaced insofar as it attempts to limit the replay of evidence to the deliberative phase. While *Harris* addressed the submission of exhibits to the jury room during deliberations, its underlying principle—that replaying evidence already admitted aids the jury in understanding the extent of the defendant's participation without substantial risk of undue weight—remains persuasive. *Harris*, 659 N.E.2d at 526-527. The probative value of ensuring the jury could hear and comprehend Casey’s statement substantially outweighs any danger of unfair prejudice or needless presentation of cumulative evidence under Indiana Evidence Rule 403. The defense had the opportunity to cross-examine Detective Leitze and present Casey's explanation that he was "scared and confused" and under the influence of "spice" when he made the statement. This context mitigates any claim that the second playing of the video unfairly prejudiced the jury by giving undue weight to the confession.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in replaying Casey’s statement. The action was taken to address specific juror hearing issues and confusion, consistent with the court’s duty to manage the trial proceedings. Casey has not demonstrated that the trial court’s decision was clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. Accordingly, we find no reversible error.

**SO ORDERED.**

## I. The Replay Exceeded What Was Necessary to Address the Juror's Hearing Difficulty

The prosecution argues that the replay was justified by "multiple juror questions" and "a specific juror's declaration that she couldn't hear." ## II. The Prosecution Misapplies *Chambers* and *Harris*

The prosecution claims *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), is "directly on point" and supports replaying the entire statement. This reading ignores critical factual distinctions.

In *Chambers*, the Indiana Supreme Court noted that "the repeated playing of the tape was cumulative evidence" but found no error because it occurred during the State's case-in-chief and served to ensure all jurors could hear. *Chambers*, 422 N.E.2d at 1204. Here, the replay occurred under fundamentally different circumstances: the State had already rested its case, and Casey had already testified. Transcript Volume II at 56-60. Similarly, the prosecution's reliance on *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522 (Ind. 1995), is misplaced. In *Harris*, the court addressed whether exhibits could be sent to the jury room during deliberations—specifically, audio tapes that had already been played during trial. *Harris*, 659 N.E.2d at 526-527. The critical distinction is that *Harris* involved evidence submitted during deliberations, not during trial testimony. The inherent risk of undue emphasis increases when evidence is replayed after the defense has presented its case, because the jury has just heard the defendant's explanation and then is forced to re-hear the prosecution's version without the benefit of cross-examination or further defense presentation.

## III. Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 Is Applicable and Was Violated

The prosecution dismisses Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 as "irrelevant" because it "governs jury requests for evidence during deliberations." This argument contradicts the statute's plain language and the defense's argument.

Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 provides that a jury may request "any exhibit" or "any part of the testimony" and the court shall grant such request "if the exhibit or testimony is material to a matter at issue." The statute contemplates that when evidence is requested, the court should provide only that which is necessary—the specific testimony or exhibit requested—not an entire category of evidence.

Here, the juror's specific request was for the answer regarding "whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid." Transcript Volume II at 44. Under Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6, the court should have provided only that specific answer, not the entire recorded statement. The prosecution's argument that the statute applies only to deliberations ignores the statute's purpose: preventing the presentation of cumulative evidence by ensuring the jury receives only what it requests.

## IV. The Replay Prejudiced Casey's Credibility Defense

The prosecution argues there was "no prejudice" because "replaying the statement did not introduce new evidence." This analysis is flawed because it ignores the timing and cumulative effect of the replay.

The replay of the entire statement—before the jury had begun deliberations—forced the jury to re-hear Casey's incriminating statement while the prosecution's explanation was fresh in their minds, but without any corresponding reinforcement of Casey's explanation. This created an asymmetric presentation that substantially prejudiced Casey's credibility defense.

In *Barker v. State*, 695 N.E.2d 925, 930 (Ind. 1998), this Court held that a trial court's ruling may be sustained on any basis in the record. However, *Barker* does not stand for the proposition that prejudice should be ignored when it exists. The prosecution has the burden to demonstrate the absence of substantial prejudice. Here, the prosecution has not met that burden.

## V. The Cumulative Nature of the Evidence Violates Rule 403

The prosecution argues that Rule 403's threshold—"substantially outweighed"—was not met because the evidence had probative value. This argument misreads the rule.

Indiana Evidence Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence "may be excluded" if its "probative value is substantially outweighed by" cumulative nature, among other factors. The question is not whether the evidence has probative value—it clearly does—but whether playing it twice substantially outweighs that value.

Here, the statement had already been played once during the State's case-in-chief. Transcript Volume II at 33. The jury had already heard Casey's admission that the shotgun was his. Under these circumstances, the cumulative nature substantially outweighed any marginal probative value.

*Chambers* itself acknowledged that the replay was "cumulative evidence." *Chambers*, 422 N.E.2d at 1204. The difference is that in *Chambers*, the court replayed the tape during the State's case-in-chief to ensure all jurors could hear. Here, the replay occurred after the defense presented its case, making the cumulative effect substantially more prejudicial.

**I. Standard of Review**

We review a trial court’s ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. *Roche v. State*, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). A decision constitutes an abuse of discretion only if it is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. *Joyner v. State*, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). While trial courts are given wide latitude in managing proceedings, *Garcia v. State*, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), this discretion is not unlimited and must be exercised within the bounds of the rules of evidence.

**II. Analysis**

We agree with the defense that the trial court’s decision to replay the entire video, rather than the specific portion missed by the juror, constituted an abuse of discretion in this specific context.

Initially, we acknowledge the principle that "[i]n order for the jurors to properly perform their duty, it is essential that they be able to hear the testimony." *Lewis*, 726 N.E.2d at 845. However, the duty to ensure audibility does not grant a trial court unfettered license to replay evidence in its entirety when a more targeted remedy is available. The record indicates that only one juror experienced a hearing deficit, specifically missing Casey's answer regarding "whose bedroom that was at 48 North Euclid." Transcript Volume II at 44. The trial court explicitly noted that the juror's question "clearly could be answered by watching the video" and discussed the option of "playing part of it, the part that she missed." Id. at 44.

Under Indiana Evidence Rule 403, relevant evidence "may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of... needlessly presenting cumulative evidence." The probative value of replaying the *entire* statement was minimal, as the jury had already heard the recording in its entirety during the State's case-in-chief. The specific need—clarifying a single answer regarding the bedroom—could have been satisfied by replaying only that specific segment or by having Detective Leitze provide the answer from the witness stand. By choosing to replay the entire video, the court presented evidence that was cumulative and served to "hammer" the confession, a concern raised by defense counsel at trial.

The risk of undue emphasis is significantly higher in this context, as the jury was deprived of the immediate contextual balance provided by the defense's testimony.

The State's reliance on Indiana Code § 34-1-21-6 to argue that the statute is inapplicable because it governs deliberations does not alleviate the concern. The court had the authority and the means to address the specific juror's question without inundating the jury with the entire statement again.

The trial court abused its discretion by replaying the entirety of Casey's statement when a more limited replay would have cured the specific hearing deficit. The cumulative nature of the evidence, combined with the prejudicial timing of the replay immediately following the defense's case, requires reversal. **FINAL RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

We review a trial court’s ruling on the admission or exclusion of evidence for an abuse of discretion. *Roche v. State*, 690 N.E.2d 1115, 1134 (Ind. 1997). A decision constitutes an abuse of discretion only if it is clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances. *Joyner v. State*, 678 N.E.2d 386, 390 (Ind. 1997). While trial courts are given wide latitude in managing proceedings, *Garcia v. State*, 517 N.E.2d 402, 405 (Ind. 1988), this discretion is not unlimited and must be exercised within the bounds of the rules of evidence.

**II. Analysis**

The State argues that the trial court acted within its discretion by replaying Casey’s entire recorded statement in response to multiple juror questions and a specific hearing deficit. The State contends that the replay was necessary to ensure juror comprehension and that the trial court’s decision was a reasonable exercise of its authority to manage the proceedings. We have considered these arguments but find them unpersuasive in light of the specific facts and the prejudicial timing of the replay.

**A. The Scope of Juror Questions Did Not Justify a Full Replay**

The State emphasizes that the jury submitted multiple questions (Questions 1 through 5), implying that a full replay was necessary to address all of them. However, a review of the record reveals that the video of Casey’s statement could not have answered all of these inquiries. The jury’s questions included "what time did you get the tip" (Question 2), "how long does it take to get a warrant" (Question 3), and "how did detective know [Casey] was unlawfully in the possession" (Question 4). Transcript Volume II at 41. These questions pertain to the detective’s investigation and knowledge, not the content of Casey’s recorded interview. Consequently, replaying the entire video would not have provided answers to Questions 2, 3, or 4.

The only questions relevant to the video were Question 1 ("what was the question before the confrontation about finding the gun") and Question 5 (regarding the juror who missed the answer about "whose bedroom that was"). Transcript Volume II at 41, 44. The trial court’s specific deliberation focused on Question 5, noting the juror’s inability to hear and the availability of playing "part of it, the part that she missed." Id. at 44. While the court has a duty to ensure jurors can hear testimony, *Lewis v. State*, 726 N.E.2d 836, 845 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000), that duty did not require replaying the entire statement when the specific deficit was isolated and the other questions were unrelated to the video’s content.

**B. The Timing of the Replay Constituted an Abuse of Discretion**

The State relies on *Chambers v. State*, 422 N.E.2d 1198 (Ind. 1981), to argue that replaying cumulative evidence to ensure audibility is permissible. In *Chambers*, the replay occurred during the State's case-in-chief. Here, however, the replay occurred after the State had rested and after Casey had testified. This distinction is fatal to the State’s argument.

Casey testified that he claimed ownership of the shotgun because he was "scared and confused" and had smoked "spice." Transcript Volume II at 56-57. This testimony was the cornerstone of his defense, aimed at explaining away the damaging confession. By replaying the entire confession immediately after Casey presented this explanation, the trial court allowed the State to reinforce its most damaging evidence without giving the defense an opportunity to counter the renewed impression. This created an asymmetric presentation that prejudiced Casey’s defense.

The State argues that the court’s admonition to wait until closing arguments cured any prejudice. We disagree. While admonitions are helpful, they cannot erase the immediate psychological impact of hearing a confident confession immediately after hearing the defendant's explanation that he was impaired and fearful. The defense was deprived of the chance to rebut the "fresh" impression of the confession, other than through closing arguments, which are not evidence. The timing of the replay effectively allowed the State to rebut the defense's case before the defense had rested its evidentiary presentation.

**C. Rule 403 and the Needlessly Cumulative Nature of the Evidence**

Under Indiana Evidence Rule 403, relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of needlessly presenting cumulative evidence. The probative value of replaying the *entire* statement was minimal, as the jury had already heard it in full. The specific need—clarifying a single answer regarding the bedroom—could have been satisfied by replaying only that segment. By choosing to replay the entire video, the court presented evidence that was needlessly cumulative. The defense counsel’s objection that the replay was "cumulative to just keep hammering on it over, and over, and over" was well-founded. Transcript Volume II at 45.

The State’s reliance on *Harris v. State*, 659 N.E.2d 522, 526-527 (Ind. 1995), is misplaced. *Harris* involved sending exhibits to the jury room during deliberations, where the risk of undue weight is low because the jury has already heard all the evidence and arguments. Here, the replay occurred during the evidentiary phase, before closing arguments, where the risk of undue emphasis is significantly higher.

**III. Conclusion**

The trial court abused its discretion by replaying the entirety of Casey's statement when a more limited replay would have cured the specific hearing deficit. The cumulative nature of the evidence, combined with the prejudicial timing of the replay immediately following the defense's case, requires reversal. The State's arguments regarding the scope of juror questions and the applicability of *Chambers* do not overcome the fact that the court's decision was clearly against the logic and effect of the facts and circumstances.
