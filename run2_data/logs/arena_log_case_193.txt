## ARGUMENT

### I. The Trial Court Correctly Granted the Exception of No Cause of Action

A cause of action requires a legal right enforceable in court. *See Louisiana Code of Civil Procedure Article 931*. Here, J.M.Y.'s petition fails to state a cause of action because Louisiana law does not recognize a claim for reimbursement of child support payments by a presumed father who has not disavowed his paternity.

Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 provides that a child born or conceived during marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband. This presumption is fundamental to the stability of family relations and the protection of children. As the Louisiana Supreme Court held in *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 444 So. 2d 722 (La. 1984), the presumption of paternity is conclusive unless and until the husband successfully disavows it.

The defense acknowledges that *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux* addressed the question of whether the presumption could be contested, but the principle underlying that decision extends to the precise issue before this Court: the presumption creates a legal father whose obligations are absolute. A legal father who seeks to shift his support obligations to another must first remove the legal impediment created by the presumption. This requires a disavowal proceeding, not a collateral action for reimbursement.

The First Circuit Court of Appeal explicitly addressed this issue in *Smith v. Smith*, 589 So. 2d 537 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), holding that a presumed father who has not disavowed paternity has no right to recover child support payments from a biological father. The court reasoned that such a claim would undermine the integrity of the family unit and the child's right to support from the legal father. This Court should follow that sound reasoning.

J.M.Y.'s petition is notably lacking in any allegation that he has initiated or intends to initiate disavowal proceedings. He seeks to retain his legal status as father while simultaneously shifting his financial burden to another. This the law does not permit. The presumption of paternity exists precisely to ensure that a child has an identifiable legal father responsible for support. To allow the legal father to sue for reimbursement without first disavowing would create an untenable situation in which the child's support is contingent on the outcome of a reimbursement action rather than on the clear legal obligation of the presumed father.

Furthermore, J.M.Y.'s claim against A.F.Y. for reimbursement fails for similar reasons. A mother who receives child support from a legal father pursuant to a court order has no obligation to reimburse that father for payments he was legally obligated to make. **FINAL RULING**

This Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the Appellees, R.R. and A.F.Y. Upon consideration of the record, the applicable law, and the arguments presented, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

The dispositive issue in this appeal is whether a presumed father, who has not initiated disavowal proceedings, maintains a right of action to recover child support payments from a third party. As argued by the Appellees and conceded by the Appellant, the answer is unequivocally no.

The Appellees correctly identify that the Appellant, J.M.Y., has effectively conceded the merits of this appeal. In his filings, J.M.Y. acknowledged that the trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. He admitted that Louisiana law does not permit a presumed father to shift financial obligations to a third party without first disavowing paternity through proper judicial proceedings. This admission is fatal to his appeal, as a party cannot simultaneously argue that the trial court erred while conceding that the court correctly applied the law to the facts.

Even in the absence of this concession, the Appellees' arguments grounded in Louisiana jurisprudence compel the same result. Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 establishes a presumption of paternity for a child born during the marriage. This presumption is "conclusive" and creates an "absolute obligation of support" that cannot be circumvented through collateral litigation. *Boudreaux v. Boudreaux*, 444 So. 2d 722 (La. 1984). The Supreme Court of Louisiana in *Boudreaux* emphasized that this presumption serves the paramount public interest of ensuring certainty in legal parentage and the child’s right to support from a legally recognized parent.

The specific application of this principle to reimbursement claims is controlled by the precedent of this Court. In *Smith v. Smith*, 589 So. 2d 537 (La. App. 1st Cir. 1991), the First Circuit held that a presumed father who has not successfully challenged paternity through disavowal proceedings "has no right of action to recover payments from a third party." The court reasoned that permitting such claims would "undermine the integrity of the family unit" and expose children to "uncertainty and instability" by making their support contingent on the outcome of speculative litigation.

The undisputed facts reveal that J.M.Y. has never initiated disavowal proceedings. He retained the legal status of father and the associated obligation of support throughout the period for which he seeks reimbursement. His attempt to seek reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. without first disavowing paternity is precisely the type of collateral attack prohibited by *Boudreaux* and *Smith*. As argued by the Appellees, allowing such a claim would create perverse incentives, enabling presumed fathers to evade their obligations without following the legislatively mandated process designed to protect the welfare of the child.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action is affirmed. The suit filed by J.M.Y. against R.R. and A.F.Y. remains dismissed.
