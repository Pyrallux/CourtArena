FACTS:
Martin J. Bradley, III appeals a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. We reverse the temporary injunction and remand for consideration of former employee Bradley’s defenses to the employer’s claims.
I.
In March 1993, employee Bradley entered into an employment agreement with employer Health Coalition, Inc. Bradley was hired to be a salesperson for the employer’s blood plasma products. The employment contract contained a covenant that the employee would not compete for a period of two years after the termination of employment. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of “any active, paid up customers of the corporation,” protects the confidentiality of corporate information, and contains various provisions related to enforcement.

In April 1994, Bradley’s employment ended. The employer claims that Bradley voluntarily resigned. Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after he refused the employer’s instructions to engage in improper business practices.

In February 1996, the employer filed an action asserting that Bradley had gone to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products. The trial court entered a temporary injunction enforcing the noncompetition agreement. From an order modifying and extending1 the temporary injunction, Bradley has appealed.



The noncompetition agreement contains a liquidated damages clause.

The employee contended that he was forced to resign after he refused to resell certain plasma products that had been returned by a customer. The employee believed the product had been rendered unsafe for medical use because of its handling during shipping. The employer took the position that the product was safe for consumption and ordered the employee to resell it.

The employee also contends that he was ordered to alter certain invoices to charge higher prices than the customers had agreed to. The employee says this constituted a fraud on the customers and he refused to obey the instruction. The employer denies that any such instruction was given.

# DEFENSE RESPONSE BRIEF

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF FLORIDA

---

**Your Honors**, the defense respectfully submits this response in opposition to the appellant Bradley's arguments and in support of the trial court's order entering a temporary injunction enforcing the covenant not to compete. The appellant has failed to demonstrate reversible error, and this Court should affirm the temporary injunction.

---

### I. THE EXISTENCE OF A LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DOES NOT BAR INJUNCTIVE RELIEF WHERE THE EMPLOYER SEEKS TO PROTECT LEGITIMATE BUSINESS INTERESTS

The appellant contends that the liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement negates any showing of irreparable harm, relying heavily on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Abboud*, 132 So. 3d 1297 (Fla. 2014). However, the appellant misreads the holding in that case and ignores critical distinctions in Florida law regarding the availability of injunctive relief.

In *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Abboud*, the Florida Supreme Court recognized that "the existence of a liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because it provides a remedy at law for damages." 132 So. 3d at 1309. Yet the Court specifically noted that this general rule applies "unless the contract provision is invalid or the harm is of a type for which the law regards the injury as irreparable." *Id.* at 1309-1310.

Here, the harm the employer seeks to prevent is the misappropriation of customer relationships and the inevitable disclosure of confidential business information. As this Court held in *Davis v. E. Jackson, Inc.*, 164 So. 3d 1, 9 (Fla. 4th DCA 2015), "the loss of customer relationships and goodwill cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages because these assets are inherently difficult to value with precision." The covenant not to compete protects precisely these interests—the goodwill of active, paid-up customers that the employer spent years cultivating through Bradley's efforts.

Furthermore, Florida courts consistently recognize that injunctive relief remains available even when contractual remedies exist, particularly where the covenant protects intangible business interests. In *Wallace v. Holiday Islands, Inc.*, 381 So. 2d 234, 236 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980), this Court affirmed injunctive relief enforcing a noncompete despite the existence of damage provisions, recognizing that "equitable remedies may be appropriate to prevent ongoing irreparable harm even where monetary remedies exist."

The trial court properly found irreparable harm based on the employer's sworn assertions that Bradley was actively soliciting the employer's customers on behalf of Bio-Med, Inc., and that such solicitation would cause permanent damage to the employer's business relationships—harm that cannot be precisely calculated or remedied after the fact.

---

### II. THE INJUNCTION PROPERLY ENFORCES THE COVENANT AND COMPLIES WITH FLORIDA'S REASONABLENESS REQUIREMENTS

The appellant argues that the trial court's injunction is overbroad because it restrains Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc. altogether, rather than limiting the restraint to the specific solicitation of active customers. This argument mischaracterizes the scope of both the covenant and the injunction.

The covenant explicitly prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The trial court's injunction tracks this language precisely, enjoining Bradley from "soliciting any active, paid-up customers of Health Coalition, Inc." The injunction does not prohibit Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc. in any capacity; it only prevents him from using his knowledge of the employer's customer base to divert those relationships to the competing business.

The appellant relies on *FMC Corp. v. Montoya*, 881 So. 2d 621 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), for the proposition that covenants must be "narrowly tailored" and that broad restraints are unenforceable. In *FMC Corp.*, this Court struck down a covenant that prohibited the employee from "engaging in any business competitive with the employer" as an impermissible "general restraint of trade." 881 So. 2d at 624. The Court held that "covenants not to compete must be limited in scope to protect only legitimate business interests" and that "a covenant that prohibits general competition is unenforceable." *Id.*

Here, the covenant is precisely the type this Court approved in *FMC Corp.*—it is limited to the solicitation of active, paid-up customers and does not prohibit competition generally. The covenant does not prevent Bradley from working in the blood plasma industry, selling to other customers, or even competing with the employer in any manner that does not involve the employer's protected customer relationships. As this Court recognized in *FMC Corp.*, "a covenant that prohibits only the solicitation of specific customers or the use of confidential information is enforceable." 881 So. 2d at 625.

The trial court properly limited the injunction to the precise scope of the covenant, and the appellant cannot demonstrate any abuse of discretion in this regard.

---

### III. THE APPELLANT'S CLAIMS REGARDING HIS RESIGNATION DO NOT VOID THE COVENANT AND PRESENT FACTUAL DISPUTES PROPERLY RESOLVED BY THE TRIAL COURT

The appellant argues that the employer "voided the covenant" by allegedly instructing him to engage in improper conduct, and that he cannot be held to the covenant because he resigned after refusing to comply with those instructions. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the appellant has not established that the employer's alleged instructions constituted the type of misconduct that Florida courts have recognized as voiding a covenant. In *PPG Industries, Inc. v. Jaxson*, 727 So. 2d 1107 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999), the Fifth District Court of Appeal refused to enforce a noncompete where the employer's misconduct was "of such a character as to render the contract void as against public policy." 727 So. 2d at 1109. The court emphasized that the employer's conduct must be "egregious" and "in contravention of law or public policy" to void a covenant. *Id.*

The appellant alleges that the employer instructed him to resell plasma products that he believed were unsafe and to alter invoices to charge higher prices. Even accepting these allegations as true for purposes of this appeal, the appellant has not demonstrated that the employer actually engaged in conduct that violates law or public policy. The employer disputes that any such instructions were given. More importantly, the employer's position—that the products were safe and that no fraudulent invoicing was directed—presents a factual dispute that the trial court was entitled to resolve.

Second, even if the appellant could establish that the employer engaged in some misconduct, this does not automatically void the covenant. Florida courts have consistently held that an employee's resignation, whether voluntary or involuntary, does not excuse the employee from honoring a valid covenant not to compete. In *Austin v. Precision Airmotive Corp.*, 591 So. 2d 1084, 1087 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), the Court held that "an employee who voluntarily resigns cannot escape the consequences of a valid noncompete agreement simply because he disagrees with his employer's business decisions."

The proper remedy for an employee who believes his employer is engaging in improper conduct is to report that conduct to appropriate authorities, not to resign and then violate his contractual obligations. The appellant chose to resign, and he cannot now use that resignation as a shield against enforcement of a covenant that he voluntarily signed.

Third, the trial court was entitled to credit the employer's version of events—which was that the appellant voluntarily resigned—and to reject the appellee's self-serving allegations about the reasons for his resignation. On appellate review of a temporary injunction, this Court does not reweigh evidence or make independent factual findings. The trial court's resolution of these factual disputes should be affirmed.

---

### IV. THE APPELLANT HAS FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE ANY ABUSE OF DISCRETION BY THE TRIAL COURT

Temporary injunctive relief is within the sound discretion of the trial court, and this Court reviews that discretion under an abuse of standard. The appellant bears the burden of demonstrating that the trial court abused its discretion, and he has not met that burden here.

The trial court properly applied the four-factor test for temporary injunctive relief: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) likelihood of irreparable harm absent temporary relief; (3) balance of hardships; and (4) public interest. The court found that all four factors favored the employer, and the record supports these findings.

The employer demonstrated a likelihood of success by presenting the executed employment agreement containing the covenant not to compete, the employer's verified complaint alleging Bradley's breach, and evidence that Bradley was actively working for Bio-Med, Inc. and soliciting the employer's customers. The employer demonstrated irreparable harm through sworn testimony that such solicitation would cause permanent damage to the employer's business relationships. The balance of hardships favored the employer, as the employer would suffer permanent loss of customers while Bradley would only be restrained from soliciting those specific customers. The public interest favors the enforcement of valid contracts and the protection of legitimate business interests.

**Your Honors**, the appellant has failed to demonstrate any reversible error in the trial court's order entering a temporary injunction. The trial court properly found irreparable harm despite the liquidated damages clause because the harm involves the loss of customer relationships that cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages. The injunction properly tracks the scope of the covenant and complies with Florida's reasonableness requirements. The appellee's allegations regarding his resignation do not void the covenant and present factual disputes that were properly resolved by the trial court.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order and remand for further proceedings.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Based upon the review of the arguments presented by the prosecution and the defense, and the facts provided in the case background, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in entering the temporary injunction. The presence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement significantly undermines the claim of irreparable harm, and the appellant has raised material defenses regarding the employer's conduct that require a full hearing on the merits. Accordingly, the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Liquidated Damages Clause Precludes a Finding of Irreparable Harm**

The trial court's issuance of a temporary injunction was predicated on a finding of irreparable harm. However, the employment agreement between Mr. Bradley and Health Coalition, Inc. contains a valid liquidated damages clause. Under Florida law, the existence of such a clause generally negates the necessity for equitable relief because it provides an agreed-upon remedy at law for any breach of the covenant.

In *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Abboud*, 132 So. 3d 1297, 1309 (Fla. 2014), the Florida Supreme Court held that "the existence of a liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because it provides a remedy at law for damages." While the defense argues that the loss of customer goodwill constitutes a unique type of harm that cannot be adequately compensated by money, citing *Davis v. E. Jackson, Inc.*, 164 So. 3d 1, 9 (Fla. 4th DCA 2015), the specific provision for liquidated damages in this contract demonstrates that the parties themselves contemplated that monetary damages would be a sufficient remedy for a breach. The defense's reliance on *Wallace v. Holiday Islands, Inc.*, 381 So. 2d 234, 236 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980), is unpersuasive in light of the controlling precedent set by *R.J. Reynolds*. Because the employer has an adequate remedy at law, the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm sufficient to justify injunctive relief.

**II. The Injunction Must Be Strictly Construed to Match the Scope of the Covenant**

Florida law requires that covenants not to compete be strictly construed to protect only legitimate business interests. The prosecution correctly argues that the trial court’s injunction may be overbroad. The covenant in question restricts only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not prohibit general competition or employment with a competitor.

In *FMC Corp. v. Montoya*, 881 So. 2d 621, 624 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), the court held that covenants prohibiting "general competition" are unenforceable and must be limited to specific activities such as soliciting specific customers. The defense contends that the injunction tracks the language of the covenant precisely; however, the prosecution asserts that the order broadly restrains Mr. Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc. altogether. Given the strict standard for enforcing noncompete agreements, any ambiguity or overbreadth in the injunction must be resolved in favor of the employee. The trial court failed to ensure that the injunction was narrowly tailored to the specific legitimate interests identified in the covenant, thereby constituting an abuse of discretion.

**III. The Employer’s Alleged Misconduct Raises Material Defenses That Must Be Considered**

Mr. Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in unlawful and unethical business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. These allegations, if proven, constitute a significant defense to the enforcement of the noncompete agreement.

Under *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Jaxson*, 727 So. 2d 1107, 1109 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999), a court may refuse to enforce a restrictive covenant where the employer’s misconduct is of such a character that it renders the contract void as against public policy. The defense argues that these allegations are merely factual disputes that do not void the covenant, citing *Austin v. Precision Airmotive Corp.*, 591 So. 2d 1084, 1087 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), for the proposition that a voluntary resignation does not excuse a breach. However, the nature of the alleged misconduct—potential fraud and endangerment of public health—goes beyond a mere disagreement over business decisions. These are material issues of fact that go to the validity of the contract itself. The trial court improperly entered the injunction without affording Mr. Bradley an opportunity to fully develop and litigate these defenses.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction. The liquidated damages clause provides an adequate remedy at law, precluding a finding of irreparable harm. Furthermore, the scope of the injunction must be strictly limited to the terms of the covenant, and the appellant has raised substantial defenses regarding the employer's conduct that require further factual development. Therefore, the order entering the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for consideration of Mr. Bradley’s defenses and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

Montoya*, 881 So. 2d 621, 624 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004).  

In *FMC Corp.*, this Court invalidated a covenant prohibiting "engaging in any business competitive with the employer" as an impermissible "general restraint of trade." *Id.* The Court emphasized that "covenants not to compete must be limited in scope to protect only legitimate business interests" and that "a covenant prohibiting general competition is unenforceable." *Id.* Here, the trial court’s order—by restraining Bradley from *all employment* with Bio-Med—sweeps far beyond the covenant’s restriction on "solicitation of active, paid-up customers." It effectively prohibits Bradley from working in the blood plasma industry in any capacity not involving direct solicitation of the employer’s customers, which is precisely the type of overbroad restraint *FMC Corp.* condemned. The defense’s attempt to conflate "solicitation" with "employment" ignores Florida law’s strict requirement that noncompetes be narrowly tailored to the specific contractual language.  

### III. THE EMPLOYER’S ALLEGED MISCONDUCT RAISES MATERIAL DEFENSES THAT MUST BE HEARD BEFORE INJUNCTIVE RELIEF IS GRANTED  

The defense dismisses Bradley’s allegations regarding the employer’s conduct as "factual disputes" that do not "void the covenant." This is legally erroneous. Under *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Jaxson*, 727 So. 2d 1107, 1109 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999), a court may refuse to enforce a restrictive covenant where the employer’s misconduct is "of such a character as to render the contract void as against public policy." The court must evaluate whether the misconduct is "egregious" and "in contravention of law or public policy." *Id.*  

Bradley alleges the employer instructed him to resell plasma products he reasonably believed were unsafe for medical use due to shipping mishandling and to alter invoices to defraud customers. These allegations, if proven, implicate public health and safety (potentially violating FDA regulations) and constitute fraud—both of which are squarely within *PPG Industries*’s public policy exception. The defense’s claim that the employer "disputes" these allegations is irrelevant at the temporary injunction stage. *PPG Industries* requires courts to consider such defenses *before* granting injunctive relief, as they go to the very validity of the contract. The trial court was correct to reverse the injunction because it could not resolve these material factual disputes without a full hearing on the merits.  

The defense’s reliance on *Austin v. Precision Airmotive Corp.*, 591 So. 2d 1084, 1087 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), is also misplaced. *Austin* addresses whether resignation excuses performance of a valid covenant, but it does not apply where the employer’s conduct voids the contract under public policy. *PPG Industries* explicitly supersedes *Austin* in cases involving egregious misconduct. The trial court properly declined to credit the employer’s version of events without evidence, as the temporary injunction standard demands that courts weigh all material defenses.  

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT DID NOT ABUSE ITS DISCRETION; INSTEAD, IT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE TEMPORARY INJUNCTION STANDARD  

The defense claims the trial court properly applied the four-factor test for temporary injunctive relief. This is false. To grant an injunction, the trial court must find: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) irreparable harm; (3) balance of hardships favoring the movant; and (4) public interest. Here, the liquidated damages clause negated irreparable harm, the injunction was overbroad, and Bradley’s defenses undermined the likelihood of success on the merits. The trial court’s reversal was the only legally correct outcome.  

As this Court held in *R.J. Reynolds*, when a liquidated damages clause exists, the "remedy at law" precludes equitable relief absent extraordinary circumstances. 132 So. 3d at 1309. The defense identifies none. Similarly, *FMC Corp.* requires strict scrutiny of injunction scope, and the trial court correctly noted the order’s overbreadth. Finally, *PPG Industries* mandates that courts consider public policy defenses before granting relief—yet the defense seeks to bypass this requirement. The trial court did not abuse its discretion; it faithfully applied binding precedent.  

### CONCLUSION  

The trial court’s reversal of the temporary injunction was legally sound. The liquidated damages clause negates irreparable harm, the injunction exceeds the covenant’s scope, and Bradley’s defenses regarding public policy violations require factual development. The defense’s arguments rely on misread precedents and factual distortions that cannot justify an injunction. This Court should affirm the reversal and remand for a full hearing on the merits.

**FINAL RULING**

Based upon the review of the rebuttal arguments presented by both the appellant and the appellee, and the facts provided in the case background, the Court affirms the reversal of the temporary injunction. The trial court abused its discretion in entering the injunction because the existence of a liquidated damages clause negates the presumption of irreparable harm, the injunction was not narrowly tailored to the specific legitimate business interests protected by the covenant, and the appellant raised material defenses regarding the employer's alleged misconduct that preclude the granting of equitable relief at this stage. The case is remanded for proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Liquidated Damages Clause Precludes a Finding of Irreparable Harm**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court properly granted a temporary injunction in the presence of a valid liquidated damages clause. The appellant argues, and this Court agrees, that the clause negates the claim of irreparable harm. The appellee contends that *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Abboud*, 132 So. 3d 1297 (Fla. 2014), which establishes that such clauses "generally negate" the claim of irreparable harm, leaves room for exceptions where ongoing harm might not be compensable.

While the appellee correctly notes that *R.J. Reynolds* uses the term "generally," the appellee has failed to demonstrate that any exception applies here. The liquidated damages clause specifically addresses the breach of the covenant not to compete, indicating the parties' intent that monetary damages would be an adequate remedy. As held in *R.J. Reynolds*, "the existence of a liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because it provides a remedy at law for damages." 132 So. 3d at 1309. The appellee's speculation that the clause might be inadequate to cover "ongoing" violations is unsupported by the record and contradicts the contractual language chosen by the parties. Therefore, the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm sufficient to justify equitable relief.

**II. The Injunction Must Be Strictly Constrained to the Scope of the Covenant**

Florida law mandates that covenants not to compete be strictly construed to protect only legitimate business interests. The appellant argues that the trial court's injunction was overbroad because it prohibited all employment with Bio-Med, Inc., rather than merely the solicitation of "active, paid up customers." The appellee disputes this characterization, asserting the injunction tracked the covenant language.

Even assuming the appellee's characterization is correct, the standard for enforcing noncompete agreements requires strict adherence to the contractual terms. In *FMC Corp. v. Montoya*, 881 So. 2d 621, 624 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), the court held that covenants prohibiting "general competition" are unenforceable and must be limited to specific activities such as soliciting specific customers. The covenant in this case restricts only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not prohibit general competition or employment with a competitor. If the trial court's order restrained Mr. Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc. in any capacity, it exceeded the scope of the covenant. The appellee's concession that a "narrowly tailored injunction" would be appropriate on remand implicitly acknowledges that the current injunction may have been overbroad. Because the trial court failed to ensure the injunction was narrowly tailored to the specific legitimate interests identified in the covenant, it abused its discretion.

**III. The Employer’s Alleged Misconduct Raises Material Defenses That Preclude Injunctive Relief**

Mr. Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in unlawful business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. The appellee argues these are mere allegations unproven by evidence and that *Austin v. Precision Airmotive Corp.*, 591 So. 2d 1084 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), prevents an employee from avoiding a covenant based on dissatisfaction with employer directives.

The appellee's reliance on *Austin* is misplaced. *Austin* addresses whether a voluntary resignation excuses performance of a valid covenant, but it does not address the scenario where the employer's conduct potentially renders the contract void as against public policy. Under *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Jaxson*, 727 So. 2d 1107, 1109 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999), a court may refuse to enforce a restrictive covenant where the employer’s misconduct is "of such a character as to render the contract void as against public policy."

The allegations of reselling unsafe medical products and falsifying invoices to defraud customers strike at the heart of public health and safety. While these are indeed factual disputes, as the appellee notes, the existence of such material disputes is precisely why a temporary injunction is inappropriate. A temporary injunction is an extraordinary remedy that requires the movant to show a likelihood of success on the merits. Where the appellant has raised substantial defenses that, if proven, would void the contract under *PPG Industries*, the employer cannot demonstrate a clear right to relief. The trial court improperly granted the injunction without affording Mr. Bradley an opportunity to litigate these material defenses.

**IV. The Remand Should Allow for Pursuit of Contractual Remedies**

The appellee correctly argues that the reversal of the temporary injunction does not bar the employer from pursuing its contractual remedies. The reversal is based on the inadequacy of the showing for equitable relief, not on a determination that the covenant is unenforceable as a matter of law. On remand, the employer is free to pursue its claim for liquidated damages and, if it can meet the high burden required by *R.J. Reynolds*, seek a properly narrowly tailored injunction. However, the current injunction cannot stand.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The liquidated damages clause provides an adequate remedy at law, precluding a finding of irreparable harm. Furthermore, the scope of the injunction must be strictly limited to the terms of the covenant, and the appellant has raised substantial defenses regarding the employer's conduct that require further factual development. Therefore, the order entering the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for consideration of Mr. Bradley’s defenses and for the employer to pursue its appropriate legal remedies.

**FINAL RULING**

Based upon the review of the closing arguments provided by both parties, and the facts contained in the case background, the Court affirms the reversal of the temporary injunction. The trial court abused its discretion in entering the injunction because the existence of a liquidated damages clause negates the presumption of irreparable harm, the injunction was not narrowly tailored to the specific legitimate business interests protected by the covenant, and the appellant raised material defenses regarding the employer's alleged misconduct that preclude the granting of equitable relief at this stage. The case is remanded for proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Liquidated Damages Clause Precludes a Finding of Irreparable Harm**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court properly granted a temporary injunction in the presence of a valid liquidated damages clause. Both arguments presented to this Court agree that the trial court erred in this regard. The controlling precedent, *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Abboud*, 132 So. 3d 1297 (Fla. 2014), establishes that "the existence of a liquidated damages clause generally negates the claim of irreparable harm because it provides a remedy at law for damages." 132 So. 3d at 1309.

The arguments correctly note that the appellee failed to demonstrate that any exception to this rule applies. The appellee's speculation regarding "ongoing harm" is insufficient to overcome the parties' contractual intent that monetary damages would be an adequate remedy for breach of the covenant not to compete. As argued, the clause specifically addresses the breach at issue, and without evidence that the clause is unenforceable or inadequate, the trial court was required to adhere to the presumption that legal remedies were sufficient. Therefore, the trial court abused its discretion in finding irreparable harm.

**II. The Injunction Was Not Narrowly Tailored to the Scope of the Covenant**

Florida law mandates that covenants not to compete be strictly construed to protect only legitimate business interests. The covenant in this case restricts only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not prohibit general competition or employment with a competitor.

The arguments highlight that the trial court's order prohibited Mr. Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc. in any capacity. This overreach violates the principle established in *FMC Corp. v. Montoya*, 881 So. 2d 621, 624 (Fla. 4th DCA 2004), which holds that covenants prohibiting "general competition" are unenforceable and must be limited to specific activities such as soliciting specific customers. Because the trial court failed to ensure the injunction was narrowly tailored to the specific legitimate interests identified in the covenant—solicitation of active customers—it exceeded the scope of the agreement and abused its discretion.

**III. The Employer’s Alleged Misconduct Raises Material Defenses That Preclude Injunctive Relief**

Mr. Bradley asserts that he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in unlawful business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. The arguments distinguish *Austin v. Precision Airmotive Corp.*, 591 So. 2d 1084 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), which holds that mere dissatisfaction with employer directives does not excuse performance, from the scenario presented here.

Under *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Jaxson*, 727 So. 2d 1107, 1109 (Fla. 5th DCA 1999), a court may refuse to enforce a restrictive covenant where the employer’s misconduct is "of such a character as to render the contract void as against public policy." The allegations of reselling unsafe medical products and falsifying invoices to defraud customers raise substantial public policy concerns. While these are factual disputes, the existence of such material disputes is precisely why a temporary injunction is inappropriate. A temporary injunction requires the movant to show a likelihood of success on the merits. Where the appellant has raised substantial defenses that, if proven, would void the contract under *PPG Industries*, the employer cannot demonstrate a clear right to relief. The trial court improperly granted the injunction without affording Mr. Bradley an opportunity to litigate these material defenses.

**IV. The Remand Should Allow for Pursuit of Contractual Remedies**

The reversal of the temporary injunction does not bar the employer from pursuing its contractual remedies. The reversal is based on the inadequacy of the showing for equitable relief, not on a determination that the covenant is unenforceable as a matter of law. On remand, the employer is free to pursue its claim for liquidated damages and, if it can meet the high burden required by *R.J. Reynolds*, seek a properly narrowly tailored injunction. However, the current injunction cannot stand.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The liquidated damages clause provides an adequate remedy at law, precluding a finding of irreparable harm. Furthermore, the scope of the injunction must be strictly limited to the terms of the covenant, and the appellant has raised substantial defenses regarding the employer's conduct that require further factual development. Therefore, the order entering the temporary injunction is reversed, and the case is remanded for consideration of Mr. Bradley’s defenses and for the employer to pursue its appropriate legal remedies.
