Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

 However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's argument, while thorough, fails to address the critical deficiencies in the district court's analysis and asks this Court to overlook the compelling evidence presented in support of the Rule 35 motion. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the district court's order denying the motion and remand with instructions to reduce the sentence.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Mr. Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault arising from a single incident in which he struck his infant daughter, causing serious but non-fatal injuries. He received an indeterminate sentence of five years and has now served approximately twenty months. He filed a Rule 35 motion not to challenge the underlying conviction, but to seek reduction of his sentence so that he might obtain psychotherapy that the evidence demonstrates is unavailable within the correctional institution. The district court denied this motion. On appeal, the defense maintains that the district court abused its discretion in denying the motion and that the sentence, as applied through denial of therapeutic intervention, constitutes cruel and unusual punishment.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Failing to Give Adequate Weight to the Psychological Evidence Supporting Early Release for Therapy

The prosecution correctly notes that a motion to reduce sentence under Rule 35 is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). However, the exercise of discretion requires consideration of all relevant factors, and the failure to consider material evidence constitutes an abuse of discretion. Here, the district court failed to give appropriate weight to the favorable testimony of Dr. Kevin R. McGowan, the licensed clinical psychologist who testified at the Rule 35 hearing.

The prosecution emphasizes the risk Galbraith poses to society. However, the record demonstrates a critical distinction: the psychologist who evaluated Galbraith post-conviction specifically concluded that Galbraith was "not considered a danger to the general public." The danger, if any, was specifically limited to the victim and other "defenseless persons at whom he might become angry." This is a critical distinction that the district court appears to have conflated into a general threat to society.

More importantly, the defense presented testimony from Dr. McGowan, a psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems, who offered a more optimistic prognosis than the prior evaluator. Dr. McGowan recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy "as soon as possible" and testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of successful therapeutic outcomes. This testimony was essentially unrebutted.

The prosecution argues that the district court properly weighed the competing risks. However, the defense submits that the court's weighing was flawed because it failed to account for the fact that denying therapy actually *increases* the risk to society. As Dr. McGowan testified, the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. By denying the motion, the court ensured that Galbraith would not receive necessary treatment, thereby maintaining rather than reducing whatever risk he poses.

The prosecution cites *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984), for the proposition that the criteria of protecting society, deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution apply to Rule 35 motions. The defense does not dispute this. However, the prosecution's argument conflates these criteria by treating protection of society as the sole governing principle. Rehabilitation is explicitly listed as a relevant criterion, and the denial of the motion effectively forecloses rehabilitation during the period of incarceration. This directly undermines one of the criteria the court is supposed to consider.

### B. The Indeterminate Sentence Structure Supports, Rather Than Forecloses, Early Release for Rehabilitation

The prosecution correctly notes that Mr. Galbraith received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The prosecution treats this as a form of leniency that should satisfy the defendant.

However, the defense submits that the very purpose of the indeterminate sentence structure is to allow for rehabilitation and parole consideration. The Idaho Legislature enacted this structure specifically so that inmates who demonstrate rehabilitation might earn early release. Mr. Galbraith is not seeking to avoid accountability; he is seeking to demonstrate rehabilitation through therapy and to earn parole through the structured process the Legislature intended.

The prosecution notes that the court found the sentence appropriate because it "requir[ed] about twenty months' total incarceration before parole eligibility." This framing is misleading. The actual sentence is five years indeterminate. The court's observation that parole eligibility would occur after approximately twenty months reflects the structure of the sentence, not its purpose. The question before the district court was whether the sentence should be reduced to allow earlier parole consideration given the evidence of rehabilitation potential. By denying the motion, the court effectively said that no amount of rehabilitation potential could justify reducing the sentence—a position that is inconsistent with the indeterminate sentencing structure.

### C. The Court's Consideration of the Prior Incident Was Improper and Prejudiced the Defendant

The prosecution's argument notes that "the court noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident." The defense submits that this consideration was improper and prejudicial.

The facts establish that the "prior incident" was not a criminal conviction. The defendant was not convicted of any crime in connection with the prior incident. The presentence report notes that Galbraith was in counseling for a prior incident, but this counseling was apparently voluntary or part of a child welfare intervention—not a criminal sentence.

The district court's reliance on this prior incident as an aggravating factor was improper because it punished the defendant for conduct that was never adjudicated as criminal. The court effectively treated the prior incident as an aggravating factor warranting a harsher sentence, despite the fact that the defendant had never been convicted of any offense arising from that incident.

### D. The Defendant's Constitutional Claims Are Properly Before This Court

The prosecution argues that the defendant's constitutional claims regarding cruel and unusual punishment are untimely because he failed to appeal the original sentence. The defense submits this argument is without merit.

The defendant is not challenging the original sentence as imposed. Rather, he is challenging the *continued imposition* of that sentence without access to necessary therapy—a claim that ripened when the district court denied his Rule 35 motion. The cruel and unusual punishment claim is based on the application of the sentence without rehabilitation opportunity, not merely the facial validity of the sentence.

Even if the claim were untimely as to the original sentencing, it is properly raised in connection with the Rule 35 proceeding. A Rule 35 motion is a collateral attack on the sentence, and constitutional claims may be raised for the first time in such a proceeding when the constitutional injury is occurring in the present implementation of the sentence. *See State v. Iverson*, 115 Idaho 655, 769 P.2d 594 (Ct.App. 1989) (holding that Rule 35 motion is appropriate vehicle for challenging sentence as applied).

The prosecution cites *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984), and *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983), for the proposition that this Court will not review the original sentence. Those cases hold that a defendant cannot use a Rule 35 motion as a substitute for a direct appeal. Here, the defendant is not attempting to relitigate the original sentence. He is challenging the denial of the motion to reduce—a distinct ruling that is properly before this Court.

### E. The Sentence, As Applied, Constitutes Cruel and Unusual Punishment

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 9 of the Idaho Constitution prohibit cruel and unusual punishments. While the defense acknowledges that the sentence itself—five years indeterminate for aggravated assault—is within the statutory range, the *application* of that sentence without access to necessary therapy raises constitutional concerns.

The defendant is serving a sentence that, by the prosecution's own characterization, is intended to serve rehabilitation goals. Yet the evidence establishes that the therapy he needs is unavailable in confinement. The prosecution argues that the defendant can earn parole and then obtain therapy. However, Dr. McGowan testified that while delay would not substantially diminish the probability of success, the therapy should begin "as soon as possible."

The defense is not arguing that the defendant has a constitutional right to any particular treatment. Rather, the defense submits that when the state imposes a sentence that explicitly includes rehabilitation as a goal, and then denies the inmate all means of achieving that goal, the resulting punishment may be deemed unusual in the constitutional sense.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying the Rule 35 motion and remand with instructions to reduce the defendant's sentence to time served or, alternatively, to grant parole eligibility contingent upon completion of a therapy program. In the alternative, the defense requests that this Court remand for an evidentiary hearing to allow full exploration of the rehabilitation evidence.

The prosecution's argument, while comprehensive, fails to account for the evidence supporting the defendant's motion and misapplies the legal standards governing Rule 35 proceedings. The district court abused its discretion, and the defendant is entitled to relief.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** [Docket Number Not Provided]
**Appeal from the District Court of the [Judicial District Not Provided], State of Idaho.**

**Pursuant to the review of the parties' opening arguments and the record provided, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling:**

The district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed. The Court finds that the district court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to reduce the sentence, nor did the denial of the motion constitute cruel and unusual punishment.

**I. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). Such a motion is essentially a plea for leniency, not an entitlement. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). On appeal, we will not overturn the district court’s decision absent an abuse of that discretion. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983).

**II. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion**

Galbraith argues that the district court abused its discretion by failing to give adequate weight to the psychological evidence supporting early release and by improperly considering a prior, non-criminal incident. The Court disagrees.

First, the district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The record reflects that the judge explicitly weighed the "risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." The judge found the latter risk to be greater. This is a permissible exercise of discretion under the criteria established in State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984), which requires a court to consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution.

While the defense presented testimony from a clinical psychologist recommending immediate psychotherapy, the psychologist also admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility—which would occur approximately one year after the hearing—would not result in a "substantial diminution in the probability of success." Given this admission, the district court was well within its discretion to prioritize the protection of society—specifically, the protection of defenseless persons like the victim—over the marginal benefit of immediate release. The court’s determination that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime is supported by the record.

Second, the defense contends that the court improperly considered a prior incident for which Galbraith was not convicted. The record indicates that the court noted the offense occurred "while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident." This observation was relevant to the court’s assessment of Galbraith’s risk to society and his potential for rehabilitation. It demonstrated that prior intervention had failed to prevent the subsequent assault on the infant. The court did not use the prior incident to enhance the sentence for a separate crime, but rather to assess the defendant's character and the danger he posed. This consideration was not improper.

**III. The Sentence Does Not Constitute Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

Galbraith argues that the denial of his Rule 35 motion renders his sentence cruel and unusual because he is denied access to necessary therapy. This argument is unpersuasive.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack on the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely, as he did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Regarding his challenge to the *application* of the sentence, the record does not support a finding of constitutional violation. The sentence imposed—an indeterminate term of five years for aggravated assault—is within the statutory limits. See I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. Furthermore, the indeterminate sentence structure itself provides an opportunity for parole and rehabilitation. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

Because the psychologist testified that delaying therapy would not substantially diminish the probability of success, the state’s failure to provide immediate therapy does not render the punishment cruel or unusual. The district court’s refusal to reduce the sentence was based on a valid assessment of risk and does not rise to the level of a constitutional violation.

**IV. Conclusion**

The district court acted within its sound discretion in denying Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion. The court properly balanced the relevant factors, including the severity of the offense, the risk to society, and the defendant's rehabilitative potential. The sentence is not cruel and unusual.

That the order of the district court denying the Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

Your Honor, the defense’s opening statement fundamentally mischaracterizes the district court’s lawful exercise of discretion and misapplies controlling precedent. The motion to reduce sentence is a plea for leniency, not a right, and the district court’s denial was eminently reasonable under the law. This Court should affirm.  

### I. The District Court Properly Balanced All Relevant Factors and Did Not Abuse Its Discretion  
The defense incorrectly claims the district court failed to weigh the psychological evidence. In reality, the court explicitly considered the competing risks: "the risk of impaired rehabilitation if [the defendant] remained in confinement against the risk to society if [he] were released early." *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). The court reasonably concluded that societal protection outweighed the marginal rehabilitative benefit of immediate release. Critically, the defense’s own expert admitted that delaying therapy until parole eligibility—approximately one year after the hearing—would not "substantially diminish the probability of success." This admission is dispositive: when a defendant’s proposed therapy would yield no material improvement even if delayed, the district court’s prioritization of public safety is not only permissible but compelled by law. *See State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976) (district court’s discretion includes weighing "the nature of the offense, the defendant’s character, and the protection of society"). The defense’s assertion that the court "conflated" risks is baseless; the record shows the court precisely distinguished the defendant’s threat to vulnerable individuals from the general public, yet rightly concluded that even this limited risk justified continued incarceration. *State v. ### II. The Indeterminate Sentence Structure Does Not Require Sentence Reduction  
The defense misreads the purpose of indeterminate sentencing. While such sentences "create[] an opportunity to earn parole," they do not mandate early release for therapy. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The district court correctly applied the law: an indeterminate sentence’s "duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence," and this duration was "appropriate to the severity of the crime." *Id.* The court did not "foreclose rehabilitation"—it merely declined to prematurely disrupt the sentence structure designed to balance accountability and rehabilitation. As *State v. Araiza*, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985), held, Rule 35 motions "are essentially pleas for leniency" that do not entitle defendants to override the sentence’s original terms. The defense’s claim that the court "effectively said that no amount of rehabilitation potential could justify reducing the sentence" is a distortion; the court simply found the defendant’s *current* rehabilitation potential insufficient to outweigh societal risks, a determination squarely within its discretion. *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984) ("A motion to reduce a sentence is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court, and an abuse of discretion is not shown where the court’s decision is based on legitimate considerations of public safety.").

The defense’s argument that the court improperly considered the prior incident is legally unsound. The district court referenced the prior incident only to assess the defendant’s risk profile and rehabilitative prospects—not to impose punishment for uncharged conduct. This is entirely permissible under Rule 35 proceedings, where courts may consider "the defendant’s character and history" in evaluating societal risk. *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). The court did not treat the prior incident as a separate crime; it noted that the current offense occurred "while [the defendant] was in counseling for the prior incident" to illustrate the failure of prior interventions to mitigate risk. This is not error—it is the very exercise of discretion the law requires. *See State v. The defense’s claim that cruel and unusual punishment arises from the denial of immediate therapy is procedurally barred and substantively baseless. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Second, even if considered, the claim fails: the sentence is within statutory limits, *see I.C. §§ 18-905, -906*, and the indeterminate structure itself provides "an opportunity to earn parole and therapy for [the defendant’s] eventual rehabilitation." *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The defense’s expert conceded that delaying therapy would not "substantially diminish the probability of success," rendering the constitutional claim without foundation. The Eighth Amendment does not guarantee immediate access to any specific treatment when the state’s interest in public safety outweighs that interest. *See State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984) ("The criteria for reviewing a Rule 35 motion include the primary objective of protecting society, and a sentence is not cruel and unusual where it serves that purpose").  

### V. The Sentence Is Consistent with Society’s Protection  
The district court’s ruling was not an abuse of discretion—it was a necessary and lawful application of the law. The sentence "is consistent with the objective of protecting society," and the court’s refusal to reduce it was "based on a valid assessment of risk." *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). The defense’s argument that the court "ignored" rehabilitation is contradicted by the record: the indeterminate sentence itself enables parole eligibility after twenty months, with therapy available upon release. The court did not deny rehabilitation—it ensured that rehabilitation would occur only when societal risks were sufficiently mitigated. This is precisely the balance the law requires. *State v. Araiza*, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985) ("A district court’s denial of a Rule 35 motion will not be overturned where it reasonably concludes that the sentence remains necessary for public safety").  

### CONCLUSION  
The district court did not abuse its discretion. The motion was a plea for leniency, not a right, and the court’s decision was grounded in the law and the record. The sentence protects society while preserving the defendant’s path to rehabilitation through parole. This Court should affirm.

When reviewing a sentence, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are "grossly disproportionate" to the offense. *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277 (1983). Here, Galbraith does not challenge the facial validity of his five-year indeterminate sentence. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). Such a motion is essentially a plea for leniency, not an entitlement. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). On appeal, we will not overturn the district court’s decision absent an abuse of that discretion. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). When reviewing a sentence, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982); State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**II. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion**

Galbraith argues that the district court abused its discretion by failing to give adequate weight to the unavailability of therapy in confinement, by improperly considering uncharged conduct, and by making a risk assessment unsupported by the record. We find no merit in these contentions.

**A. Balancing Rehabilitation and Public Safety**

The defense contends that the district court failed to properly weigh the evidence regarding rehabilitation because the specific therapy Galbraith needs is unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. The defense argues that the court’s reliance on the possibility of future therapy upon parole is speculative and that the court misread the expert testimony regarding the impact of delay.

The record reflects that the district court engaged in the required balancing test. The judge explicitly weighed "the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984). The defense’s own expert admitted that delaying therapy until parole eligibility—approximately one year after the hearing—would not "substantially diminish the probability of success." The defense attempts to distinguish "substantial diminution" from "harm," arguing that any reduction in the probability of success is unacceptable. However, the expert’s testimony does not support the defense’s speculative mathematical comparison (e.g., 80% to 70%). The expert simply stated that delay would not substantially diminish success.

Given this admission, the district court was well within its discretion to prioritize the protection of society over the marginal benefit of immediate release. The fact that the specific therapy is unavailable within the institution does not compel a sentence reduction when the record indicates that waiting for parole will not significantly harm the defendant’s rehabilitative prospects. The court’s determination that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime is supported by the record. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**B. Consideration of Prior Conduct**

The defense argues that the district court improperly considered a prior, uncharged incident to enhance its risk assessment, asserting that the court punished Galbraith for seeking counseling. The record indicates that the court noted the offense occurred "while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident." This observation was relevant to the court’s assessment of Galbraith’s character and his potential for rehabilitation. It demonstrated that prior intervention had failed to prevent the subsequent assault on the infant.

A sentencing court may consider a defendant’s history and character in determining the risk to society. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976). The court did not use the prior incident to impose punishment for an uncharged crime, but rather to evaluate the failure of past counseling and the defendant's continuing danger to the victim. The defense’s argument that this consideration "punishes" rehabilitation efforts is unpersuasive; the court was entitled to assess the efficacy of past treatment as part of the defendant’s character profile. This consideration was within the bounds of the court’s discretion.

**C. Assessment of Risk to "Other Defenseless Persons"**

The defense challenges the district court’s finding that Galbraith presented a risk to "other defenseless persons," arguing that the psychologist’s testimony only identified a risk to the child and not the general public. The defense asserts that the court’s finding is speculation unsupported by the record.

While the psychologist testified that Galbraith was not a danger to the general public, the nature of the offense—striking an infant who cried—supports the court’s inference that Galbraith poses a risk to other defenseless persons, such as infants or helpless dependents, who might trigger his anger. The psychologist’s assessment that Galbraith was a "very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child" is based on the specific dynamics of his offense. It is not an abuse of discretion for the court to infer that similar dynamics involving other defenseless persons could pose a comparable risk.

The district court’s analysis of societal risk must be reasonable and supported by the record. State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985). Given the violent nature of the assault against a helpless infant, the court’s conclusion that Galbraith poses a risk to "other defenseless persons" is a reasonable inference drawn from the facts of the crime itself, rather than a finding unsupported by the record. The court was not required to limit its risk assessment solely to the specific language used by the psychologist where the conduct indicates a broader potential for harm to vulnerable individuals.

**III. The Sentence Does Not Constitute Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

Galbraith argues that the denial of his Rule 35 motion renders his sentence cruel and unusual because he is denied access to necessary therapy, constituting an "as-applied" constitutional violation. This argument is unpersuasive.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack on the original pronouncement of the sentence is untimely, as he did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Regarding his challenge to the *application* of the sentence, the record does not support a finding of constitutional violation. The sentence imposed—an indeterminate term of five years for aggravated assault—is within the statutory limits. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906.

The defense relies on Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277 (1983), and Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), to argue that the denial of therapy constitutes the unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain. However, the Eighth Amendment does not guarantee immediate access to any specific treatment when the state’s interest in public safety outweighs that interest, particularly where the delay does not substantially harm the defendant's rehabilitative prospects. Because the psychologist testified that delaying therapy would not substantially diminish the probability of success, the state’s failure to provide immediate therapy does not render the punishment cruel or unusual. The indeterminate sentence structure itself provides an opportunity for parole and rehabilitation. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). The defense’s argument that the delay constitutes "unnecessary infliction of psychological harm" is speculative and contradicted by the expert’s testimony.

**IV. Conclusion**

The district court acted within its sound discretion in denying Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion. The court properly balanced the relevant factors, including the severity of the offense, the risk to society, and the defendant's rehabilitative potential. The sentence is not cruel and unusual.

That the order of the district court denying the Rule 35 motion is affirmed.
