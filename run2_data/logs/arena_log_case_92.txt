COURT ARENA LOG - Case 92
FACTS:
I. Introduction

This court has been asked to provide guidance to the United States District Court for the District of Hawai'i (“District Court”) on questions of Hawai'i law. At issue is whether the parties may use, or be compelled to produce, the confidential medical records of over 100 cancer patients, in an effort to prosecute or defend against claims that the Plaintiff doctors steered these patients away from treatment at Defendant Queens’ Medical Center. The patients are not parties to the underlying lawsuit, although 19 of them have been granted intervenor status. All of them have intervened solely to assert their right to privacy and seek a prohibition on the use and production of their medical records.

The District Court1 certified the following questions to this court:

1. May a third party who is in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records use, or be compelled to produce, these records in litigation where the patient is not a party?
2. If a third party may use and/or produce a patient’s confidential medical records in litigation, is a de-identification *16process sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the third party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public?
This court may “reformulate the relevant state law questions as it perceives them to be, in light of the contentions of the parties.” Allstate Ins. Co. v. Alamo Rent-A-Car, Inc., 137 F.3d 634, 637 (9th Cir.1998) (citations and quotation marks omitted). To avoid confusion, we reformulate the certified questions to clarify that the term “party” refers to the parties to the litigation, not to the parties to the physician-patient relationship. We believe that the “parties” in this case are the plaintiffs and defendants, and the “third parties” in this case are the patient intervenors. We also reformulate the question so that a negative answer to the first certified question will not preclude us from answering the second certified question to the extent we can. Therefore, the reformulated certified questions are:

1. May a party who is in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records use, or be compelled to produce, these records in litigation where the patient is not a party?
2. Is a de-identification process sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public?2
Hawai'i Rules of Appellate Procedure (“HRAP”) Rule 13 (2000) governs certified questions. It provides, in relevant part, ‘When a federal district ... court certifies to the Hawai'i Supreme Court that there is involved in any proceeding before it a question concerning the law of Hawai'i that is determinative of the cause and that there is no clear controlling precedent in the Hawai'i judicial decisions, the Hawai'i Supreme Court may answer the certified question by written opinion.” We therefore confine our answer to the “law of Hawai'i that is determinative of the cause,” namely article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution. That constitutional provision states, in relevant part, “The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest.”

We answer the first certified question in the negative. Article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution protects the health information of patient intervenors to this case. Pursuant to that provision, and under the facts of this case, the parties cannot use, or be compelled to produce, confidential patient medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party, absent a compelling state interest.

As to the second certified question, we do not address whether sufficient de-identification is possible where one party already allowed its agents access to the patient’s records and its agents inadvertently made part of the patient’s medical information public. The de-identification process and requirements are set forth under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (“HIPAA”), Pub L. No. 104-191, 110 Stat. 1936 (1996), and its corresponding regulations; therefore, the sufficiency of de-identification does not “eoneern[ ] the law of Ha-wai'i that is determinative of the cause.” HRAP Rule 13. Whether the use and production of de-identified medical records is “sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests,” however, is a question this court can address under article I, section 6. We hold that the use and production of de-identified medical information of patients who are not parties to the litigation violates those patients’ right to privacy under article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i constitution, as no *17compelling state interest has been shown in this case.

II. Background

The Plaintiffs in this case are Pacific Radiation Oncology, LLC; PRO Associates, LLC; John Lederer, M.D.; Vincent Brown, M.D.; Paul DeMare, M.D.; Thanh Huynh, M.D.; Laeton Pang, M.D.; and Eva Bieniek, M.D. (collectively, “PRO”). The Plaintiffs filed an Amended Complaint for Declaratory and Injunctive Relief and for Damages (“Amended Complaint”) against Defendants Queens’ Medical Center and Queens’ Development Corporation (collectively “QMC”).3 The Amended Complaint alleged that the Plaintiffs “had a long-standing, 40-year relationship with QMC to provide professional radiation oncology therapy services to PRO patients at facilities owned by QMC, using equipment, technician support, and other services provided by QMC.” QMC is the only Nuclear Regulatory Commission-approved hospital at which radiation oncologists can operate on patients. Plaintiffs also acknowledged that they had “a one-third interest in The Cancer Centers of Hawai'i [‘TCCH’],” a competitor of QMC.

According to the Amended Complaint, QMC notified Plaintiffs that the QMC Board had decided to convert QMC to a “closed radiation therapy department,” meaning that only physicians employed by QMC could exercise clinical privileges to provide professional radiation oncology services at QMC. QMC explained that it arrived at its decision to terminate PRO’S privileges after determining that PRO had “transferred] patients to other facilities for no medical reason or patient request....” Plaintiffs alleged in them Amended Complaint that QMC’s action was intended to destroy their ability to treat patients at facilities competing with QMC.

Plaintiffs’ Amended Complaint raised ten claims for relief: a claim of denial of procedural and substantive due process; a claim of violation of QMC bylaws and governing regulations; three separate claims of intentional and tortious interference; four separate elaims of unfair, deceptive, anti-competitive and illegal trade practices in violation of HRS Chapter 480; and a claim of breach of fiduciary duty and bad faith owed to a partner.

QMC filed an Answer and Counterclaim. Relevant to this certified question, QMC counterclaimed that Plaintiffs Lederer, Brown, DeMare, Huynh, and Pang “consulted with and/or began treatment of patients referred to them at QMC and then induced the patients to receive treatment at TCCH without making timely written disclosure of their ownership interests in TCCH,” which constituted unfair competition in violation of HRS § 480-2.

During the course of the litigation, QMC’s law firm publicly filed a list naming 132 patients PRO was alleged to have diverted to TCCH; also included were the patients’ QMC identification numbers and the PRO doctors who consulted and treated each patient. The list was attached as an exhibit to (1) a subpoena to TCCH’s custodian of records and (2) a discovery request to PRO. The filing was subsequently sealed.

Plaintiffs then moved for a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction to prevent further violations of patient privacy. In their moving papers, they alleged that QMC had accessed the electronic medical records of 133 cancer patients, without consent, to determine if Plaintiffs were directing patients to TCCH, and, if so, how much revenue QMC lost as a result. Plaintiffs argued that cancer patient medical records would likely include “history and physicals” information regarding “the most confidential and sensitive inquiries, including prior pregnancies, abortions, sexual activities, potency, drug use, psychological issues, depression, AIDS info, family history, prior diseases, substance dependency, etc., etc.” QMC’s alleged breach of patient privacy culminated in the disclosure of 132 of the patients’ names in the exhibits to the subpoena and discovery request. Plaintiffs sought to enjoin “QMC, its attorneys, and its consultants *18from reviewing and more importantly from publishing the highly confidential information including the names of these many, many cancer patients.”

The District Court granted in part, and denied in part, the Plaintiffs’ motion. Construing the Plaintiffs’ pleading as a discovery motion, the District Court granted it, in part, and sanctioned defense counsel for publicly filing the list naming the 132 cancer patients, in willful violation of the parties’ Amended Stipulated Protective Order. The District Court denied the motion for a TRO and/or preliminary injunction, in part, because the Plaintiffs’ Amended Complaint alleged no claims of improper review and use of confidential patient information.4 As to whether the Plaintiffs could prevent Defendants from obtaining or using confidential patient information, the District Court concluded that “the parties must address these issues through the normal discovery process.”

The Magistrate Judge, in turn, issued his Order Regarding Discovery Issues. He found the 132 cancer patients’ confidential medical records to be relevant to the parties’ claims and counterclaims. He ordered the records discoverable as follows:

Although the patient medical records contain protected health information (“PHI”), this does not preclude them discovery. As noted by Judge Kobayashi in the TRO Order, “[o]nce health information has been deidentified, it is no longer protected by HIPAA” or state law. TRO Order at 29. It reasonably follows that PHI is discoverable if de-identified. Accordingly, the 132 patient medical records shall be de-identi-fied. Upon de-identification, the medical records will be discoverable and shall be produced.
The Plaintiffs appealed the Magistrate Judge’s decision to the District Court. After granting 19 affected patients’ motion to intervene, the District Court reserved ruling on the Plaintiffs’ appeal and certified the instant questions to this court.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Supreme Court of the State of Hawai'i

### Certified Questions from the United States District Court for the District of Hawai'i

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

May it please the Court:

The defense respectfully submits this opening response on behalf of Queens' Medical Center and Queens' Development Corporation (collectively, "QMC" or "Defendants"). While we deeply respect the privacy interests of all patients, we must respectfully disagree with the Plaintiff's characterization of this matter and urge this Court to adopt a more nuanced approach that balances constitutional privacy protections against the legitimate needs of parties engaged in litigation to obtain relevant evidence.

The Plaintiff doctors ("PRO") initiated this lawsuit seeking millions of dollars in damages and injunctive relief against QMC, alleging that QMC's decision to restrict clinical privileges constituted various torts and violations of Hawai'i law. In defending against these claims, QMC is entitled to discover evidence relevant to its counterclaim, which alleges that PRO doctors referred patients to a competing facility in which they held ownership interests without proper disclosure—an alleged violation of Hawai'i Revised Statutes § 480-2 (unfair competition).

The fundamental error in the Plaintiff's argument is its attempt to transform a routine discovery dispute in a commercial case into a constitutional crisis. Article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution unquestionably protects privacy interests, but it does not create an absolute shield that precludes any and all disclosure of medical information in all circumstances. This Court has recognized that privacy rights, while fundamental, are not unlimited and must be balanced against competing interests. The Plaintiff's position would render medical records completely untouchable in any litigation where the patient is not a party—a result that would severely impair the ability of courts to resolve disputes fairly and would contradict longstanding principles of discovery law.

---

## I. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON BRENDE, NAIPO, AND COHAN IS MISPLACED

The Plaintiff cites *Brende*, *Naipo*, and *Cohan* for the proposition that medical records receive the highest level of constitutional protection under Article I, section 6. While the defense does not dispute that medical records occupy a sensitive position within the constitutional privacy framework, the Plaintiff misapplies these precedents by ignoring their distinct factual contexts and the specific governmental nature of the invasions at issue in those cases.

In *Brende v. B. P. Bishop Estate*, this Court addressed whether government agencies could obtain private medical records without patient consent. The case involved the Hawaii State Department of Health's attempt to access patient records during an investigation. The critical distinction is that *Brende* concerned government action—the Department of Health seeking records pursuant to its regulatory authority. This Court properly applied strict scrutiny because governmental intrusion into private medical records implicates the fullest concerns of constitutional privacy protection.

Similarly, in *Naipo v. DHS*, the invasion of privacy was effectuated by a government agency—the Department of Human Services—seeking to obtain medical information for purposes of determining eligibility for public benefits. *Cohan v. City and County of Honolulu* likewise involved government access to medical records. In each of these cases, the Court was concerned with the government as an actor seeking to penetrate the physician-patient privilege without adequate constitutional safeguards.

The present case is fundamentally different. This is a private commercial dispute between two sets of healthcare providers. QMC is not a governmental entity seeking to regulate or investigate the patients. QMC is a defendant in litigation initiated by the Plaintiff doctors, and it seeks to obtain relevant evidence to defend against claims for damages and to prosecute its counterclaim. The constitutional analysis must account for this critical distinction between governmental invasion of privacy and private-party discovery in litigation.

This Court has never held that Article I, section 6 prevents any disclosure of medical records in private litigation. Such a rule would be unprecedented and unworkable. Courts across the country recognize that medical records are discoverable in appropriate circumstances, and nothing in the Hawai'i Constitution compels a different result.

---

## II. NO COMPELLING STATE INTEREST IS REQUIRED FOR PRIVATE-PARTY DISCOVERY

The Plaintiff's argument erroneously conflates two distinct concepts: the constitutional requirement for governmental invasion of privacy and the standards applicable to private-party litigation. Article I, section 6 provides that privacy rights shall not be infringed "without the showing of a compelling state interest." The language refers to state action—governmental infringement. The phrase "state interest" contemplates action by the government or its agencies, not the private interests of litigants.

In the context of private litigation, the "compelling state interest" language does not apply with the same force. When private parties engage in discovery, they are not acting under color of state authority to infringe constitutional rights. They are exercising their constitutional right of access to the courts to resolve disputes. The discovery process is fundamentally a private matter—a mechanism by which parties to litigation may obtain evidence relevant to their claims and defenses.

To apply the strict "compelling state interest" test to private-party discovery requests would turn the constitutional framework on its head. It would grant third-party patients a veto over discovery in any case where their medical information might be relevant, simply by claiming privacy concerns. This cannot be what the framers of the Hawai'i Constitution intended.

The proper framework for analyzing disclosure of non-party patient medical records in private litigation balances the patient's privacy interests against the requesting party's need for the information. Where the information is highly relevant to claims or defenses in the litigation, and where protective measures can mitigate privacy concerns, disclosure may be appropriate. This balancing approach respects both constitutional privacy interests and the fundamental right of parties to obtain evidence.

---

## III. QMC HAS A LEGITIMATE AND SIGNIFICANT INTEREST IN THE PATIENT RECORDS

The Plaintiff attempts to dismiss QMC's interests as merely "commercial" in nature, arguing that economic interests cannot justify the invasion of patient privacy. This characterization is both legally incorrect and factually incomplete.

QMC's counterclaim alleges that the Plaintiff doctors engaged in unfair competition by referring patients to a facility in which they held ownership interests without making required disclosures. This is not merely a business dispute about "market share," as the Plaintiff suggests. It is an allegation of potential violations of Hawai'i's unfair competition statutes, specifically HRS § 480-2. The Legislature of the State of Hawai'i has determined that such conduct is sufficiently harmful to warrant legal action and potential remedies. This constitutes a legitimate legal interest, not merely an economic preference.

Moreover, the patient records are directly relevant to QMC's defense against the Plaintiffs' claims. The Plaintiffs filed suit alleging that QMC's decision to restrict their clinical privileges was wrongful and caused them damages. QMC has asserted that it acted properly because the Plaintiffs were diverting patients to their own competing facility without proper disclosure. The patient records are central to proving this defense. The records would show whether patients were in fact referred to TCCH, whether they were informed of the doctors' ownership interests, and whether the referrals were medically appropriate or financially motivated.

To deny QMC access to this evidence would effectively prevent it from defending against the Plaintiffs' claims and from prosecuting its counterclaim. This would constitute a denial of justice—a result that itself raises serious constitutional concerns.

---

## IV. DE-IDENTIFICATION PROVIDES MEANINGFUL PRIVACY PROTECTION

The Plaintiff's argument against de-identification misunderstands the purpose and effect of the de-identification process. The Plaintiff contends that de-identification is merely a "dangerous fiction" that does not cure the constitutional violation. This position is incorrect.

De-identification, when properly performed in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 ("HIPAA") and its implementing regulations, effectively removes the connection between medical information and individual patients. The HIPAA de-identification standard, set forth at 45 C.F.R. § 164.514, requires either the "Safe Harbor" method (removing 18 specific identifiers) or the "Expert Determination" method (certification by a qualified statistical expert that the risk of re-identification is very small). These standards were developed through extensive deliberation by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and reflect a careful balance between privacy protection and the legitimate need for medical information in research and other contexts.

The Plaintiff argues that privacy is not merely about anonymity—that the "very fact that their medical histories are being scrutinized" constitutes a harm. This argument proves too much. If the mere fact of scrutiny without disclosure of identity constitutes a constitutional violation, then no medical research, no epidemiological studies, no quality improvement initiatives could ever be conducted without patient consent. This is not and has never been the law.

The Court in *Brende* and similar cases was concerned with the disclosure of identifiable medical information to government agencies and the potential for that information to be used against patients. When medical information is properly de-identified, these concerns are substantially mitigated. The patient's identity is protected, and the information cannot be used to target or harm the patient.

The Plaintiff points to QMC's alleged earlier access to patient records and the inadvertent disclosure of patient names in court filings as evidence that de-identification is insufficient. But these alleged past violations do not demonstrate that de-identification itself is constitutionally inadequate. They demonstrate that protective orders and proper procedures must be followed—and QMC has not opposed such protections. Indeed, the Magistrate Judge's order provided for de-identification before production, which would prevent any further disclosure of patient identities.

The Court should hold that de-identification, properly implemented, is sufficient to protect patient privacy interests in the context of private litigation where no compelling governmental interest in the specific patient identities has been shown.

---

## V. THE PATIENTS' INTERVENTION DOES NOT CREATE AN ABSOLUTE VETO

The Plaintiff emphasizes that 19 patients have intervened to assert their privacy rights. While the defense respects these patients' interests, intervention does not grant them a veto over all discovery in the case. The patients are not parties to the underlying dispute; they are intervenors seeking to protect their privacy interests. Their interests must be balanced against the litigation interests of the actual parties.

Courts routinely permit discovery of information concerning non-parties when that information is relevant to the claims and defenses in the case. Non-parties may seek protection through protective orders, but they cannot block discovery entirely merely by claiming privacy concerns. The patients' remedy lies in seeking protective measures that permit discovery while safeguarding their identities—not in preventing any discovery whatsoever.

---

## VI. ALTERNATIVE PROTECTIVE MEASURES ARE AVAILABLE

Even if this Court were to find that some privacy concerns remain after de-identification, the appropriate remedy is not to prohibit all disclosure but rather to require additional protective measures. Courts have broad authority to issue protective orders governing the handling of sensitive information produced in discovery. Such orders may include:

1. Requiring that all medical information be produced only in de-identified form
2. Limiting access to the information to counsel of record and experts retained for this litigation
3. Prohibiting any further disclosure of patient identities
4. Requiring that any filings containing medical information be under seal
5. Imposing sanctions for any violations of the protective order

These measures would substantially protect patient privacy while permitting the parties to obtain evidence necessary for the fair resolution of their dispute. The Plaintiff's request for a complete prohibition on any use or production of patient medical records is overbroad and unnecessary.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court:

1. Clarify that the "compelling state interest" requirement of Article I, section 6 applies primarily to governmental invasions of privacy, and that private-party discovery requests are analyzed under a balancing framework that considers both the patient's privacy interests and the requesting party's need for relevant evidence;

2. Hold that de-identification in accordance with HIPAA standards is sufficient to protect patient privacy interests in the context of private litigation, particularly where protective orders limit further disclosure; and

3. Recognize that the appropriate remedy for patient privacy concerns is the entry of appropriate protective orders, not a complete prohibition on discovery.

The Plaintiff initiated this litigation seeking substantial damages from QMC. QMC has the right to defend against those claims and to pursue its counterclaim. The patient medical records are directly relevant to these matters. While patient privacy is a significant concern, it does not trump all other interests. A proper balancing of interests supports allowing discovery of de-identified medical records under appropriate protective measures.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Defendants QMC**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

This court has been asked to determine whether the confidential medical records of non-party patients may be used or produced in litigation between private healthcare providers, and whether de-identification of those records is sufficient to protect the patients' privacy interests under article I, section 6 of the Hawai’i Constitution. After reviewing the opening arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the court renders the following preliminary ruling.

**I. Certified Question 1: Use and Production of Confidential Medical Records**

The court must first determine whether a party in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records may use or be compelled to produce them in litigation where the patient is not a party. The Defense argues that the strict scrutiny standard established in *Brende*, *Naipo*, and *Cohan* is inapplicable here because those cases involved government action, whereas this case involves private litigation. The Defense contends that applying the "compelling state interest" test to private discovery requests would grant third-party patients a veto over relevant evidence and impair the administration of justice.

The court is not persuaded by the Defense’s attempt to distinguish the constitutional applicability based on the identity of the actor. Article I, section 6 of the Hawai’i Constitution provides broadly that "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." The text does not limit this protection to infringements by the state; rather, it establishes the right of the people and the standard required to infringe it. As noted in the case background, this court has previously held that it may "reformulate the relevant state law questions as it perceives them to be," and we have already determined that the "parties" to the litigation are distinct from the "third parties" (the patients) whose rights are at stake.

The Prosecution correctly argues that the "compelling state interest" standard is the bedrock of Hawai’i privacy jurisprudence. The Defense characterizes the need for these records as a "legitimate and significant interest" in defending against unfair competition claims under HRS § 480-2. However, the Defense fails to demonstrate how a commercial dispute between private healthcare providers constitutes a "compelling state interest." As the Prosecution notes, this is not a matter of public health, criminal justice, or national security. It is a business rivalry. The court finds that the economic interests of private litigants, even when vindicating statutory rights, do not rise to the level of a compelling state interest sufficient to override the fundamental privacy rights of non-parties.

Therefore, the court agrees with the preliminary view stated in the case background: the answer to the first certified question is in the negative. The Plaintiff misreads Article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution by extending its plain language beyond its intended scope. **UPDATED RULING**

This court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense regarding the certified questions concerning the privacy rights of non-party patients under article I, section 6 of the Hawai’i Constitution. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, the court adheres to its preliminary ruling with the following clarifications.

**I. Certified Question 1: Use and Production of Confidential Medical Records**

The first certified question asks whether a party in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records may use or be compelled to produce them in litigation where the patient is not a party. The Defense argues that the strict scrutiny standard established in *Brende v. Bishop Estate*, 658 P.2d 1031 (Haw. 1983), *Naipo v. Department of Public Safety*, 74 Haw. 552, 847 P.2d 675 (1993), and *Cohan v. City & County of Honolulu*, 73 Haw. 212, 828 P.2d 1234 (1992), is inapplicable because those cases involved direct government action, whereas this case involves private litigation. The Defense contends that the court acts merely as a neutral arbiter in private discovery disputes and that the state’s interest in adjudicating disputes under HRS Chapter 480 constitutes a "compelling state interest."

The court is not persuaded by the Defense’s attempt to limit the applicability of article I, section 6 based on the identity of the litigants. As the Prosecution correctly notes, the plain language of the Constitution provides that "The right of the people to privacy is recognized and shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." Haw. Const. art. I, § 6. This text is agnostic as to the actor; it prohibits infringement of the right absent a compelling interest. When a court compels a party to produce confidential medical records through a discovery order, the judiciary—an arm of the state—is exercising its coercive power to force disclosure. This constitutes state action. To hold otherwise would allow private litigants to circumvent constitutional protections merely by filing a lawsuit, rendering the privacy right illusory.

Furthermore, the Defense’s argument that the state’s general interest in adjudicating private disputes constitutes a "compelling state interest" is rejected. While the state undoubtedly has an interest in the fair administration of justice, this interest does not rise to the level of a "compelling state interest" sufficient to override the fundamental right to privacy of non-parties. As held in *Brende*, the privacy interest in medical records is fundamental, and any infringement must be justified by a compelling interest. *Brende*, 658 P.2d at 1038. The enforcement of private rights of action for unfair competition, however important to commercial regulation, does not equate to the compelling interests of public health, safety, or national security required by strict scrutiny. The Defense’s reliance on *Akinaka v. City & County of Honolulu*, 91 Haw. 516, 984 P.2d 1124 (1999), regarding the balancing of interests under discovery rules, is misplaced because statutory discovery rules cannot supersede the constitutional floor established by article I, section 6.

Therefore, the court reaffirms its answer to the first certified question in the negative. A party may not use or be compelled to produce confidential medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party absent a showing of a compelling state interest, which the Defense has failed to demonstrate.

**II. Certified Question 2: Sufficiency of De-identification**

The second certified question asks whether a de-identification process is sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party has already allowed its agents access to the records and inadvertently made part of the information public. The Defense argues that de-identification under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (“HIPAA”), Pub L. No. 104-191, 110 Stat. 1936 (1996), severs the link between the data and the individual, thereby eliminating the privacy harm. The Defense further suggests that the patients’ privacy interests are diminished because their names were previously disclosed.

The court rejects the Defense’s assertion that HIPAA compliance is dispositive of the constitutional inquiry. HIPAA establishes a federal regulatory floor for the protection of health information, but it does not define the scope of privacy protections under the Hawai’i Constitution. Article I, section 6 protects the "right of the people to privacy," which encompasses the confidentiality of sensitive medical information itself, not merely the anonymity of the patient. The unauthorized scrutiny and use of a patient’s medical history for purposes unrelated to medical care—such as proving market share losses in a commercial dispute—constitutes an infringement of privacy, regardless of whether the patient’s name is attached to the record.

The Defense’s argument that the prior inadvertent disclosure of patient names diminishes their privacy interest is unavailing. The fact that a privacy breach has already occurred does not justify further intrusion; rather, it underscores the fragility of the privacy interest and the need for robust protection. Because the court has determined that no compelling state interest exists to justify the use of these records, the question of whether de-identification is "sufficient" is moot. Even if the records are de-identified, their use in this litigation remains an unconstitutional infringement absent a compelling state interest.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the court declines to adopt the Defense’s proposed balancing test or its interpretation of "compelling state interest." The fundamental right to privacy guaranteed by article I, section 6 of the Hawai’i Constitution bars the use and production of the non-party patients’ confidential medical records in this private commercial litigation. The Defense has not demonstrated that the state’s interest in adjudicating this dispute overrides the patients' constitutional rights. Accordingly, the preliminary ruling is affirmed.

**FINAL RULING**

This court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the parties regarding the certified questions of Hawai'i law. The arguments have been carefully considered in light of the applicable constitutional provisions, precedent, and the specific factual record presented. For the reasons set forth below, the court adheres to its preliminary rulings and provides its final answers to the certified questions.

**I. Certified Question 1: Use and Production of Confidential Medical Records**

The first certified question asks whether a party in lawful possession of a patient’s confidential medical records may use or be compelled to produce them in litigation where the patient is not a party. The Defense argues that while discovery orders constitute state action, the state’s interest in adjudicating private disputes is "substantial" and should be recognized as a sufficient, if not compelling, interest to justify disclosure. The Defense further contends that the context of private litigation, where the court acts as a neutral arbiter rather than an aggressor, warrants a different application of the strict scrutiny standard established in cases like *Brende v. Bishop Estate*, 658 P.2d 1031 (Haw. 1983).

The court is not persuaded by the Defense’s attempt to distinguish the nature of the state’s interest or to lower the constitutional threshold. Article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution provides that the right to privacy "shall not be infringed without the showing of a compelling state interest." This language is mandatory and leaves no room for a "tiered" approach where the standard is relaxed based on the type of litigation or the court's role as a neutral arbiter. When the judiciary compels the production of confidential medical records, it is utilizing the coercive power of the state to infringe upon a fundamental right. This constitutes state action requiring strict scrutiny, regardless of whether the underlying dispute is public or private.

The Defense asserts that the state’s interest in the "fair adjudication of disputes" and the "integrity of the judicial process" constitutes a compelling interest. However, this court has previously held that the state’s interest in the efficient operation of government and the protection of the public fisc does not rise to the level of a compelling state interest sufficient to override the privacy right in medical records. See *Brende*, 658 P.2d at 1038 (holding that the state's interest in auditing medical records to detect fraud was not compelling). If the state’s interest in protecting public funds and ensuring the integrity of government programs is not compelling, it follows that the state’s interest in resolving a private commercial dispute between healthcare providers—however important to the parties—is not compelling under article I, section 6.

The Defense relies on *Akinaka v. City & County of Honolulu*, 91 Haw. 516, 984 P.2d 1124 (1999), to argue for the court's inherent authority to manage discovery. While *Akinaka* confirms the court's procedural authority, it does not empower the court to circumvent constitutional protections. Procedural rules governing discovery must yield to the supreme law of the state. Because the Defense has failed to demonstrate that the adjudication of this private unfair competition claim serves a compelling state interest, the strict scrutiny standard is not satisfied.

Accordingly, the court answers the first certified question in the negative. A party may not use or be compelled to produce confidential medical records in litigation where the patient is not a party absent a showing of a compelling state interest.

**II. Certified Question 2: Sufficiency of De-identification**

The second certified question asks whether a de-identification process is sufficient to protect the patient’s privacy interests where the party has already allowed its agents access to the records and inadvertently made part of the information public. The Defense argues that de-identification severs the link between the data and the individual, thereby eliminating the privacy harm, and that the prior disclosure of patient names further diminishes the privacy interest at stake.

The court rejects the Defense’s contention that privacy under article I, section 6 is solely a matter of anonymity. The constitutional protection extends to the "right of the people to privacy," which encompasses the confidentiality of sensitive personal information itself. The medical records at issue contain highly intimate details, including "history and physicals" information regarding prior pregnancies, abortions, sexual activities, drug use, and psychological conditions. The unauthorized scrutiny and use of this content for purposes unrelated to medical care—specifically, to prove market share losses in a commercial dispute—constitutes an infringement of privacy, regardless of whether the patient’s name is attached to the record.

The Defense’s reliance on the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (“HIPAA”), Pub L. No. 104-191, 110 Stat. 1936 (1996), is misplaced. While HIPAA establishes a regulatory floor for the protection of health information, it does not define the outer limits of privacy protections under the Hawai'i Constitution. The state constitution may provide greater protection than federal statutes, and in this case, it does. The fact that HIPAA permits the use of de-identified data does not render such use constitutional under article I, section 6 where no compelling interest exists.

Furthermore, the Defense’s argument that the prior inadvertent disclosure of patient names diminishes the privacy interest in the content of their medical records is unavailing. The fact that a privacy breach has occurred does not license further intrusion; rather, it highlights the vulnerability of the information and the necessity of strict protection. The incremental harm of exposing the detailed medical histories of these cancer patients, even in a de-identified format, is significant.

Because the court has determined that no compelling state interest justifies the use of these records, the question of whether de-identification is "sufficient" is ultimately moot. Even if the records are de-identified, their compelled production and use in this litigation violates the patients' right to privacy under article I, section 6.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the court declines to adopt the Defense’s proposed balancing test or its interpretation of "compelling state interest." The fundamental right to privacy guaranteed by article I, section 6 of the Hawai'i Constitution bars the use and production of the non-party patients’ confidential medical records in this private commercial litigation. The Defense has not demonstrated that the state’s interest in adjudicating this dispute overrides the patients' constitutional rights.
