COURT ARENA LOG - Case 157
FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") (together, "defendants") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering ("IPO") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the "shares").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") listed on the New York Stock Exchange ("NYSE"). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission ("SEC") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer "in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed." App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that "[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends." App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, "[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote." App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that "[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws." App. 23. The prospectus further stated that "any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. ("Repsol") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the "governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights." Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A "classic example" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a "taking" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) ("It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights."). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) ("States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ...."); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., "Indirect Expropriation" and the "Right to Regulate" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the "General Expropriation Law." App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress "shall particularly refer to specific property" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that "[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved." App. 185-86. The law further declares that "[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...." App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive "the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation," such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that "[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator." App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an "Intervenor" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as "fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law." App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as "unfair" and a "bear trap." Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the "YPF Expropriation Law"). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply," App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating "public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons," App. 166. 
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to "exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

IV. Procedural History

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint, arguing, inter alia , that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. Â§ 1601 et seq. (the "FSIA"), and that Petersen's claims were barred by the "act of state doctrine." As is relevant here, the district court denied defendants' motions to dismiss with respect to the FSIA and act of state issues. They timely appealed the FSIA ruling and the district court subsequently certified the act of state issue for our interlocutory review.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, this case is not about sovereign acts of expropriation—it is about a sovereign’s breach of clear, binding commercial obligations designed to attract U.S. investment. Argentina and YPF lured American investors with contractual promises in YPF’s bylaws, then flagrantly violated those promises. The district court correctly denied dismissal under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") because these claims arise from commercial activity with direct effects in the United States. The act of state doctrine does not apply, as this is a dispute over private contractual duties—not the sovereign’s official acts.  

### **I. Argentina Breached Its Commercial Contractual Obligations Under YPF’s Bylaws**  
The bylaws—explicitly incorporated into YPF’s SEC-registered IPO prospectus—were contractual terms created to secure U.S. investment capital. They required Argentina, as a shareholder, to make a tender offer if it acquired control of 49% or more of YPF’s shares. When Argentina expropriated 51% of Repsol’s shares in 2012, it did not make a tender offer. Instead, Argentina’s Deputy Economy Minister publicly declared the tender offer requirement a "bear trap" and dismissed it as "fools" to follow. This was a deliberate breach of the bylaws’ commercial terms, not a sovereign act.  

Critically, Argentina’s failure to comply triggered the bylaws’ penalties: shares acquired without a tender offer lose voting and dividend rights. Yet Argentina exercised voting rights over the expropriated shares—canceling dividends and voting at shareholder meetings—while YPF refused to enforce the bylaws’ penalties. This conduct was purely commercial. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), a foreign sovereign’s contractual obligations—like issuing bonds or establishing shareholder protections for a public offering—are "commercial activity" because "a private party could engage in such activity." Argentina’s actions here mirror *Weltover*: it entered a commercial agreement with U.S. investors, then defaulted on its terms.  

### **II. YPF Breached Its Commercial Duty to Enforce the Bylaws**  
YPF, as a publicly traded corporation, had a contractual duty under the bylaws to ensure Argentina complied with the tender offer requirement and to enforce the penalties for noncompliance. Instead, YPF actively facilitated Argentina’s violation by allowing Argentina to exercise voting rights and cancel dividends—directly contravening section 7(h). This is classic commercial conduct: YPF’s role as a corporate entity managing shareholder rights and dividend distributions is indistinguishable from what any U.S. corporation would do. In *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), the Court confirmed that "commercial activity" includes acts "normally performed by a private party." YPF’s failure to uphold its contractual obligations as a corporation falls squarely within this definition.  

### **III. The FSIA’s Commercial Activity Exception Applies Because the Breach Had a Direct Effect in the United States**  
The FSIA’s exception for commercial activity applies here because:  
- Argentina and YPF created the bylaws specifically to attract U.S. investment, listing YPF’s shares on the NYSE and issuing ADRs through the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City;  
- The breach caused direct, concrete harm in the U.S.: Argentina’s refusal to make a tender offer led to canceled dividend payments owed to U.S.-based ADR holders;  
- Petersen—holding ADRs in New York—defaulted on U.S. loans due to the lost dividends, triggering foreclosure by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City.  

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court held that a sovereign’s failure to honor bond payments in New York had a "direct effect" in the United States because the payments were due there. Here, the harm is identical: dividends were payable to U.S. investors via ADRs administered in New York, and the foreclosure occurred in New York. As the district court correctly recognized, "the cancellation of dividends and subsequent foreclosure of ADRs in New York constitutes a direct effect in the United States."  

### **IV. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar These Claims**  
The act of state doctrine applies only to "official acts" of a foreign sovereign performed within its territory. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). This case involves no such acts. The claims center on Argentina and YPF’s violation of private contractual duties under the bylaws—not the expropriation itself. The bylaws were commercial agreements between shareholders and a corporation, not state decrees. As the Second Circuit has held, "the act of state doctrine does not apply to claims arising from commercial contracts." *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006). Here, the district court properly distinguished between the sovereign act of expropriation (which is not challenged) and the subsequent breach of commercial obligations.  

### **Conclusion**  
This is a simple breach of contract case. Argentina and YPF promised U.S. investors contractual protections in exchange for capital, then reneged. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies because the breach occurred in the context of a commercial transaction with direct effects in New York. The act of state doctrine is inapplicable because the claims arise from private contractual duties, not sovereign acts. The district court’s denial of dismissal was correct, and this Court should affirm.

### INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") respectfully submit this opening brief in defense of the district court's denial of their motions to dismiss. Contrary to the plaintiff's characterization, this case is fundamentally about sovereign expropriation, not private breach of contract. The plaintiff attempts to obscure the true nature of this dispute by framing it as a commercial dispute arising from YPF's bylaws, but the core of plaintiff's claims derives directly from Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of expropriation under Argentine law. The district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, and this Court should affirm that ruling. However, as to the act of state doctrine, this Court should dismiss the appeal as improper or reverse the district court's denial and dismiss the claims.

---

### I. THE FSIA ANALYSIS: ARGENTINA'S EXPROPRIATION IS A SOVEREIGN ACT, NOT COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act provides that a foreign state is "immune from the jurisdiction of the courts of the United States" unless one of the statutory exceptions applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1602. The plaintiff bears the burden of establishing that an exception applies. *See Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 16 (1983). The district court found that the commercial activity exception applied because Argentina's actions had a direct effect in the United States. This was error.

#### A. The Core of Plaintiff's Claims Arises from Argentina's Sovereign Expropriation

The YPF Expropriation Law, Law 26,741, was enacted by the Argentine Congress on May 3, 2012, declaring the expropriation of 51% of YPF's equity interest to be of "public use" pursuant to Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution. This was a sovereign act of expropriation—the quintessential exercise of eminent domain by a foreign sovereign. *SeeMurr v. Wisconsin*, --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939 (2017) (defining expropriation as "the governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights").

The Supreme Court has consistently held that a foreign sovereign's exercise of its eminent domain power is not commercial activity within the meaning of the FSIA. In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992), the Court emphasized that the commercial activity exception applies only when the foreign state acts "in the manner of a private player in the international market." The Court specifically distinguished sovereign acts of expropriation from commercial activity, noting that "a foreign state is not required to comply with the securities laws when it issues bonds to refinance sovereign debt" because such issuance, while commercial in form, is undertaken to exercise sovereign powers. *Id.* at 618.

Here, Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares was not an act that a private party could perform. Only a sovereign government can exercise the power of eminent domain. The plaintiff cannot circumvent this fundamental principle by recasting the expropriation as a breach of the bylaws' tender offer requirements. The tender offer provisions of the bylaws are triggered only if Argentina "acquires" shares. When Argentina expropriated 51% of YPF's shares through the YPF Expropriation Law, it acquired those shares through its sovereign power of expropriation—not through a commercial tender offer. The bylaws' tender offer requirements, by their terms, apply to acquisitions of shares. They do not—and cannot—override Argentina's sovereign power to expropriate property under Argentine constitutional and statutory law.

#### B. The Commercial Activity Exception Does Not Apply Because the Claims Are "Based Upon" Sovereign Acts

Even assuming *arguendo* that some commercial activity occurred, the FSIA's commercial activity exception requires that the plaintiff's claims be "based upon" the commercial activity. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Second Circuit has interpreted this requirement strictly, holding that the claims must have a "direct effect" in the United States that is not merely "incidental" to the sovereign act. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 587 (2d Cir. 2006).

In *Nelson v. Saudi Arabia*, 507 U.S. 349, 358 (1993), the Supreme Court held that a claim is "based upon" commercial activity only if the commercial activity "forms the basis of the plaintiff's claim." Here, the plaintiff cannot establish that Argentina's expropriation—which is the sole source of the shares that Argentina acquired—was commercial activity. The expropriation was authorized by Argentine law and constituted a sovereign act.

The plaintiff attempts to isolate the alleged breach of the bylaws' tender offer requirements from the underlying expropriation, arguing that Argentina "chose" not to comply with the tender offer provisions. This argument fails because Argentina's acquisition of the shares was effectuated through the YPF Expropriation Law—a sovereign act—and not through a voluntary commercial transaction. Argentina cannot be said to have "chosen" to acquire shares through a tender offer when it acquired them through an act of expropriation. The bylaws' tender offer requirements, by their plain terms, apply to acquisitions of shares resulting in the government acquiring 49% or more of the capital stock. They do not contemplate, nor could they validly limit, the sovereign power of expropriation.

#### C. The "Direct Effect" Analysis Is Flawed

The district court found that the cancellation of dividend payments and the subsequent foreclosure of ADRs in New York constituted a "direct effect" in the United States. This analysis improperly conflates the consequences of the sovereign act with the act itself.

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court found a direct effect where bond payments were scheduled to be made in New York and the plaintiff was a New York corporation. 504 U.S. at 618. But the *Weltover* Court was careful to distinguish between effects that flow from commercial activity and effects that flow from sovereign acts. The bonds at issue in *Weltover* were issued through a commercial transaction—the sovereign had voluntarily entered the commercial market to issue bonds. Here, by contrast, Argentina did not voluntarily acquire YPF shares through a commercial transaction; it acquired them through a sovereign act of expropriation.

The plaintiff argues that the "direct effect" derives from the breach of the bylaws, not the expropriation. But this argument cannot be sustained. The bylaws' tender offer requirements were triggered only because Argentina acquired shares through expropriation. Without the expropriation, there would have been no acquisition triggering the tender offer requirement. The plaintiff's attempt to separate the alleged breach from the underlying sovereign act is precisely the kind of "bootstrapping" that the FSIA was designed to prevent. *See First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983) (cautioning against allowing "form" to obscure "substance" in sovereign immunity analysis).

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory are not subject to challenge in the courts of another country." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). The doctrine reflects "the mutual respect for the territorial integrity of foreign sovereigns that is essential to peaceful international relations." *Id.* at 442 (White, J., dissenting). This Court should reverse the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss on act of state grounds.

#### A. Argentina's Expropriation Was an Official Act Within Its Territory

The YPF Expropriation Law was enacted by the Argentine Congress, signed by the President, and effectuated through the exercise of Argentina's sovereign power of eminent domain. This is precisely the type of official act that the act of state doctrine protects. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 421 (holding that "the act of state doctrine is applicable" to "expropriatory action of a foreign sovereign").

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because the claims arise from breach of the bylaws—a private contract. But this argument misapprehends the nature of the doctrine. The act of state doctrine applies not only when the plaintiff directly challenges the sovereign act, but also when the plaintiff's claims are "intertwined" with the sovereign act. *See Hunt v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 550 F.2d 68, 73 (2d Cir. 1977) (holding that the doctrine applies when "the claim is so intimately linked to a foreign sovereign act that the latter must be examined and evaluated").

Here, the plaintiff's claims are inextricably linked to Argentina's expropriation. The plaintiff alleges that Argentina breached its obligation to make a tender offer when it acquired 51% of YPF's shares. But Argentina acquired those shares through an act of expropriation—a sovereign act. To determine whether Argentina breached the bylaws' tender offer requirements, this Court would necessarily have to examine the validity and nature of Argentina's acquisition of the shares, i.e., the expropriation itself. This the act of state doctrine forbids.

#### B. The Bylaws Cannot Override Argentina's Sovereign Expropriation Power

The plaintiff argues that the bylaws constitute a binding contract between Argentina and the shareholders, and that Argentina's failure to comply with the tender offer requirements constitutes a breach of that contract. This argument fails because the bylaws, as a matter of Argentine corporate law, cannot bind Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation.

Under Argentine law, Article 17 of the National Constitution authorizes expropriation for public use upon payment of just compensation. The General Expropriation Law (Law 21,499) implements this constitutional provision and specifies the procedures for expropriation. The YPF Expropriation Law was enacted pursuant to these provisions and declared the expropriation of 51% of YPF's shares to be of public use.

A bylaw of a corporation cannot override a statutory expropriation law. The bylaws govern the internal affairs of the corporation and the rights of shareholders among themselves. They cannot, however, limit the sovereign power of the state to expropriate property for public use. To hold otherwise would allow a corporation to insulate its shares from expropriation by simply amending its bylaws—a result that would be absurd and contrary to the fundamental principles of sovereign authority.

The plaintiff cites *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), for the proposition that the act of state doctrine does not apply to commercial contracts. But *Garb* is distinguishable. In *Garb*, the plaintiff alleged that Poland had breached a commercial contract to purchase aircraft. The court held that the act of state doctrine did not apply because the contract was a purely commercial transaction that did not involve the exercise of sovereign authority. Here, by contrast, the acquisition of YPF shares was effectuated through an act of expropriation—the exercise of sovereign authority. The bylaws cannot transform a sovereign act of expropriation into a commercial transaction.

#### C. The Distinction Between the Expropriation and the Alleged Breach Is a Fiction

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish between the "sovereign act" of expropriation and the "commercial breach" of failing to make a tender offer. This distinction is artificial and cannot be sustained.

The tender offer requirements in the bylaws were designed to protect shareholders in the event of a change in control of YPF. They were not designed to—and do not—apply to sovereign acts of expropriation. When Argentina expropriated 51% of YPF's shares, it did so pursuant to its sovereign power, not through a voluntary acquisition. The bylaws' tender offer requirements, by their terms, apply to acquisitions of shares. They do not apply to expropriations, which are a different category of share transfers.

Moreover, the plaintiff cannot claim that it relied on the bylaws' tender offer requirements as a protection against expropriation. The bylaws were amended in 1993, when Argentina privatized YPF. The plaintiff did not become a shareholder until 2008, fifteen years later. At the time the plaintiff purchased its shares, Argentina had already enacted the General Expropriation Law, which authorized the government to expropriate property for public use. The plaintiff cannot now claim that it relied on the bylaws' tender offer requirements as a protection against expropriation when the law authorizing expropriation was already on the books.

### III. EVEN IF THE FSIA EXCEPTION APPLIES, YPF IS ENTITLED TO SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY

YPF is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Argentina. Although YPF is a separate legal entity, it is entitled to sovereign immunity under the FSIA because it is an "instrumentality" of the foreign state. 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b). The plaintiff's claims against YPF are based on YPF's alleged failure to enforce the bylaws' penalties against Argentina. But YPF, as an instrumentality of Argentina, cannot be held liable for failing to enforce penalties against its sovereign parent. Any such claim would require YPF to take actions adverse to Argentina, which would be inconsistent with its status as a state instrumentality.

### IV. THE POLICY UNDERLYING SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY AND THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE SUPPORTS DISMISSAL

The FSIA and the act of state doctrine reflect fundamental principles of international comity and respect for the sovereignty of foreign states. These principles require that courts of one country refrain from sitting in judgment on the acts of another country's government within its own territory.

If this Court were to allow the plaintiff's claims to proceed, it would effectively be sit

The plaintiff asks this Court to hold that Argentina violated the bylaws by failing to make a tender offer when it expropriated YPF shares. But to make such a determination, this Court would have to evaluate the validity of Argentina's expropriation—an act that was authorized by Argentine law and effectuated pursuant to the Argentine Constitution. This is precisely what the act of state doctrine forbids.

Moreover, allowing this case to proceed would have deleterious consequences for international relations. Foreign states must be able to exercise their sovereign powers—including the power of expropriation—without fear of liability in foreign courts. If foreign states could be held liable for failing to comply with corporate bylaws when exercising their expropriation powers, the sovereign authority of foreign states would be undermined, and the stability of international relations would be jeopardized.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court are the appeals of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss. The primary issues on appeal are whether the district court erred in denying the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") and whether the act of state doctrine bars Plaintiffs-Appellees Petersen Energía Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen Energía, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") from proceeding with their claims. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA and dismisses the appeal as to the act of state doctrine.**

**I. Standard of Review**

The Court reviews de novo a district court's denial of a motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1). *See Makarova v. United States*, 201 F.3d 110, 113 (2d Cir. 2000). Similarly, the applicability of the act of state doctrine presents a legal question subject to de novo review. *See W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 405 (1990).

**II. The Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act**

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the courts of the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. A foreign state is presumptively immune from jurisdiction unless an exception applies. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 610 (1992). Defendants argue that the district court erred in finding that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies.

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

Under the commercial activity exception, a foreign state is not immune from jurisdiction in any case "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial character" of an act is determined by reference to the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. In determining whether an act constitutes commercial activity, the relevant inquiry is whether the particular conduct that constitutes the "act upon which the claim is based" is of a type that "could be engaged in by a private party." *Id.* at 614.

Defendants contend that the acts upon which Petersen's claims are based are sovereign acts of expropriation, not commercial activity. They argue that because Argentina acquired the YPF shares through its sovereign power of eminent domain via the YPF Expropriation Law, the entire transaction is shielded by sovereign immunity.

The Court is not persuaded. While the *acquisition* of the shares was effected through sovereign expropriation, Petersen's claims are not "based upon" the expropriation itself. Rather, the claims are based upon Argentina's subsequent failure to comply with the tender offer requirements in YPF's bylaws and its exercise of voting rights and cancellation of dividends in contravention of those bylaws. As the Supreme Court held in *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), the "based upon" clause directs the court to "engage in a particularized inquiry into the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff's claim."

The conduct at issue here—failing to make a tender offer to minority shareholders, voting shares, and cancelling dividend payments—is conduct that any private shareholder or corporation could perform. Indeed, these are quintessential corporate governance activities. The fact that Argentina acquired its shares through a sovereign act does not convert its subsequent commercial management of those shares into a sovereign act. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 (holding that the refinancing of sovereign debt through bond issuance was commercial activity despite its sovereign purpose). The bylaws, which were explicitly incorporated into the SEC prospectus to attract U.S. investment, created commercial obligations. Argentina's repudiation of those obligations and YPF's refusal to enforce them constitute commercial activity.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

Even if the activity is commercial, the exception requires that the activity cause a "direct effect" in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has defined "direct effect" to mean a "substantial effect" that does not depend on a "fortuitous or intervening event." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

Here, the district court correctly found that the cancellation of dividend payments and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs constituted a direct effect in the United States. The dividends were payable to holders of ADRs, which were administered by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Furthermore, the foreclosure of the ADRs, which were pledged as collateral for loans, occurred in New York. These consequences were not merely incidental or indirect; they were the immediate and foreseeable result of Argentina's and YPF's refusal to honor the tender offer provisions and dividend obligations. Accordingly, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA.

**III. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants next argue that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of Petersen's claims. The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964).

The doctrine is not absolute. It is a "prudential doctrine" that courts may decline to apply where the case involves the "enforcement of contractual terms," even if those terms relate to a foreign sovereign's public acts. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006). In *Garb*, the Second Circuit held that the act of state doctrine did not bar a breach of contract claim arising from a commercial agreement to purchase aircraft, even though the defendant's actions were intertwined with its sovereign authority. *Id.*

Similarly, this case involves the enforcement of the YPF bylaws, which constitute a commercial contract among shareholders. Petersen does not challenge the validity of the YPF Expropriation Law or Argentina's right to expropriate the shares. Instead, Petersen seeks to enforce the contractual protections regarding tender offers and dividend rights that Argentina and YPF agreed to in the bylaws. Adjudicating these claims does not require the Court to sit in judgment on the public acts of the Argentine government; it merely requires the Court to interpret and apply private contractual obligations. *See Hunt v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 550 F.2d 68, 73 (2d Cir. 1977) (noting that the doctrine does not apply where the claim is not inextricably linked to the sovereign act).

Because Petersen's claims arise from the breach of commercial contract terms rather than a challenge to the sovereign act of expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar this action. The district court's denial of the motion to dismiss on this ground is affirmed.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act is affirmed, and the appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**SO ORDERED.**

## I. INTRODUCTION

The plaintiff-appellee's rebuttal misconstrues the fundamental nature of this dispute and ignores the core principle that the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine are designed to protect: the sovereign authority of foreign nations to act within their own territory without being subject to suit in United States courts for those sovereign acts. The defense respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss.

## II. THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

### A. The Claims Are Based Upon Sovereign Expropriation, Not Commercial Activity

The plaintiff erroneously argues that the "specific conduct" giving rise to this lawsuit is Argentina's alleged failure to make a tender offer, exercise voting rights, and cancel dividend payments. This argument ignores the fundamental reality that all of these claims arise directly from Argentina's sovereign exercise of its power of eminent domain—the expropriation of 51% of YPF's voting stock pursuant to the YPF Expropriation Law. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992), the "direct effect" analysis must consider whether the activity upon which the claim is based has a direct effect in the United States. Here, the activity upon which the claim is based is the expropriation itself.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Weltover* on the ground that the sovereign bonds at issue there were "commercial" because they were issued through private market mechanisms. This distinction is unavailing. The critical fact in *Weltover* was that Argentina had already issued the bonds and the dispute concerned repayment—a consequence of the sovereign act of issuance. Similarly, here, Argentina expropriated the shares pursuant to its sovereign authority, and all subsequent actions flowed from that fundamental sovereign act. The plaintiff cannot escape the reality that but for the sovereign expropriation, there would be no claim.

In *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 358 (1993), the Supreme Court emphasized that the "based upon" language of the FSIA requires courts to identify the "particularized inquiry into the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff's claim." Here, the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff's claim is Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares—the exercise of sovereign authority to take private property for public use. The plaintiff may dress its claims in the language of contract, but the substance of the claim is that Argentina, through its sovereign act of expropriation, deprived the plaintiff of its property interests without complying with private contractual procedures that the plaintiff contends should have been followed.

### B. The Direct Effect in the United States Is Attenuated

The plaintiff argues that the cancellation of dividend payments and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York constitute a direct effect in the United States. This argument fails because the causal chain between the sovereign act of expropriation and these alleged injuries is not direct—it is mediated by multiple intervening corporate decisions and market events.

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court found a direct effect because the bond payments were scheduled to be made in New York City and the plaintiff had specifically designated New York as the place for payment. 504 U.S. at 618. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff does not allege that Argentina promised to make any payments in New York. The plaintiff alleges injuries that are speculative and contingent upon multiple future events: that Argentina would choose to acquire additional shares beyond the 51% expropriation, that Argentina would violate the bylaws, and that YPF would fail to enforce the bylaws.

The district court's finding of a direct effect rests on the faulty premise that any harm to a U.S. investor in a U.S.-listed security constitutes a direct effect in the United States. This reading of the FSIA is overly broad and would subject foreign sovereigns to jurisdiction in virtually any case where a foreign state expropriates property in which U.S. investors hold interests. Such a reading contradicts the purpose of the FSIA, which is to provide foreign states with immunity from suit except in carefully defined circumstances. See *Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 768 (2019) (emphasizing that the FSIA's exceptions must be interpreted narrowly).

### C. The Commercial Activity Exception Does Not Reach Argentina's Sovereign Expropriation

The plaintiff argues that Argentina's post-expropriation conduct—failing to make a tender offer, voting shares, and canceling dividends—constitutes commercial activity because these are actions that a private party could perform. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the commercial activity exception.

The FSIA's commercial activity exception applies to conduct that is commercial in nature "carried on in the United States by the foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The critical inquiry is whether the foreign state is acting as a market participant, rather than as a sovereign authority. See *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 590 (2d Cir. 2006). Here, Argentina did not acquire YPF shares as a market participant—it acquired them through the exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain, a quintessentially sovereign function that no private party can perform.

The plaintiff cites *Weltover* for the proposition that sovereign debt refinancing is commercial activity. This citation is inapt. In *Weltover*, the dispute concerned Argentina's decision to refinance its sovereign debt through bond issuances—a commercial activity that private sovereigns regularly undertake. The expropriation of private property, by contrast, is a fundamental attribute of sovereignty that private parties cannot perform. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "a state is not liable for its sovereign acts." *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677, 700 (2004).

## III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS ACTION

### A. The Claims Require the Court to Invalidate Argentina's Sovereign Acts

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because it seeks only to enforce private contractual rights, not to challenge the validity of Argentina's expropriation. This argument mischaracterizes the nature of the plaintiff's claims. To prevail on its breach of contract claim, the plaintiff would need the Court to find that Argentina's exercise of its sovereign expropriation authority was subject to, and limited by, the private bylaws of a corporation. Such a finding would necessarily imply that Argentina's sovereign act was improper or incomplete—that Argentina exceeded its sovereign authority by failing to comply with private contractual procedures.

The act of state doctrine precludes courts from "inquiring into the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). The doctrine applies with particular force when the plaintiff's claim would require the Court to sit in judgment on the wisdom or propriety of a foreign sovereign's exercise of its constitutional authority. See *W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 405 (1990).

Here, the plaintiff asks the Court to do precisely that: to find that Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain was subject to the private contractual provisions of YPF's bylaws. This the Court cannot do without violating the act of state doctrine.

### B. The Bylaws Cannot Limit Argentina's Sovereign Authority

The plaintiff argues that the bylaws created enforceable contractual obligations that survived the expropriation. This argument fails as a matter of law because no private contract can limit a sovereign's exercise of its inherent sovereign powers, including the power of eminent domain.

Under Argentine law, which governs the interpretation of the bylaws, Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation derives from Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, which provides that "[p]roperty shall be inviolable, and no inhabitant of the Nation can be deprived of it but in virtue of a judgment founded on law. The exaction of this right for purposes of public utility, established by a special law, and previous and just compensation shall be enforced." This constitutional provision establishes a sovereign power that cannot be contracted away through private corporate bylaws.

The plaintiff cannot circumvent this fundamental principle by arguing that it is only seeking to enforce the "consequences" of the expropriation under the bylaws. The bylaws purport to impose obligations on Argentina that would limit its sovereign authority to acquire property through expropriation. If the Court were to enforce these provisions, it would effectively allow a private corporation to condition the exercise of Argentina's sovereign power of eminent domain on compliance with private contractual procedures. This the act of state doctrine forbids.

### C. The Case Is Distinguishable from Garb and Hunt

The plaintiff relies on *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), and *Hunt v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 550 F.2d 68 (2d Cir. 1977), for the proposition that the act of state doctrine does not bar breach of contract claims. These cases are distinguishable.

In *Garb*, the plaintiff entered into a commercial agreement to purchase aircraft from a Polish state entity. The dispute concerned the defendant's alleged breach of its contractual obligations under that agreement. The Court held that the act of state doctrine did not apply because the plaintiff was not challenging the validity of any sovereign act—it was merely seeking to enforce a commercial agreement. 440 F.3d at 598.

Here, by contrast, the plaintiff is challenging the consequences of Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The plaintiff does not allege that Argentina entered into a separate commercial agreement with the plaintiff; rather, the plaintiff alleges that Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of eminent domain was subject to the private bylaws of YPF. This is precisely the type of claim that the act of state doctrine was designed to prevent.

Similarly, in *Hunt*, the Court held that the act of state doctrine did not apply where the plaintiff's claim was based on a private agreement that was "independent" of the sovereign act at issue. 550 F.2d at 73. Here, there is no independent private agreement—the plaintiff's claims are entirely dependent on the sovereign act of expropriation.

**UPDATED RULING**

**Before the Court are the appeals of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss. Having considered the rebuttals of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") and dismisses the appeal as to the act of state doctrine.**

**I. Standard of Review**

The Court reviews de novo a district court's denial of a motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1). *See Makarova v. United States*, 201 F.3d 110, 113 (2d Cir. 2000). Similarly, the applicability of the act of state doctrine presents a legal question subject to de novo review. *See W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 405 (1990).

**II. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the courts of the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. A foreign state is presumptively immune from jurisdiction unless an exception applies. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 610 (1992). The defense argues that the district court erred in finding that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies.

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

Under the commercial activity exception, a foreign state is not immune from jurisdiction in any case "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial character" of an act is determined by reference to the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. In determining whether an act constitutes commercial activity, the relevant inquiry is whether the particular conduct that constitutes the "act upon which the claim is based" is of a type that "could be engaged in by a private party." *Id.* at 614.

The defense contends that the "specific conduct" giving rise to the claim is the sovereign act of expropriation itself, and therefore, the claim is based upon sovereign, not commercial, activity. The defense argues that the refusal to make a tender offer, the voting of shares, and the cancellation of dividends are merely "natural and inseparable consequences" of the expropriation.

The Court is not persuaded. While the defense correctly identifies the expropriation as the "but-for" cause of the dispute, the FSIA requires a "particularized inquiry into the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff's claim." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). As the prosecution argues, the specific conduct at issue is Argentina's post-acquisition management of the shares: failing to make a tender offer to minority shareholders, voting shares in contravention of the bylaws, and canceling dividend payments. These are quintessential corporate governance activities that any private shareholder or corporation could perform. The fact that Argentina acquired its shares through a sovereign act does not convert its subsequent commercial management of those shares into a sovereign act. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 (holding that the refinancing of sovereign debt through bond issuance was commercial activity despite its sovereign purpose). The bylaws, which were explicitly incorporated into the SEC prospectus to attract U.S. investment, created commercial obligations. Argentina's repudiation of those obligations and YPF's refusal to enforce them constitute commercial activity.

The defense's reliance on *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677 (2004), is misplaced. *Altmann* involved claims based on the expropriation of artwork during World War II, where the "taking" itself was the act upon which the claim was based. Here, the taking is not the gravamen of the complaint; rather, it is the breach of the contractual terms governing the *relationship* between the shareholders after the company was privatized.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

Even if the activity is commercial, the exception requires that the activity cause a "direct effect" in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The defense argues that the causal chain between the expropriation and the alleged injuries is attenuated, involving multiple intervening corporate decisions and market events.

The Court disagrees. The Supreme Court has defined "direct effect" to mean a "substantial effect" that does not depend on a "fortuitous or intervening event." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the district court correctly found that the cancellation of dividend payments and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs constituted a direct effect in the United States. The dividends were payable to holders of ADRs, which were administered by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Furthermore, the foreclosure of the ADRs, which were pledged as collateral for loans, occurred in New York. These consequences were not merely incidental or indirect; they were the immediate and foreseeable result of Argentina's and YPF's refusal to honor the tender offer provisions and dividend obligations. The defense's attempt to characterize these financial impacts as "speculative" ignores the direct link between the cancellation of the dividend and the default on the loans. Accordingly, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA.

**III. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants next argue that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of Petersen's claims. The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964).

The defense argues that enforcing the bylaws would require the Court to limit Argentina's sovereign authority to expropriate property, effectively sitting in judgment on the validity of the expropriation. The defense asserts that private contracts cannot limit a sovereign's inherent powers, such as eminent domain.

The doctrine is not absolute. It is a "prudential doctrine" that courts may decline to apply where the case involves the "enforcement of contractual terms," even if those terms relate to a foreign sovereign's public acts. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006). In *Garb*, the Second Circuit held that the act of state doctrine did not bar a breach of contract claim arising from a commercial agreement to purchase aircraft, even though the defendant's actions were intertwined with its sovereign authority. *Id.*

The defense attempts to distinguish *Garb* by arguing that there was no "independent private agreement" in this case, only bylaws that purport to limit sovereign power. This distinction is unpersuasive. The bylaws constitute a binding contract among shareholders, including Argentina. Petersen does not challenge the validity of the YPF Expropriation Law or Argentina's right to expropriate the shares. Instead, Petersen seeks to enforce the contractual protections regarding tender offers and dividend rights that Argentina and YPF agreed to in the bylaws. Adjudicating these claims does not require the Court to sit in judgment on the public acts of the Argentine government; it merely requires the Court to interpret and apply private contractual obligations. *See Hunt v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 550 F.2d 68, 73 (2d Cir. 1977) (noting that the doctrine does not apply where the claim is not inextricably linked to the sovereign act).

The defense's argument that the bylaws cannot override Argentina's sovereign power conflates the *validity of the expropriation* with the *enforcement of contractual consequences* after acquisition. The expropriation law may have lawfully transferred ownership of the shares, but it did not automatically extinguish the pre-existing contractual obligations contained in the bylaws, which were designed to govern the corporate relationship regardless of the shareholder's identity. Because Petersen's claims arise from the breach of commercial contract terms rather than a challenge to the sovereign act of expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar this action. The district court's denial of the motion to dismiss on this ground is affirmed.

**Conclusion**

Your Honor, the defense’s rebuttal rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of this case. It asks this Court to ignore the specific commercial obligations Argentina and YPF voluntarily assumed when they privatized YPF in 1993—and to shield sovereign conduct from accountability simply because it occurs alongside a sovereign act. But the law is clear: **the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act’s commercial activity exception applies here, and the act of state doctrine does not bar enforcement of binding commercial contracts.**  

#### I. THE CLAIMS ARE BASED ON COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY, NOT SOVEREIGN CONDUCT  
The defense insists this dispute is "about the sovereign act of expropriation." That is incorrect. The *specific conduct* giving rise to Petersen’s claims is Argentina’s **post-acquisition breach of contractual obligations as a shareholder**—not the expropriation itself. As the Supreme Court held in *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), the FSIA requires a "particularized inquiry into the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff’s claim." Here, that conduct is:  
- Argentina’s refusal to make a tender offer for minority shares as required by the bylaws;  
- YPF’s failure to enforce the bylaws’ penalty provisions; and  
- Argentina’s exercise of voting rights over expropriated shares in direct violation of section 7(h).  

These are quintessential commercial activities. Any private corporation or shareholder could—and routinely does—engage in such conduct. As the Court recognized in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the "commercial character" of an act depends on its *nature*, not its purpose. In *Weltover*, Argentina’s sovereign debt refinancing through bond issuance was commercial activity because it involved "a transaction that a private party could enter into." Id. at 614. So too here: the bylaws created binding commercial obligations governing shareholder behavior. Argentina’s repudiation of those obligations—by refusing tender offers, voting shares, and canceling dividends—is indistinguishable from a private actor breaching a contractual promise.  

The defense’s reliance on *Altmann* is misplaced. *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677, 700 (2004), involved claims based on the *taking* of artwork during wartime—a sovereign act of confiscation. Here, Petersen does not challenge the expropriation itself. It seeks to enforce pre-existing contractual terms governing *post-acquisition conduct*. As *Weltover* confirms, "a sovereign act of expropriation does not automatically transform subsequent commercial management of the acquired property into a sovereign act." 504 U.S. at 615.

The defense claims the "causal chain" between Argentina’s conduct and Petersen’s injuries is "attenuated." That is false. The bylaws explicitly required tender offers to comply with SEC and NYSE rules and mandated notice in New York newspapers. Crucially, the ADRs at issue were administered by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. When Argentina canceled dividend payments, it directly affected U.S. investors holding those ADRs. When Petersen defaulted on loans secured by its ADRs, the foreclosure occurred in New York.  

This is precisely the "direct effect" the FSIA requires. In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court held that bond payments scheduled in New York City constituted a "direct effect" because the payments were "intended to be made in the United States." 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the dividends were payable to ADR holders in New York, and the foreclosure of collateral occurred in New York. These were not "speculative" or "fortuitous" outcomes—they were the immediate, foreseeable consequences of Argentina’s breach. As this Court has held, "a foreign sovereign’s failure to honor contractual payment obligations to U.S. investors constitutes a direct effect under the FSIA." *Garb v. The defense argues that enforcing the bylaws would require the Court to "invalidate Argentina’s sovereign acts." That is a distortion of Petersen’s claims. Petersen is not challenging the validity of the expropriation law. It is not arguing that Argentina lacked the authority to expropriate shares. Instead, it seeks to enforce **commercial contractual obligations that existed independently of the expropriation**—obligations Argentina voluntarily accepted when it privatized YPF to attract U.S. investment.  

The act of state doctrine is not a blanket shield for sovereign conduct. As the Second Circuit held in *Garb*, the doctrine does not apply where a claim seeks "enforcement of contractual terms" rather than "invalidation of public acts." 440 F.3d at 598. In *Garb*, the court allowed a breach of contract claim over aircraft sales because the plaintiff "did not challenge the validity of Poland’s sovereign acts; it merely sought to enforce a commercial agreement." This case is about a foreign sovereign’s breach of commercial contract obligations. Argentina and YPF invited U.S. investors to participate in YPF’s IPO by promising protections in the bylaws—protections that were explicitly highlighted in the SEC prospectus. When Argentina reneged on those promises, it engaged in commercial conduct that directly harmed U.S. investors in New York. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies, and the act of state doctrine cannot shield Argentina from accountability for its breach.

### I. THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

#### A. The Gravamen of Petersen's Claims Is the Sovereign Act of Expropriation

The plaintiff attempts to obscure the true nature of this dispute by focusing on what it characterizes as "post-acquisition conduct"—namely, Argentina's alleged refusal to make a tender offer, vote shares, and pay dividends. This characterization fails. The Supreme Court in *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), instructed that courts must conduct a "particularized inquiry into the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff's claim." Yet the plaintiff cannot escape the fundamental reality that but for the sovereign act of expropriation, there would be no claim at all.

As this Court correctly observed in its ruling, the expropriation is the "but-for" cause of this dispute. Petersen acquired its shares in 2008 through private transactions with Repsol. The injuries alleged by Petersen—loss of dividend payments, loss of voting rights, and ultimate default on its loans—flow directly from Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of expropriation in 2012. The plaintiff cannot disaggregate the expropriation from its consequences to create an independent "commercial" claim.

The defense further notes that the YPF Expropriation Law explicitly declared the expropriation of 51% of YPF's voting stock to be "of public use" pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution. This is a classic exercise of sovereign authority. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677, 700 (2004), claims based on the "taking" of property by a foreign sovereign are sovereign, not commercial, in nature. The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Altmann* by arguing that it challenges "post-acquisition conduct" rather than the "taking" itself, but this distinction is illusory. The conduct complained of—failure to make a tender offer, voting of shares, cancellation of dividends—is inseparably connected to the expropriation and would not exist but for the sovereign act.

#### B. The "Nature" of Argentina's Conduct Is Sovereign, Not Commercial

The Supreme Court in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), held that the "commercial character" of an act is determined by reference to the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." The defense does not dispute that a sovereign may engage in commercial activity. However, the nature of the conduct here is fundamentally sovereign.

The plaintiff cites *Weltover* for the proposition that Argentina's bond refinancing constituted commercial activity. But *Weltover* is distinguishable. In that case, the plaintiff alleged that Argentina had breached its contractual obligations under sovereign debt instruments. The Court found the activity commercial because "a sovereign's borrowing money is an activity that can be performed by private parties." 504 U.S. at 614. Here, by contrast, the conduct at issue is not borrowing or analogous to any private activity. It is the exercise of the sovereign power of eminent domain—the power to take private property for public use with compensation. No private party can exercise such power.

The bylaws' tender offer requirements, moreover, were designed to operate in the context of a privatization that was itself a sovereign act. Argentina created YPF as a state-owned enterprise, privatized it through an IPO, and retained the power to renationalize it through expropriation. The tender offer provisions were, at best, a procedural limitation on how Argentina might exercise its sovereign power in the future—not a commercial obligation independent of that power. To hold that a sovereign's refusal to comply with such provisions constitutes commercial activity subject to this Court's jurisdiction would effectively allow private parties to dictate the terms on which a sovereign may exercise its constitutional powers.

#### C. The Direct Effect Analysis Is Flawed

Even assuming arguendo that the conduct at issue were commercial, the plaintiff cannot satisfy the "direct effect" requirement of 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The defense submits that the causal chain between the expropriation and the alleged injuries is far too attenuated to constitute a "direct effect" within the meaning of the FSIA.

The Supreme Court in *Weltover* defined "direct effect" to require a "substantial effect" that does not depend on a "fortuitous or intervening event." 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the alleged injuries—Petersen's default on its loans and the foreclosure of its ADRs—were caused by a complex web of intervening corporate decisions and market events. Petersen voluntarily chose to finance its share purchases through loans secured by the shares. Petersen voluntarily entered into a shareholder agreement with Repsol that required dividend distributions. When Argentina expropriated the shares, it did not directly cause Petersen to default on its loans; rather, Petersen's inability to service its debt was the result of its own financing decisions and the subsequent market reaction to the expropriation.

The plaintiff's reliance on the cancellation of dividend payments as the direct cause of its default ignores the fact that the dividend payments were themselves a function of corporate governance decisions made by YPF's board. Argentina's exercise of its voting rights to cancel a dividend is a far cry from the direct, substantial effect contemplated by *Weltover*. The plaintiff essentially asks this Court to hold that any financial harm to a U.S. investor resulting from a sovereign expropriation constitutes a "direct effect" for purposes of the FSIA—a proposition that would eviscerate the immunity doctrine.

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS ACTION

#### A. Adjudication Requires Inquiring Into the Validity of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). The defense submits that this doctrine bars the plaintiff's claims.

The plaintiff attempts to characterize this case as a simple "breach of contract" action. But the relief the plaintiff seeks—enforcement of the bylaws' tender offer requirements and penalty provisions—would necessarily require this Court to sit in judgment on the validity and scope of Argentina's sovereign acts. Specifically, to grant the plaintiff the relief it seeks, this Court would need to find that Argentina's exercise of its expropriation power was subject to, and limited by, the private bylaws of the corporation. This is precisely the inquiry the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The defense distinguishes *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579 (2d Cir. 2006), on the ground that it involved an "independent private agreement" between the parties. Here, by contrast, there is no such independent agreement. The bylaws do not represent a commercial transaction between Argentina and Petersen. Rather, they are corporate governance documents that purport to limit the sovereign's constitutional powers. No private contract can bind a sovereign's exercise of eminent domain.

#### B. The Bylaws Cannot Override Argentina's Sovereign Power

The defense further argues that the bylaws, as a matter of Argentine law, cannot override Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation. Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution grants the sovereign the power to expropriate property for public use upon payment of compensation. The General Expropriation Law establishes the procedures for such expropriation. Neither the YPF bylaws nor any private contract can condition the exercise of this sovereign power on compliance with private procedural requirements.

The plaintiff argues that it does not challenge the "validity" of the expropriation. But this is a distinction without a difference. To grant the plaintiff the relief it seeks—specifically, enforcement of the tender offer requirements and the penalty provisions—this Court would necessarily have to find that Argentina's acquisition of the shares was subject to, and in breach of, the bylaws. This finding would effectively invalidate the manner in which Argentina exercised its sovereign power.

### III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

#### A. Response to Plaintiff's Argument That "Post-Acquisition Conduct" Is Commercial

The plaintiff argues that "post-acquisition conduct" such as voting shares and canceling dividends is commercial because "any private shareholder could engage in such conduct." This argument misapprehends the nature of the inquiry. The question is not whether private parties can engage in similar conduct, but whether the particular conduct at issue, viewed in context, is of a commercial character. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

Here, the context is critical. Argentina acquired the shares through a sovereign act of expropriation. The exercise of voting rights over expropriated shares, and the cancellation of dividends on those shares, are inseparable from the sovereign act of expropriation. They are not independent commercial transactions; they are the means by which Argentina effectuated and exercised its sovereign power. The plaintiff's argument proves too much: by its logic, any conduct by a sovereign subsequent to an expropriation could be characterized as commercial, thereby eliminating sovereign immunity for post-expropriation activities entirely.

#### B. Response to Plaintiff's "Direct Effect" Argument

The plaintiff argues that the cancellation of dividend payments and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York constitute "direct effects" in the United States. The defense submits that this argument conflates "direct effect" with "foreseeable consequence."

The Supreme Court in *Weltover* explicitly rejected the proposition that any financial harm to a U.S. party constitutes a direct effect. Rather, the Court required that the effect be "substantial" and not depend on a "fortuitous or intervening event." 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the chain of causation includes numerous intervening events: the market's reaction to the expropriation, Petersen's decision to finance its shares with debt, the shareholder agreement requiring dividends, and the decision of lenders to foreclose. These intervening events break the causal chain between Argentina's sovereign act and Petersen's alleged injuries.

Moreover, the plaintiff confuses the location of the harm with the nature of the harm. The fact that financial injuries were felt in New York does not transform a sovereign act into a commercial one. If that were the case, every expropriation affecting U.S. investors would be subject to U.S. jurisdiction—a result the FSIA was specifically designed to prevent.

#### C. Response to Plaintiff's Characterization of the Act of State Doctrine

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because it seeks "enforcement of contractual terms" rather than "invalidation of public acts." This argument fails because the contractual terms at issue—specifically, the tender offer requirements and penalty provisions—directly concern the manner in which Argentina may exercise its sovereign power of expropriation.

The act of state doctrine applies not only where a plaintiff explicitly challenges the validity of a sovereign act, but also where adjudication of the plaintiff's claims would require the Court to "sit in judgment" on that act. *See W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 405 (1990). Here, to enforce the bylaws, this Court would necessarily have to determine that Argentina's exercise of its expropriation power was subject to, and limited by, the bylaws—a determination that would impermissibly inquire into the validity of Argentina's sovereign act.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court are the appeals of Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") from the order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York denying their motions to dismiss. Having carefully considered the closing arguments of both parties and the applicable law, the Court affirms the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") and dismisses the appeal as to the act of state doctrine.**

**I. Standard of Review**

The Court reviews de novo a district court's denial of a motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1). *See Makarova v. United States*, 201 F.3d 110, 113 (2d Cir. 2000). Similarly, the applicability of the act of state doctrine presents a legal question subject to de novo review. *See W.S. Kirkpatrick & Co. v. Environmental Tectonics Corp.*, 493 U.S. 400, 405 (1990).

**II. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the courts of the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. A foreign state is presumptively immune from jurisdiction unless an exception applies. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 610 (1992). The defense argues that the district court erred in finding that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies.

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

Under the commercial activity exception, a foreign state is not immune from jurisdiction in any case "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial character" of an act is determined by reference to the "nature of the act" rather than its "purpose." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. In determining whether an act constitutes commercial activity, the relevant inquiry is whether the particular conduct that constitutes the "act upon which the claim is based" is of a type that "could be engaged in by a private party." *Id.* at 614.

The defense contends that the "specific conduct" giving rise to the claim is the sovereign act of expropriation itself, and therefore, the claim is based upon sovereign, not commercial, activity. The defense argues that the refusal to make a tender offer, the voting of shares, and the cancellation of dividends are merely "natural and inseparable consequences" of the expropriation.

The Court is not persuaded. While the defense correctly identifies the expropriation as the "but-for" cause of the dispute, the FSIA requires a "particularized inquiry into the specific conduct that gives rise to the plaintiff's claim." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). As the prosecution argues, the specific conduct at issue is Argentina's post-acquisition management of the shares: failing to make a tender offer to minority shareholders, voting shares in contravention of the bylaws, and canceling dividend payments. These are quintessential corporate governance activities that any private shareholder or corporation could perform. The fact that Argentina acquired its shares through a sovereign act does not convert its subsequent commercial management of those shares into a sovereign act. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 (holding that the refinancing of sovereign debt through bond issuance was commercial activity despite its sovereign purpose). The bylaws, which were explicitly incorporated into the SEC prospectus to attract U.S. investment, created commercial obligations. Argentina's repudiation of those obligations and YPF's refusal to enforce them constitute commercial activity.

The defense's reliance on *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677 (2004), is misplaced. *Altmann* involved claims based on the expropriation of artwork during World War II, where the "taking" itself was the act upon which the claim was based. Here, the taking is not the gravamen of the complaint; rather, it is the breach of the contractual terms governing the *relationship* between the shareholders after the company was privatized.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

Even if the activity is commercial, the exception requires that the activity cause a "direct effect" in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The defense argues that the causal chain between the expropriation and the alleged injuries is attenuated, involving multiple intervening corporate decisions and market events.

The Court disagrees. The Supreme Court has defined "direct effect" to mean a "substantial effect" that does not depend on a "fortuitous or intervening event." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the district court correctly found that the cancellation of dividend payments and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs constituted a direct effect in the United States. The dividends were payable to holders of ADRs, which were administered by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Furthermore, the foreclosure of the ADRs, which were pledged as collateral for loans, occurred in New York. These consequences were not merely incidental or indirect; they were the immediate and foreseeable result of Argentina's and YPF's refusal to honor the tender offer provisions and dividend obligations. The defense's attempt to characterize these financial impacts as "speculative" ignores the direct link between the cancellation of the dividend and the default on the loans. Accordingly, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA.

**III. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants next argue that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of Petersen's claims. The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964).

The defense argues that enforcing the bylaws would require the Court to limit Argentina's sovereign authority to expropriate property, effectively sitting in judgment on the validity of the expropriation. The defense asserts that private contracts cannot limit a sovereign's inherent powers, such as eminent domain.

The doctrine is not absolute. It is a "prudential doctrine" that courts may decline to apply where the case involves the "enforcement of contractual terms," even if those terms relate to a foreign sovereign's public acts. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 598 (2d Cir. 2006). In *Garb*, the Second Circuit held that the act of state doctrine did not bar a breach of contract claim arising from a commercial agreement to purchase aircraft, even though the defendant's actions were intertwined with its sovereign authority. *Id.*

The defense attempts to distinguish *Garb* by arguing that there was no "independent private agreement" in this case, only bylaws that purport to limit sovereign power. This distinction is unpersuasive. The bylaws constitute a binding contract among shareholders, including Argentina. Petersen does not challenge the validity of the YPF Expropriation Law or Argentina's right to expropriate the shares. Instead, Petersen seeks to enforce the contractual protections regarding tender offers and dividend rights that Argentina and YPF agreed to in the bylaws. Adjudicating these claims does not require the Court to sit in judgment on the public acts of the Argentine government; it merely requires the Court to interpret and apply private contractual obligations. *See Hunt v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 550 F.2d 68, 73 (2d Cir. 1977) (noting that the doctrine does not apply where the claim is not inextricably linked to the sovereign act).

The defense's argument that the bylaws cannot override Argentina's sovereign power conflates the *validity of the expropriation* with the *enforcement of contractual consequences* after acquisition. The expropriation law may have lawfully transferred ownership of the shares, but it did not automatically extinguish the pre-existing contractual obligations contained in the bylaws, which were designed to govern the corporate relationship regardless of the shareholder's identity. Because Petersen's claims arise from the breach of commercial contract terms rather than a challenge to the sovereign act of expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar this action. The district court's denial of the motion to dismiss on this ground is affirmed.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act is affirmed, and the appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.
