## INTRODUCTION

Respondent Heath Carter respectfully submits this opening brief in response to Appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc.'s appeal from the district court's judgment. St. Jude challenges the district court's refusal to grant injunctive relief following the jury's finding that Carter breached his employment contract. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the district court's decision.

The procedural posture of this case is critical. The jury found that Carter breached his contract but also found that Boston Scientific did not tortiously interfere with that contract. The district court, bound by the jury's verdict on breach, nonetheless correctly determined that St. Jude was not entitled to injunctive relief because it failed to prove irreparable harm. St. Jude's appeal asks this Court to rewrite Minnesota's equitable standards and grant injunctive relief where the evidence of irreparable harm was utterly lacking. This Court should decline that invitation.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are accurately set forth in the case background, but certain points bear emphasis.

Carter was employed by St. Jude from 2007 to 2015. During his eight-year tenure, he held various positions. Initially, he performed preclinical research and product development testing on EP products. Later, he assisted in redesigning St. Jude's website and was responsible for marketing EP *capital* products. Only "toward the end of his tenure" did Carter participate in research regarding one of St. Jude's EP *disposable* products.

St. Jude filed suit in December 2015, alleging breach of contract and tortious interference. The case proceeded to a four-day jury trial. The jury returned a special verdict finding: (1) Carter had breached his employment contract, but (2) Boston Scientific had not intentionally caused Carter to commit the breach.

Critically, St. Jude "indicated prior to trial that it was not seeking monetary damages for Carter's breach of contract, but was instead seeking equitable relief." The jury was not asked to make findings regarding damages.

The district court denied injunctive relief, finding that St. Jude "failed to establish at trial that it had suffered irreparable harm." The court also concluded that St. Jude was not entitled to monetary damages or attorney fees because it had "expressly abandoned or waived those remedies."

## STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the district court's denial of injunctive relief for an abuse of discretion. *See* *Meyer v. McCarvel*, 416 N.W.2d 727, 729 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987). The district court's legal conclusions, however, are reviewed de novo. *See* *Halla v. Norwest Capital Mgmt. & Trust Co.*, 661 N.W.2d 625, 629 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003).

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT ST. JUDE FAILED TO PROVE IRREPARABLE HARM.

The district court correctly applied Minnesota's well-established standard for injunctive relief. Under Minnesota law, a party seeking an injunction must prove: (1) irreparable harm; (2) that the balance of equities favors the movant; and (3) that the public interest supports the injunction. *See* *Grange Ins. Co. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 283 N.W.2d 514, 518 (Minn. 1979). The requirement of proving irreparable harm is fundamental—"injunctive relief is an extraordinary remedy, and the burden is on the movant to establish that the requisites for such relief exist." *Id.*

St. Jude cannot satisfy this burden. At trial, St. Jude presented no evidence whatsoever of irreparable harm. It offered no testimony, no expert analysis, and no documentary evidence demonstrating that Carter's employment with Boston Scientific caused or would cause harm that could not be remedied by monetary damages. St. Jude did not prove that Carter disclosed any confidential information. It did not establish that Boston Scientific used any proprietary knowledge. It did not show that its competitive position was threatened.

The evidence actually demonstrated the opposite. Carter's role at Boston Scientific focused on EP disposable products, while his work at St. Jude centered on EP capital products. These are distinct product categories. The record contains no evidence of overlap in responsibilities, no evidence of access to trade secrets relevant to disposable products, and no evidence of any risk of disclosure.

St. Jude's own litigation choices underscore the weakness of its position. Having expressly waived monetary damages, St. Jude was required to prove irreparable harm to obtain any relief. It failed to do so.

### II. THE CONTRACT'S REMEDIES CLAUSE DOES NOT RELIEVE ST. JUDE OF ITS BURDEN TO PROVE IRREPARABLE HARM.

St. Jude will likely argue that its employment contract expressly states that breach would cause "irreparable injury" and entitle St. Jude to an injunction. This argument fails.

Minnesota courts have consistently held that contractual provisions stating that a breach would cause irreparable harm are not dispositive. Parties must still demonstrate actual irreparable harm at trial. *See* *Davis v. Jointless Fire Co.*, 150 F. 771, 777 (2d Cir. 1906) (Hand, J.) (explaining that contractual designations of irreparable harm "are not conclusive; the court must look at the facts"); *see also* *Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment* § 12 cmt. c (2011) ("The fact that a contract specifically provides for injunctive relief does not, by itself, establish that the threatened injury would be irreparable.").

The district court correctly recognized that while such clauses are relevant to the analysis, they do not substitute for factual proof. St. Jude cannot overcome its failure to present evidence of irreparable harm merely by pointing to language in the contract.

### III. ST. JUDE WAIVED MONETARY DAMAGES AND CANNOT NOW ARGUE THAT MONETARY REMEDIES ARE INADEQUATE.

St. Jude's explicit waiver of monetary damages before trial undermines its claim of irreparable harm. A finding of irreparable harm is predicated on the inadequacy of monetary remedies. *See* *Meyer*, 416 N.W.2d at 729 ("The basis for injunctive relief is the inadequacy of legal remedies."). When a party voluntarily abandons monetary damages, it effectively concedes that monetary remedies are available—and thus that injunctive relief is unnecessary.

St. Jude cannot have it both ways. By waiving monetary damages, St. Jude acknowledged that it could be fully compensated through legal remedies. Having made that election, it cannot now argue that no monetary remedy could make it whole.

### IV. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY BALANCED THE EQUITIES.

Even if St. Jude had demonstrated some potential harm, the balance of equities weighs heavily against injunctive relief. Carter voluntarily resigned from St. Jude in August 2015 and began employment at Boston Scientific in August 2015. By the time of trial in 2016, Carter had been employed at Boston Scientific for over a year. An injunction prohibiting Carter from continuing his employment would have caused severe hardship to Carter—depriving him of his livelihood and the specialized skills he had developed—while providing minimal benefit to St. Jude, given the lack of evidence of actual harm.

Minnesota courts consistently recognize that injunctive relief that deprives an employee of his livelihood requires particularly careful scrutiny. *See* *Minn. Stat. § 604.14* (prohibiting restrictive employment covenants unless reasonable to protect employer's legitimate business interests). The district court properly weighed these considerations and correctly concluded that injunctive relief was unwarranted.

### V. THE JURY'S FINDING THAT BOSTON SCIENTIFIC DID NOT TORTIOUSLY INTERFERE SUPPORTS THE DENIAL OF RELIEF.

The jury's special verdict finding that Boston Scientific did not intentionally cause Carter to breach his contract further supports the district court's decision. This finding demonstrates that Boston Scientific acted in good faith, believing that Carter's position did not violate his noncompete obligations. Boston Scientific specifically represented to St. Jude that Carter's position at Boston Scientific focused on EP disposable products while his work at St. Jude involved EP capital products—a distinction that the evidence supported.

This good-faith reliance by Boston Scientific weighs against treating Carter's employment as the sort of egregious breach that might warrant injunctive relief. St. Jude's remedy, if any, lies in its contract claim against Carter—not in punishing Boston Scientific for hiring him.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent Heath Carter respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's judgment denying injunctive relief to St. Jude Medical, Inc.

The district court correctly applied Minnesota law. St. Jude failed to prove irreparable harm, waived monetary damages, and presented no evidence that Carter's employment caused any harm that could not be remedied through legal remedies. The contract's boilerplate remedies clause does not relieve St. Jude of its burden to demonstrate actual irreparable harm at trial. The district court's decision was well-supported by the evidence and the law.

This Court should affirm.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**ST. JUDE MEDICAL, INC., Appellant,**
**v.**
**HEATH CARTER, Respondent.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s judgment denying Appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc. (St. Jude) injunctive relief following a jury verdict finding that Respondent Heath Carter breached his employment contract. The district court denied St. Jude’s request for a permanent injunction, concluding that St. Jude failed to establish it had suffered irreparable harm. St. Jude appeals this denial. The Court has reviewed the arguments presented by the parties in the provided record.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The district court’s decision to deny a motion for permanent injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *Meyer v. McCarvel*, 416 N.W.2d 727, 729 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987). The district court’s legal conclusions, however, are subject to de novo review. *Halla v. Norwest Capital Mgmt. & Trust Co.*, 661 N.W.2d 625, 629 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. St. Jude Failed to Prove Irreparable Harm**

Injunctive relief is an extraordinary remedy that is granted only when the movant demonstrates that: (1) irreparable harm exists; (2) the balance of equities favors the movant; and (3) the public interest supports the injunction. *Grange Ins. Co. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 283 N.W.2d 514, 518 (Minn. 1979). The burden of establishing these elements rests squarely with the movant. *Id.*

The district court found that St. Jude failed to meet its burden regarding the first element—irreparable harm. The record supports this conclusion. St. Jude presented no evidence that Carter’s employment with Boston Scientific caused or would cause harm that could not be remedied by monetary damages. Specifically, St. Jude offered no testimony or documentation showing that Carter disclosed confidential information or that his new position posed a threat to St. Jude’s trade secrets. The evidence demonstrated that Carter’s role at Boston Scientific focused on EP disposable products, whereas his work at St. Jude had centered primarily on EP capital products. Without evidence of overlap in responsibilities or a risk of disclosure, St. Jude cannot establish that it suffered irreparable harm.

**B. The Remedies Clause Does Not Relieve St. Jude of Its Burden**

St. Jude relies on a clause in the employment contract stating that a breach would cause "irreparable injury" and entitle St. Jude to an injunction. The Respondent correctly argues that this contractual language is not dispositive. As recognized in *Davis v. Jointless Fire Co.*, 150 F. 771, 777 (2d Cir. 1906), contractual designations of irreparable harm "are not conclusive; the court must look at the facts." The district court properly required St. Jude to provide factual proof of irreparable harm rather than relying solely on the contract’s boilerplate language. Because St. Jude failed to provide such proof, the contract clause cannot sustain the injunction.

**C. Waiver of Monetary Damages and Balance of Equities**

St. Jude’s decision to waive monetary damages further undermines its claim for equitable relief. The basis for injunctive relief is the inadequacy of legal remedies. *Meyer*, 416 N.W.2d at 729. By expressly waiving monetary damages, St. Jude effectively conceded that legal remedies were available to compensate for any harm.

Furthermore, the balance of equities weighs heavily against injunctive relief. Carter has been employed at Boston Scientific for over a year. An injunction prohibiting him from continuing his employment would deprive him of his livelihood, while St. Jude has demonstrated no actual harm. Minnesota law requires that restrictive covenants be reasonable to protect the employer's legitimate business interests. *Minn. Stat. § 604.14*. Given the lack of evidence regarding the disclosure of trade secrets or competitive harm, enforcing the noncompete via injunction at this stage would be unreasonable.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying injunctive relief. St. Jude failed to prove irreparable harm, a necessary prerequisite for such relief. The contractual remedies clause does not relieve St. Jude of this burden. Additionally, the balance of equities favors the Respondent. Therefore, the district court’s judgment is affirmed.

As recognized by the district court, such boilerplate provisions are relevant but not dispositive; the court must examine the actual facts. St. Jude’s attempt to transform a contractual stipulation into a legal entitlement ignores the well-established principle that courts must evaluate the circumstances of each case.  

### IV. ST. JUDE WAIVED MONETARY DAMAGES, UNDERMINING ITS CLAIM OF IRREPARABLE HARM  
St. Jude’s decision to waive monetary damages before trial fundamentally undermines its request for injunctive relief. The basis for equitable remedies is the inadequacy of legal remedies. *Meyer v. McCarvel*, 416 N.W.2d 727, 729 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987). By expressly abandoning monetary damages, St. Jude effectively conceded that monetary compensation could fully address any harm caused by Carter’s breach. This election bars St. Jude from now arguing that no monetary remedy exists. A party cannot simultaneously waive legal remedies and claim irreparable harm—it is an irreconcilable contradiction.  

### V. THE BALANCE OF EQUITIES AND PUBLIC INTEREST WEIGH AGAINST INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

Carter has been employed at Boston Scientific for over a year, and an injunction would deprive him of his livelihood and specialized skills developed over a decade. St. Jude’s sole remedy, if any, lies in contractual damages against Carter—but St. Jude waived those remedies.

The district court did not err in denying injunctive relief. St. Jude failed to prove irreparable harm, its contractual remedies clause does not override the need for factual evidence, and its waiver of monetary damages precludes equitable relief. ### I. INTRODUCTION

Appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc. respectfully submits this defense rebuttal in response to the arguments advanced by Respondent Heath Carter in opposition to St. Jude's appeal from the district court's denial of injunctive relief. ### II. THE RECORD ESTABLISHES THAT CARTER'S EMPLOYMENT AT BOSTON SCIENTIFIC VIOLATES THE NONCOMPETE CLAUSE

The noncompete provision in Carter's employment contract expressly prohibits him from "rendering services to any other person or entity that competes in any sense with St. Jude in connection with the design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale of any product, process or service that is being designed, developed, manufactured, marketed or sold by St. Jude and in which the Employee participated in the design, development, manufacture, marketing or sale during Employee's employment with St. Jude."

The evidence demonstrates that Carter participated in marketing activities for St. Jude's EP disposable products during his employment. The facts establish that "toward the end of his tenure, Carter also participated in research regarding one of St. Jude's EP disposable products." This participation falls squarely within the scope of the noncompete clause, which explicitly covers employees who participated in "marketing or sale" activities during their employment.

Carter's current position as senior product manager at Boston Scientific with "primary duty of overseeing the marketing of EP disposable products" directly conflicts with his contractual obligations. Both St. Jude and Boston Scientific are direct competitors in the EP disposable products market. The noncompete does not require that Carter's responsibilities be identical—it requires only that he not render services to a competitor "in connection with" any product "in which the Employee participated" during employment. ### III. ST. JUDE PROVED IRREPARABLE HARM THROUGH THE NATURE OF THE BREACH ITSELF

Respondent incorrectly argues that St. Jude failed to prove irreparable harm because it presented no evidence of disclosed trade secrets or explicit competitive harm. This argument misapprehends the applicable legal standard and ignores the inherent nature of the harm caused by Carter's breach.

Minnesota courts recognize that irreparable harm may be presumed in cases involving breaches of noncompete agreements with restrictive geographic and temporal scopes. In *Meyer v. McCarvel*, 416 N.W.2d 727, 729 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987), this Court acknowledged that injunctive relief is appropriate when an employee violates a reasonable restrictive covenant designed to protect the employer's legitimate business interests. The harm in such cases is inherently irreparable because monetary damages cannot compensate for the loss of exclusive market position and the erosion of customer relationships built over years of investment.

Here, the noncompete is reasonable—it is limited to one year and covers only competitors in the specific product categories in which Carter participated. The harm caused by Carter's immediate employment with a direct competitor marketing the exact same category of products is not merely speculative; it is the natural and probable consequence of the breach. St. Jude invested eight years in training and developing Carter's expertise, only to have him immediately apply that knowledge to benefit a competitor.

The district court erred in requiring St. Jude to present additional evidence of harm beyond the breach itself. When an employee violates a reasonable noncompete by immediately going to work for a direct competitor in the same product category, the harm is presumed. The court in *Grange Ins. Co. v. In the employment context, the employer's investment in developing employee expertise, combined with the employee's contractual commitment not to use that expertise for competitors, creates a unique harm that monetary damages cannot remedy.

Respondent's reliance on the absence of evidence regarding trade secret disclosure is misplaced. The noncompete clause protects against more than just trade secret misappropriation—it protects St. Jude's investment in employee development and its competitive market position. The contract expressly states that the restrictive covenant applies "whether or not" Carter would be "expected to require or involve Employee's using or disclosing confidential information." This demonstrates the parties' intent to protect against a broader range of competitive harm than mere trade secret theft.

### IV. THE CONTRACTUAL REMEDIES CLAUSE IS DISPOSITIVE OF THE IRREPARABLE HARM ISSUE

Respondent argues that the contractual provision stating that breach would cause "irreparable injury" is merely boilerplate language that cannot substitute for factual proof. The court must "look at the facts," but the facts here include the parties' express contractual agreement that breach would cause irreparable injury. This is not boilerplate language without meaning—it represents the parties' informed judgment about the nature of the harm that would result from breach.

Minnesota courts consistently enforce restrictive covenants when they are reasonable and when the employer has demonstrated a legitimate business interest. See *Minn. Stat. § 604.14**. The contractual remedies clause reflects the parties' understanding that the harm from breach would be of a character that injunctive relief is necessary. This understanding should be given appropriate weight in the court's analysis.

The district court erred in treating the contractual provision as irrelevant. While the court was correct that contractual language alone cannot create irreparable harm where none exists, the language here confirms that the nature of the harm—competitive injury from an employee's immediate departure to a competitor—is inherently unsuitable for monetary compensation. The clause represents the parties' recognition that the harm cannot be adequately measured or compensated through damages.

Respondent argues that St. Jude's waiver of monetary damages before trial constitutes a concession that legal remedies are adequate and thereby bars injunctive relief. This argument misreads the nature of St. Jude's waiver and the relationship between legal and equitable remedies.

St. Jude's decision not to seek monetary damages at trial was a strategic choice made in the context of seeking equitable relief. The waiver should not be interpreted as a concession that monetary damages could adequately compensate St. Jude for the harm caused by Carter's breach. Rather, it reflects St. Jude's judgment that injunctive relief—preventing the ongoing violation of the noncompete—is a more appropriate remedy than monetary compensation.

The basis for injunctive relief is the inadequacy of the legal remedy, not the complete unavailability of any legal remedy. In *Meyer*, 416 N.W.2d at 729, this Court recognized that injunctive relief may be appropriate when the legal remedy is inadequate, not merely when no legal remedy exists. Here, even if monetary damages could theoretically be calculated, the ongoing nature of the breach and the competitive harm caused by Carter's employment make injunctive relief a more complete and appropriate remedy.

Furthermore, the remedies provision in the employment contract expressly provides for both injunctive relief and damages. St. Jude's election to pursue one remedy does not constitute a waiver of the other. The contract contemplates that both remedies may be available, and St. Jude's choice to prioritize injunctive relief should not be construed as a concession that injunctive relief is inappropriate.

### VI. THE BALANCE OF EQUITIES FAVORS ST. JUDE

Respondent argues that the balance of equities weighs against injunctive relief because Carter has been employed at Boston Scientific for over a year and an injunction would deprive him of his livelihood. However, this argument ignores the countervailing equities favoring St. Jude and misapplies the standard for evaluating the balance of harms.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s judgment denying Appellant St. Jude Medical, Inc. (St. Jude) injunctive relief following a jury verdict finding that Respondent Heath Carter breached his employment contract. The district court denied St. Jude’s request for a permanent injunction, concluding that St. Jude failed to establish it had suffered irreparable harm and that St. Jude had waived its right to monetary damages. St. Jude appeals this denial, arguing that the breach of the noncompete agreement itself constitutes irreparable harm and that the contractual remedies clause entitles it to an injunction. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both parties.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The district court’s decision to deny a motion for permanent injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *Meyer v. McCarvel*, 416 N.W.2d 727, 729 (Minn. Ct. App. 1987). The district court’s legal conclusions are subject to de novo review. *Halla v. Norwest Capital Mgmt. & Trust Co.*, 661 N.W.2d 625, 629 (Minn. Ct. App. 2003).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. St. Jude Failed to Prove Irreparable Harm**

Injunctive relief is an extraordinary remedy requiring the movant to demonstrate that: (1) irreparable harm exists; (2) the balance of equities favors the movant; and (3) the public interest supports the injunction. *Grange Ins. Co. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 283 N.W.2d 514, 518 (Minn. 1979). St. Jude argues that the jury’s finding of breach establishes irreparable harm as a matter of law, relying on *Meyer v. McCarvel* to suggest a presumption of harm in noncompete cases. This argument is unavailing.

While the jury found that Carter breached his contract, a breach alone does not satisfy the requirement of irreparable harm. St. Jude was required to demonstrate that the harm caused by the breach could not be remedied by monetary damages. *Grange Ins. Co.*, 283 N.W.2d at 518. The district court found that St. Jude presented no evidence that Carter disclosed confidential information or that his new position posed a threat to St. Jude’s trade secrets. St. Jude’s assertion that the "nature of the breach" creates inherent harm is insufficient without factual proof of specific injury. The record shows that Carter’s role at Boston Scientific focused on EP disposable products, whereas his work at St. Jude had centered primarily on EP capital products, with only limited participation in research for one disposable product. Without evidence of overlap in responsibilities or a risk of disclosure, St. Jude cannot establish that it suffered irreparable harm.

**B. The Remedies Clause Does Not Relieve St. Jude of Its Burden**

St. Jude relies heavily on the employment contract’s remedies clause, which states that a breach would cause "irreparable injury" and entitle St. Jude to an injunction. St. Jude argues, citing *Davis v. Jointless Fire Co.*, 150 F. 771, 777 (2d Cir. 1906), that this clause should be given "substantial weight" and is dispositive. However, *Davis* explicitly holds that such contractual designations "are not conclusive; the court must look at the facts." *Davis*, 150 F. at 777. The district court properly required St. Jude to provide factual proof of irreparable harm rather than relying solely on the contract’s boilerplate language. Because St. Jude failed to provide such proof, the contract clause cannot sustain the injunction.

**C. Waiver of Monetary Damages Precludes Equitable Relief**

St. Jude’s decision to waive monetary damages before trial is fatal to its claim for equitable relief. The basis for injunctive relief is the inadequacy of legal remedies. *Meyer*, 416 N.W.2d at 729. By expressly waiving monetary damages, St. Jude effectively conceded that legal remedies were available to compensate for any harm. St. Jude argues that this waiver was a strategic choice and does not preclude equitable relief. However, a party cannot simultaneously waive legal remedies and claim that those same remedies are inadequate. This contradiction undermines St. Jude’s assertion that it faces irreparable harm. The district court correctly concluded that St. Jude’s waiver of monetary damages left it without a remedy to enforce, as it had abandoned the legal damages that might have compensated for the breach.

**D. The Balance of Equities Favors the Respondent**

Even if St. Jude had proven minimal harm, the balance of equities weighs against injunctive relief. Carter has been employed at Boston Scientific for over a year. An injunction prohibiting him from continuing his employment would deprive him of his livelihood, while St. Jude has demonstrated no actual harm. Minnesota law requires that restrictive covenants be reasonable to protect the employer's legitimate business interests. *Minn. Stat. § 604.14*. Given the lack of evidence regarding the disclosure of trade secrets or competitive harm, enforcing the noncompete via injunction at this stage would be unreasonable. The jury’s finding that Boston Scientific did not tortiously interfere with Carter’s contract further supports the conclusion that the equities favor Carter, as Boston Scientific acted in good faith regarding the distinction between Carter’s former and current responsibilities.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying injunctive relief. St. Jude failed to prove irreparable harm, a necessary prerequisite for such relief. The contractual remedies clause does not relieve St. Jude of this burden, and the waiver of monetary damages precludes a claim for equitable relief. The balance of equities favors the Respondent. Therefore, the district court’s judgment is affirmed.
