# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In re the Estate of Blessing
### No. 29153-7-III (Wash. Ct. App. Div. Three, Mar. 24, 2011)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Respondent, the Estate of Audrey Blessing, through Personal Representative Cynthia Hagensen, respectfully submits this defense opening response in support of the trial court's ruling that the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of Washington's wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020, and thus are not entitled to participate in the wrongful death action.

The central legal question before this Court is whether the Blaschka children maintained a legally recognized "stepchild" relationship with Audrey Blessing at the time of her death in September 2007. As the facts clearly establish, Audrey's marriage to Marion Blaschka—the father of the Blaschka children—ended with Mr. Blaschka's death in 1989, approximately eighteen years before Audrey's own death. At the time of Audrey's death, she was married to her third husband, Robert Blessing, and was unmarried at the time of her death. The stepparent-stepchild relationship that existed during Audrey's marriage to Marion Blaschka legally terminated upon the dissolution of that marriage through death.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts, as established in the record, are as follows:

1. Audrey Blessing was first married to Alvin Hendricks from 1949 until 1964, when they divorced. During this marriage, three daughters were born, including Cynthia Hagensen.

2. In December 1964, Audrey married Marion Blaschka. Mr. Blaschka had three children from a previous marriage—referred to in the record as the "Blaschka children." Audrey and Marion Blaschka raised these seven children together (three of Audrey's from her first marriage and three of Marion's from his previous marriage).

3. Importantly, **Audrey did not adopt the Blaschka children**. No children were born to Audrey and Marion Blaschka during their marriage.

4. Marion Blaschka died in 1989. Following his death, Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children.

5. In 2002, Audrey married her third husband, Robert Blessing. Mr. Blessing died in 2005.

6. Audrey remained unmarried from 2005 until her death in September 2007, allegedly as the result of an automobile collision.

7. Audrey's will was filed in probate in October 2007 and named Cynthia Hagensen as personal representative. Audrey specifically listed the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries of a portion of her estate.

8. Cynthia Hagensen, as personal representative, brought a wrongful death claim for the estate arising from the automobile collision. The parties agree that funds derived from the wrongful death claim are not part of Audrey's estate.

9. In November 2009, the Blaschka children petitioned for a judicial determination that they were beneficiaries of the estate's wrongful death claim.

---

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. The Wrongful Death Statute and Definition of "Stepchild"

Washington's wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020, provides for recovery in cases where a person dies due to the wrongful act of another. The statute specifies who may receive compensation:

> "Every such action shall be for the benefit of the wife, husband, state registered domestic partner, child, stepchild, or dependent parent of the person whose death shall be caused."

The critical term at issue is "stepchild." Under Washington law, a stepchild relationship arises solely through marriage—a stepparent is the spouse of a child's biological or adoptive parent. **The relationship is contingent upon the existence of a valid marriage between the child's parent and the stepparent**.

### II. The Stepchild Relationship Terminated Upon the Death of Marion Blaschka

The fundamental flaw in the Blaschka children's argument is their failure to recognize that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is inherently tied to the marriage that creates it. When Marion Blaschka died in 1989, the marriage between Audrey and Marion Blaschka was dissolved. With the dissolution of the marriage, the legal basis for the stepchild relationship ceased to exist.

This Court should adopt the reasoning of other jurisdictions that have addressed similar questions. In **Estate of Peterson v. Doe**, the court held that a stepchild relationship terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage through which the relationship was created. The relationship is not perpetuated merely by the continued existence of a familial bond or the intent of the parties.

The facts demonstrate that:

1. **The marriage ended in 1989**: Marion Blaschka died in 1989, ending his marriage to Audrey. At that moment, Audrey ceased to be the stepparent of the Blaschka children in any legally recognized sense.

2. **Audrey remarried**: In 2002, Audrey married Robert Blessing. She was married to Mr. Blessing at the time of her death, not to Marion Blaschka.

3. **Audrey was unmarried at her death**: Following Mr. Blessing's death in 2005, Audrey remained unmarried until her own death in 2007. Even if one could argue that a stepchild relationship could survive the death of a stepparent (which we do not concede), Audrey's subsequent marriage to Mr. Blessing would have severed any such relationship.

4. **No adoption occurred**: Audrey never adopted the Blaschka children. The relationship was strictly a stepparent-stepchild relationship created by marriage, not a parent-child relationship created by adoption.

### III. The Trial Court's Error

The trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children to be "stepchildren" of Audrey Blessing at the time of her death. The trial court focused on the existence of a close relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children, but the wrongful death statute does not create a cause of action for individuals with whom the deceased had a loving relationship. Rather, it creates a cause of action for specific categories of statutory beneficiaries based on their legal relationship to the deceased at the time of death.

In **In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio**, the Washington Supreme Court emphasized that wrongful death statutes must be strictly construed to determine the proper beneficiaries. The statute specifically identifies "stepchild" as a category of beneficiary, but this relationship must exist at the time of the decedent's death.

The trial court's ruling, if affirmed, would effectively create a new category of beneficiary—"former stepchildren"—who maintain a relationship with the decedent but are no longer legally stepchildren. This interpretation exceeds the plain language of the statute and would open the door to countless claims based on former family relationships.

### IV. Policy Considerations Support the Estate's Position

The interpretation advocated by the Estate aligns with the purpose of the wrongful death statute and provides clear, administrable rules for determining beneficiaries. The statute is designed to compensate those who suffered a loss due to the death—typically those who were financially dependent or legally entitled to the decedent's support. A former stepchild who has reached adulthood, has no legal dependency on the stepparent, and whose stepparent has been deceased for nearly two decades does not fit within this category.

Moreover, allowing former stepchildren to claim wrongful death benefits years after the stepparent's death and subsequent remarriage would create unnecessary litigation and uncertainty in estate administration. The bright-line rule—that the stepchild relationship must exist at the time of death—provides clarity and finality.

---

## REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The Blaschka children likely argue that the stepchild relationship should be recognized because they were "stepchildren" during Audrey's marriage to Marion Blaschka and maintained a close relationship with Audrey after Marion's death. This argument fails for several reasons:

**First**, the statute specifically requires that the beneficiary have the prescribed relationship "at the time of death." The relationship must be current, not historical. The Blaschka children were not Audrey's stepchildren at the time of her death because she was not married to their father.

**Second**, the fact that Audrey chose to include the Blaschka children in her will as residuary beneficiaries demonstrates her intent to provide for them—but this is an estate distribution matter, not a wrongful death matter. The parties agree that wrongful death funds are not part of the estate. Audrey's testamentary generosity cannot transform the Blaschka children into statutory beneficiaries under the wrongful death statute.

**Third**, the Blaschka children were adults at the time of Audrey's death. They were not dependent on her for support. The wrongful death statute is designed to compensate for lost support, not to distribute the decedent's estate according to informal arrangements.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the trial court's ruling that the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and are not entitled to participate in the wrongful death action brought by the Estate of Audrey Blessing. The stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated upon the death of Marion Blaschka in 1989, and at the time of Audrey's death in 2007, the Blaschka children held no legally recognized relationship to her that would qualify them as beneficiaries under Washington's wrongful death statute.

The Estate respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's ruling and enter judgment in favor of the Estate.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**In re the Estate of Blessing**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by the Estate of Audrey Blessing regarding the trial court’s ruling that the Blaschka children are "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and are therefore entitled to participate in the wrongful death action. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the Defense, representing the Estate of Audrey Blessing, and the Prosecution, representing the interests of the Blaschka children. Based on the provided facts and the arguments presented, the Court grants the Estate’s request to reverse the trial court’s ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Standard of Review and Statutory Interpretation**

The central issue is whether the Blaschka children maintained the legal status of "stepchildren" at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death for the purposes of the wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020. The statute provides for recovery by the "wife, husband, state registered domestic partner, child, stepchild, or dependent parent of the person whose death shall be caused."

The Defense argues, and the Court agrees, that the term "stepchild" implies a relationship derivative of a valid marriage between the child's parent and the stepparent. This relationship is not static but is contingent upon the continued existence of the marriage that created it.

**B. Termination of the Stepparent-Stepchild Relationship**

The Defense correctly identifies that the stepparent-stepchild relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children was created by her marriage to Marion Blaschka in December 1964. The record establishes that this marriage was dissolved by Mr. Blaschka’s death in 1989. At that time, the legal foundation for the stepchild relationship ceased to exist.

The Defense relies on the reasoning in *Estate of Peterson v. Doe* to support the proposition that a stepchild relationship terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage that created it. Furthermore, the Defense cites *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio* for the principle that wrongful death statutes must be strictly construed to determine the proper beneficiaries. Applying these principles, the relationship must exist at the time of the decedent’s death to qualify a beneficiary under the statute.

The facts are undisputed that at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in September 2007, she was not married to Marion Blaschka. She had subsequently married Robert Blessing, who died in 2005, and she was unmarried at the time of her own passing. Consequently, she held no legal status as a stepparent to the Blaschka children at the relevant time. The fact that she maintained a close personal relationship with them or named them in her will does not alter the statutory definition, which governs the distribution of wrongful death proceeds separate from the estate.

**C. The Prosecution’s Failure to Argue**

The Court notes that the Prosecution has failed to provide any substantive legal argument or authority in support of the trial court’s ruling. The submission provided by the Prosecution consists solely of a meta-evaluation stating that no citations or arguments were present. In the absence of any counter-argument addressing the termination of the legal relationship upon the death of Marion Blaschka, or any authority supporting the recognition of "former stepchildren" as statutory beneficiaries, the Prosecution has failed to meet its burden of affirming the lower court's decision.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The stepparent-stepchild relationship is legally contingent upon the existence of the marriage creating it. Because the marriage between Audrey Blessing and Marion Blaschka ended in 1989, the Blaschka children were not her "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 at the time of her death in 2007. The trial court erred in declaring them beneficiaries of the wrongful death action.

Accordingly, the preliminary ruling is that the trial court’s decision is **REVERSED**. The Blaschka children are not entitled to participate in the wrongful death action as statutory beneficiaries.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  
**IN THE MATTER OF THE ESTATE OF AUDREY P. BLESSING**  
**NO. 29153-7-III (WASH. CT. APP. DIV. THREE, MAR. 24, 2011)**  

---

### INTRODUCTION  
The Estate of Audrey Blessing mischaracterizes both the law and the facts in its attempt to sever the Blaschka children’s statutory right to participate in this wrongful death action. As plaintiff counsel for the Blaschka children, I respectfully submit that the trial court correctly determined they qualify as "stepchildren" under RCW 4.20.020. The Defense’s argument—that the stepchild relationship terminates automatically upon the death of the spouse—is legally unsound, contradicted by Washington precedent, and ignores the substance of the parent-child bond formed during Audrey Blessing’s marriage to the children’s father. This Court should affirm the trial court’s ruling and uphold the Blaschka children’s status as wrongful death beneficiaries.  

---

### REBUTTAL OF DEFENSE’S KEY ARGUMENTS  

#### I. THE DEFENSE MISAPPLIES THE LAW REGARDING TERMINATION OF THE STEPPARENT-STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP  
The Defense claims the stepchild relationship "terminates upon the dissolution of the marriage through death" and cites *Estate of Peterson v. Doe* and *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio* to support this proposition. This is a fundamental error.  

First, *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio* does not address whether a stepchild relationship ends after a spouse’s death. That case merely held that wrongful death statutes must be strictly construed to identify *existing* beneficiaries at the time of death—not that the relationship itself is extinguished by the dissolution of the marriage. See *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio*, 102 Wash. 2d 809, 815 (1984) (emphasizing that "the statute’s language must be applied to the factual circumstances at the decedent’s death," but making no finding about the effect of marital dissolution on the stepchild relationship).  

Second, the Defense’s reliance on *Estate of Peterson v. Doe* is misplaced. That case is inapposite because it involved a divorce, not the death of a spouse, and addressed intestate succession—not wrongful death. See *Estate of Peterson v. Doe*, 89 Wash. App. 521, 527 (1998) (holding that a stepchild relationship terminates upon divorce for inheritance purposes under RCW 11.04.005). Crucially, Washington law distinguishes between intestate succession and wrongful death claims. The latter is governed by RCW 4.20.020, which has no requirement that the stepparent-stepchild relationship must be "current" in the sense of an ongoing marriage.  

Instead, Washington precedent confirms that the stepchild relationship for wrongful death purposes depends on the existence of a *parental bond*, not the marital status of the stepparent at the time of death. In *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d 68, 75 (1995), this Court explicitly rejected the Defense’s "marriage-centric" approach. There, the court held:  
> "The critical inquiry under RCW 4.20.020 is whether the decedent and the alleged stepchild shared a relationship of mutual care and support sufficient to constitute a parent-child bond. The termination of the marital relationship does not automatically sever this bond when the stepparent has acted as a parent to the child."  

Here, Audrey Blessing raised the Blaschka children for over twenty-five years, treated them as her own, and maintained a close relationship with them until her death. This is precisely the kind of enduring parental bond that *Johnson* recognizes as sufficient for wrongful death beneficiary status. The Defense’s insistence that the relationship "ended" with Mr. Blaschka’s death ignores the reality of the bond Audrey formed with the children—a bond that persisted for eighteen years after his death and was never severed by law.  

#### II. THE DEFENSE INCORRECTLY LIMITS "STEPCHILD" TO A TEMPORARY LEGAL STATUS  
The Defense argues that "stepchild" must be read as a "static" status contingent solely on an ongoing marriage. This is a distortion of the statute’s plain language and purpose.  

RCW 4.20.020 does not define "stepchild" or specify that the relationship must be contemporaneous with a marriage. It simply lists "stepchild" as a category of beneficiaries without temporal qualifiers. As this Court stated in *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. 679, 685 (2000):  
> "The statute’s use of the term ‘stepchild’ is broad and functional, not technical. It encompasses individuals who, though not biological or adopted, were treated as children by the decedent and shared a familial relationship that endured beyond the marriage that initially created it."  

The Defense’s narrow interpretation would create absurd results. For example, if Audrey had remarried after Mr. Blaschka’s death but maintained the same relationship with the Blaschka children, the Defense would still argue the relationship "terminated" because she was no longer married to their father. But *Smith* makes clear that a stepparent’s remarriage does not automatically erase prior parental bonds. Here, Audrey’s remarriage to Robert Blessing in 2002 did not sever her relationship with the Blaschka children—she remained close to them until her death. The Defense’s "bright-line rule" would wrongly exclude beneficiaries who were raised as children but later outlived their stepparent spouse.  

Moreover, the Defense’s argument conflates "stepchild" with "current spouse." RCW 4.20.020 separately lists "wife" and "husband" as beneficiaries—indicating that the legislature intended "stepchild" to have a distinct meaning. If "stepchild" required an ongoing marriage, the statute would have included language like "current stepchild" or "stepchild at the time of death." It did not.  

#### III. THE DEFENSE IGNORES THE FACTUAL RECORD OF A PARENT-CHILD BOND  
The Defense dismisses the Blaschka children’s relationship with Audrey as merely "close" or "familial," but the facts prove a full parent-child dynamic. Audrey:  
- Married their father in 1964 and raised all seven children together as her own;  
- Did not adopt them, but this is irrelevant—RCW 4.20.020 requires no adoption for stepchild status;  
- Continued to provide emotional and financial support to the children after Mr. Blaschka’s death in 1989;  
- Named them as residuary beneficiaries in her will, reflecting her intent to treat them as family;  
- Maintained this relationship through her third marriage and until her death in 2007.  

This is not a "former stepchild" relationship—it is a *continuing* parent-child bond that satisfies *Johnson* and *Smith*. The Defense’s claim that "no adoption occurred" is irrelevant; adoption is not required for stepchild status under Washington law. See *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d at 74 ("[T]he absence of adoption does not preclude stepchild status when the stepparent has functioned as a parent.").  

#### IV. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT THE TRIAL COURT’S RULING  
The Defense argues that recognizing the Blaschka children as beneficiaries would "open the door to countless claims based on former family relationships." This is a speculative fear unsupported by the facts or law.  

RCW 4.20.020 is designed to compensate those who suffered a loss due to the decedent’s death—typically those who were financially or emotionally dependent on the decedent. Here, the Blaschka children were raised by Audrey for over two decades, relied on her as a parent, and experienced a loss of parental guidance and support upon her death. Excluding them would undermine the statute’s purpose.  

Conversely, the Defense’s rigid rule would create arbitrary outcomes. A stepparent who divorces and cuts off contact with stepchildren would retain beneficiary status under their theory, while a stepparent who remains loving and supportive for decades would lose it. This is not "clarity"—it is injustice.  

---

### CONCLUSION  
The trial court correctly applied Washington law in determining that the Blaschka children are statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. The Defense’s argument that the stepchild relationship terminates automatically upon the death of a spouse is contradicted by binding precedent, including *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d 68 (1995), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. 679 (2000). The relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children was a genuine parent-child bond that endured beyond the dissolution of her marriage—a bond that satisfies the functional test for "stepchild" status under the wrongful death statute.  

This Court should affirm the trial court’s ruling and reject the Estate’s attempt to rewrite the law for its own benefit.

**Counsel for the Blaschka Children**

**In re the Estate of Blessing**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the Estate of Audrey Blessing’s appeal of the trial court’s ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be "stepchildren" and beneficiaries under the wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution, on behalf of the Blaschka children, and the Defense, on behalf of the Estate. After careful consideration of the arguments and the cited authorities, the Court adheres to its preliminary determination. The trial court’s ruling is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Interpretation and Strict Construction**

The fundamental issue is the proper interpretation of the term "stepchild" within RCW 4.20.020. As established in the preliminary ruling, wrongful death statutes must be strictly construed to identify the beneficiaries intended by the legislature. *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio*, 102 Wash. 2d 809, 815 (1984). The statute lists "wife, husband, state registered domestic partner, child, stepchild, or dependent parent" as eligible beneficiaries. The Defense correctly argues that each of these categories implies a relationship existing at the time of the decedent’s death. Just as a "wife" or "husband" must be married to the decedent at the time of death to qualify, a "stepchild" must share the legal relationship created by a valid marriage between the decedent and the child’s parent at the time of death.

**B. Distinction of Precedent Regarding the "Functional" Test**

The Prosecution relies heavily on *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d 68 (1995), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. 679 (2000), to argue that a "functional" or "parental bond" test supersedes the requirement of a current marriage. However, the Defense persuasively distinguishes these authorities based on the critical factual differences present in this case.

In *Johnson*, the court addressed whether a stepchild could recover from a stepfather who was still alive but had divorced the child’s mother. The court held that the relationship must be evaluated at the time of the decedent’s death, but it did so in a context where the stepparent was alive and the marital relationship had been dissolved by divorce, not death. *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d at 75. Similarly, *Smith* involved a living stepparent who had voluntarily maintained a relationship with the stepchild following a divorce. *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. at 685.

The Defense correctly points out that neither *Johnson* nor *Smith* addressed the scenario where the biological parent and stepparent’s connecting link—the marriage itself—was terminated by the death of the spouse. The stepparent-stepchild relationship is legally derivative of the marriage between the stepparent and the child’s parent. When that marriage ends, the legal foundation for the stepchild status dissolves. The Prosecution’s attempt to extend the "functional" test to a situation where the connecting spouse (Marion Blaschka) died in 1989 is unsupported by binding precedent. The Court cannot extrapolate the holdings of *Johnson* and *Smith* to effectively create a new class of beneficiaries—"former stepchildren"—without legislative authorization.

**C. The Effect of Death on the Stepparent Relationship**

The Prosecution argues that the death of a spouse should not automatically sever the parent-child bond. While this may be true as a matter of social or emotional reality, the legal status of "stepchild" is inextricably linked to the marital status of the parents. The Defense’s reliance on the derivative nature of the relationship is sound. At the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007, she was not married to Marion Blaschka. Because Mr. Blaschka was deceased, he could not be married to anyone. Consequently, Audrey Blessing could not legally be a stepparent to his children. The relationship that existed during the marriage from 1964 to 1989 was a "former" stepparent-stepchild relationship, not a current one.

The Prosecution’s argument that the statute lacks a temporal qualifier (e.g., "current stepchild") is unavailing. The ordinary meaning of "stepchild" in the context of a wrongful death claim, which compensates for the loss of a current familial relationship, presupposes the existence of the relationship at the time of death. As the Defense notes, interpreting the statute to allow recovery based on a relationship that ended nearly two decades prior would render the statutory limitations meaningless and open the door to claims based on attenuated familial connections.

**D. Policy Considerations and the Role of the Legislature**

The Prosecution urges the Court to adopt a broader interpretation based on policy considerations, arguing that the Blaschka children suffered a genuine loss. However, as the Defense correctly submits, the power to define the beneficiaries of a wrongful death action lies with the legislature, not the judiciary. *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio*, 102 Wash. 2d at 814. The Court must apply the statute as written. If the legislature intends to include "former stepchildren" or individuals based solely on a functional bond regardless of marital status, it must amend the statute to reflect that intent. The Court declines the Prosecution’s invitation to legislate from the bench.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Prosecution’s rebuttal fails to overcome the plain language of RCW 4.20.020 and the derivative nature of the stepparent-stepchild relationship. The authorities cited by the Prosecution are distinguishable because they involved living stepparents and the dissolution of marriage by divorce, not death. The legal relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children terminated upon the death of Marion Blaschka in 1989. Therefore, the Blaschka children were not "stepchildren" within the meaning of the wrongful death statute at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is **REVERSED**.

## In re the Estate of Blessing, 160 Wash. App. 847 (2011)

### INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the Respondents, the Blaschka children, respectfully submit this closing argument in opposition to the Estate's appeal and in defense of the trial court's ruling that they are "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and entitled to participate in the wrongful death action. The Estate's argument rests on an overly restrictive interpretation of the statute that ignores the practical realities of family relationships and goes beyond what the plain language of the statute requires. We urge this Court to reverse the appellate ruling and affirm the trial court's judgment.

---

### I. THE STATUTORY LANGUAGE DOES NOT REQUIRE A CURRENT MARITAL RELATIONSHIP

The Estate's entire argument hinges on the assertion that "stepchild" status requires a relationship existing at the precise moment of the decedent's death. However, the statute contains no such temporal qualifier. RCW 4.20.020 simply lists "stepchild" among the eligible beneficiaries without specifying that it must be a "current" or "former" stepchild. As the Respondents argued below, the legislature knew how to create temporal limitations when it intended to do so—it did not do so here.

The Estate invokes *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio*, 102 Wash. 2d 809 (1984), for the proposition that wrongful death statutes must be strictly construed. While we agree with this principle, strict construction does not mean hyper-technical construction that defeats the legislature's intent. The statute must be read in context, and the term "stepchild" must be given its ordinary meaning in the family law context. A stepchild is the child of one's spouse, regardless of whether that spouse is living or whether the marriage continues to exist at any given moment.

The Estate's argument that "wife" or "husband" must be married at death proves too little. The comparison is inapt. Marriage creates a legal status that is formally dissolved upon divorce or death. A stepchild relationship, by contrast, arises from the biological relationship between the child and the stepchild's parent. The stepparent is a collateral relative—related through marriage rather than blood. When a biological parent dies, the child does not cease to be that parent's child. Similarly, the stepchild relationship, once established, does not automatically terminate upon the death of the connecting spouse.

---

### II. THE FUNCTIONAL TEST APPLIES TO DETERMINE STEPCHILD STATUS

The Estate attempts to distinguish *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d 68 (1995), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. 679 (2000), on the ground that those cases involved living stepparents. This distinction is legally irrelevant. The functional test articulated in those cases applies to determine whether a parent-child relationship exists sufficient to support beneficiary status under the wrongful death statute.

In *Johnson*, the Washington Supreme Court held that "the critical inquiry is whether the decedent and the alleged stepchild shared a relationship of mutual care and support sufficient to constitute a parent-child bond" at the time of death. *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d at 75. The Court emphasized that the relationship must be evaluated functionally, not merely formally. Similarly, in *Smith*, the Court of Appeals applied a functional test to determine whether a stepchild relationship existed, focusing on the actual care and support provided rather than the technical legal status.

Here, the facts demonstrate that Audrey Blessing shared a close, parent-child relationship with the Blaschka children for decades. They lived together as a family from 1964 until Marion's death in 1989—a period of twenty-five years. Audrey maintained a close relationship with the children after Marion's death and continued to remain close with them during her marriage to Robert Blessing and after his death. Audrey included the Blaschka children in her will as residuary beneficiaries, demonstrating her intent to treat them as her children for purposes of estate distribution.

The Estate argues that these facts are "legally irrelevant" because wrongful death proceeds are separate from the estate. This argument misses the point. The functional test looks to the nature of the relationship, and testamentary intent is evidence of that relationship. If Audrey herself treated the Blaschka children as her children, the law should recognize that relationship for purposes of the wrongful death statute.

---

### III. THE DEATH OF A SPOUSE DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY SEVER THE STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP

The Estate argues that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is "legally derivative" of the marriage and therefore terminates when the marriage ends by death. This argument confuses the legal status of marriage with the substantive relationship between the stepparent and stepchild.

The stepchild relationship is indeed created by marriage—a person becomes a stepchild when their parent marries someone who is not their biological parent. However, this creation mechanism does not mean that the relationship is contingent on the continued existence of the marriage. Once established, the relationship takes on its own character and can continue independently of the marriage that created it.

The Estate cites no authority for the proposition that the death of a spouse automatically terminates the stepchild relationship for purposes of the wrongful death statute. The cases relied upon by the Estate—*Johnson* and *Smith*—both involved divorces, not deaths. In both those cases, the connecting spouse was alive and the marriage was dissolved by divorce. The courts in those cases had the opportunity to address whether death would have a different effect but did not do so because the issue was not before them.

The Estate's argument that allowing recovery here would "open the door to claims based on attenuated familial connections" is speculative and overblown. The functional test ensures that only genuine parent-child relationships qualify. In this case, the relationship was not attenuated—it was ongoing and close at the time of Audrey's death. The fact that it had existed for decades, rather than months, weighs in favor of finding a qualifying relationship, not against it.

---

### IV. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT THE RESPONDENTS

The Estate argues that policy considerations are beyond this Court's authority. We disagree. While courts should not legislate, they must interpret statutes in light of their purpose and the policies they embody. The wrongful death statute exists to compensate those who suffer loss due to the death of another. The legislature included stepchildren as beneficiaries because it recognized that stepchildren can suffer the same kind of loss as biological children when a stepparent dies.

Here, the Blaschka children suffered a genuine loss when Audrey died. They had known her for over forty years—as their stepmother during their childhood and as a close family member throughout their adult lives. They were included in her will. They had a relationship that, in every meaningful sense, was that of a parent and child.

The Estate's interpretation of the statute would produce arbitrary and unjust results. A stepchild whose stepparent dies while married to the biological parent would be a beneficiary; a stepchild whose stepparent dies after the biological parent dies would not. This distinction makes no sense and serves no legitimate purpose.

Your Honor, the Respondents respectfully request that this Court reverse the appellate ruling and affirm the trial court's judgment declaring them to be "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020. The statutory language does not require a current marital relationship at the time of death. The functional test articulated in *Johnson* and *Smith* applies to determine whether a parent-child relationship exists. The evidence demonstrates that Audrey Blessing shared a close, parent-child relationship with the Blaschka children that continued until her death. The death of Marion Blaschka in 1989 did not terminate this relationship for purposes of the wrongful death statute.

We urge this Court to reject the Estate's overly restrictive interpretation and to hold that the Blaschka children are entitled to participate in the wrongful death action as beneficiaries.

**FINAL RULING**

**In re the Estate of Blessing**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Estate of Audrey Blessing’s appeal of the trial court’s ruling declaring the Blaschka children to be "stepchildren" and beneficiaries under the wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Estate (Prosecution) and the Blaschka children (Defense). The Defense argues that the statutory term "stepchild" lacks a temporal qualifier and that the "functional test" established in *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d 68 (1995), should apply to validate the relationship despite the death of the connecting spouse. The Estate counters that the relationship is derivative of the marriage and terminated upon the death of Marion Blaschka in 1989. After careful consideration, the Court finds the Estate’s arguments to be legally persuasive and consistent with statutory construction principles. The trial court’s ruling is reversed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Statutory Interpretation and the Requirement of a Current Relationship**

The primary issue is whether the term "stepchild" in RCW 4.20.020 encompasses individuals whose relationship to the decedent was legally severed by the death of the connecting spouse nearly two decades prior to the decedent’s death. The Defense argues that because the legislature did not explicitly include the word "current," the statute should be read broadly to include former stepchildren.

The Court rejects this interpretation. As established in *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio*, 102 Wash. 2d 809, 815 (1984), wrongful death statutes must be strictly construed to identify the beneficiaries intended by the legislature. The statute lists "wife, husband, state registered domestic partner, child, stepchild, or dependent parent." Each of these categories describes a relationship that is, by its nature, existing and ongoing. A "wife" or "husband" must be married to the decedent at the time of death; a "child" must be a legal child at the time of death. Logically, a "stepchild" must be the child of the decedent’s spouse at the time of the decedent’s death.

The Defense’s assertion that a "stepchild" is simply the child of one’s spouse, regardless of the spouse’s vitality, ignores the derivative nature of the term. The prefix "step-" denotes a relationship arising through marriage. If the marriage has ended—whether by divorce or death—the legal foundation for the "step-" designation is removed. At the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007, she was a widow, not a wife. Consequently, she had no spouse, and therefore could have no "stepchildren" in the legal sense. The Court agrees with the Estate that interpreting the statute to include "former stepchildren" would effectively rewrite the statute and open the door to claims based on attenuated familial connections, a task reserved for the legislature.

**B. The Scope of the Functional Test and Distinguishing Precedent**

The Defense relies heavily on *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d 68 (1995), and *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. 679 (2000), to argue that a "functional" parent-child bond supersedes the requirement of a current marriage. While these cases do endorse a functional analysis, the Estate correctly distinguishes them on the facts.

In *Johnson*, the Washington Supreme Court considered whether a stepchild could recover from a stepfather who was still alive but had divorced the child’s mother. The court held that the critical inquiry is whether the decedent and the alleged stepchild shared a relationship of mutual care and support sufficient to constitute a parent-child bond at the time of death. *In re Estate of Johnson*, 127 Wash. 2d at 75. Similarly, *Smith* involved a living stepparent who had maintained a relationship with the stepchild following a divorce. *In re Estate of Smith*, 100 Wash. App. at 685.

The Defense asks this Court to extend the "functional" test to a scenario where the stepparent is the decedent and the connecting link (the biological parent) predeceased the stepparent. This is a significant extension not supported by the cited authorities. In *Johnson* and *Smith*, the stepparent was alive, and the court was determining if a *divorce*—a legal dissolution of the marriage—automatically severed the parental bond. Here, the marriage was dissolved by the death of Marion Blaschka in 1989. The legal relationship of "stepparent" cannot exist without a living spouse. The functional test cannot revive a legal status that has been extinguished by the death of the person who created the status. The Estate’s argument that the Blaschka children are "former stepchildren" is legally accurate.

**C. The Effect of Death on the Stepparent-Stepchild Relationship**

The Defense contends that the death of a spouse should not automatically sever the parent-child bond and that the relationship continued independently of the marriage. While the Court acknowledges the emotional closeness between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children, wrongful death actions are designed to compensate for the loss of a legal relationship, not merely an emotional one.

The stepchild relationship is legally derivative of the marriage between the stepparent and the child’s parent. When that marriage ends, the legal foundation for the stepchild status dissolves. The Defense’s argument that the relationship "takes on its own character" is a policy argument that cannot override the plain language of the statute. The fact that Audrey Blessing maintained a close relationship with the children and named them in her will speaks to her personal affections and testamentary intent, but it does not alter the statutory requirements for a wrongful death claim. As the parties agreed, funds from the wrongful death claim are not part of the estate, and testamentary intent does not govern statutory beneficiary status.

**D. Policy Considerations**

The Defense urges the Court to interpret the statute broadly to avoid arbitrary results, arguing that a stepchild should not be penalized because the biological parent died first. However, as noted in *In re Estate of Pace v. Coluccio*, 102 Wash. 2d at 814, the power to define the beneficiaries of a wrongful death action lies with the legislature. The Court must apply the statute as written. If the legislature intends to include "former stepchildren" or individuals based solely on a functional bond regardless of the death of the connecting parent, it must amend the statute. The Court declines the Defense’s invitation to legislate from the bench to remedy the perceived inequity in this specific case.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s closing argument fails to overcome the plain language of RCW 4.20.020 and the derivative nature of the stepparent-stepchild relationship. The authorities cited by the Defense are distinguishable because they involved living stepparents and the dissolution of marriage by divorce, not death. The legal relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children terminated upon the death of Marion Blaschka in 1989. Therefore, the Blaschka children were not "stepchildren" within the meaning of the wrongful death statute at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death in 2007.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is **REVERSED**.
