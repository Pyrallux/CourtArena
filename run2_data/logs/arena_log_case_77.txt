### ARGUMENT

## I. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY GRANTED THE EXCEPTION OF NO RIGHT OF ACTION

### A. J.M.Y. Lacks Standing to Pursue Reimbursement Without Having Disavowed Paternity

Under Louisiana law, a child born during marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband. *See* La. Civ. Code art. 184. This presumption is among the strongest in family law, reflecting the state's public policy interest in protecting the stability of family relationships and the child's legal identity. *See Succession of Brown*, 388 So.2d 1131, 1133 (La. 1980) (explaining that the presumption of paternity is founded on public policy and the best interests of the child).

Critically, this presumption can only be rebutted through a disavowal action filed pursuant to Civil Code Article 184. *See* La. Civ. Code art. 189 (establishing the exclusive procedures for disavowing paternity). A presumed father who fails to timely institute such an action is bound by the presumption, with all its legal consequences—including the obligation to provide support.

In *Dugas v. Dugas*, 462 So.2d 1275 (La. 1985), this Court addressed precisely the situation presented here. In *Dugas*, the husband had paid child support for a child born during the marriage but later discovered that he was not the biological father. He sought reimbursement from the biological father without having filed a disavowal action. This Court held that "the presumption of paternity may only be overcome by a disavowal proceeding, and a husband who fails to institute such an action cannot later seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." *Dugas*, 462 So.2d at 1277.

The *Dugas* Court emphasized that allowing reimbursement without disavowal would "undermine the public policy of protecting children from the uncertainties of paternity disputes" and "disturb the stability of family units." *Id.* This reasoning applies with equal force to the present case.

Here, J.M.Y. has never filed a disavowal action. He has never sought to legally disavow the child or to challenge the presumption of paternity through the proper statutory channels. Rather, he attempts to circumvent the disavowal process entirely while simultaneously accepting its benefits—he seeks to retain the legal status of father (and presumably the emotional benefits of the parent-child relationship) while shifting the financial burden to another.

Louisiana law provides no remedy for such an end-run around the statutory scheme. J.M.Y. lacks standing to pursue reimbursement because he has not complied with the prerequisites established by law. The trial court correctly granted the exception of no right of action.

### B. The Presumption of Paternity Remains Conclusive Against J.M.Y

The presumption of paternity is not merely a procedural hurdle—it is a substantive legal determination that fixes the father-child relationship for all legal purposes. *See* La. Civ. Code art. 184; *see also* La. Civ. Code art. 189 (establishing time limitations for bringing disavowal actions).

A presumed father in J.M.Y.'s position bears the responsibility of support precisely because the law recognizes him as the legal father. He cannot selectively accept the legal benefits of paternity while rejecting its responsibilities. *See* *In re Succession of Robinson*, 983 So.2d 1052, 1061 (La. App. 2 Cir. 2008) (explaining that the obligation of support flows from the legal relationship of paternity, not biological fact alone).

The respondents acknowledge that biological paternity may differ from legal paternity. However, Louisiana has established a comprehensive statutory scheme that addresses this distinction. A man who believes he is not the biological father may bring a disavowal action; a man who is found to be the biological father of a child born to a married woman may bring an action to establish paternity. *See* La. Civ. Code arts. 184, 189, 191-195. But a presumed father who fails to utilize these procedures cannot later reopen the question of paternity to recover payments he made in fulfillment of his legal obligation.

The trial court correctly applied these principles. J.M.Y. has no right of action because Louisiana law provides no mechanism for a presumed father to recover support payments from another without first having disavowed the child.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY GRANTED THE EXCEPTION OF NO CAUSE OF ACTION

### A. J.M.Y.'s Petition Fails to State a Claim Upon Which Relief Can Be Granted

Beyond the question of standing, the appellant['s] petition fails to state a cause of action because Louisiana law simply does not provide a remedy for the relief sought. The appellant seeks reimbursement for child support payments made pursuant to a court order while he was the presumed legal father. No Louisiana statute or jurisprudence authorizes such reimbursement under these circumstances.

Civil Code Article 227, upon the appellant relies, is inapplicable. That article provides that "[a] spouse who is not the biological or adoptive parent of a child may not be held responsible for the support of the child unless the spouse has acknowledged the child as his or her own." *See* La. Civ. Code art. 227. This provision addresses the *obligation* of support—it clarifies that a spouse who is not a biological or adoptive parent does not automatically bear support obligations.

However, Article 227 says nothing about reimbursement. It creates no right to recover payments made in satisfaction of a support obligation. To the contrary, by its terms, the article assumes that the legal father (whether biological or presumed) bears the support obligation. The appellant, as the legal father by virtue of the presumption, assumed the support obligation under Article 227. The article creates no reciprocal right to reimbursement.

The appellant['s] attempt to transform Article 227 into a reimbursement statute finds no support in the text of the law or the jurisprudence interpreting it. The article is designed to protect non-parent spouses from automatic support obligations—it does not provide a mechanism for presumed fathers to shift their responsibilities to others.

### B. The Respondents Have No Obligation to Reimburse J.M.Y

The respondents' obligations, if any, run to the child—not to the appellant. Under Louisiana law, the obligation of support is owed to the child, not to another parent who has discharged all or part of that obligation. *See* La. Civ. Code arts. 227-229.

A.F.Y., as the mother, bears her own obligation of support. *See* La. Civ. Code art. 227 (providing that parents are obligated to support their children). However, this obligation is independent of—and does not create a duty to reimburse—a presumed father who has paid support. The appellant seeks to impose a novel obligation on A.F.Y. that Louisiana law does not create.

Similarly, R.R., if he is the biological father, may owe a support obligation to the child. *See* La. Civ. Code art. 228 (providing that biological fathers owe support to their children). However, this obligation runs to the child, not to a presumed father who has already paid support. The appellant seeks to stand in the shoes of the child to collect reimbursement—a role for which Louisiana law provides no authorization.

The trial court correctly concluded that the appellant['s] petition states no cause of action because Louisiana law provides no legal basis for the relief sought.

## III. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT THE TRIAL COURT'S JUDGMENT

This expectation serves the child's best interests and promotes family stability.

If this Court were to allow presumed fathers to pay support for years—accepting the legal benefits of the father-child relationship—and then recover reimbursement from biological fathers, it would create chaos in family law. Biological fathers would face potential liability for support to children with whom they have no legal relationship. Children would face uncertainty about their legal fathers and support obligations. The integrity of marriage as the foundation of family law would be undermined.

The appellant['s] position would also allow presumed fathers to game the system—enjoying the emotional benefits of fatherhood while shifting the financial burden to others—without ever having formally disavowed the child. Louisiana law properly closes this loophole by requiring that paternity be challenged through the statutory disavowal process before any reimbursement may be sought.

This Court should affirm the trial court's judgment to preserve these important policy interests.

The central issue on appeal is whether a presumed father, who has not initiated a disavowal action, maintains a right of action to seek reimbursement of child support payments from the biological father or the mother. The arguments presented establish that Louisiana law provides no such remedy.

The arguments correctly assert that this presumption can only be rebutted through a disavowal action filed pursuant to Civil Code Article 189.

The controlling precedent on this issue is *Dugas v. Dugas*, 462 So.2d 1275 (La. 1985). In *Dugas*, the Louisiana Supreme Court held that "the presumption of paternity may only be overcome by a disavowal proceeding, and a husband who fails to institute such an action cannot later seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." *Dugas*, 462 So.2d at 1277. The Court further emphasized that allowing reimbursement without disavowal would undermine public policy by disturbing the stability of family units and exposing children to the uncertainties of paternity disputes.

The facts provided indicate that J.M.Y. was married to A.F.Y. at the time of the child's birth in 1994, establishing the presumption of paternity. ## II. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS REGARDING THE PRESUMPTION OF PATERNITY

The plaintiff appears to argue that he should be permitted to challenge paternity and seek reimbursement despite never filing a disavowal action. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

### A. The Presumption of Paternity Is Conclusive Without Disavowal

Under Louisiana Civil Code Article 184, "A child born or conceived during the marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband." This presumption is not merely a procedural mechanism but a substantive legal determination rooted in profound public policy considerations. As this Court recognized in *Succession of Brown*, 388 So.2d 1131, 1133 (La. 1980), the presumption exists to protect the stability of family relationships and the best interests of children.

The Louisiana Supreme Court in *Dugas v. Dugas*, 462 So.2d 1275, 1277 (La. 1985), held unequivocally that "the presumption of paternity may only be overcome by a disavowal proceeding, and a husband who fails to institute such an action cannot later seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." The Court emphasized that permitting reimbursement without disavowal would undermine public policy by disturbing the stability of family units and exposing children to the uncertainties of paternity disputes.

The plaintiff has not disputed that he was married to A.F.Y. at the time the child was born in 1994, thereby triggering the presumption under Article 184. He has further not disputed that he never filed a disavowal action pursuant to Article 189. Under *Dugas*, these undisputed facts are fatal to his claims.

### B. The Plaintiff Cannot Circumvent the Statutory Scheme

The plaintiff attempts to characterize his action as something other than a challenge to paternity—suggesting that he merely seeks reimbursement without formally disavowing the child. This attempt must fail. The relief sought by the plaintiff necessarily depends upon a determination that he is not the biological father of the child. He cannot obtain the economic benefit of disavowal (reimbursement of support payments) without undergoing the formal disavowal process.

Louisiana law provides a single, comprehensive statutory mechanism for challenging paternity: the disavowal action under Civil Code Article 189. This scheme reflects a deliberate legislative choice to centralize paternity disputes within a structured procedural framework that protects all parties, particularly the child. The plaintiff cannot circumvent this scheme by characterize his claim as one for "reimbursement" rather than "disavowal."

In *In re Succession of Robinson*, 983 So.2d 1052, 1061 (La. App. 2 Cir. 2008), the court held that a presumed father who failed to timely file a disavowal action was bound by the presumption and could not later assert claims against the alleged biological father. This Court should apply the same reasoning here.

This article addresses when a support obligation arises—it clarifies that non-biological spouses are not automatically liable for support absent acknowledgment.

The article says nothing about reimbursement. It creates no right for a spouse who has paid support to recover those payments from third parties. ### I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The Appellant, J.M.Y., seeks to reverse the trial court's dismissal of his action wherein he attempted to recover reimbursement for child support payments made over a period of approximately fourteen years. The Appellant and A.F.Y. were married at the time their son was born in 1994. Following their divorce, the Appellant was ordered to pay child support. The defense maintains that the trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The Appellant's claims are legally barred because he failed to challenge the presumption of paternity through the proper statutory channels, and Louisiana law provides no basis for the reimbursement remedy he seeks.

---

### II. THE APPELLANT CANNOT CIRCUMVENT THE STATUTORY DISAVOWAL SCHEME

The central principle underlying Louisiana's paternity framework is that a child born during marriage is conclusively presumed to be the child of the husband. La. Civ. Code art. 184. This presumption is among the strongest in family law and serves the fundamental purpose of protecting the stability of family units and the best interests of children. *Succession of Brown*, 388 So.2d 1131, 1133 (La. 1980).

The Louisiana Supreme Court has unequivocally held that this presumption may only be overcome through a formal disavowal proceeding instituted pursuant to La. Civ. Code art. 189. *Dugas v. Dugas*, 462 So.2d 1275, 1277 (La. 1985). In *Dugas*, the Court specifically addressed the precise scenario presented here: a husband who has paid child support for a child born during the marriage and who seeks reimbursement from the alleged biological father without having filed a disavowal action. The Court held that "the presumption of paternity may only be overcome by a disavowal proceeding, and a husband who fails to institute such an action cannot later seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." *Id.* at 1277.

The defense submits that the Appellant's attempt to characterize his action as a claim for "reimbursement" rather than a challenge to paternity must fail. The relief sought by the Appellant necessarily depends upon a determination that he is not the biological father of the child. He cannot obtain the economic benefit of disavowal—specifically, the reimbursement of support payments—without undergoing the formal disavowal process mandated by Civil Code Article 189. To allow such a claim would permit a party to circumvent the comprehensive statutory framework established by the legislature. *In re Succession of Robinson*, 983 So.2d 1052, 1061 (La. App. 2 Cir. 2008).

The facts establish that the Appellant never filed a disavowal action. He paid child support for approximately fourteen years without challenging paternity. Under *Dugas*, this failure renders the presumption of paternity conclusive. The Appellant is bound by the legal obligations of fatherhood, including the duty to support the child.

---

### III. CIVIL CODE ARTICLE 227 DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE RELIEF SOUGHT

The Appellant reliance upon La. Civ. Code art. 227 is misplaced. That article provides: "A spouse who is not the biological or adoptive parent of a child may not be held responsible for the support of the child unless the spouse has acknowledged the child as his or her own." La. Civ. Code art. 227.

The defense submits that Article 227 addresses the origin of the support obligation, not the right to reimbursement. This provision clarifies when a non-biological spouse becomes liable for support. It is silent regarding any right to recover support payments from third parties. The obligation of support under Louisiana law runs to the child, not to the other parent. See La. Civ. Code arts. 227-229.

The Appellant cannot "stand in the shoes" of the child to seek reimbursement from R.R. or A.F.Y. for payments he was legally obligated to make as the presumed father. No statute or jurisprudence authorizes the relief sought by the Appellant, and interpreting Article 227 to create such a right would contradict the exclusive disavowal procedures outlined in Articles 184 and 189.

---

### IV. THE APPELLANT LACKS STANDING AND A RIGHT OF ACTION

The defense further submits that the Appellant lacks standing to pursue these claims against R.R. and A.F.Y. R.R., as the alleged biological father, has no legal relationship to the child absent a successful disavowal and acknowledgment. Under the presumption of Article 184, the Appellant remains the legal father. Therefore, R.R. owes no duty of support to the child and certainly owes no duty of reimbursement to the Appellant.

Similarly, the Appellant has no right of action against A.F.Y. for reimbursement of past payments. Those payments were made in fulfillment of his own legal obligation as the presumed father. The Appellant cannot recover funds that he was legally obligated to pay.

---

### V. PUBLIC POLICY DEMANDS DISMISSAL OF THIS ACTION

Public policy strongly supports the dismissal of this action. As noted in *Dugas*, permitting a presumed father to "accept the benefits of paternity while shifting the financial burden" would "disturb the stability of family units" and expose children to the "uncertainties of paternity disputes." *Dugas*, 462 So.2d at 1277.

The Appellant paid child support for approximately fourteen years without challenging paternity. Allowing reimbursement now would upset the child's settled expectations and undermine the integrity of the family structure that the presumption of paternity is designed to protect. The child has a right to rely on the stability of his legal family relationships, and the Appellant's delayed attempt to relitigate paternity through a reimbursement claim threatens that stability.

The defense submits that the Appellant's delay in bringing this action further supports dismissal. Having accepted the benefits and responsibilities of fatherhood for over a decade, the Appellant should not be permitted to now shift the financial burden to another party. Such a result would reward inaction and punish the child for the Appellant's failure to timely pursue his legal remedies.

**FINAL RULING**

**INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by J.M.Y., seeking to reverse the trial court’s judgment granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing his suit against R.R. and A.F.Y. The appellant seeks reimbursement for past child support payments and contribution for future payments, predicated on the claim that R.R. is the biological father of the child born during the marriage of J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. These arguments uniformly reinforce the conclusion that the appellant’s claims are legally barred due to his failure to challenge the presumption of paternity through the proper statutory channels.

**ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether a presumed father, who has not initiated a disavowal action, maintains a right of action to seek reimbursement of child support payments from the biological father or the mother. The closing arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense establish that Louisiana law provides no such remedy and that the appellant’s attempt to recover funds constitutes an impermissible circumvention of the statutory paternity scheme.

**I. The Presumption of Paternity Cannot Be Circumvented by a Reimbursement Claim**

Under Louisiana law, a child born or conceived during the marriage is presumed to be the child of the husband. La. Civ. Code art. 184. This presumption is "among the strongest in family law" and is designed to protect the stability of family units and the best interests of the child. *Succession of Brown*, 388 So.2d 1131, 1133 (La. 1980). Both the prosecution and the defense correctly identify the controlling precedent, *Dugas v. Dugas*, 462 So.2d 1275 (La. 1985), which holds that "the presumption of paternity may only be overcome by a disavowal proceeding, and a husband who fails to institute such an action cannot later seek reimbursement for child support payments from the biological father." *Dugas*, 462 So.2d at 1277.

The prosecution argues persuasively that the appellant attempts to characterize his action as a claim for reimbursement rather than a challenge to paternity. The defense echoes this sentiment, articulating that the relief sought by the appellant necessarily depends upon a determination that he is not the biological father. The appellant cannot obtain the economic benefit of disavowal—specifically, the reimbursement of support payments—without undergoing the formal disavowal process mandated by Civil Code Article 189. To allow such a claim would permit a party to circumvent the comprehensive statutory framework established by the legislature. *In re Succession of Robinson*, 983 So.2d 1052, 1061 (La. App. 2 Cir. 2008). Because J.M.Y. never filed a disavowal action, the presumption of paternity remains conclusive, and he is bound by the legal obligations of fatherhood, including the duty to support.

**II. Civil Code Article 227 Does Not Create a Right of Reimbursement**

The appellant relies on Civil Code Article 227 to support his claim for reimbursement. Both the prosecution and the defense correctly identify that this reliance is misplaced. Article 227 provides that a spouse who is not the biological or adoptive parent "may not be held responsible for the support of the child unless the spouse has acknowledged the child as his or her own." La. Civ. Code art. 227.

As argued by the defense, this article addresses the *origin* of the support obligation; it clarifies when a non-biological spouse becomes liable for support. It is silent regarding any right to *recover* support payments from third parties. The prosecution correctly notes that the obligation of support under Louisiana law runs to the child, not to the other parent. *See* La. Civ. Code arts. 227-229. Consequently, J.M.Y. cannot "stand in the shoes" of the child to seek reimbursement from R.R. or A.F.Y. for payments he was legally obligated to make as the presumed father. No statute or jurisprudence authorizes the relief sought by the appellant, and interpreting Article 227 to create such a right would contradict the exclusive disavowal procedures outlined in Articles 184 and 189.

**III. Standing and Public Policy Considerations**

The defense further argues that the appellant lacks standing to pursue these claims against R.R. and A.F.Y. R.R., as the alleged biological father, has no legal relationship to the child absent a successful disavowal and acknowledgment by J.M.Y. Under the presumption of Article 184, J.M.Y. remains the legal father. Therefore, R.R. owes no duty of support to the child and certainly owes no duty of reimbursement to J.M.Y. Similarly, J.M.Y. has no right of action against A.F.Y. for reimbursement of past payments, as those payments were made in fulfillment of his own legal obligation.

Public policy strongly supports the dismissal of this action. As noted by the prosecution, permitting a presumed father to "accept the benefits of paternity while shifting the financial burden" would "disturb the stability of family units" and expose children to the "uncertainties of paternity disputes." *Dugas*, 462 So.2d at 1277. The appellant paid child support for approximately fourteen years without challenging paternity. Allowing reimbursement now would upset the child's settled expectations and undermine the integrity of the family structure that the presumption of paternity is designed to protect.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the closing arguments and the facts presented, the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. The appellant, J.M.Y., is bound by the conclusive presumption of paternity arising from his marriage to A.F.Y. because he never filed a disavowal action. He cannot circumvent the statutory disavowal process by framing his claim as one for reimbursement. Under the mandate of *Dugas v. Dugas*, 462 So.2d 1275 (La. 1985), and the applicable Civil Code articles, he has no legal right to seek reimbursement from the alleged biological father or the mother. The public policy of preserving the integrity of family relationships and protecting children from paternity disputes supports this conclusion.

Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
