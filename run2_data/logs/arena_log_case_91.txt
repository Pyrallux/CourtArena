COURT ARENA LOG - Case 91
FACTS:
This case involves the admissibility of expert testimony in child sexual abuse cases. Last Kony challenges his convictions of sexual assault of a minor on the grounds that expert testimony regarding child sexual abuse is no longer relevant, the statistical data presented during his trial was misleading and highly prejudicial, and the evidence presented during his trial improperly profiled him as a sex offender. We affirm the trial court’s ruling as to the relevancy of the expert testimony in this ease regarding the unique characteristics of child sexual abuse victims admitted to assist the jury “to comprehend something not commonly known or understood”—delayed reporting. State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 557-58, 799 P.2d 48, 52-54 (1990). 

I. Background

Kony was charged in the Circuit Court of the First Circuit (circuit court) with sexual assault of a minor. The charges consisted of three counts of sexual assault in the first degree and six counts of sexual assault in the third degree.1 The complaining witness (Minor) lived in the same household with Kony, along with six family members and one other person. Kony was the boyfriend of Minor’s half-sister and father to two children in the home.2 Minor was fifteen years of age at the time of the alleged sexual assaults.

B. Testimony adduced by State

At trial, Minor testified that from May to August 2011, Kony sexually assaulted her three times in her room at the shared home. Minor testified that she was asleep in her bedroom with her door locked but the lock could be picked with a fingernail. During the second incident, Kony told her that there would be problems if her family found out about his “coming in her room.” The last incident occurred a few days before Minor reported the events to her mother on August 12, 2011.

Minor indicated that Kony was not in her room for more than five minutes during any of the incidents. E. ICA Proceedings

Kony’s opening brief, raised a single point of error—that the circuit court erred in allowing Dr. Bivens’ testimony. Kony argued that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was not of assistance to the jury because the commonness of delayed disclosure of child victims no longer constituted specialized knowledge outside the common experience of the jury, Kony also asserted that Dr. Bivens’ testimony relied on statistics in a manner that was misleading and highly prejudicial. Kony further contended that the statistical and profile evidence cited by Dr. Bivens vouched for Minor’s credibility.

In its answering brief, the State argued that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was relevant to assist the jury in understanding behaviors and phenomena associated with sexual abuse. The State contended Kony was not prejudiced by Dr. Bivens’ testimony and maintained that Kony did not object to the testimony on the grounds that it was profile evidence or that the doctor’s use of statistics was highly prejudicial. The State asserted •that it did not misinterpret any statistics mentioned by Dr. Bivens or improperly suggest during closing argument that Kony was guilty because he matched a profile of individuals who sexually assault children.

In reply to the State, Kony argued that he did not waive his challenge to Dr. Bivens’ testimony. Kony contended that he argued in his motion in limine that Dr. Bivens’ testimony was not relevant and that its minimal probative value was outweighed by its prejudicial effect. Kony maintained that he was not required to continuously object throughout the trial after the court had definitively ruled to allow Dr. Bivens’ testimony because the court’s invitation for the defense to object was only if defense counsel believed that the State was exceeding the scope of the court’s ruling.

The ICA issued a Summary Disposition Order (SDO) affirming the circuit court’s judgment of conviction. The ICA rejected Kony’s argument that expert testimony regarding delayed reporting is no longer helpful to juries. The ICA also assumed that Kony preserved his objections to Dr. Bivens’ *8testimony for improper bolstering of the complaining witnesses’ credibility and profiling of Kony as a sex offender and held that those arguments were meritless. The ICA noted that Dr. Bivens testified that error rates are always associated with percentages and that Kony acknowledged that Dr. Bivens did not have any information about the case.

Judge Reifurth concurred in the result, but he wrote separately to clarify his understanding of the Hawai'i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rule 403 analysis that would apply— had Kony properly raised his objections that elements of Dr. Bivens’ testimony were misleading and unduly prejudicial. Judge Rei-furth explained that the potential prejudice of the evidence should be assessed against its probative value under HRE Rule 403. Judge Reifurth stated that he would have resolved the issue of prejudice, arising from expert testimony regarding the typical characteristics and behaviors of child sexual abusers, as waived because Kony failed to object at tidal to any testimony of Dr. Bivens as prejudicial.

II. Standards of Review

We apply the righVwrong standard in reviewing challenges to a court’s relevancy decision under HRE Rules 703, 401, and 402. State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). “Evidentiary decisions based on HRE Rule 403, which require a ‘judgment call’ on the part of the trial court, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion.” State v. Richie, 88 Hawai'i 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998) (footnote omitted) (quoting State v. Arceo, 84 Hawai'i 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996)). “An abuse of discretion occurs when the decisionmaker ‘exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party.’ ” Vliet, 95 Hawai'i at 107, 19 P.3d at 55 (quoting In re Water Use Permit Applications, 94 Hawai'i 97, 183, 9 P.3d 409, 495 (2000)).

III. Discussion

Kony contends that the ICA gravely erred in holding that the circuit court did not err in allowing the testimony of Dr. Bivens as an expert in clinical psychology and the dynamics of child sexual abuse and permitting testimony on the issue of delayed reporting. Kony maintains that the testimony “was not relevant, not proper expert testimony, improperly profiled [Kony] as a sex offender and relied on statistical data that was misleading and highly prejudicial.”

### INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Kony ("Kony") respectfully submits this opening brief in response to the prosecution's arguments and respectfully urges this Court to reverse the judgment of the Intermediate Court of Appeals ("ICA") and the Circuit Court of the First Circuit. The trial court committed reversible error by admitting the expert testimony of Dr. Alexander Jay Bivens, whose testimony exceeded the scope of permissible expert testimony, improperly profiled Kony as a sex offender, and relied upon misleading statistical evidence that substantially prejudiced Kony's right to a fair trial.

### I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts, as established in the record, demonstrate that Kony was charged with sexual assault of a minor who was fifteen years of age at the time of the alleged incidents. Kony was the boyfriend of Minor's half-sister and father to two children in the household. The Minor testified that three incidents occurred between May and August 2011, with the first incident occurring on May 6, 2011, just after midnight on her fifteenth birthday.

Critically, the Minor reported the alleged assaults to her mother on August 12, 2011—approximately three months after the alleged first incident. The Minor explained that she did not tell her parents earlier because she was not "close" to any of them and was concerned about "family being messed up." She first told her cousin while the incidents were "happening."

Prior to trial, Kony filed a motion in limine to exclude Dr. Bivens' testimony, asserting that the testimony would be irrelevant and that its probative value would be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The circuit court ruled that it would allow Dr. Bivens to testify on the issue of delayed reporting but indicated it would rule "question by question" on objections.

At trial, Dr. Bivens testified extensively about statistics regarding child sexual abuse, including that 85% of sexual abuse victims have a pre-existing non-sexual relationship with their molester, that over 95% of sexual crimes against children are committed by males, and that delayed disclosure is "the rule, not the exception." Dr. Bivens also provided percentages regarding why victims delay reporting, including that 29% expected not to be believed, 25% were embarrassed, and 24% didn't want to cause family chaos.

---

### II. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting Dr. Bivens' Testimony Because It Exceeded the Scope of Permissible Expert Testimony and Was Not Relevant to Assist the Jury

The prosecution argues that Dr. Bivens' testimony was relevant to assist the jury in understanding behaviors and phenomena associated with sexual abuse. However, the defense maintains that the testimony was improperly admitted because it exceeded the limited scope for which it was offered and improperly vouched for the complaining witness's credibility.

Under the Hawai'i Rules of Evidence ("HRE") Rule 702, expert testimony is admissible if "scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge" will "assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue." The Hawaii Supreme Court has recognized that expert testimony regarding the dynamics of child sexual abuse may be admissible to assist juries in understanding delayed reporting behavior, which is "not commonly known or understood." State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 557-58, 799 P.2d 48, 52-54 (1990).

However, the instant case is distinguishable from Batangan. In Batangan, the expert testimony was limited to explaining why children might delay in reporting sexual abuse—knowledge that is indeed outside the common experience of most jurors. Here, however, Dr. Bivens' testimony went far beyond explaining delayed reporting. He testified extensively about statistics regarding where abuse occurs (100% of incest offenders commit offenses in their own home), the gender of abusers (over 95% male), the pre-existing relationships between victims and abusers (85% have pre-existing non-sexual relationships), and the specific reasons victims delay reporting with exact percentages.

This Court should hold that the trial court exceeded the bounds of reason in allowing testimony that went beyond the narrow issue of delayed reporting and instead constituted improper profile evidence designed to suggest that Kony fit the profile of a child sex abuser. The testimony that "100% of incest offenders commit their offenses in their own home" and that "over 95% of sexual crimes against children are committed by males" served no legitimate purpose in assisting the jury to understand delayed reporting. Rather, this testimony was designed to and did improperly bolster the prosecution's case by suggesting that Kony, as a male living in the home, fit the statistical profile of a child sex abuser.

#### B. The Statistical Data Presented by Dr. Bivens Was Misleading and Highly Prejudicial

The prosecution contends that Kony was not prejudiced by Dr. Bivens' testimony and that the statistics were not misleading. The defense responds that the testimony was highly prejudicial and its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

HRE Rule 403 provides that relevant evidence "may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury." The decision to admit or exclude evidence under Rule 403 is within the sound discretion of the trial court and will not be disturbed on appeal unless the trial court exceeded the bounds of reason. State v. Richie, 88 Hawai'i 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998); State v. Arceo, 84 Hawai'i 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996).

Dr. Bivens' testimony regarding statistics was misleading in several respects. First, while Dr. Bivens acknowledged that "there are error rates associated with percentages always" and that percentages should "only be used to be given a general idea," he nonetheless provided exact percentages that implied a precision the data did not support. Second, the statistics regarding where abuse occurs (100% for incest offenders) and the gender of abusers (over 95% male) were presented in a manner that suggested Kony specifically fit these profiles, when in fact these general statistics had no bearing on whether Kony committed the specific acts alleged.

The defense specifically objected to portions of Dr. Bivens' testimony on relevancy grounds, particularly when Dr. Bivens began discussing children ages five to seven years old—evidence wholly unrelated to a fifteen-year-old victim. The trial court indicated it would "start sustaining objections" to such testimony, yet the prosecution was permitted to continue with other irrelevant and prejudicial statistics.

This Court should find that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting statistical evidence that was misleading and highly prejudicial. The danger of unfair prejudice substantially outweighed any minimal probative value, as the statistics served primarily to improperly suggest that Kony was guilty because he fit the profile of a child sex offender rather than to assist the jury in understanding delayed reporting.

#### C. The Testimony Improperly Profiled Kony as a Sex Offender

The prosecution argues that Kony did not object to the testimony on the grounds that it was profile evidence. The defense maintains that the motion in limine and the objections made during trial were sufficient to preserve this issue for appeal.

The motion in limine specifically asserted that Dr. Bivens' testimony would be irrelevant and that its probative value would be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, specifically Kony's propensity to commit the crime for which he was charged. This objection encompasses the profile evidence argument, as profile evidence is precisely the type of evidence that improperly suggests propensity.

Even assuming arguendo that the profile evidence objection was not properly preserved, this Court should recognize that the admission of profile evidence constitutes plain error affecting Kony's substantial rights. The testimony that 100% of incest offenders commit offenses in their own home, that over 95% of child sex offenders are male, and that 85% of victims have pre-existing relationships with their abusers—all facts that perfectly describe Kony's situation—constituted improper profile evidence that denied Kony his right to a fair trial.

The prosecution's closing argument likely compounded this error by suggesting that Kony was guilty because he matched the profile of a child sex offender. While the prosecution claims it did not "improperly suggest during closing argument that Kony was guilty because he matched a profile," the very admission of this profile evidence in the first place improperly influenced the jury's consideration of the case.

#### D. The Trial Court Erred in Failing to Conduct a Proper HRE Rule 403 Analysis

Judge Reifurth's concurrence correctly identified that a proper HRE Rule 403 analysis was required regarding the prejudice arising from expert testimony regarding typical characteristics and behaviors of child sexual abusers. However, Judge Reifurth would have resolved the issue as waived because Kony failed to object at trial to any testimony of Dr. Bivens as prejudicial.

The defense maintains that the motion in limine and the objections made during trial were sufficient to preserve the Rule 403 issue. The trial court was on notice that Kony specifically contended that the testimony's probative value would be substantially outweighed by its prejudicial effect. The court's ruling that it would allow the testimony but rule "question by question" on objections did not relieve the court of its obligation to conduct a Rule 403 analysis when specific objections were made.

This Court should hold that the trial court erred in failing to conduct a proper Rule 403 analysis and that the error was not waived by Kony. The appropriate remedy is to reverse the convictions and remand for a new trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standards of Review**

We review the trial court’s decision regarding the relevancy of expert testimony under the right/wrong standard. State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). An abuse of discretion occurs when the court "exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party." Id. (quoting State v. **I. Standards of Review**

We review the trial court’s decision regarding the relevancy of expert testimony under the right/wrong standard. State v. Vliet, 95 Hawai'i 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). But the logical inference for the jury was clear: the defendant fits the profile of a typical child sex offender. The statistics were not presented in a vacuum; they were presented in a case where the defendant was accused of exactly the conduct described—sexual assault of a minor in the home by a male with a pre-existing relationship.

In State v. Batangan, this Court emphasized that expert testimony must assist the jury in understanding "something not commonly known or understood." The prosecution argues that delayed reporting fits this description. But the statistics about offender demographics do not assist the jury in understanding delayed reporting; they assist the jury in determining whether the defendant is guilty based on whether he fits a criminal profile.

This is precisely the kind of evidence that HRE Rule 404 and the common law prohibition on character evidence were designed to prevent. The prosecution was not entitled to prove that the defendant is a "type of person" who commits sexual assault. Yet that is exactly what Dr. Bivens' testimony accomplished.

The prosecution argues that Dr. Bivens "explicitly stated he had no information about the case" and was not linking the statistics to the defendant. This is a red herring. An expert need not explicitly identify the defendant as fitting a profile for that profile to influence the jury. The cumulative effect of the testimony—combined with the specific facts of this case—created an impermissible inference of guilt based on the defendant's perceived character.

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS LACK MERIT

The prosecution argues that the defense cannot show prejudice because the other evidence of guilt was substantial. This argument conflates two distinct issues. Even if sufficient evidence supports the conviction, the improper admission of prejudicial evidence violates the defendant's right to a fair trial. The question is not whether the jury could have convicted without the improper evidence, but whether the improper evidence affected the verdict.

The prosecution also argues that the defense "did not object to the testimony on the grounds that it was profile evidence." This is incorrect. The defense's motion in limine explicitly argued that the testimony's "probative value would be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, specifically Kony's propensity to commit the crime for which he was charged." This is precisely a profile evidence objection.

Finally, the prosecution argues that the defense failed to object at trial to "any testimony of Dr. Bivens as prejudicial." The record shows otherwise. The defense objected when Dr. Bivens began discussing children ages five to seven, and the court indicated it would sustain such objections. The defense also objected to testimony about "what causes delayed reporting" on relevancy grounds. The prosecution's characterization of the record is inaccurate.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the ICA's affirmance and remand for a new trial. The circuit court erred in admitting Dr. Bivens' testimony because:

1. The testimony exceeded the scope of State v. Batangan by including offender profile evidence that did not assist the jury in understanding delayed reporting.

2. The statistical data was misleading in its precision and highly prejudicial, creating an inference that the defendant fit the profile of a typical child sex offender.

3. The defense properly preserved its objections through its motion in limine and was not required to continuously object during trial when the court had issued a definitive ruling on the scope of permissible testimony.

4. The circuit court abused its discretion under HRE Rule 403 by failing to balance the probative value of the offender profile evidence against its substantial prejudicial effect.

The prosecution's arguments rely on procedural technicalities and mischaracterizations of the record. The fundamental truth is that the jury heard expert testimony designed to paint the defendant as a "typical" sex offender, not testimony designed to explain victim behavior. This evidence was irrelevant, misleading, and highly prejudicial. The defendant is entitled to a new trial.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. Standards of Review**

We review the trial court’s decision regarding the relevancy of expert testimony under the right/wrong standard. State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). Evidentiary decisions based on Hawai'i Rules of Evidence (HRE) Rule 403, which involve balancing the probative value of evidence against its potential for unfair prejudice, are reviewed for an abuse of discretion. State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998). An abuse of discretion occurs when the court "exceeds the bounds of reason or disregards rules or principles of law or practice to the substantial detriment of a party." Id. (quoting State v. Arceo, 84 Haw. 1, 11, 928 P.2d 843, 853 (1996)).

**II. Analysis**

**A. Relevance of Expert Testimony Regarding Delayed Reporting**

Kony argues that the expert testimony regarding delayed reporting was irrelevant because the phenomenon is within the common knowledge of the jury. The State counters that the testimony was admissible to assist the jury in understanding "something not commonly known or understood," specifically the dynamics of delayed disclosure in child sexual abuse cases.

We agree with the State. In State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 557-58, 799 P.2d 48, 52-54 (1990), this court held that expert testimony regarding the unique characteristics of child sexual abuse victims is admissible to explain delayed reporting. The testimony offered by Dr. Bivens explained why victims often delay disclosure, citing reasons such as fear of not being believed, embarrassment, and a desire to avoid family chaos. This information falls within the realm of specialized knowledge intended to aid the trier of fact under HRE Rule 702. Therefore, the circuit court did not err in determining that Dr. Bivens’ testimony regarding delayed reporting was relevant under HRE Rules 401 and 402.

**B. Scope of Testimony, Alleged Profiling, and Waiver**

Kony contends that Dr. Bivens’ testimony exceeded the scope of permissible expert testimony by improperly profiling him as a sex offender. Specifically, Kony challenges the admission of statistics regarding the gender of offenders (over 95% male), the location of offenses (100% of incest offenses occur in the home), and the pre-existing relationship between victim and abuser. Kony argues that these statistics constitute improper character evidence suggesting his propensity to commit the crime and that they were irrelevant to the issue of delayed reporting.

The defense argues that the motion in limine preserved the objection to this profiling evidence and that the court’s ruling was "definitive," thereby relieving the defense of the obligation to object "question by question" during trial. See State v. Vliet, 95 Haw. 94, 107, 19 P.3d 42, 55 (2001). However, the record shows that the circuit court’s ruling was not a definitive admission of all potential testimony from Dr. Bivens. Rather, the court ruled that it would allow Dr. Bivens to testify "on this issue of delayed reporting" and explicitly stated, "aside from that, we’re just going to go question by question." This ruling conditioned the admission of the testimony on strict adherence to the topic of delayed reporting and placed the burden on the defense to object if the State’s questioning exceeded that scope.

While the defense correctly notes that statistics regarding offender characteristics do not directly explain why a victim delays reporting—and thus may be of questionable relevance under *Batangan*—the defense failed to preserve this specific objection for appeal. When the State elicited the specific statistics regarding offender gender, location, and relationships, defense counsel did not object. The court’s "question by question" instruction provided a mechanism for the defense to exclude evidence that fell outside the scope of delayed reporting. By failing to object to these specific questions at trial, the defense waived the right to challenge the admission of this particular evidence on appeal. See State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998) (noting that failure to make a timely and specific objection constitutes a waiver).

Furthermore, even if the objection had been preserved, the trial court has discretion in determining whether the danger of unfair prejudice substantially outweighs the probative value under HRE Rule 403. The State argues that the statistics provided necessary context for the dynamics of the abuse environment. While the defense’s argument that the statistics served as improper profile evidence is compelling on the merits, we cannot conclude that the circuit court exceeded the bounds of reason in admitting the evidence, particularly given the defense’s failure to object and the court’s management of the testimony.

**C. Misleading Statistical Data**

Kony argues that the statistical data was misleading because Dr. Bivens provided exact percentages that implied precision not supported by the data, such as the statement that "100% of incest offenders" commit offenses in the home. The defense contends this false precision was highly prejudicial.

The State responds that Dr. Bivens provided the necessary safeguards by explaining to the jury that percentages have error rates and are used only to give a "general idea" of how phenomena occur. We find that this cautionary instruction mitigated the risk of the jury being misled by the precision of the statistics. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting this evidence, as the jury was made aware of the limitations of the statistical data.

**III. Conclusion**

Based on the arguments presented in the rebuttals and the facts provided, the circuit court did not err in admitting Dr. Bivens’ testimony. The testimony regarding delayed reporting was relevant and admissible under State v. Batangan, 71 Haw. 552, 557-58, 799 P.2d 48, 52-54 (1990). The defense’s challenge to the specific statistical evidence regarding offender characteristics is waived due to the failure to object during trial, as the court’s "question by question" ruling required the defense to raise specific objections to evidence exceeding the scope of delayed reporting. Alternatively, the admission of such evidence did not constitute an abuse of discretion under State v. Richie, 88 Haw. 19, 37, 960 P.2d 1227, 1245 (1998).

Accordingly, we affirm the trial court’s ruling admitting the expert testimony.
