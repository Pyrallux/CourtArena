**Opening Argument on Joinder Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1)**

Your Honor, the State respectfully submits that the joinder of the intimidation and sexual assault charges was unequivocally proper under Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1). The statute permits joinder when offenses are "based on the same conduct or are part of a common scheme or plan." Here, the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were inextricably linked by a unified pattern of coercive control, shared victims, and a singular objective: silencing the victims to prevent exposure of the defendant’s broader pattern of abuse. This is not a case of unrelated charges improperly lumped together—it is a seamless narrative of ongoing criminal behavior that must be viewed as a whole to understand the full scope of Salinas’s conduct.

The evidence demonstrates that Salinas’s intimidation of M.S. and V.G. through his jailhouse phone calls was not an isolated incident tied solely to the domestic violence case. Rather, it was a direct continuation of the same coercive tactics he employed during the sexual assaults. V.G. testified that Salinas repeatedly threatened to "take her little brother away or send her away" if she reported the sexual assaults. Those exact same threats—using the children as leverage to control and silence the victims—were central to the intimidation charges. In the recorded phone calls, Salinas told M.S., "I can never talk to you because... you better start thinking that one day I’m coming out... Daughter of your fucking mother... If I get out, you are going to be sorry." This was not mere posturing; it was a calculated effort to prevent disclosure of the sexual assaults by ensuring M.S. and V.G. remained compliant. As the circuit court correctly noted, the intimidation charges directly involved V.G.—the same victim of the sexual assaults—and the evidence of the phone calls was necessary to explain why V.G. delayed reporting the sexual assaults for years. This is precisely the type of interconnectedness § 971.12(1) was designed to address.

This Court has repeatedly held that joinder is proper when offenses share a "common purpose or plan" and "form a continuous sequence of events." *State v. Pitsch*, 124 Wis. 2d 628, 370 N.W.2d 359 (Ct. App. 1985). In *Pitsch*, the court upheld joinder of multiple sexual assault charges against the same victim because they were part of a "continuous course of conduct" driven by the defendant’s "pattern of domination and control." Here, Salinas’s sexual assaults and intimidation tactics were not discrete acts but manifestations of the same predatory behavior: using physical violence, psychological threats, and familial leverage to maintain power over his victims. The threats to "take the children away" during the sexual assaults were identical in substance to the threats made in the phone calls to prevent M.S. and V.G. from cooperating with law enforcement. As the court observed in *State v. Bollig*, 2000 WI 6, 232 Wis. 2d 561, 605 N.W.2d 199, "when offenses involve the same victim and are motivated by the same intent to control or silence, they constitute a common scheme." Bollig’s holding directly applies: Salinas’s intimidation of M.S. and V.G. through phone calls was motivated by the same intent—preventing disclosure of his crimes—that drove his sexual assaults against V.G. The two sets of charges were not merely "connected"; they were two sides of the same coin.

Moreover, joinder was appropriate because the evidence of each set of charges was mutually relevant. The State needed to present the phone calls to explain V.G.’s delayed reporting of the sexual assaults—a critical element of the prosecution’s case. As this Court held in *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 101, 237 Wis. 2d 531, 614 N.W.2d 151, "joinder is proper when the evidence of each offense is relevant to the other, and trying the cases together avoids piecemeal litigation that would burden victims and courts." Here, the intimidation charges provided essential context for why V.G. remained silent for years: Salinas’s threats during the phone calls reinforced the same fear that kept her from reporting the sexual assaults earlier. Trying the cases separately would have required V.G. and M.S. to testify twice about overlapping trauma—a needless and traumatic burden. The circuit court correctly determined that joinder would not confuse the jury; instead, it would allow the jury to see the full picture of Salinas’s behavior. As the court stated in *State v. Smith*, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912, "juries are capable of parsing evidence when properly instructed, and joinder is favored where the offenses are part of a single narrative."

Salinas’s argument that the intimidation charges were "completely separate" from the sexual assaults ignores the reality of his conduct. His threats to "take the children away" during the sexual assaults were the same threats he reiterated in the phone calls to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements. The domestic violence incident was merely the catalyst that exposed the broader pattern of abuse—Salinas’s attempts to silence the victims were not about the domestic violence alone but about preventing exposure of the sexual assaults. The circuit court’s ruling was grounded in sound legal principles and the facts of this case. The joinder was proper under § 971.12(1), and this Court should affirm it.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Argument on Behalf of Respondent Luis Salinas

### I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The State asks this Court to affirm the circuit court's joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges. This Court should decline that invitation. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed Salinas's convictions, finding that the circuit court erred in joining the intimidation counts with the sexual assault counts. The joinder was improper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) because the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were not based on the same conduct, nor were they part of a common scheme or plan. Rather, the intimidation charges arose from Salinas's conduct in the domestic violence case—a case that was entirely separate from the sexual assault allegations, which were not reported until after the intimidation conduct had concluded. The State's attempt to recast the intimidation as part of a unified pattern of abuse to silence sexual assault victims contradicts the undisputed facts and misapplies the governing legal standards. This Court should affirm the Court of Appeals decision.

### II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

Whether charges were properly joined under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. The question of whether joinder was proper involves interpreting the statute and applying it to the facts—matters this Court considers without deference to the lower courts. When a defendant has not moved for severance, this Court still reviews joinder rulings for prejudicial error, as improper joinder can deprive a defendant of his right to a fair trial.

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Intimidation Charges and Sexual Assault Charges Were Not Based on the Same Conduct and Were Not Part of a Common Scheme or Plan

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses when they are "based on the same conduct or are part of a common scheme or plan." The statute must be construed narrowly because joinder deprives a defendant of his right to have each charge decided by a separate jury and risks unfair prejudice. As this Court held in *State v. Lehman*, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 407, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982), the policy behind joinder is to promote efficiency, but that policy must yield when joinder would prejudice the defendant.

The facts of this case demonstrate that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were based on entirely separate conduct. The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's telephone calls from jail between October 2009 and May 2010. As the record clearly shows, these calls were made while Salinas was awaiting sentencing on the domestic violence charges. The purpose of the calls, as the State explicitly acknowledged below, was to pressure M.S. to change her statement to police about the domestic violence incident and to convince V.G. to help him with the sentencing judge in that same case. The circuit court expressly found that the intimidation charges related to "a prior conviction for hitting M.S. and V.G." and were "completely separate from any sort of sexual assault allegation."

The sexual assault charges, by contrast, alleged criminal conduct that occurred between 2007 and October 2009—a completely different time period and involving different conduct. Critically, the sexual assaults were not reported until May 13, 2010, two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence case. Salinas could not have been intimidating witnesses to prevent disclosure of sexual assaults that no one—including Salinas—knew had been reported. The temporal gap alone demonstrates that the two sets of charges were not part of a single scheme or plan.

The State's argument that Salinas's threats to "take the children away" during the sexual assaults were "identical in substance" to the threats made in the phone calls conflates distinct conduct. The threats during the alleged sexual assaults were made to compel V.G.'s compliance with sexual acts—a distinct criminal objective. The threats in the phone calls were made to influence testimony in an already-completed domestic violence prosecution—a separate criminal objective. That both sets of threats involved threats regarding children does not transform them into a "common scheme." To hold otherwise would eviscerate the protections of § 971.12(1) by allowing joinder of any offenses that share superficial similarities.

This Court has consistently held that joinder requires more than mere factual overlap or chronological proximity. In *State v. Pitsch*, 124 Wis. 2d 628, 370 N.W.2d 359 (Ct. App. 1985), the court emphasized that joinder is proper only when the offenses are "connected so as to form a single narrative." The intimidation charges here do not form a single narrative with the sexual assault charges—they involve different victims (M.S. was a victim of the intimidation but not the sexual assaults), different time periods, and different criminal objectives. The Court of Appeals correctly found the joinder improper.

#### B. The Evidence of Each Offense Was Not Mutually Relevant

The State argues that joinder was proper because the evidence was "mutually relevant"—specifically, that the phone calls were necessary to explain V.G.'s delayed reporting of the sexual assaults. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the phone calls were not relevant to the sexual assault charges. The calls were made regarding the domestic violence case, not the sexual assault case. V.G. did not report the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010, but the intimidation conduct relevant to the charges occurred between October 2009 and May 11, 2010—the date of Salinas's sentencing. The phone calls could not have been intended to silence V.G. about sexual assaults that had not been reported and were unknown to anyone but V.G. and Salinas.

Second, even if some marginal relevance existed, that is insufficient under § 971.12(1). The statute requires that the offenses be "based on the same conduct" or part of a "common scheme or plan"—not merely that some evidence might be tangentially relevant to both. As this Court explained in *State v. Williams*, 2002 WI 58, 253 Wis. 2d 447, 644 N.W.2d 919, joinder is improper when the offenses are "unrelated" and the evidence of one offense would not be admissible in a trial for the other offense.

Third, the State's argument that the evidence was needed to explain delayed reporting improperly bootstraps prejudice into relevance. Evidence that a defendant made threats to witnesses in an unrelated case does not make the sexual assaults more likely to have occurred. The proper way to address V.G.'s delayed reporting was through cross-examination and argument—not by joining entirely separate charges.

#### C. The Joinder Was Prejudicial

Even if some basis for joinder existed, the circuit court's decision to join the cases was not harmless. The prejudice from joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was overwhelming.

During the State's opening statement, the prosecutor told the jury the evidence would show a "pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, [that] repeated sexual assault of a child occurred." The prosecutor explicitly tied the October 2009 domestic violence incident—which was not itself on trial—to the sexual assaults, telling the jury "that is the day that stopped what was happening to V.G." This opening statement effectively told the jury that Salinas was a dangerous, violent man who had committed multiple crimes against the same family—a narrative that would have been impossible to separate in the jurors' minds.

The evidence of the domestic violence incident and the phone calls was highly prejudicial. M.S. was permitted to testify in detail about Salinas putting his hands on her neck, attempting to choke her, and holding a knife while threatening to kill himself and her son. This evidence had no relevance to the sexual assault charges beyond showing Salinas's bad character—a classic forbidden purpose under Wisconsin's rules of evidence. As Salinas's lawyer objected at trial, this evidence was "unfairly prejudicial" and was being used to convince the jury "look how bad Luis Salinas is. He must have done it."

The circuit court's limiting instruction—that prior convictions could be used only for credibility and not as proof of guilt—was insufficient to cure this prejudice. The cumulative effect of hearing about Salinas's violence against M.S., his threats to kill himself and his son, and his attempts to influence witnesses necessarily tainted the jury's consideration of the sexual assault charges. As this Court held in *State v. Douglas*, 130 Wis. 2d 491, 501, 388 N.W.2d 692 (Ct. App. 1986), "the danger of prejudice from joinder is greatest when evidence of one crime would not be admissible in a separate trial of the other crime."

Here, the evidence of the intimidation charges would not have been admissible in a trial of the sexual assault charges because it concerned a different crime against different victims for different purposes. The jury heard evidence of Salinas as a violent, controlling man—not as a sexual predator. The two narratives were toxic to each other, and joining them deprived Salinas of his right to have each charge decided on its own merits.

#### D. The Court of Appeals Correctly Reversed

The Court of Appeals properly applied the law to the facts of this case. The court recognized that the intimidation charges arose from Salinas's attempts to influence witnesses in the domestic violence case—conduct that was complete before the sexual assaults were ever reported. The court correctly found that the circuit court erred in joining the charges and that the error was not harmless.

The State's reliance on *State v. Bollig*, 2000 WI 6, 232 Wis. 2d 561, 605 N.W.2d 199, is misplaced. In *Bollig*, this Court upheld joinder of multiple sexual assault charges against the same victim because they were part of a "continuous course of conduct." Here, by contrast, the intimidation charges involved different victims (M.S. was a victim of the intimidation but not the sexual assaults), different time periods, and different criminal objectives. The State's attempt to extend *Bollig* to join entirely unrelated charges would swallow the rule that joinder must be narrowly construed.

Similarly, the State's reliance on *State v. Kuntz*, 2000 WI 101, 237 Wis. 2d 531, 614 N.W.2d 151, is unavailing. In *Kuntz*, this Court upheld joinder where the evidence of each offense was directly relevant to the other. Here, the evidence of the intimidation was not relevant to proving the sexual assaults—it only showed Salinas's general propensity for threats and violence. The circuit court erred in joining the cases.

### IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Court of Appeals decision reversing Salinas's convictions. The circuit court improperly joined the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), and that error was not harmless. The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's conduct in the domestic violence case—a case entirely separate from the sexual assault allegations. The evidence of the intimidation was not mutually relevant to the sexual assault charges, and the joinder allowed the State to present highly prejudicial evidence that denied Salinas his right to a fair trial on each set of charges.

The State argues that joinder was proper because Salinas's conduct formed a "unified pattern of coercive control." But the facts do not support this argument. Salinas was convicted of intimidating M.S. and V.G. to influence the domestic violence case. He was separately charged with sexually assaulting V.G. over a three-year period. These were not one continuous scheme—they were separate crimes against separate victims for separate purposes. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed, and this Court should affirm that decision.

Whether charges are properly joined under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is a question of law that this Court reviews de novo. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982). The statute permits joinder only when the offenses "are based on the same conduct or are part of a common scheme or plan." The policy behind joinder is to promote judicial economy, but this policy must yield when joinder risks prejudice to the defendant. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982).

**II. Analysis of Joinder Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1)**

**A. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The State argues that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of a "unified pattern of coercive control" and thus a common scheme, citing State v. Pitsch, 124 Wis. 2d 628, 370 N.W.2d 359 (Ct. App. 1985), and State v. Bollig, 2000 WI 6, 232 Wis. 2d 561, 605 N.W.2d 199. The intimidation charges arose specifically from Salinas's conduct between October 2009 and May 11, 2010, where he utilized phone calls to pressure M.S. and V.G. to alter their statements and influence the sentencing in the *domestic violence* case. The sexual assault charges, conversely, involved conduct occurring between 2007 and October 2009, which was not reported to authorities until May 13, 2010—two days after the domestic violence sentencing concluded.

Crucially, the Defense argues that the intimidation could not have been part of a common scheme to conceal the sexual assaults because the sexual assaults were unknown to law enforcement at the time the intimidation occurred. The specific intent required for the intimidation charge was to influence a witness in a specific, pending proceeding (the domestic violence case). This objective is legally distinct from the objective of the sexual assaults, which was gratification and control through sexual violence. As noted in State v. Williams, 2002 WI 58, 253 Wis. 2d 447, 644 N.W.2d 919, joinder is improper when offenses are "unrelated" and the evidence of one would not be admissible in the trial of the other. Here, the intimidation was directed at a concluded legal matter regarding domestic violence, not the yet-unreported sexual assaults. Therefore, the charges do not satisfy the "common scheme or plan" requirement of § 971.12(1).

**B. Prejudice and Admissibility of Evidence**

The State contends that joinder was proper to provide context and explain the victim's delay in reporting, citing State v. Kuntz, 2000 WI 101, 237 Wis. 2d 531, 614 N.W.2d 151. However, the Defense persuasively argues that this rationale improperly bootstraps prejudicial evidence into relevance.

In a standalone trial for sexual assault, this specific evidence of domestic violence might have been excluded or severely limited as other acts evidence, as it describes distinct criminal conduct with a high potential to persuade the jury on an improper basis (i.e., that Salinas is a "bad man" who must be guilty of the sexual assaults). State v. Douglas, 130 Wis. 2d 491, 388 N.W.2d 692 (Ct. App. 1986) (holding that the danger of prejudice is greatest when evidence of one crime would not be admissible in a separate trial of the other).

The circuit court's limiting instruction, cited by the State in reliance on State v. Smith, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912, was insufficient to cure the prejudice. The jury was compelled to hear a narrative of Salinas as a violent strangler and knife-wielder before determining his guilt on the sexual assault charges. The cumulative effect of this evidence, which was only admissible because the charges were joined, denied Salinas a fair trial on the sexual assault counts.

**III. Conclusion**

The Defense has demonstrated that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were based on separate conduct and separate legal objectives, failing to meet the statutory standard for joinder. Furthermore, the joinder resulted in significant prejudice by admitting highly inflammatory evidence regarding the domestic violence incident that would likely have been inadmissible in a separate sexual assault trial. Therefore, the circuit court erred in joining the charges, and the Court of Appeals correctly reversed Salinas's convictions.

**IT IS ORDERED that the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.**

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S "SAME VICTIM" ARGUMENT DOES NOT ESTABLISH JOINDER

This conduct has no connection to the sexual assaults, which V.G. had not disclosed to anyone at that time. The prosecution cannot bootstrap the victim overlap into a common scheme when the underlying conduct was legally and factually distinct.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S "CONTEXT" ARGUMENT IMPROPERLY BOOTSTRAPS PREJUDICE INTO RELEVANCE

The prosecution argues that the domestic violence evidence was necessary to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting the sexual assaults, citing State v. Kuntz, 2000 WI 101, 237 Wis. 2d 531, 614 N.W.2d 151. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution's reliance on Kuntz is misplaced. In Kuntz, the Court permitted joinder where evidence of one offense "explains the context" of another, such as why a victim delayed reporting. However, the Court in Kuntz specifically cautioned that this rationale applies when the evidence is "mutually relevant"—that is, when evidence of the joined offense is relevant to proving an element of the charged offense. Here, the domestic violence evidence was not necessary to prove any element of the sexual assault charges. The elements of repeated sexual assault of a child do not require evidence of domestic violence; they require evidence of sexual contact with a child. The domestic violence evidence was introduced solely to paint Salinas as a "bad man" and to prejudice the jury against him.

Second, even if the domestic violence evidence had some minimal relevance to explaining V.G.'s delay, its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The evidence included detailed testimony about Salinas choking M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill his own child—highly inflammatory conduct that had no connection to the sexual assault charges. As this Court correctly noted in its preliminary ruling, in a standalone trial for sexual assault, this evidence would likely have been excluded as other acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04, as its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. State v. Douglas, 130 Wis. 2d 491, 388 N.W.2d 692 (Ct. App. 1986).

Third, the prosecution's argument that the evidence "would have been admissible under Wisconsin's other-acts rule even if the cases were severed" misstates the standard. The question is not whether the evidence could theoretically be admissible in some other proceeding, but whether its probative value in the joined trial substantially outweighed its prejudice. Here, the detailed testimony about the domestic violence incident served no purpose other than to inflame the jury's passions against Salinas.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION OVERSTATES THE EFFECTIVENESS OF THE LIMITING INSTRUCTION

The prosecution argues that the circuit court's limiting instruction "adequately mitigated any risk of prejudice." This argument fails. The limiting instruction told the jury that prior convictions could only be used for credibility and not as proof of guilt. However, this instruction did not address the core problem: the jury heard extensive, detailed testimony about Salinas's violent conduct toward M.S. and his threats toward V.G.—conduct that was not the subject of any charge in the sexual assault trial.

In State v. Smith, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912, this Court held that limiting instructions can cure prejudice when joinder is proper. However, the Court in Smith also recognized that limiting instructions are insufficient when the evidence is "highly inflammatory" and "would not be admissible in a separate trial of the other." Here, the domestic violence evidence was precisely that—highly inflammatory evidence that would not have been admissible in a standalone sexual assault trial. The jury was compelled to hear a narrative of Salinas as a violent strangler and knife-wielder before determining his guilt on the sexual assault charges. No limiting instruction could cure the cumulative prejudice of this evidence.

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S CHARACTERIZATION OF THE EVIDENCE IS FLAWED

The prosecution argues that Salinas's threats during the October 26, 2009 incident and during the sexual assaults were "consistent tactics in a continuous pattern of control." This characterization ignores the temporal and factual distinctions between these events. The October 26, 2009 domestic violence incident was a discrete event that led to Salinas's arrest and the pending domestic violence case. The sexual assaults, by contrast, occurred over a period of years and were unknown to law enforcement at the time of the intimidation. The fact that Salinas used threats in both contexts does not establish a "common scheme or plan" for purposes of joinder—it merely establishes that Salinas was a man who used threats, which is insufficient under the statute.

Furthermore, the prosecution's argument that Salinas "pressured M.S. to alter her police statement and V.G. to support his sentencing in the domestic violence case precisely to prevent the sexual assault allegations from surfacing" is speculative and finds no support in the record. At the time of the phone calls, no sexual assault allegations existed. V.G. had not disclosed the assaults to anyone. Salinas could not have been attempting to prevent the disclosure of allegations that did not exist.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that the Court affirm its preliminary ruling and uphold the Court of Appeals' decision reversing Salinas's convictions. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were "based on the same conduct or are part of a common scheme or plan" under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The charges arose from distinct conduct, involved distinct legal objectives, and were temporally separate. The joinder resulted in significant prejudice by admitting highly inflammatory evidence regarding the domestic violence incident that would not have been admissible in a standalone sexual assault trial. The circuit court erred in joining the charges, and the Court of Appeals correctly reversed Salinas's convictions.

**UPDATED RULING**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court adheres to its preliminary determination that the circuit court improperly joined the charges of misdemeanor intimidation of a witness with the charges of repeated sexual assault of a child. The defense has successfully demonstrated that the statutory requirements for joinder under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) were not met and that the joinder resulted in prejudicial error that was not harmless.

**I. Standard of Review**

The propriety of joinder under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) presents a question of law which this Court reviews de novo. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982). The statute permits joinder only when the offenses "are of the same or similar character" or "are based on the same conduct or are part of a common scheme or plan." While judicial economy is a goal of joinder, it must not come at the expense of the defendant's right to a fair trial. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982).

**II. Analysis of Joinder Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1)**

**A. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The prosecution argues in its rebuttal that the offenses were part of a "unified pattern of coercive control" and thus a common scheme, relying on State v. Pitsch, 124 Wis. 2d 628, 370 N.W.2d 359 (Ct. App. 1985), and State v. Bollig, 2000 WI 6, 232 Wis. 2d 561, 605 N.W.2d 199. The defense correctly counters that this characterization stretches the statutory language beyond its limits.

The record establishes that the intimidation charges arose from phone calls made by Salinas between October 2009 and May 11, 2010. The content of these calls, as detailed in the facts, was specifically aimed at pressuring M.S. and V.G. to alter their statements regarding the *domestic violence* incident and to influence the sentencing in that specific case. For example, Salinas told M.S. to change her statement to say he did not try to strangle her and asked V.G. to tell the sentencing court she missed him.

Crucially, the sexual assaults were not reported to law enforcement until May 13, 2010—two days after the domestic violence sentencing concluded. As the defense argues, Salinas could not have been attempting to suppress the sexual assault allegations through these phone calls because the allegations had not yet been made. The specific intent of the intimidation was to obstruct the domestic violence proceeding, a legal objective distinct from the sexual assaults, which had already ceased upon Salinas's arrest in October 2009.

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Bollig, 2000 WI 6, 232 Wis. 2d 561, 605 N.W.2d 199, to assert a "continuous course of conduct" is misplaced. In Bollig, the joined offenses were part of an ongoing series of events sharing a common purpose. Here, the sexual assaults (2007–2009) and the intimidation (2009–2010) were temporally and legally distinct. The intimidation was a reaction to the arrest for domestic violence, not a continuation of the sexual assault scheme. Therefore, the charges do not satisfy the "common scheme or plan" requirement of § 971.12(1).

**B. Prejudice and Admissibility of Evidence**

The prosecution contends that the domestic violence evidence was admissible to explain the context of V.G.'s delayed reporting, citing State v. Kuntz, 2000 WI 101, 237 Wis. 2d 531, 614 N.W.2d 151. This evidence was not merely background; it was the focal point of the prosecution's opening statement and closing argument. In a standalone trial for sexual assault, this specific evidence of violent domestic violence would likely have been excluded or severely limited under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) as other acts evidence, because its probative value in explaining the delay was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. State v. Douglas, 130 Wis. 2d 491, 388 N.W.2d 692 (Ct. App. 1986).

As stated in State v. Smith, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912, limiting instructions are not a panacea for prejudice, particularly when the evidence is so inflammatory that it prevents the jury from considering the charges separately based on the evidence. The jury was effectively compelled to view Salinas as a violent strangler and knife-wielder before deciding his guilt on the sexual assault charges, denying him a fair trial.

The defense has demonstrated that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were not part of a common scheme or plan under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence proceeding, while the sexual assaults were separate, unreported offenses. Furthermore, the joinder resulted in significant prejudice by admitting highly inflammatory evidence regarding the domestic violence incident that would likely have been inadmissible in a separate sexual assault trial. The circuit court's limiting instruction was insufficient to cure this prejudice. Therefore, the circuit court erred in joining the charges, and the Court of Appeals correctly reversed Salinas's convictions.

## II. THE EVIDENCE OF DOMESTIC VIOLENCE WAS INADMISSIBLE IN A STANDALONE SEXUAL ASSAULT TRIAL

Even if some minimal context regarding the domestic violence incident might have been relevant to explain V.G.'s delayed reporting, the **quantity** and **nature** of evidence admitted in this joined trial far exceeded what any court would permit under Wisconsin Rule of Evidence 904.04(2).

The prosecution presented extensive, graphic testimony regarding:

- Salinas choking M.S. in the kitchen
- Salinas holding a knife while threatening to kill his four-year-old son A.S.
- Salinas's threats to kill himself if V.G. called police
- The physical altercation on October 26, 2009

This was not background context—it was the centerpiece of the prosecution's case. As this Court recognized, in a standalone sexual assault trial, this evidence would have been excluded as other acts evidence because its probative value was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. *See State v. Douglas*, 130 Wis. 2d 491, 388 N.W.2d 692 (Ct. App. 1986).

The prosecution argues that limiting instructions cured any prejudice. However, as this Court correctly noted, the limiting instruction addressed only Salinas's prior convictions for credibility purposes. It did **nothing** to limit the inflammatory testimony regarding the domestic violence incident itself. The jury heard vivid descriptions of Salinas as a violent strangler and knife-wielder—evidence that painted him as a dangerous monster before the jury ever considered the sexual assault charges.

In *State v. Smith*, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912, this Court acknowledged that limiting instructions are not a panacea for prejudice, particularly when the evidence is so inflammatory that it prevents the jury from considering the charges separately. That is precisely what occurred here.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION CANNOT REWRITE THE RECORD

The prosecution's closing argument suggests that Salinas's threats to V.G. during the sexual assaults (to "take her little brother away or send her away") were somehow connected to his later threats during the phone calls. This is a misreading of the record.

The threats during the sexual assaults were instrumentally directed at keeping V.G. silent about those specific assaults. The threats during the phone calls were directed at influencing the domestic violence proceeding. These are legally distinct purposes, and the prosecution cannot conflate them to manufacture a "common scheme."

Furthermore, the prosecution's claim that the domestic violence incident was the "direct reason V.G. waited until May 13, 2010, to disclose the assaults" ignores V.G.'s own testimony. V.G. testified she did not report the sexual assaults because she was "afraid and ashamed" and worried her mother "would be ashamed." She specifically testified that after Salinas was arrested, "I just wanted him gone because I thought that with him gone it just wouldn't be going through all the things that I was going through." The domestic violence arrest, rather than prompting disclosure, actually caused V.G. to hope the sexual assaults would stop. She only disclosed after Salinas was released and began pressuring her at sentencing.

---

## IV. THE DEFENSE IS ENTITLED TO A FAIR TRIAL ON SEPARATE CHARGES

The fundamental principle underlying joinder requirements is that defendants are entitled to have each charge decided on its own merits, without the prejudice of evidence from unrelated offenses contaminating the jury's consideration. *See State v. Lehman*, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (Wis. 1982).

Here, Salinas was denied exactly that. The jury heard inflammatory evidence of domestic violence that had no bearing on whether Salinas sexually assaulted V.G. The prosecution's opening statement explicitly told the jury the case was about a "pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, repeated sexual assault of a child." This framing made it impossible for the jury to separate the charges.

The prosecution argues that severance would have forced V.G. "to relive her trauma twice." But this concern does not override a defendant's constitutional right to a fair trial. Moreover, V.G. could have testified regarding the sexual assaults without the jury hearing detailed testimony about choking, knife-wielding, and threats to kill a child. The prosecution made the choice to present the maximum possible inflammatory evidence, and that choice was error.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and as this Court has already determined in its thorough ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals, reverse the judgments of the circuit court, and remand for proper separate trials on the sexual assault charges and the intimidation charges.

The prosecution has not demonstrated that the charges were properly joined under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence proceeding, not the unreported sexual assaults. The joinder resulted in the admission of highly prejudicial evidence that would have been inadmissible in a standalone sexual assault trial. The limiting instruction was insufficient to cure this prejudice. Salinas was denied his right to a fair trial.

The defense respectfully submits that justice requires severance.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, and upon a thorough review of the record and applicable law, the Court adheres to its determination that the circuit court improperly joined the charges of misdemeanor intimidation of a witness with the charges of repeated sexual assault of a child. The defense has demonstrated that the statutory requirements for joinder were not satisfied and that the error was not harmless.

**I. Standard of Review**

The propriety of joinder under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) presents a question of law which this court reviews de novo. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982). The statute permits joinder only when the offenses "are of the same or similar character" or "are based on the same conduct or are part of a common scheme or plan." While judicial economy is a goal of joinder, it must not supersede the defendant's right to a fair trial. State v. Lehman, 108 Wis. 2d 400, 321 N.W.2d 245 (1982).

**II. Joinder Was Improper Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1)**

The prosecution argues that the offenses were part of a "unified pattern of coercive control" constituting a common scheme or plan. However, the defense correctly identifies that the legal objectives of the intimidation and the sexual assaults were distinct and temporally separated.

The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made by Salinas between October 2009 and May 11, 2010. The record reflects that the content of these calls was specifically directed at influencing the sentencing for the domestic violence incident. Salinas pressured M.S. to change her statement regarding the strangulation and pressured V.G. to provide a favorable sentencing recommendation. The sexual assaults were not reported to law enforcement until May 13, 2010, two days after the domestic violence sentencing concluded. Consequently, Salinas could not have been attempting to suppress the sexual assault allegations through these phone calls, as the allegations had not yet been made.

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Bollig, 2000 WI 6, 232 Wis. 2d 561, 605 N.W.2d 199 (2000), to infer a "continuous course of conduct" is unavailing. In Bollig, the joined offenses were part of an ongoing series of events sharing a common purpose. Here, the sexual assaults (2007–2009) and the intimidation (2009–2010) were legally distinct. The intimidation was a reaction to the arrest for domestic violence, not a continuation of the sexual assault scheme. As held in State v. Williams, 2002 WI 58, 253 Wis. 2d 447, 644 N.W.2d 919 (2002), joinder is improper when offenses are unrelated and evidence of one offense would not be admissible in the trial of the other. Because the intimidation was aimed at the domestic violence proceeding, it does not share the "common scheme or plan" required by § 971.12(1) with the subsequently charged sexual assaults.

**III. The Joinder Resulted in Prejudicial Error**

The prosecution contends that the domestic violence evidence was admissible to explain the context of V.G.'s delayed reporting, citing State v. Kuntz, 2000 WI 101, 237 Wis. 2d 531, 614 N.W.2d 151 (2000). While the general fact of the domestic violence arrest may provide some context, the defense persuasively argues that the *nature* and *quantity* of the evidence admitted were highly prejudicial.

The jury heard detailed, graphic testimony regarding Salinas choking M.S., holding a knife, and threatening to kill his four-year-old son. This evidence was not merely background; it was the focal point of the prosecution's opening statement and closing argument. In a standalone trial for sexual assault, this specific evidence of violent domestic violence would likely have been excluded or severely limited under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) as other acts evidence, because its probative value in explaining the delay was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. State v. Douglas, 130 Wis. 2d 491, 388 N.W.2d 692 (Ct. App. 1986).

The prosecution asserts that the circuit court's limiting instruction cured any prejudice, relying on State v. Smith, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912 (2004). However, the defense correctly notes that the limiting instruction was limited to Salinas's prior convictions and did not address the inflammatory testimony regarding the domestic violence incident itself. As stated in State v. Smith, 2004 WI 103, 274 Wis. 2d 273, 682 N.W.2d 912 (2004), limiting instructions are not a panacea for prejudice, particularly when the evidence is so inflammatory that it prevents the jury from considering the charges separately based on the evidence. The jury was effectively compelled to view Salinas as a violent strangler and knife-wielder before deciding his guilt on the sexual assault charges, denying him a fair trial.

**IV. Conclusion**

The defense has demonstrated that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were not part of a common scheme or plan under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was specifically directed at the domestic violence proceeding, while the sexual assaults were separate, unreported offenses. Furthermore, the joinder resulted in significant prejudice by admitting highly inflammatory evidence regarding the domestic violence incident that would likely have been inadmissible in a separate sexual assault trial. The circuit court's limiting instruction was insufficient to cure this prejudice. Therefore, the circuit court erred in joining the charges, and the Court of Appeals correctly reversed Salinas's convictions.

**IT IS ORDERED that the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.**
