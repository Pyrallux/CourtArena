FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1
Applying these standards, and based on the paltry evidence before us, defendants have not presented a compelling case of prejudice. To begin with, they cite only newspaper articles and editorials. There is no *108evidence whatsoever of prejudiced radio or television coverage or public sentiment generally. But see Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723; Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1487. Furthermore, the newspaper articles, while prone to hyperbole, are not inflammatory. Granted, some of the headlines describe the defendants of a “prostitution ring” or “sex ring.” But, the articles make an effort to point out that the defendants have not been found guilty; rather, they are alleged to have committed the crimes. In fact, most of the articles contain an attempt to reproduce testimony and arguments from court, heard in a public forum open to all. That is, they contain information that an average citizen would be privy to were he to go to court himself. Finally, the defendants reproduced only six articles, a far cry from the type of overwhelming saturation which can lead to a presumption of prejudice. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

### Opening Argument for the Prosecution  

Your Honor, the prosecution respectfully submits that the defendants have utterly failed to meet their extraordinary burden to justify a change of venue. The motion was properly denied because the defendants cannot demonstrate—through the record before this Court—either actual or presumed prejudice from pretrial publicity, nor can they substantiate claims of cultural bias warranting relocation. Here, voir dire has not occurred, making any such finding "premature." *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The defendants’ failure to await this critical step alone dooms their motion.  

Second, the defendants have not established *presumed prejudice*, a remedy reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). Presumed prejudice requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage," *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985)—a standard the defendants’ evidence utterly fails to satisfy. They cite only *six newspaper articles*, none of which are inflammatory. The headlines referencing a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring" are tempered by explicit disclaimers that the defendants are "presumed innocent" and that charges are "alleged." Critically, the defendants produced *zero evidence* of radio or television coverage, let alone the pervasive, sensationalized media saturation seen in cases where presumed prejudice was found. Compare *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963) (reversal warranted where defendant’s televised confession aired "several times to tens of thousands" in the community), with *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1491–1537 (no presumption where coverage was "not overwhelmingly extensive or sensational").  

Third, the defendants’ claim of "cultural bias against foreign-born defendants" is unsupported by the record. The trial court correctly found no evidence that pretrial publicity reflected "widespread societal prejudice." *See Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue*, at *108*. While the defendants cite letters to the editor, the court explicitly declined to consider these as indicative of community sentiment—just as this Court has held that isolated, biased opinions do not equate to pervasive prejudice. *See Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961) (prejudice requires "the community is saturated with prejudicial publicity" beyond isolated expressions).  

Finally, a change of venue is *impractical and ineffective* in American Samoa’s unique context. As the trial court noted, the Territory has "but one jury roll" comprising all registered voters island-wide. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (confirming jury trials are "practical and not anomalous" in small island communities). Transferring the case to another location within American Samoa would not alter the jury pool, as "the compact social structure of this island community" ensures any prejudice would permeate the entire Territory. *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (change of venue "unsuitable" in small jurisdictions with limited jury pools). A.S.C.A. § 46.0601—which the defendants cite—merely authorizes physical relocation for logistical convenience, not a remedy for alleged prejudice. *See id.* (contrasting with Fed. R. Crim. P. 21, which applies to larger jurisdictions).  

In short, the defendants have not come close to satisfying the "heavy burden" required for a change of venue. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The motion was rightly denied, and this Court should affirm that decision. The prosecution stands ready to proceed with a fair trial before an impartial jury, with voir dire designed to rigorously test any potential bias.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the High Court of American Samoa appellate Division

### Defendants-Appellants' Opening Brief in Support of Appeal from Denial of Motion for Change of Venue

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the prosecution's arguments and in support of our position that the trial court erred in denying the motion for change of venue. We address each of the prosecution's contentions in turn.

---

## I. The Trial Court Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard by Requiring Demonstrable juror taint Before Addressing the Venue Question

The prosecution erroneously contends that actual prejudice cannot be assessed until after voir dire. However, this argument misconstrues the nature of the defendants' motion and ignores the unique circumstances of this Territory. The defense did not merely speculate about prejudice; we presented evidence of pervasive pre-trial publicity that, even under the trial court's own reasoning, should have triggered concern.

In *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), the Supreme Court recognized that where "the community is saturated with prejudicial publicity," voir dire becomes an inadequate remedy because potential jurors have already formed preconceptions. The Court stated that "the light nature of the evidence of prejudice at voir dire does not undermine the conclusion that the community as a whole was tainted." Id. at 728. The prosecution's argument that we must wait for voir dire puts the cart before the horse—the very purpose of a change of venue motion is to prevent the taint from occurring in the first place.

The trial court acknowledged this principle when it stated that "if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors." Order Denying Motions at *109*. This reasoning is circular and places an impossible burden on defendants. By requiring us to demonstrate actual juror bias before any jury is selected, the trial court effectively eliminated the availability of a change of venue as a preventive remedy.

---

## II. The Pre-Trial Publicity Created Presumed Prejudice Under the Applicable Legal Standards

The prosecution minimizes the significance of the newspaper coverage by noting that only six articles were submitted. This argument ignores the qualitative nature of the publicity and the quantitative limitations of the media landscape in American Samoa.

### A. The Nature of the Publicity Was Inherently Prejudicial

The headlines describing a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring" are not merely descriptive—they carry profound pejorative connotations that irreparably damage the defendants' reputation in a small community. In *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966), the Supreme Court held that where "news media comments" create a "carnival atmosphere" around a trial, due process is violated regardless of whether specific jurors can be shown to be biased. The Court emphasized that the "trial court was not compelled to sit by and let the defendant be tried in a press atmosphere defying justice." Id. at 363.

The prosecution argues that the articles contained disclaimers of innocence. However, the damage is already done when potential jurors encounter headlines labeling defendants as participants in organized "rings." As the Supreme Court observed in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), "the broadcasting of such a spectacle, and the trial proceedings conducted in a community so pervasively exposed to such a spectacle could be but a hollow formality." Id. at 727. The same principle applies here—once the community is exposed to inflammatory characterizations, subsequent disclaimers cannot erase the initial impression.

### B. The Small Community Context Amplifies the Impact of Limited Publicity

The prosecution's reliance on cases from large mainland jurisdictions is misplaced. American Samoa presents unique circumstances where a handful of articles may constitute "oversaturation" precisely because of the limited number of media outlets and the close-knit nature of the community.

In *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), the First Circuit recognized that in small jurisdictions, even limited publicity can have a disproportionate impact. The court noted that "the small community context" means that "any publicity is likely to reach potential jurors." Id. at 731. While the prosecution cites this case for the proposition that a change of venue is unsuitable, the reasoning actually supports our position—the trial court should have acknowledged that the limited publicity in American Samoa is qualitatively equivalent to the extensive coverage in mainland cases.

The trial court stated that "if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest corners of the Territory." Order Denying Motions at *109*. This acknowledgment undermines the denial of our motion—if the prejudice has already permeated the entire Territory, then a change of venue within American Samoa would indeed be ineffective, but this恰恰 demonstrates that the defendants are entitled to relief through alternative means.

---

## III. The Defendants' Claim of Cultural Bias Is Supported by the Record

The prosecution incorrectly states that the trial court found "no evidence" of cultural bias. To the contrary, the record contains letters to the editor expressing prejudice against foreign-born individuals. The trial court acknowledged these letters but declined to consider them because "the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society." Order Denying Motions at *108*.

This reasoning is erroneous. The existence of First Amendment protection for speech does not mean that such speech is irrelevant to the question of community prejudice. In *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), Justice Frankfurter's concurrence emphasized that the question is not whether certain speech is protected, but whether "the community as a whole" has been exposed to prejudicial publicity. The Court reversed the conviction precisely because the community had been saturated with publicity, much of which consisted of protected speech.

The prosecution argues that isolated letters do not represent community sentiment. However, in a jurisdiction with limited media outlets, even a small number of published letters may represent the predominant sentiment in the community. The trial court's own observation that the media outlets are "virtual monopolies" (Order Denying Motions at *108*, n.2) means that what appears in print is more likely to reflect—and shape—community opinion.

Furthermore, the unique cultural dynamics of American Samoa support the defendants' claim. As the motion stated, the publicity reflects "bigotry from a 'culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here.'" This is not mere speculation—it is a sociological reality acknowledged in the record. The trial court erred by failing to consider this context when evaluating the impact of the publicity.

---

## IV. The Trial Court Erred in Its Interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601

The prosecution defends the trial court's conclusion that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 does not authorize a change of venue. However, the statute's language is broad: "the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa." American Samoa Code Annotated § 46.0601.

The trial court interpreted this statute as merely authorizing logistical relocation for witness convenience. However, nothing in the statutory text limits the authority to such circumstances. The phrase "interest of justice" encompasses the need to ensure a fair trial free from prejudice. See *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977) (recogning that jury trials are constitutionally required in American Samoa).

The prosecution argues that federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 21 distinguishes between transfer within a district and transfer to another district, and that the American Samoa statute only covers the former. However, this argument ignores that American Samoa is not a federal district—it is a territory with its own court system. The statute must be interpreted to serve its purpose of ensuring fair trials within the unique constraints of this Territory.

Even if the statute does not explicitly authorize a change of venue, the trial court has inherent authority to ensure due process. In *Moran v. Johns*, 151 F. Supp. 2d 1270 (D. Alaska 2001), the court recognized that in small communities where venue transfer is impractical, alternative remedies such as expanded voir dire or change of judge may be necessary to ensure a fair trial. The trial court acknowledged that "we need only assure a fair trial" (Order Denying Motions at *109*, n.3) but failed to provide any meaningful alternative remedy.

---

## V. The Defense Is Entitled to Relief

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm the denial of the motion based on the defendants' failure to meet an "extraordinary burden." However, the burden is not extraordinary—it is the burden established by the Constitution to ensure due process. The defendants have presented evidence of pre-trial publicity that, while perhaps not reaching the level of *Rideau v. Louisiana*, nonetheless raises serious concerns about the ability to select an impartial jury in American Samoa.

The trial court acknowledged that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives." Order Denying Motions at *109*. Yet the court failed to explore any alternatives, instead simply denying the motion and suggesting that voir dire would be sufficient.

We submit that the proper remedy is not necessarily a change of venue within American Samoa—which the trial court correctly noted would be ineffective—but rather a transfer of proceedings to a federal court in another jurisdiction where an impartial jury can be selected. While this remedy is unconventional, it is necessary to preserve the defendants' constitutional rights.

Alternatively, this Court could order a change of judge, expanded voir dire procedures, or other safeguards to ensure a fair trial. The trial court's suggestion of a voir dire questionnaire is inadequate because it does not address the underlying problem: in a small community where the defendants have already been publicly labeled as "prostitution ring" operators, any potential juror has likely been exposed to prejudicial information.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order denying the motion for change of venue and remand with instructions to grant appropriate relief to ensure a fair trial. The defendants are entitled to have their case heard before an impartial jury, free from the taint of pre-trial publicity and cultural bias that has pervaded this community.

Respectfully submitted,

**Counsel for Defendants-Appellants**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Appellate Division on the defendants’ appeal from the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, seek reversal of the trial court’s decision, arguing that the court erred in finding neither presumed nor actual prejudice from pre-trial publicity and cultural bias, and that it misinterpreted the statutory authority to grant a change of venue. Upon review of the record, the arguments presented by the prosecution, and the response by the defense, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the trial court applied an incorrect legal standard by requiring a showing of juror taint prior to a venue change and that the pre-trial publicity created presumed prejudice. To establish presumed prejudice, the defendants must demonstrate that the publicity was so "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" that the entire community was tainted, rendering the trial a "hollow formality." *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963). This is a "highly difficult task" reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The defendants carry the heavy burden of showing "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. The defense contends that the headlines labeling the operation a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring" are inherently prejudicial and that the small community context of American Samoa amplifies the impact of the six articles submitted. The trial court’s characterization of the evidence as "paltry" is supported by the record. **B. Actual Prejudice**

The defense argues that the trial court erred by deferring the assessment of prejudice to voir dire. The defense’s assertion that this places an "impossible burden" on them ignores the legal reality that presumed prejudice must be established independently of voir dire. The defendants contend that the trial court ignored evidence of cultural bias stemming from letters to the editor expressing prejudice against foreign-born individuals. The trial court acknowledged these letters but found they represented the views of a "very limited section of society" rather than pervasive community sentiment. The existence of protected speech containing biased views does not equate to a presumption of prejudice against the defendants. *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728. The defense has not provided evidence that these isolated opinions permeated the jury pool to the extent that a fair trial is impossible. The trial court did not err in declining to find that these letters established a constitutional violation.

**D. Remedy and Statutory Authority**

Finally, the defendants argue that the trial court erred in interpreting A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and that the court should have ordered a transfer to a federal court in another jurisdiction. The trial court correctly determined that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, enacted prior to the mandate for criminal jury trials in American Samoa, is designed to physically relocate court sessions for logistical convenience, not to effect a change of venue for prejudice. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977).

The defense’s proposal to transfer the case to a federal court in another jurisdiction is not supported by the provided facts or the statutory framework. The trial court noted that American Samoa has a single jury roll of all registered voters; thus, a physical move within the Territory would not change the jury pool. *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. The trial court acted within its discretion by denying the motion for change of venue and offering alternative remedies, including a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire, to ensure the defendants' right to a fair trial.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants have failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion. They have not met the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice, and actual prejudice cannot be assessed absent voir dire. Furthermore, the remedy sought is impractical and statutorily unsupported. Accordingly, the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed.

### I. The Defense Has Not Met the "Heavy Burden" for Presumed Prejudice
Presumed prejudice is reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The defense’s claim that six newspaper articles constitute "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" publicity is legally indefensible. The Supreme Court has reversed convictions for presumed prejudice only in extreme circumstances—such as *Rideau v. As the trial court observed, these articles "contain information that an average citizen would be privy to were he to go to court himself." The defense’s attempt to transform six articles into "massive coverage" in a territory with a single newspaper ignores that the Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected such hyperbolic claims. *See Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961) (holding that "the fact that the community may have been exposed to some publicity does not mean that the jury panel cannot be impartial").

The defense’s reliance on *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), is equally misplaced. *Moreno* does not lower the burden for small jurisdictions; it explicitly recognizes that "a change of venue is often unsuitable in such territories" and that "alternative remedies are appropriate." *Id.* at 731. The defense’s argument that American Samoa’s size automatically magnifies prejudice ignores that courts have consistently upheld jury trials in small communities without venue changes. *See King v. The trial court’s conclusion that a physical transfer within the Territory would not alter the single jury roll—a fact undisputed by the defense—is legally sound and forecloses the defense’s venue-change argument.

### II. Actual Prejudice Cannot Be Assessed Without Voir Dire
The defense’s claim that deferring to voir dire is a "hollow formality" contradicts settled law. To grant a venue change *before* voir dire would invert due process: the burden rests on the defendants to prove prejudice, not on the court to assume it.

### III. Cultural Bias Claims Fail as a Matter of Law
The defense’s argument that letters to the editor establish "pervasive" cultural bias is legally unsound. The trial court correctly found these letters represented "a very limited section of society," not community-wide prejudice. *See Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728 (holding that isolated expressions of bias do not equate to constitutional prejudice). The defense confuses the protection of free speech with proof of juror taint. As *Irvin* makes clear, "the fact that the community may have been exposed to some publicity does not mean that the jury panel cannot be impartial." *Id.* The defense cannot transform protected speech into a constitutional violation simply because it was published. No precedent supports the proposition that a single newspaper’s publication of reader letters—without evidence they were widely read or influenced the jury pool—creates presumed prejudice. The defense’s attempt to conflate editorial policy with community sentiment is speculative and unsupported by the record.

### IV. The Statutory Framework Does Not Permit a Venue Change
Finally, the defense’s reliance on A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 is misplaced. The defense’s request to "transfer the case to another jurisdiction" is not just unsupported by statute—it is logistically impossible. As the appellate court noted, American Samoa has "but one jury roll," and "transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same." A constitutional right to a fair trial does not require a venue change when the entire Territory shares a single jury pool. *See King*, 452 F. Supp. at 17 (holding jury trials are "practical" in American Samoa, meaning venue changes are unnecessary).

Your Honors, the defense asks this Court to rewrite constitutional law by lowering the standard for presumed prejudice based on the size of a jurisdiction. But precedent is clear: the burden remains "heavy" regardless of geography. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The defense has produced no evidence of "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" publicity like that in *Rideau*, no proof that voir dire would be futile, and no showing that cultural bias permeated the jury pool. The trial court’s denial of the motion—and the appellate court’s affirmation—were legally sound. To grant a change of venue here would set a dangerous precedent, transforming every high-profile case in a small community into a venue-change request. We urge this Court to affirm the denial of the motion for change of venue.

However, *Nevers* does not stand for the proposition that defendants must await voir dire to prove prejudice when the publicity itself is so pervasive as to create presumed prejudice. The defense has established that the cumulative effect of the newspaper coverage and the cultural bias evidence creates a presumption of prejudice that should be addressed before trial, not after.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Appellate Division for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the prosecution and the defense. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, appeal the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants argue that the unique characteristics of American Samoa, combined with pre-trial publicity and cultural bias, create a presumption of prejudice that necessitates a transfer of venue, potentially to a federal court outside the Territory. Having carefully considered the arguments presented, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Presumed Prejudice and the "Unique Circumstances" of American Samoa**

The defense contends that the "heavy burden" for establishing presumed prejudice should be applied differently in American Samoa due to the Territory's small population and single media outlet. They argue that six newspaper articles in a monopoly market have a "functional impact" equivalent to massive coverage in a larger jurisdiction, thereby satisfying the standard set forth in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).

The Court is not persuaded that the constitutional standard for presumed prejudice is flexible based solely on the size of the jurisdiction. To establish presumed prejudice, defendants must demonstrate that the publicity was so "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" that the entire community was tainted, rendering the trial a "hollow formality." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 727. This is a "highly difficult task" reserved for "exceptional cases." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). The defendants carry the burden of showing "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage." *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985).

While the defense correctly notes that American Samoa has a single newspaper, this fact does not automatically elevate six articles to the level of "oversaturation." In *Rideau*, the Supreme Court found presumed prejudice where a televised confession was aired "several times to tens of thousands of people." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726. Here, the articles, while using hyperbolic headlines such as "prostitution ring," consistently noted the defendants' presumption of innocence and reported on public court proceedings. The trial court found the evidence of prejudice "paltry," a finding supported by the record. The defense's theory of "functional impact" is speculative; without evidence that the coverage saturated the community to the point of inherent prejudice, the standard for presumed prejudice is not met. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961).

**B. Actual Prejudice and the Necessity of Voir Dire**

The defense argues that deferring the inquiry into prejudice to voir dire places an impossible burden on them and characterizes voir dire as a "fishing expedition." They assert that the cumulative effect of the publicity and cultural bias creates a presumption of prejudice that should be addressed now.

However, the law is clear that actual prejudice "is discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999). Because voir dire has not yet occurred, the trial court correctly found that an inquiry into actual prejudice was premature. *Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow*, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). The defense's dissatisfaction with the voir dire process does not negate its status as the established procedural mechanism for assessing actual prejudice. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966). The trial court's order allowing for a detailed questionnaire and individual voir dire provides sufficient safeguards to identify any actual bias.

**C. Cultural Bias**

The defense argues that the publication of letters expressing prejudice against foreign-born individuals signals that such views are acceptable and pollutes the jury pool. They contend this creates an atmosphere of prejudice that "negatively pervades the proceedings."

The trial court acknowledged these letters but found they represented the views of a "very limited section of society." The existence of protected speech containing biased views does not equate to a presumption of prejudice against the defendants. *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728. The defense has not provided evidence that these isolated opinions permeated the jury pool to the extent that a fair trial is impossible. The Court agrees with the trial court that the airing of such views, while unfortunate, does not establish the pervasive community prejudice required to overturn the venue denial.

**D. Statutory Authority and Remedy**

Finally, the defense argues that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 should be interpreted broadly to allow for a transfer to a federal court in another jurisdiction, asserting that the constitutional right to a fair trial requires this remedy when the local jury pool is tainted.

The defense's request for a transfer to a federal court is not supported by the statutory framework provided. A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 authorizes the court to "adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa." The statute is limited to locations within the Territory and was enacted for logistical convenience, not to effect a change of venue for prejudice. The defense's proposed remedy exceeds the plain language of the statute.

Furthermore, as noted in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), a change of venue is often unsuitable in small territories. American Samoa has a single jury roll of all registered voters. Transferring the case to a federal court outside the Territory is not authorized by the provided facts or statutes, and transferring the case to another location within the Territory would not change the jury pool. *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). The trial court acted within its discretion by denying the motion and offering alternative remedies to ensure a fair trial.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion. They have not met the heavy burden required to show presumed prejudice, and actual prejudice cannot be assessed absent voir dire. The statutory authority does not support the requested transfer to a federal court, and the unique characteristics of American Samoa do not lower the constitutional standard for presumed prejudice. Accordingly, the Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
