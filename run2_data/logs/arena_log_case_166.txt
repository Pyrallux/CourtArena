## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are drawn from Plaintiff's Complaint, which the superior court admitted as true for purposes of the demurrer.

Plaintiff Gary Ross suffers from strain and muscle spasms in his back resulting from injuries sustained while serving in the United States Air Force. ## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

When a superior court sustains a demurrer without leave to amend, this Court reviews the complaint de novo to determine whether it states a valid cause of action. The Court accepts as true all well-pleaded material facts but does not accept contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. ## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights or Impose Duties on Employers

The central question in this case is whether the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 creates private rights of action or imposes obligations on employers regarding medical marijuana use. This Court answered that question definitively in Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883, holding that the Compassionate Use Act "does not impose any obligation on employers to accommodate medical marijuana use" and leaves intact an employer's right to maintain a drug-free workplace.

The Compassionate Use Act, codified at Health and Safety Code section 11362.5, was added by voter initiative in 1996. Its text is explicit: the Act provides "a defense to certain state criminal charges involving the drug, including possession." The Act says nothing about employment, workplace policies, or the rights of employers vis-à-vis employees who use medical marijuana.

As this Court observed in Loder, the voters who passed the Compassionate Use Act "did not intend the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees." The Act was designed to provide a narrow criminal defense for patients who use marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation—not to reshape the employment relationship. To interpret the Act as creating employment rights would require this Court to insert language that the voters never enacted and that finds no support in the Act's text or legislative history.

Plaintiff's argument that his termination violates the Compassionate Use Act conflates two distinct concepts: (1) a defense to criminal prosecution, and (2) a private right of action against an employer. The Act provides the former; it does not create the latter. An employee who uses medical marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation may have a defense to criminal possession charges, but that defense does not immunize the employee from adverse employment actions based on employer's lawful workplace policies.

### B. Defendant's Preemployment Drug-Testing Policy Is Lawful Under California Law

Under California law, an employer may require preemployment drug tests and may consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions. In Loder, this Court explicitly held that "an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions" without violating state law. Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883.

Defendant's drug-testing policy is neutral and uniformly applied to all new employees. The policy serves legitimate business interests, including maintaining a drug-free workplace and ensuring that employees can perform their jobs safely and effectively. Plaintiff does not allege that the policy targets him individually or that it is applied differently to employees who use medical marijuana compared to those who use other substances.

Plaintiff argues that Defendant should have accommodated his medical marijuana use by exempting him from the drug test. However, California law does not require employers to create exceptions to neutral workplace policies for employees who use medical marijuana. The law is clear: employers may enforce drug-free workplace policies even against employees who use marijuana for medical purposes pursuant to a physician's recommendation.

The policy at issue here is not discriminatory. It applies equally to all job applicants regardless of their medical conditions or use of prescribed medications. Plaintiff was not terminated because of his disability; he was terminated because he tested positive for THC, a substance whose use, even when medically authorized, remains subject to employer regulation under California law.

### C. Plaintiff Fails to State a Valid FEHA Claim for Disability Discrimination

Plaintiff's FEHA claim fails because he cannot establish that he was discriminated against because of his disability. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff was terminated because he tested positive for THC—not because of his back condition. Plaintiff himself alleges that his "disability and use of marijuana to treat pain do not affect his ability to do the essential functions of the job."

Under the FEHA, it is unlawful for an employer to discriminate against a qualified individual with a disability because of the disability. However, the FEHA does not protect employees from adverse employment actions based on conduct that is independent of their disability. Here, Defendant terminated Plaintiff because of his positive drug test, not because of his back pain or his status as a disabled person.

Plaintiff attempts to frame his termination as discrimination based on disability by arguing that his termination was motivated by his medical marijuana use, which is related to his disability. However, this argument proves too much. If accepted, it would effectively immunize any employee who uses any substance—whether legal or illegal, prescribed or unprescribed—from adverse employment actions, so long as the use is related to a disability. The FEHA does not go that far, and this Court should not extend it in a way that would read employers' legitimate drug-free workplace policies out of the law.

### D. Plaintiff Fails to State a Valid FEHA Claim for Failure to Accommodate

Plaintiff's failure-to-accommodate claim also fails as a matter of law. The FEHA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations for known disabilities unless doing so would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the employer's business. However, the duty to accommodate is not unlimited. Employers are not required to accommodate disabilities in ways that would violate legitimate business policies or other laws.

Here, Plaintiff requested that Defendant exempt him from its preemployment drug-testing policy so that he could continue using medical marijuana. This is not a reasonable accommodation. A reasonable accommodation is designed to enable a disabled employee to perform the essential functions of the job. Plaintiff does not allege that his back condition prevents him from performing his job; he explicitly alleges the opposite. He seeks not an accommodation that would allow him to work, but an exemption from a workplace policy that applies to all employees.

In Loder, this Court rejected a similar argument, holding that the Compassionate Use Act does not require employers to accommodate medical marijuana use. The same principle applies here. Defendant's drug-testing policy serves legitimate business interests, and requiring Defendant to exempt Plaintiff from that policy would impose an undue hardship and undermine the policy's purpose.

Moreover, Plaintiff cannot show that accommodation was possible. The accommodation Plaintiff sought—exemption from a preemployment drug test—is fundamentally incompatible with a drug-free workplace policy. There is no way to accommodate Plaintiff's request without fundamentally altering Defendant's policy.

### E. Plaintiff Fails to State a Valid Claim for Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy

Plaintiff's wrongful termination claim fails because he cannot identify a public policy that Defendant violated. Under California law, a wrongful termination claim requires a showing that the employer's conduct violates a policy that is fundamental to the state's public policy. See Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178; see also Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887.

Plaintiff argues that his termination violates the public policy underlying the Compassionate Use Act—that patients should be able to use medical marijuana without fear of criminal prosecution. However, the Compassionate Use Act does not create a public policy favoring medical marijuana use in employment; it creates a criminal defense. The Act's purpose was to provide a defense to criminal charges, not to establish employment rights.

Moreover, even if the Compassionate Use Act expressed a public policy, that policy does not override an employer's right to maintain a drug-free workplace. As this Court held in Loder, the Act was not intended to address employer-employee relationships. To hold that termination for medical marijuana use violates public policy would effectively create a private right of action that the Act itself does not provide and that the voters never intended.

Plaintiff's termination does not violate any fundamental public policy of this state. Defendant terminated Plaintiff for testing positive for THC, consistent with its lawful drug-free workplace policy. This is not a violation of public policy; it is the exercise of a right that California law explicitly protects.

**A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

The plaintiff’s argument hinges on the assertion that the Compassionate Use Act (CUA) protects his use of marijuana in the employment context. The facts, as alleged in the complaint and admitted for purposes of demurrer, establish the following: Plaintiff Gary Ross suffers from a disability—strain and muscle spasms in his back—resulting from his service in the United States Air Force. On his physician's recommendation pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act of 1996, Plaintiff began using marijuana in September 1999 to treat chronic pain. In September 2001, Defendant offered Plaintiff a position as lead systems administrator, conditioned upon passing a preemployment drug test. Plaintiff tested positive for THC, was suspended, and ultimately terminated from employment. Plaintiff does not allege that his disability or marijuana use affected his ability to perform the essential functions of his job.

Despite these facts, the law is clear that Plaintiff has failed to state a valid cause of action.

---

## II. THE COMPASSIONATE USE ACT DOES NOT CREATE EMPLOYMENT RIGHTS

The central legal principle governing this case was established by this Court in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200]. In *Loder*, this Court explicitly held that the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) was enacted solely to provide a defense to criminal prosecution for the possession and cultivation of marijuana and contains no language addressing employer-employee relationships, workplace policies, or accommodation duties.

The text and history of the Compassionate Use Act are silent regarding employment relationships. As this Court reasoned in *Loder*, the voters who enacted Proposition 215 "did not intend the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees." *Loder*, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883. The Compassionate Use Act "does not impose any obligation on employers to accommodate medical marijuana use" and leaves intact an employer's right to enforce drug-free workplace policies. *Id.*

Plaintiff correctly concedes this point. The Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to certain state criminal charges involving marijuana, including possession under Health and Safety Code section 11357. It does not create any statutory right to use medical marijuana free from employment consequences. Defendant therefore had the legal authority to maintain its neutral drug-testing policy and to terminate Plaintiff for testing positive for THC, regardless of the fact that Plaintiff possessed a physician's recommendation for medical marijuana use.

---

## III. THE FEHA CLAIM FOR FAILURE TO ACCOMMODATE FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW

The California Fair Employment and Housing Act, codified at Government Code section 12900 et seq., prohibits employment discrimination based on disability and requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations for known disabilities. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a).) However, the duty to accommodate is not unlimited and does not extend to accommodations that conflict with legitimate business interests or lawful workplace rules.

As this Court held in *Loder v. City of Glendale*, supra, 14 Cal.4th at pages 882-883, employers may "require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." This principle controls the present case.

Plaintiff's requested accommodation—an exemption from Defendant's neutral drug-testing policy—is not designed to enable him to perform the essential functions of his job. Rather, it seeks to exempt him from a lawful rule uniformly applied to all applicants and employees. As Plaintiff himself alleges, his disability and marijuana use do not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of the position for which he was hired. He has worked in the same field since beginning to use marijuana and has performed satisfactorily.

A reasonable accommodation must enable the employee to perform the essential functions of the job. Here, Plaintiff does not need an accommodation to perform his job duties—he needs an exemption from a neutral workplace policy. Such an accommodation is unreasonable as a matter of law because it would require Defendant to waive an essential component of its lawful employment policy. This Court should decline to impose such a requirement on employers.

---

## IV. THE WRONGFUL TERMINATION CLAIM FAILS

To establish a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the termination violated a public policy that is fundamental and well-established. *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

The public policy embodied in the Compassionate Use Act is limited to providing a criminal defense for patients and physicians who use marijuana for medical purposes. It does not create a public policy favoring medical marijuana use in the workplace, nor does it limit an employer's right to maintain drug-free workplace policies.

As this Court held in *Loder*, California law explicitly permits employers to enforce drug-testing policies, and termination for violating such a policy does not implicate a fundamental public policy. Plaintiff correctly acknowledges that he cannot identify a public policy that overrides Defendant's right to maintain a drug-free workplace. The claim for wrongful termination must therefore fail.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on review of the closing arguments submitted by Plaintiff Gary Ross and Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. In his rebuttal, Plaintiff explicitly concedes that his claims cannot withstand the controlling legal authority. We review a judgment entered after a demurrer is sustained without leave to amend by determining whether the complaint states a valid cause of action. We treat the demurrer as admitting the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations of material fact but not its contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. (*Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241].)

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

Plaintiff concedes that the Compassionate Use Act (CUA) was enacted solely to provide a defense to criminal prosecution for the possession and cultivation of marijuana and does not address the rights and duties of employers and employees. This concession is consistent with the holding of *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], where this Court held that the CUA "does not impose any obligation on employers to accommodate medical marijuana use" and leaves intact an employer’s right to enforce drug-free workplace policies. Because the text and history of the CUA are silent regarding employment relationships, Plaintiff cannot establish a statutory right to use medical marijuana free from employment consequences.

**B. The FEHA Claim for Failure to Accommodate Fails**

Plaintiff further concedes that his claim for failure to accommodate under the FEHA fails. The FEHA requires an employer to provide reasonable accommodation for the known disability of a qualified individual, unless doing so would impose an undue hardship. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a).) However, as argued by both parties, the duty to accommodate does not require an employer to violate the essential functions of the job or to accommodate the use of illegal substances in a manner that conflicts with a lawful, neutral employment policy.

In *Loder v. City of Glendale*, supra, 14 Cal.4th at pages 882-883, the Court confirmed that employers may "require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." Plaintiff admits that his disability and marijuana use do not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of the job. Consequently, his requested accommodation—an exemption from Defendant’s neutral drug-testing policy—is not designed to enable him to perform his duties but rather to exempt him from a lawful rule uniformly applied to all employees. Such an accommodation is unreasonable as a matter of law.

**C. The Wrongful Termination Claim Fails**

Finally, Plaintiff concedes that his claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy must fail. To establish such a claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the termination violated a public policy that is fundamental and well-established. (*Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].)

The public policy embodied in the CUA is limited to providing a criminal defense for patients and physicians. It does not extend to creating a public policy favoring medical marijuana use in the workplace. As held in *Loder*, California law permits employers to enforce drug-testing policies, and termination for violating such a policy does not implicate a fundamental public policy. Plaintiff correctly acknowledges that he cannot identify a public policy that overrides Defendant’s right to maintain a drug-free workplace.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, and in light of Plaintiff’s concessions, the Court finds that the Plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The Compassionate Use Act does not require employers to accommodate medical marijuana use, and the Defendant’s neutral drug-testing policy is lawful.
