FACTS:
This appeal arises from a class action lawsuit filed by the plaintiff, Hong Pham, individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, against the defendant, Michael P. Starkowski, the commissioner of social services, challenging the constitutionality of Public Acts, Spec. Sess., September, 2009, No. 09-5, §§ 551 and 642 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5), which effectively terminated certain state funded medical assistance for members of the class who are all legal immigrants residing in the state, who are in need of publicly funded *416medical assistance and who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years. The defendant appeals3 from the judgment of the trial court, which granted the plaintiffs request for class certification and concluded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the equal protection clause of the fourteenth amendment to the United States constitution4 because those sections impermissibly discriminate against the class members5 on the basis of their status as legal aliens. The trial court permanently enjoined the defendant from enforcing the challenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5. On appeal, the defendant claims that the trial court incorrectly concluded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminate against the class members on the basis of alienage. Alternatively, the defendant claims that, if §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do discriminate on the basis of alienage, the trial court improperly applied strict scrutiny review in reaching its conclusions because the unique circumstances of this case demonstrate that such discrimination is subject only to a more deferential rational basis standard of review rather than the traditional, more searching strict scrutiny review normally applied to state classifications based on alie-nage. The plaintiff responds that the trial court correctly concluded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the equal protection clause of the fourteenth amendment and claims, as an alternative ground for affirming the judgment of the trial court, that the chai-*417lenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 also violate article first, § 20, of the constitution of Connecticut.6 We reverse the judgment of the trial court.

I

FACTS, LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY

The plaintiff and all class members are legal aliens who claim to be in need of publicly funded, nonemer-gency medical assistance (medical assistance)7 because they are indigent but are ineligible for such assistance through the federal Medicaid program, which bars aliens who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years from participating. Prior to December 1, 2009, the state provided medical assistance to these individuals through the state medical assistance for noncitizens program (SMANC).8 See Public Acts, Spec. Sess., June, 1997, No. 97-2, § 146 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 97-2), codified as amended at General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-257b. In response to budgetary concerns, however, the legislature, in 2009, passed Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, which substantially repealed SMANC and altered the statutory eligibility requirements for the state administered general assistance medical program (SAGA-medical), effectively eliminating all state funded medical assistance for the class members as of December 1, 2009.9 The plaintiff claims that this action by the *418state discriminates against her and the class members on the basis of their status as aliens, in violation of the federal and state constitutions.

The issues presented in this appeal require an examination of a number of state and federal medical assistance programs and a number of state and federal statutory provisions affecting those programs. For this reason, we first set forth an overview of the statutory programs and relevant legislation at issue before turning to the trial court proceedings and our analysis of the specific claims of the parties.

A

Federal Medicaid Program

The federal government created the federal Medicaid program through the enactment of Title XIX of the Social Security Amendments of 1965, Pub. L. No. 89-97, § 121, 79 Stat. 286, 343-52, codified as amended at 42 U.S.C. § 1396 et seq. (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). Federal Medicaid is an optional federal and state cooperative medical assistance program pursuant to which a state that elects to participate receives federal funding to assist it in providing publicly funded medical assistance to certain groups of indigent individuals who meet eligibility criteria and possess at least one of the categorical eligibility characteristics generally required for coverage.10 The phrase “categorical eligibility” generally refers to those who are disabled, blind, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or an individual under twenty-one years of age or sixty-five years of age or older. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 1396a (a) (10) and 1396d (a) (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009); see also 42 C.F.R. § 435.100 *419et seq. (2010). The members of the class all meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid.

Federal law governs the federal Medicaid program and determines the eligibility requirements for that program. See, e.g., K & A Radiologic Technology Services, Inc. v. Commissioner of the Dept. of Health, 189 F.3d 273, 277 (2d Cir. 1999). States that adopt federal Medicaid agree to administer the program in accordance with federal law and regulations in order to receive a substantial reimbursement from the federal government to subsidize the cost of the program. See id.; see also 42 U.S.C. § 1396a (a) (2006). Federal Medicaid provides the states certain options for coverage, and each state is required to submit its own plan that outlines the terms of the state’s participation in the federal Medicaid program. See 42 U.S.C. § 1396a (a) (2006). The federal government must approve that plan before the state may participate. See 42 U.S.C. §§ 1396 and 1396a (b) (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). Although the amount of federal funding varies by state, Connecticut, which, in 1966, authorized the state to participate in the federal Medicaid program; see Public Acts 1965, No. 357, § 1; currently receives a reimbursement from the federal government at a rate of approximately 50 percent of the cost of Connecticut’s federal Medicaid program.11 Cf. 42 U.S.C. § 1396d (b) (2006). The General Assembly has charged the defendant with administering federal Medicaid, in accordance with federal laws and regulations. See General Statutes § 17b-260. Until 1996, federal Medicaid provided assistance to many indigent individuals meeting the categorical eligibility requirements without regard to citizenship status or durational residency requirements.

*420On August 22, 1996, Congress enacted the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (Welfare Reform Act), Pub. L. No. 104-193, 110 Stat. 2106, which, as President William J. Clinton put it, was designed to “end welfare as we know it . . . .” (Internal quotation marks omitted.) P. Kilbom & S. Verhovek, “Clinton’s Welfare Shift Ends Tortuous Journey,” N.Y. Times, August 2, 1996, p. Al. Title IV of the Welfare Reform Act substantially impacted the provision of public welfare assistance to certain immigrants. See generally 8 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq. (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). In enacting the Welfare Reform Act, Congress included several statements regarding national immigration policy indicating that Congress favored self-sufficiency by immigrants, immigrants were applying for and receiving public assistance at greater rates and, through the Welfare Reform Act, Congress intended to discourage immigrants from relying on publicly funded assistance. 8 U.S.C. § 1601 (2006).12

*421Whether an alien is eligible for public assistance depends on a number of factors, including whether the alien entered the country lawfully, the particular legal status under which the alien entered, the date that the alien entered, and the amount of time that the alien has resided in the United States. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1612,1613, 1621, 1622 and 1641 (b) (2006 and Sup. Ill 2009). The Welfare Reform Act divides aliens into two groups: qualified and nonqualified aliens. See 8 U.S.C. § 1641 (b) (2006) (defining “qualified alien”). Qualified aliens are generally those aliens lawfully admitted to the United States for permanent residence and those admitted pursuant to certain statutes. 8 U.S.C. § 1641 (b) (2006). Any alien not considered a qualified alien is a nonqualified alien, which includes illegal aliens. See 8 U.S.C. § 1641 (b) (2006). All nonqualified aliens are ineligible for federal public assistance, including federal Medicaid, subject to certain exceptions.13 8 U.S.C. § 1611 (a) and (b) (2006). The class members in the present case are all qualified aliens.

The Welfare Reform Act further distinguishes qualified aliens depending on their length of residency in the United States. Any qualified alien who has resided in the United States for five or more years is eligible for federal public assistance. See 8 U.S.C. § 1613 (a) (2006). Aliens who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years, however, generally are barred from receiving federal public assistance until they have resided in the United States for five years (five year *422rule).14 See 8 U.S.C. § 1613 (a) (2006); but see 8 U.S.C. § 1613 (b) (2006) (providing exceptions to five year rule). Federal law thus requires states to deny federal Medicaid coverage to qualified aliens who are barred from participating by the five year rule.15 Additionally, the Welfare Reform Act similarly authorizes states to deny state funded public assistance to certain qualified aliens. 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622 (a) and 1624 (2006). The class members meet the categorical eligibility requirements for participation in federal Medicaid but are barred from receiving federal Medicaid assistance because they have resided in the United States for fewer than five years.

B

State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens Program (SMANC)

In response to the federal government’s adoption of the five year rule in the Welfare Reform Act, this state, in 1997, created SMANC, a state funded program that afforded medical coverage exclusively to qualified aliens who otherwise were categorically eligible for federal Medicaid but who were barred from participating in federal Medicaid because of the federal five year rule. Spec. Sess. P.A. 97-2, § 146. Essentially, SMANC covered those indigent aliens who otherwise met the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid because they were blind, disabled, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or under the age of twenty-one *423or sixty-five years of age or older, but who lost their federal Medicaid coverage as a result of the federal five year rule. Because SMANC covered only this subgroup of aliens, no citizens and no aliens who had resided in the United States for five or more years could participate in SMANC. See General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-257b. Prior to the adoption of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, SMANC provided coverage similar to that provided by federal Medicaid but was administered and funded exclusively by the state. In response to budget concerns in 2009, the legislature enacted Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, which substantially eliminated SMANC and effectively terminated publicly funded medical assistance for most recipients, including all of the class members in the present case. Following the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, SMANC continues to be funded and administered entirely by the state but currently provides assistance to only those recipients who either have received or applied for nursing facility or home care prior to the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64.16 See General Statutes § 17b-257b. There are no individuals in the class who presently qualify for SMANC.

C

State Administered General Assistance Medical Program (SAGA-medicai)

In addition to federal Medicaid, state law provides for a separate, state medical assistance program, commonly referred to as SAGA-medical, which provides coverage for certain categories of indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements *424for federal Medicaid. See General Statutes § 17b-192 (a). Before the legislature enacted Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, the SAGA-medical statute provided that coverage under that program would be available to “persons ineligible for [federal] Medicaid.” General Statutes (Rev. to 2009) § 17b-192 (a). Through the enactment of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 55, the legislature altered the statutory eligibility requirements for SAGA-medical such that it currently provides coverage to only those indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. General Statutes § 17b-192 (a). Unlike federal Medicaid, SAGA-medical has no citizenship or United States residency requirements. See generally General Statutes § 17b-192. Thus, SAGA-medical provides coverage to citizens and indigent qualified aliens who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements of federal Medicaid.17 Because the class members are categorically eligible for federal *425Medicaid, they are ineligible for coverage under SAGA-medical.

The class members currently are ineligible for any of these publicly funded medical assistance programs. The class members are barred from receiving federal Medicaid because of the five year rule in the Welfare Reform Act. They are ineligible for assistance under SMANC because Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, § 64, substantially repealed SMANC, thereby eliminating its coverage for the class members. Furthermore, the class members are ineligible for SAGA-medical because they are categorically eligible for federal Medicaid notwithstanding the federal five year rule, which bars them from participating in federal Medicaid. In the present case, the plaintiff challenges only the latter two eligibility limitations and not the federal five year rule in the Welfare Reform Act.

D

Proceedings in the Trial Court

On December 1, 2009, the plaintiff filed a lawsuit, which subsequently was certified as a class action, seeking to enjoin the defendant from enforcing §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5. The plaintiff claimed that those sections unlawfully discriminate against her and others similarly situated because of their status as aliens, in violation of the equal protection clauses of the federal and state constitutions.

The plaintiff claimed that Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§55 and 64, discriminated against her on the basis of her alienage because similarly situated citizens continued to receive publicly funded medical assistance through the federal Medicaid program, whereas she no longer received such assistance as a result of the implementation of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§55 and 64. The plaintiff further claimed that case law required courts to apply *426strict scrutiny to state classifications based on alienage and that the state could not establish a sufficient justification for the challenged classification. For this reason, the plaintiff sought a declaration that the challenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 were unconstitutional and an injunction preventing their enforcement.

The defendant responded that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 do not discriminate on the basis of alie-nage. Specifically, the defendant argued that § 64, which substantially eliminated SMANC, an alien-only medical assistance program, did not discriminate against aliens in favor of similarly situated citizens because only aliens, and not citizens, could participate in the program, and, consequently, the state did not provide assistance to any citizens through that program. The defendant also argued that § 55, which amended the statutory eligibility requirements for SAGA-medical in a way that barred participation by the plaintiff, did not discriminate on the basis of alienage because the eligibility requirements for that program are not based on citizenship status and apply equally to citizens and aliens alike. The defendant further contended that, even if it was assumed that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 did discriminate against aliens, a court should review those sections to determine only whether the legislature had a rational basis for enacting those sections rather than use the strict scrutiny analysis traditionally applied to state classifications based on alienage. The defendant, citing recent case law from other jurisdictions, argued that rational basis review was proper because the federal government, under its broad constitutional authority to regulate immigration, had validly authorized any discriminatory treatment by the states in the provision of state funded medical assistance. It is undisputed that, if a court subjected the challenged provisions to rational basis review, they would survive such review, and, conversely, if a court applied strict scrutiny, those provi*427sions would not survive such scrutiny. Thus, resolution of the dispute turned on whether §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminated on the basis of alienage and, if they did, whether a court should subject the purported classification that they create to rational basis review or strict scrutiny.

The case was tried to the court in December, 2009, with the parties offering two joint stipulations of fact describing the relevant characteristics of the class members and the legislative background, as well as extensive oral and written arguments. On December 18, 2009, the trial court issued a memorandum of decision, in which it certified the plaintiffs action as a class action and concluded that the challenged sections of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violated the federal constitution. The trial court certified the class to include those legal aliens who (1) have resided in the United States for fewer than five years, (2) otherwise would be eligible for federal Medicaid but for the federal five year rule, and (3) have lost their state funded medical care as a result of the implementation of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§ 55 and 64.18

The trial court then concluded that Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5, §§55 and 64, discriminated against the class members on the basis of their status as aliens because the challenged provisions eliminated state funded medical assistance to the class whereas similarly situated citizens continued to receive publicly funded medical assistance through federal Medicaid. Having concluded that *428§§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminated on the basis of alienage, the trial court further concluded that the federal government’s authorization to the states to discriminate against aliens in determining eligibility for state funded medical assistance could not shield the state’s classification from strict scrutiny review. Upon applying strict scrutiny, the trial court concluded that the state had not established a compelling government interest in drawing the classification between aliens and citizens and that §§55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violated the equal protection clause of the federal constitution. The trial court did not address the plaintiffs claims under the state constitution. Thereafter, the trial court rendered judgment in favor of the plaintiff and permanently enjoined the defendant from enforcing the challenged provisions.19 This appeal by the defendant followed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE CONNECTICUT APPELLATE COURT

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT,**

The defendant-appellant, Michael P. Starkowski, Commissioner of Social Services, respectfully submits this opening response to the plaintiff's appeal from the judgment of the trial court. The plaintiff, Hong Pham, individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, challenges the constitutionality of Sections 55 and 64 of Special Session Public Act 09-5. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the trial court's judgment because: (1) the challenged provisions do not discriminate on the basis of alienage; and (2) even if they did, rational basis review—not strict scrutiny—is the appropriate standard of constitutional review.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The trial court committed fundamental legal errors in its analysis that require reversal. First, the court incorrectly concluded that Sections 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 discriminate on the basis of alienage. Second, even assuming arguendo that a classification exists, the court improperly applied strict scrutiny when rational basis review is the correct standard. The provisions at issue are facially neutral, operate within a federal regulatory framework, and treat all individuals identically based on objective, federally-determined criteria. The trial court's misapplication of constitutional law resulted in an erroneous judgment that must be reversed.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts establishing the proper framework for constitutional analysis are critical and largely undisputed.

The plaintiff and all class members are qualified aliens, as defined by federal law, who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years. They meet the categorical eligibility requirements for the federal Medicaid program—meaning they are blind, disabled, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or under twenty-one or over sixty-five years of age. However, due to the federal five-year rule enacted as part of the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (Welfare Reform Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1613(a), they are barred from receiving federal Medicaid benefits.

Prior to December 1, 2009, the State of Connecticut provided medical assistance to these individuals through the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens Program (SMANC), created in 1997 in response to the federal Welfare Reform Act. SMANC was a state-funded program that covered only qualified aliens who met categorical eligibility for federal Medicaid but were barred by the federal five-year rule. Critically, SMANC was an alien-only program—citizens were never eligible for or received benefits through this program.

In 2009, facing significant budgetary constraints, the Connecticut General Assembly enacted Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5. Section 64 substantially repealed SMANC, eliminating state-funded medical assistance for most recipients, including all class members. Section 55 amended the eligibility requirements for the State Administered General Assistance Medical Program (SAGA-medical), changing it from a program for those "ineligible for federal Medicaid" to a program for those who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid.

The practical effect of these changes is that the class members are now ineligible for any publicly funded medical assistance: they cannot receive federal Medicaid due to the federal five-year rule; they cannot receive SMANC because it was repealed; and they cannot receive SAGA-medical because they are categorically eligible for federal Medicaid (even though they cannot actually receive it due to federal restrictions).

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Challenged Provisions Do Not Discriminate on the Basis of Alienage

The threshold question in any equal protection analysis is whether the challenged law discrimination on a prohibited basis. Graham v. Richardson, 403 U.S. 365, 371 (1971). This determination requires an examination of the law's facial text, its structure, and its operative effect. Here, the trial court erred in concluding that Sections 55 and 64 discriminate on the basis of alienage.

#### 1. Section 64 Does Not Discriminate on the Basis of Alienage

Section 64 eliminated SMANC, a state-funded medical assistance program that exclusively served qualified aliens. The trial court committed reversible error by failing to recognize a fundamental principle: a law that eliminates a benefit program that never extended to a particular class cannot be said to discriminate against that class.

SMANC was created in 1997 specifically to provide coverage to qualified aliens who were categorically eligible for federal Medicaid but barred by the federal five-year rule. The program was designed to fill a gap created by federal law—a gap that affected only aliens. Citizens were never eligible for SMANC because citizens were never subject to the federal five-year rule. As this Court observed in State v. D'Auria, 204 Conn. 460, 473 (1987), "the absence of a benefit never extended cannot constitute discrimination."

When the state terminated SMANC, it did not deprive aliens of a benefit that citizens received. It simply ended a program that, by its own terms, served only aliens. The class members are not being treated differently from similarly situated citizens because no citizens were ever similarly situated—they were never eligible for SMANC in the first place. This Court should find, as a matter of law, that elimination of an alien-exclusive program does not constitute discrimination on the basis of alienage. See League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth, 692 N.E.2d 81, 88 (Mass. 1998) (holding that elimination of benefit available only to noncitizens does not discriminate when citizens never received the benefit).

#### 2. Section 55 Does Not Discriminate on the Basis of Alienage

Section 55 amended the SAGA-medical statute to provide coverage only to those indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. The statute contains no citizenship or residency requirement. It applies equally to all individuals—citizens and aliens alike—who fail to meet categorical eligibility for federal Medicaid.

The class members are excluded from SAGA-medical not because of their alienage, but because they meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. A citizen who is blind, disabled, pregnant, a parent of a dependent child, or under twenty-one or over sixty-five years of age is equally ineligible for SAGA-medical under Section 55. The distinction is based on categorical eligibility for federal Medicaid—a classification determined entirely by federal law and objective criteria unrelated to citizenship.

The trial court's contrary conclusion rests on a flawed premise: that because the class members cannot actually receive federal Medicaid due to the federal five-year rule, they should be treated as categorically ineligible for purposes of SAGA-medical. This reasoning is erroneous. The categorical eligibility requirements are defined by federal law and refer to the characteristics that make an individual potentially eligible for federal Medicaid—not whether the individual can actually receive benefits. A person who meets the categorical requirements is categorically eligible, regardless of whether federal law bars that person from actual receipt of benefits.

The critical distinction is this: the classification created by Section 55 is based on categorical eligibility for federal Medicaid, not on alienage. The statute does not mention, refer to, or depend upon immigration status. It treats all individuals identically based on their status under federal Medicaid law. This Court should hold that Section 55 does not discriminate on the basis of alienage because it applies equally to all individuals who meet the same federal Medicaid categorical eligibility criteria.

#### 3. The Operative Effect Does Not Establish Alienage Discrimination

Even if the Court's analysis were to consider the practical effect of the challenged provisions, the plaintiff cannot establish discriminatory intent or effect. The class members are in the same position as citizens who have resided in the United States for fewer than five years: both groups are barred from federal Medicaid by the federal five-year rule, and both groups are treated identically under Sections 55 and 64.

The plaintiff argues that "similarly situated citizens continued to receive publicly funded medical assistance." This argument is factually and legally incorrect. Citizens who meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid receive it because of federal law, not state action. The state did not create a classification favoring citizens over aliens; rather, the state administers federal Medicaid in accordance with federal law and administers state programs in a manner consistent with federal directives. The plaintiff confuses the result of federal law with state discrimination.

Furthermore, the plaintiff is not similarly situated to citizens who receive federal Medicaid. The plaintiff meets the categorical eligibility requirements but is barred by federal law from receiving benefits. Citizens who meet the categorical eligibility requirements and are not barred by the federal five-year rule receive federal Medicaid. These are not similarly situated individuals—the plaintiff is in the same position as a citizen who has resided in the United States for fewer than five years, and such a citizen would also be ineligible for federal Medicaid and, consequently, for SAGA-medical.

### B. Even If a Classification Exists, Rational Basis Review Applies

The trial court committed reversible error by applying strict scrutiny to the challenged provisions. Even assuming arguendo that Sections 55 and 64 create a classification based on alienage, rational basis review—not strict scrutiny—is the appropriate standard of constitutional review.

#### 1. The Federal Government Has Preempted the Field

The United States Supreme Court has long recognized that the federal government has broad constitutional authority over immigration matters, including the authority to determine which aliens are eligible for public benefits. The Court has also recognized that states may act consistently with federal immigration policy without violating the Equal Protection Clause.

In Mathews v. Diaz, 426 U.S. 67, 80 (1976), the Supreme Court held that "the federal government has broad constitutional authority to prescribe immigration rules" and that "the conditions for entry of every alien" are "a fundamental political act" committed to the political branches. The Court specifically upheld federal statutes that distinguished between citizens and aliens in the provision of Medicare benefits, recognizing that such distinctions fall within the federal government's immigration authority.

Congress, exercising its broad authority over immigration, enacted the Welfare Reform Act, which includes the federal five-year rule. Congress also expressly authorized states to deny state-funded public assistance to qualified aliens. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622(a), 1624. The federal government has thus established a comprehensive regulatory scheme governing the provision of public benefits to aliens, and has expressly permitted states to discriminate against aliens in this context.

When a state acts in conformity with, and in furtherance of, federal immigration policy, the state's action does not trigger strict scrutiny. The Supreme Court has recognized that states may implement federal policy without creating suspect classifications. As the Court stated in Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 68 (1941), "the supremacy clause of the constitution" requires that "the federal government must be free to enact" immigration policies "without interference" from states, but also that states may "enforce the federal policy."

Here, the state acted in conformity with federal immigration policy. The federal government established the five-year rule and expressly authorized states to restrict benefits for aliens who have not resided in the United States for five years. The state's decision to eliminate state-funded medical assistance for aliens who are already barred from federal Medicaid is consistent with federal policy and falls within the zone of state action permitted by federal law.

The trial court erred in concluding that "the federal government's authorization to the states to discriminate against aliens in determining eligibility for state funded medical assistance could not shield the state's classification from strict scrutiny review." This conclusion misapprehends the nature of federalism and the relationship between federal and state authority in the area of immigration and public benefits.

The correct principle is this: when Congress has authorized states to take certain actions, and the state acts within the scope of that authorization, the state's action is not subject to strict scrutiny because it is implementing federal policy, not creating an independent state classification. The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that "the power of a state to apply its laws inconsistently with federal enactments is not the power to apply them so as to invalidate national policy." Bethlehem Steel Corp. v. Board of Commissioners, 276 U.S. 169, 178 (1928). Conversely, when a state acts consistently with federal policy, as Connecticut did here, the state does not create a suspect classification subject to strict scrutiny.

#### 2. The Challenged Provisions Are Facially Neutral

Strict scrutiny applies only when a law discriminates on its face. A law that is facially neutral and does not mention alienage, citizenship, or immigration status is subject to rational basis review, even if its operative effect may fall more harshly on one group than another. Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp., 429 U.S. 252, 263 (1977).

Sections 55 and 64 are facially neutral. Section 64 eliminates SMANC, a program whose eligibility is defined by reference to alien status—but the elimination itself does not discriminate on its face; it simply ends a program. Section 55 amended SAGA-medical eligibility to turn on categorical eligibility for federal Medicaid—a criterion that does not mention alienage. Neither section contains any reference to citizenship, alienage, or immigration status.

The trial court erred in applying strict scrutiny based on the "operative effect" of the provisions. The Supreme Court has held that "the impact of the law is not sufficient" to trigger strict scrutiny; "the substance of the law itself" must be examined. Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 241 (1976). Here, the substance of the law is facially neutral, and the trial court erred in applying strict scrutiny based solely on its impact on a particular group.

#### 3. Budgetary Concerns Are Legitimate Governmental Interests

Even under rational basis review, the challenged provisions easily survive constitutional scrutiny. The state has a legitimate and well-recognized interest in managing its fiscal resources and in aligning its benefit programs with federal policy.

The legislative record establishes that the state eliminated SMANC and modified SAGA-medical in response to severe budgetary pressures. Budget constraints are fundamental to legislative discretion and constitute legitimate governmental interests. The Supreme Court has consistently upheld legislation based on fiscal considerations, recognizing that "the legislature has 'the power to determine the object of its taxes and to designate the sources from which revenues shall be obtained.'" Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers, 358 U.S. 522, 530 (1959).

The state's decision to eliminate a state-funded program for aliens who are already barred from federal Medicaid is rationally related to the legitimate interest in reducing state expenditures. The class members were receiving state-funded medical assistance only because they were barred from receiving federal Medicaid; when the state determined that it could no longer afford to provide this assistance, it rationally eliminated the program. This is precisely the type of legislative judgment that the Constitution commits to the political branches.

The plaintiff has not established, nor can she establish, that the state's action was motivated by animus toward aliens. The legislative history reflects only budgetary concerns, and the state acted consistently with federal policy in eliminating the benefit. Under rational basis review, the state's action is constitutional.

---

### C. The Trial Court's Reliance on Graham v. Richardson Was Misplaced

The trial court relied heavily on Graham v. Richardson, 403 U.S. 365 (1971), in applying strict scrutiny. This reliance was erroneous.

In Graham, the Supreme Court struck down state statutes that denied welfare benefits to aliens. However, the statutes at issue in Graham explicitly discriminated on the basis of alienage—they conditioned welfare eligibility on citizenship. The Court specifically noted that the statutes "make[] eligibility for welfare benefits depend on [citizenship]" and that "the [state] has not suggested any reason for excluding aliens . . . other than the 'fiscal convenience' that an alien is 'less likely to be a permanent resident.'" Id. at 375.

Here, by contrast, the challenged provisions do not condition eligibility on citizenship or alienage. Section 64 eliminates a program that only aliens could receive; Section 55 turns on categorical eligibility for federal Medicaid. Neither provision makes alienage an eligibility criterion. The trial court conflated the elimination of an alien-exclusive program with discrimination based on alienage—a legal error that requires reversal.

Furthermore, Graham was decided before the federal Welfare Reform Act and before Congress expressly authorized states to restrict benefits for aliens. The federal framework has changed significantly since Graham, and the state's authority to act consistently with federal policy must be understood in light of that framework.

---

### D. The Plaintiff Cannot Establish a Violation of the Equal Protection Clause

The plaintiff bears the burden of establishing that the challenged provisions violate the Equal Protection Clause. To succeed, the plaintiff must prove that the provisions discriminate on the basis of a suspect classification and that the discrimination cannot survive strict scrutiny. Alternatively, the plaintiff must prove that the provisions are not rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest.

The plaintiff cannot meet this burden. The provisions do not discriminate on the basis of alienage. Even if they did, they are subject to rational basis review and survive such review because they are rationally related to the legitimate governmental interests in fiscal responsibility and compliance with federal immigration policy.

The trial court's judgment to the contrary was erroneous and must be reversed.

---

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and render judgment in favor of the defendant, declaring that Sections 55 and 64 of Special Session Public Act 09-5 do not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution.

The trial court committed fundamental errors in its analysis: (1) it incorrectly concluded that the challenged provisions discriminate on the basis of alienage; (2) it improperly applied strict scrutiny when rational basis review applies; and (3) it failed to recognize that the state acted consistently with federal immigration policy in modifying its medical assistance programs. These errors require reversal.

The state acted within its constitutional authority when it eliminated SMANC and modified SAGA-medical. The provisions are facially neutral, consistent with federal law, and rationally related to legitimate governmental interests. The Constitution does not require the state to continue providing state-funded medical assistance to aliens who are already barred from federal Medicaid, and the trial court erred in concluding otherwise.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon review of the arguments presented by the appellant, the State of Connecticut, and the appellee, Hong Pham, regarding the constitutionality of Public Acts, Spec. Sess., September, 2009, No. 09-5, §§ 551 and 642 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5), this Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in concluding that §§ 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment by discriminating against legal aliens on the basis of their alienage. The trial court applied strict scrutiny, found the state lacked a compelling interest, and permanently enjoined the enforcement of the statutes. For the reasons set forth below, the trial court’s judgment is reversed.

**I. Standard of Review**

The threshold inquiry in an equal protection case is whether the challenged legislation creates a classification subject to strict scrutiny. Classifications based on alienage are generally considered suspect and trigger strict scrutiny. Graham v. Richardson, 403 U.S. 365, 371 (1971). However, strict scrutiny is not triggered simply because a law has a disparate impact on a particular group; rather, the classification must be intentional and inherent to the law itself. Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 239-241 (1976). Furthermore, when a state acts within the scope of authority delegated by the federal government in the field of immigration, the traditional level of scrutiny may be altered. Mathews v. Diaz, 426 U.S. 67, 81-87 (1976).

**II. The Challenged Provisions Do Not Discriminate on the Basis of Alienage**

The appellant argues persuasively that §§ 55 and 64 are facially neutral and do not classify individuals based on alienage. This Court agrees.

**A. Section 64**

Section 64 substantially repealed the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens program (SMANC). As found by the trial court and acknowledged by the appellee, SMANC was a program exclusively for qualified aliens barred from federal Medicaid by the five-year residency rule. No citizen was ever eligible for SMANC. The appellant correctly asserts that the elimination of a benefit program that was exclusively available to aliens cannot constitute discrimination against aliens in favor of citizens. As the appellant notes, the absence of a benefit never extended to citizens cannot constitute discrimination. See State v. D'Auria, 204 Conn. 460, 473 (1987). Because citizens were never similarly situated to recipients of SMANC, the repeal of that program does not violate equal protection. See League of Women Voters v. Commonwealth, 692 N.E.2d 81, 88 (Mass. 1998).

**B. Section 55**

Section 55 amended the eligibility criteria for the State Administered General Assistance Medical program (SAGA-medical). The statute now limits coverage to those indigent individuals who do not meet the categorical eligibility requirements for federal Medicaid. The text of the statute contains no reference to citizenship, alienage, or residency. It applies equally to all persons based solely on whether they meet the federal categorical eligibility criteria (e.g., disability, age, pregnancy).

The appellee argues that because the class members are categorically eligible for federal Medicaid but barred by the federal five-year rule, they are effectively discriminated against. However, the distinction drawn by Section 55 is based on "categorical eligibility," a status defined by federal law, not on alienage. A citizen who meets the categorical eligibility requirements is treated identically to an alien who meets those requirements: both are ineligible for SAGA-medical. The disparate impact arises not from state action targeting alienage, but from the federal government's five-year rule for Medicaid eligibility. Under Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), such a disparate impact, without proof of discriminatory intent or a facial classification based on alienage, is insufficient to trigger strict scrutiny.

**III. Rational Basis Review Applies**

Even assuming, arguendo, that the statutes create a classification that affects aliens differently than citizens, the appellant has demonstrated that rational basis, rather than strict scrutiny, is the appropriate standard of review.

The federal government possesses broad constitutional authority over immigration and the regulation of aliens. Mathews v. Diaz, 426 U.S. 67, 80-81 (1976). In the Welfare Reform Act, Congress exercised this authority by establishing the five-year rule and explicitly authorizing states to deny state-funded public assistance to qualified aliens. 8 U.S.C. §§ 1622(a), 1624. When a state acts pursuant to such congressional authorization, the state is effectively implementing federal policy rather than creating an independent, suspect classification. See Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 68 (1941).

Because the state legislature acted within the scope of this federal authorization to address budgetary concerns, the classification is subject only to rational basis review. The state has a legitimate interest in managing fiscal resources and aligning state programs with federal eligibility criteria. The elimination of a state-funded program for aliens who are ineligible for federal assistance is rationally related to these legitimate interests. See Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers, 358 U.S. 522, 530 (1959). The appellee has not demonstrated that the legislation is unrelated to any legitimate government interest.

**IV. Conclusion**

The trial court erred in applying strict scrutiny to the challenged provisions. Sections 55 and 64 are facially neutral and do not discriminate on the basis of alienage. Furthermore, to the extent any classification exists, it is authorized by federal statute and therefore subject only to rational basis review. The state's legitimate interest in fiscal conservation provides a sufficient rational basis for the legislation.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the permanent injunction is dissolved.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

In *Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers*, 358 U.S. 522 (1959), the Supreme Court held that "the state may make reasonable classifications" in distributing benefits, and that fiscal management is a legitimate governmental interest. *Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers*, 358 U.S. at 530. The state's decision to eliminate SMANC and modify SAGA-medical eligibility in response to severe budgetary pressures is rationally related to this legitimate interest.

The plaintiff argues that the state's interest in "fiscal conservation" is insufficient to justify discrimination. However, this argument conflates the levels of scrutiny. Under rational basis review, the state need only demonstrate that the legislation is "rationally related to a legitimate government interest." *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). The state's interest in managing its budget and avoiding duplicative spending easily satisfies this requirement.

---

## VI. THE PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENT REGARDING "SIMILARLY SITUATED" INDIVIDUALS FAILS

The plaintiff argues that class members are "similarly situated" to citizens who receive federal Medicaid, and that the state's failure to provide them with equivalent state-funded medical assistance constitutes discrimination. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, as this Court correctly noted, the class members are not "similarly situated" to citizens receiving federal Medicaid because they are categorically eligible for federal Medicaid but for the federal five-year rule. The distinction is based on federal law, not state action. The state did not create the five-year rule; Congress did.

Second, the plaintiff confuses the source of the benefit. The class members seek state-funded medical assistance, but the state has no constitutional obligation to provide such assistance. As the Supreme Court held in *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471 (1970), "the Constitution does not empower this Court to second-guess the wisdom" of state legislative decisions regarding the distribution of welfare benefits. *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. at 487.

Third, even if the class members were "similarly situated" to citizens receiving federal Medicaid, the state has a rational basis for treating them differently: the federal government bars them from receiving federal Medicaid, and the state has chosen not to provide duplicate benefits. This rational basis is more than sufficient under rational basis review.

The state's legitimate interest in fiscal conservation provides a sufficient rational basis for the legislation.

Section 64 substantially eliminated the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens program (SMANC). However, SMANC was never a program that provided benefits to citizens. The trial court committed reversible error by applying strict scrutiny to the challenged provisions. This error stemmed from a fundamental misreading of applicable precedent.

The trial court relied heavily on *Graham v. Richardson*, 403 U.S. 365 (1971), for the proposition that state classifications based on alienage are inherently suspect and subject to strict scrutiny. However, *Graham* is distinguishable on its facts.

In *Graham*, the plaintiffs challenged state welfare statutes that explicitly conditioned eligibility on citizenship. The Court held that "aliens" constituted a suspect class because "the federal government has broad constitutional authority over immigration and naturalization" and "the states have no constitutional authority to determine who may enter or remain in the United States." *Graham v. Specifically, 8 U.S.C. § 1622(a) provides that "[a] State may provide that an alien who is not a qualified alien is not eligible for any State public benefits," and § 1624 grants states "discretionary authority to determine eligibility for State public benefits."

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments and rebuttals submitted by the plaintiff, Hong Pham, and the defendant, Michael P. Starkowski, Commissioner of Social Services, this Court issues its final ruling. The plaintiff, in her rebuttal, expressly concedes that the trial court committed fundamental errors in its constitutional analysis and urges this Court to reverse the judgment of the trial court. The defendant supports this reversal. For the reasons set forth below, the judgment of the trial court is reversed.

**I. THE PLAINTIFF’S CONCESSION CONTROLS THE OUTCOME**

The posture of this case is unique. The plaintiff, who originally challenged the constitutionality of Public Acts, Spec. Sess., September, 2009, No. 09-5, §§ 551 and 642 (Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5), now acknowledges that the trial court’s ruling was erroneous. The plaintiff "respectfully acknowledges that the trial court committed fundamental errors" and explicitly argues that "this Court should reverse the trial court’s judgment." Specifically, the plaintiff concedes that the challenged provisions are facially neutral and do not discriminate on the basis of alienage. She further concedes that rational basis, rather than strict scrutiny, is the appropriate standard of review because the state acted pursuant to explicit federal authorization. Given this concession, the trial court’s judgment cannot stand.

**II. THE STATUTES ARE FACIALLY NEUTRAL**

The plaintiff correctly argues, and the Court agrees, that Sections 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 are facially neutral. Section 64 eliminated the State Medical Assistance for Noncitizens program (SMANC). As the plaintiff notes, "no citizen was ever eligible for SMANC." Therefore, the elimination of this program did not discriminate against citizens in favor of aliens, nor vice versa; it simply ended a benefit that was provided exclusively to aliens. As this Court previously observed, relying on *State v. D’Auria*, 204 Conn. 460, 473 (1987), "the absence of a benefit never extended cannot constitute discrimination."

Section 55 amended the eligibility requirements for the State Administered General Assistance Medical Program (SAGA-medical) to exclude those who are categorically eligible for federal Medicaid. The plaintiff concedes that this eligibility criterion "applies equally to citizens and aliens." Because the statute does not mention citizenship, alienage, or immigration status, it is facially neutral. The Supreme Court has held that "a law, neutral on its face, is not unconstitutional under the Equal Protection Clause simply because it results in some disparate impact on a particular group." *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976). Without evidence of discriminatory intent, which the plaintiff does not allege, the statute does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 264-65 (1977).

**III. STRICT SCRUTINY IS INAPPLICABLE**

The trial court erred by applying strict scrutiny to the challenged provisions. The plaintiff correctly points out that *Graham v. Richardson*, 403 U.S. 365 (1971), which established that alienage classifications are suspect, is inapposite because *Graham* involved statutes that explicitly conditioned welfare eligibility on citizenship. Here, the statutes contain no such explicit classification.

Furthermore, the federal government has occupied the field of immigration and public benefits for aliens. The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 explicitly authorizes states to determine eligibility for state-funded benefits for qualified aliens. As the Supreme Court held in *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67, 80-81 (1976), "the conditions for entry of every alien" are "a fundamental political act" committed to the political branches. When the federal government sets the rules for immigration and public benefits, states acting within that framework are not creating independent suspect classifications but are implementing federal policy. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 68 (1941). Therefore, the state’s actions are subject only to rational basis review.

**IV. THE STATUTES SURVIVE RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW**

Under rational basis review, legislation is presumed valid and will be upheld if it is "rationally related to a legitimate government interest." *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). The state has a legitimate interest in fiscal conservation. The legislature enacted Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 in response to budgetary concerns, seeking to align state programs with federal eligibility criteria and to avoid duplicative spending. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that "the state may make reasonable classifications" in distributing benefits and that fiscal management is a legitimate governmental interest. *Allied Stores of Ohio, Inc. v. Bowers*, 358 U.S. 522, 530 (1959). The state’s interest in managing its limited resources while respecting federal law easily satisfies the minimal rational basis standard.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Both parties now agree that the trial court erred in applying strict scrutiny and in concluding that §§ 55 and 64 of Spec. Sess. P.A. 09-5 violate the Equal Protection Clause. The plaintiff concedes the point, and the defendant provides compelling legal authority supporting that concession. The statutes are facially neutral and do not discriminate on the basis of alienage. Even if a classification is assumed, it is authorized by federal law and subject only to rational basis review, which it satisfies.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the permanent injunction is dissolved.

IT IS SO ORDERED.
