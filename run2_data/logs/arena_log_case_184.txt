**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, today we ask this Court to reverse a clear and fundamental error by the trial court: its improper modification and extension of a temporary injunction enforcing a noncompetition agreement against Mr. Bradley. The trial court’s decision was legally unsound because it ignored critical evidence that Health Coalition, Inc. materially breached its employment agreement with Mr. Bradley—thereby excusing him from any obligation under the covenant—and failed to apply the well-established legal standards required for injunctive relief. As a result, the trial court abused its discretion, and this Court must correct that error to uphold the principles of contract law and judicial integrity.  

To begin, the trial court erred by granting and extending the injunction without ensuring that Health Coalition met its burden to demonstrate a *substantial likelihood of success on the merits*. For a temporary injunction to issue, Florida law requires the movant to prove, among other things, that it is likely to prevail on the underlying claim. *See* **R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown, 617 So. 2d 305, 307 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993)**. Here, Mr. Bradley presented uncontroverted evidence that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement before his resignation. Specifically, he testified that the employer wrongfully refused to pay commissions he had lawfully earned and directed him to alter customer invoices to charge higher prices than agreed—a practice he reasonably believed constituted fraud. These actions, if proven, would violate the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing inherent in every contract under Florida law. *See* **W. B. Johnson, Inc. v. C. C. S., 747 So. 2d 455, 457 (Fla. 4th DCA 1999)**.  

Critically, Florida precedent is unequivocal: when an employer commits a material breach of an employment contract, the employee is relieved of all obligations under the agreement—including noncompetition covenants. In **R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown**, the First District Court of Appeal reversed a temporary injunction enforcing a noncompete because the employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that "excused the employee from performing his contractual duties." *See* 617 So. 2d at 308. Similarly, in **W. B. Johnson, Inc. v. C. C. S.**, the Fourth District held that "genuine issues of fact regarding an employer’s alleged breach of contract preclude a finding that the movant has a substantial likelihood of success on the merits," mandating reversal of the injunction. *See* 747 So. 2d at 458. Here, the trial court dismissed Mr. Bradley’s breach-of-contract defense without proper analysis—despite his testimony about unpaid commissions and invoice alterations—and proceeded to enforce the covenant as if Health Coalition had fulfilled all its obligations. That was legal error.  

Moreover, the trial court ignored the threshold requirement that Health Coalition suffer *irreparable harm* absent the injunction. The employer’s claim of "harm" from competition is entirely contingent on the enforceability of the noncompete—and that enforceability is defeated by its own breach. *See* **F. B. Johnson, Inc., 747 So. 2d at 459**.  

Your Honor, the trial court’s decision rested on a foundational misunderstanding of contract law: it treated the noncompete as inviolable despite clear evidence that Health Coalition’s own actions destroyed its enforceability. This error requires reversal. ## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF POSITION

Your Honor, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this response in opposition to Bradley's attempt to overturn the trial court's well-reasoned temporary injunction. Contrary to Bradley's assertions, the trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting and modifying the injunction, and Bradley has failed to demonstrate the requisite abuse of discretion warranting reversal. The evidence presented at the injunction hearing established that Health Coalition met its burden under Florida law, and Bradley's alleged "defenses" amount to self-serving testimony insufficient to defeat the injunction. This Court should affirm the trial court's order.

---

## I. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND A SUBSTANTIAL LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS

Bradley contends that the trial court erred by failing to find that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, Bradley's version of events remains wholly disputed. At the injunction hearing, Health Coalition denied that any instruction was given to alter invoices, and contested Bradley's characterization of his resignation as being "forced." The trial court, as the finder of fact, was entitled to credit Health Coalition's version of events. See *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc., 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995)* (holding that on temporary injunction review, the trial court resolves credibility conflicts and the appellate court will not reweigh evidence).

Second, even accepting Bradley's testimony as true for purposes of the injunction, he has not established a material breach sufficient to void the entire employment agreement. The alleged refusal to pay commissions, if proven, would constitute a breach of the compensation provisions—but the employment agreement contains a liquidated damages clause specifically addressing such disputes. The presence of such a clause indicates the parties contemplated continued performance despite disputes over compensation, and Florida courts have consistently held that a breach involving discrete monetary obligations does not excuse the employee's entire performance under the agreement. See *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co., 580 So. 2d 268, 270 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991)* (distinguishing between material breaches that defeat the purpose of the contract and isolated breaches subject to alternative remedies).

Third, regarding the invoice alteration allegation, Bradley's testimony established only that he *believed* such an instruction constituted fraud—he did not testify that any actual fraud occurred, that any customer was actually overcharged, or that any harm resulted. Speculation about potential fraud does not equate to proof of material breach. The trial court properly declined to void the entire agreement based on such attenuated evidence.

Bradley relies heavily on *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown, 617 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993)*, but that case is distinguishable. In Reynolds, the employer admitted it had not paid earned commissions and had no defense for that failure. Here, by contrast, Health Coalition has denied the breach and presented evidence that Bradley voluntarily resigned. The trial court was entitled to find, as it did, that the evidence did not establish a clear material breach defeating Health Coalition's likelihood of success.

---

## IRREPARABLE HARM WAS PROPERLY FOUND

Bradley argues that Health Coalition failed to demonstrate irreparable harm, but this argument ignores the nature of the harm inherent in covenant enforcement.

The noncompetition agreement specifically protects "active, paid up customers of the corporation" and contains confidentiality provisions. Once a former employee begins working for a competitor and selling competing products, the employer faces ongoing and potentially irreparable harm through customer relationships developed over years of investment. As this Court has recognized, "the nature of the business relationship with customers is inherently relational and cannot be protected by monetary damages once lost." *See Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc., 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995)*.

Bradley argues that Health Coalition failed to show he specifically solicited "active, paid up customers." However, the evidence established that Bradley went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and was selling blood plasma products—the exact same products he sold for Health Coalition. Given the identical product lines and the customer relationships Bradley developed during his employment, the trial court properly inferred a likelihood of solicitation of protected customers. Bradley's argument that specific evidence of solicitation was required ignores the practical realities of enforcing noncompetition agreements and the inherent difficulty of proving solicitation before it occurs.

---

## THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS FAVORS HEALTH COALITION

The balance of hardships strongly supports the injunction. Bradley voluntarily entered into the noncompetition agreement in March 1993, receiving consideration in the form of employment. He is now seeking to avoid that agreement after having already benefited from the employment it provided.

To the extent Bradley claims hardship from being unable to work in his chosen field, this Court should note that he chose to sign the agreement and could have negotiated different terms at the time. The hardship he now faces is the foreseeable consequence of his contractual commitment. Conversely, Health Coalition invested in training Bradley and developing its customer base, and it is entitled to the benefit of its bargain.

Bradley argues that enforcing the injunction "penalizes an employee for his employer's alleged wrongdoing." This argument iself-serving and unproven. The trial court specifically found that Bradley failed to establish his alleged defenses by the requisite standard, and this Court should defer to that factual finding.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction. The trial court properly applied the legal standards for injunctive relief, and Bradley has failed to demonstrate the abuse of discretion necessary for reversal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** (As Assigned)

**Pursuant to the review of the opening arguments submitted by both the Appellant and the Appellee, this Court renders the following preliminary ruling:**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case arises from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Martin J. Bradley, III appeals that order, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction because Health Coalition, Inc. materially breached the employment agreement. Health Coalition contends the trial court properly resolved factual disputes in its favor and met the requirements for injunctive relief.

A trial court’s ruling on a request for a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would not be adverse to the public interest. See *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305, 307 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Material Breach**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in determining that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. Bradley argues that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by failing to pay earned commissions and by instructing him to alter customer invoices. He asserts that such a material breach relieves him of his obligations under the noncompetition agreement.

Florida law is clear that an employer’s material breach of an employment contract excuses the employee from performance, including adherence to noncompetition covenants. In *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, the court held that an employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constituted a material breach that excused the employee from performing under a noncompete agreement. 617 So. 2d at 308. Similarly, in *W. B. Johnson, Inc. v. C. C. S.*, the court noted that genuine issues of material fact regarding an employer’s alleged breach preclude a finding that the movant has a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. 747 So. 2d 455, 458 (Fla. 4th DCA 1999).

Here, Bradley presented evidence that Health Coalition wrongfully refused to pay commissions he had earned. While Health Coalition disputes the characterization of the resignation and denies the instruction to alter invoices, the non-payment of commissions appears to be a factual dispute central to the contract's validity. The trial court’s entry of the injunction implies a finding that Health Coalition was likely to succeed on its claim that the covenant was enforceable. However, given the evidence of unpaid commissions, this finding is questionable. As stated in *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, a covenant not to compete is unenforceable where the employer has engaged in conduct that breaches the underlying agreement. 580 So. 2d 268, 270 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991).

Health Coalition argues that the presence of a liquidated damages clause indicates the parties contemplated continued performance despite disputes over compensation, citing *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d at 270. However, the existence of a remedy for a breach does not necessarily prevent the breach from being material enough to void the restrictive covenant. The defense relies heavily on the trial court’s discretion to weigh credibility under *Wells*, but where the alleged breach involves the failure to pay the compensation that formed the basis of the employment relationship, the trial court’s finding of likelihood of success on the merits is shaky. The evidence of unpaid commissions, if proven, constitutes a material breach that would excuse Bradley’s non-performance under *R. J. Reynolds*.

**B. Irreparable Harm**

Furthermore, Health Coalition was required to demonstrate irreparable harm. The harm alleged is the loss of customer relationships and confidential information. However, if the noncompetition agreement is unenforceable due to the employer’s material breach, the harm is not irreparable in the context of enforcing an invalid contract. See *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d at 270. Health Coalition relies on *Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc.*, 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995), to argue that the loss of customer relationships is inherently irreparable. While this is generally true, the enforceability of the covenant is a prerequisite to such protection. Because Bradley has raised a valid defense of material breach, the likelihood of success on the merits necessary to sustain the finding of irreparable harm is lacking.

**C. Balance of Hardships and Public Interest**

Finally, the balance of hardships and public interest considerations do not support the injunction. Enforcing a noncompetition agreement against an employee who may have been forced to resign due to the employer’s improper conduct, and who was denied earned commissions, would be inequitable. The public interest is not served by enforcing contracts that have been materially breached by the party seeking enforcement.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellant has raised significant factual and legal issues regarding the Appellee’s material breach of the employment agreement, which precludes the Appellee from demonstrating a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. Accordingly, it is ORDERED that the temporary injunction be reversed, and the case be remanded for consideration of Bradley’s defenses to the employer’s claims.

**DONE and ORDERED.**

**APPELLANT’S REBUTTAL TO HEALTH COALITION’S OPENING STATEMENT**  

Your Honor, Health Coalition’s opening statement mischaracterizes the law, ignores binding precedent, and improperly shifts the burden of proof at the temporary injunction stage. Contrary to Health Coalition’s assertions, the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction because it failed to recognize that Bradley’s material breach defense—which remains unresolved—precludes Health Coalition from demonstrating a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. This Court must reverse the injunction and remand for full consideration of Bradley’s defenses, as required by Florida law.  

### I. HEALTH COALITION FAILED TO ESTABLISH A SUBSTANTIAL LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS BECAUSE THE TRIAL COURT IMPROPERLY RESOLVED MATERIAL FACTUAL DISPUTES  

Health Coalition claims the trial court “properly exercised its discretion” by crediting its version of events and dismissing Bradley’s breach defense. This argument is legally untenable. At the temporary injunction stage, the court *cannot* resolve credibility conflicts or weigh evidence on the merits. *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995). Instead, the movant must show a *prima facie* likelihood of success, which Health Coalition utterly failed to do.  

Bradley presented uncontroverted evidence that Health Coalition wrongfully refused to pay earned commissions. As this Court recognized in its Round 1 ruling, “the non-payment of commissions appears to be a factual dispute central to the contract’s validity.” *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305, 308 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993), squarely holds that an employer’s failure to pay earned commissions constitutes a material breach that excuses the employee from performing under a noncompetition covenant. Health Coalition’s attempt to dismiss this dispute as “unproven” ignores the standard for temporary injunctions: when a defendant presents evidence of a material breach, the plaintiff cannot establish a likelihood of success because the breach defense goes to the heart of the contract’s enforceability. *W. B. Johnson, Inc. v. C. C. S.*, 747 So. 2d 455, 458 (Fla. 4th DCA 1999) (holding that “genuine issues of material fact regarding an employer’s alleged breach preclude a finding that the movant has a substantial likelihood of success on the merits”).  

Health Coalition’s reliance on the liquidated damages clause is equally misguided. The clause does not insulate the covenant from being voided by material breach; it merely provides a remedy for monetary damages. *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d 268, 270 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991) (explicitly stating that “an employer’s breach of the underlying agreement renders the covenant unenforceable,” regardless of other contractual remedies). By conflating a remedy for breach with the contract’s continued enforceability, Health Coalition misapplies the law. The trial court’s error here was fatal: it granted the injunction without acknowledging that the commission dispute alone destroys Health Coalition’s likelihood of success.  

### II. HEALTH COALITION CANNOT DEMONSTRATE IRREPARABLE HARM BECAUSE THE COVENANT IS UNENFORCEABLE  

Health Coalition claims irreparable harm flows from the “loss of customer relationships,” but this argument presupposes the covenant’s validity. *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d at 270, makes clear that “if the noncompetition agreement is unenforceable due to the employer’s material breach, the harm is not irreparable in the context of enforcing an invalid contract.” Health Coalition’s reliance on *Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc.*, 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995), is misplaced because that case assumed the covenant was enforceable. Here, the commission dispute—if proven—invalidates the covenant entirely, rendering the “irreparable harm” analysis irrelevant.  

Moreover, Health Coalition’s argument that Bradley “solicited active, paid-up customers” is speculative. The evidence shows only that Bradley works for a competitor and sells blood plasma products—without any proof of actual solicitation of protected customers. The trial court improperly inferred solicitation based on mere employment, which violates Florida law. *See* *Hoffman-La Roche*, 580 So. 2d at 270 (holding that injunctions require evidence of *actual* harm, not hypothetical risks).  

### III. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHARDS AND PUBLIC INTEREST FAVOR REVERSAL  

Health Coalition argues that Bradley “voluntarily entered” the agreement and thus “chose to bear the consequences.” But this ignores the foundational principle that courts will not enforce contracts where the employer has materially breached first. *R. J. Reynolds*, 617 So. 2d at 308. Forcing Bradley to forgo his chosen profession while Health Coalition allegedly withholds earned commissions would be profoundly inequitable. As the Round 1 ruling correctly noted, “enforcing a noncompetition agreement against an employee who may have been forced to resign due to the employer’s improper conduct... would be inequitable.” The public interest is not served by upholding agreements tainted by alleged employer misconduct.  

### CONCLUSION  

The trial court erred by granting the temporary injunction because Health Coalition failed to meet its burden on every element of injunctive relief. The commission dispute alone creates a material breach issue that precludes a finding of likelihood of success, and without an enforceable covenant, there can be no irreparable harm. The balance of hardships and public interest further compel reversal. This Court should reverse the injunction and remand for a full trial on Bradley’s defenses.

**APPELLEE'S REBUTTAL TO APPELLANT'S REBUTTAL TO OPENING STATEMENT**

Your Honor, the appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, through his rebuttal, has attempted to relitigate the merits of his material breach defense under the guise of responding to Health Coalition's opening statement. However, the appellant misapplies the law, ignores the limited scope of temporary injunction proceedings, and fails to address the fundamental procedural deficiencies in his position. The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction, as Health Coalition has demonstrated all elements required for such relief.

---

## I. THE APPELLANT MISCONSTRUES THE STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTIONS

The appellant argues that Health Coalition "failed to establish a substantial likelihood of success" because Bradley raised a material breach defense. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of temporary injunction proceedings. At the temporary injunction stage, the Court does not resolve disputed facts or determine the ultimate merits of the case. *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995). Rather, the Court must determine whether the movant has demonstrated a *prima facie* case that entitles it to provisional relief pending full adjudication.

The trial court, in granting the temporary injunction, properly found that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits of its claim that the covenant not to compete was enforceable. The covenant was validly executed as part of a legitimate employment agreement, and the appellant voluntarily signed the agreement in March 1993. The mere assertion of a material breach defense by the appellant does not, by itself, negate the likelihood of success where the employer has presented evidence supporting the enforceability of the agreement.

The appellant relies heavily on *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993), for the proposition that non-payment of commissions constitutes material breach excusing employee performance. However, the appellant ignores a critical distinguishing feature of that case: in *R. J. Reynolds*, the court found material breach based on *proven* non-payment of commissions over an extended period with no dispute as to the amounts owed. Here, by contrast, the appellant has not proven any non-payment; he has merely alleged it. The existence of a dispute regarding commissions—whether they were earned, whether they were payable, and whether the appellant is entitled to them—does not equate to proof of material breach.

Moreover, the appellant conflates two distinct issues: (1) whether commissions are owed, and (2) whether the covenant not to compete remains enforceable. Even assuming *arguendo* that some commission dispute exists, the covenant contains a liquidated damages clause specifically addressing breaches related to compensation. This provision demonstrates the parties' intent that monetary remedies—not rescission—would be the consequence of any compensation dispute. The presence of such a clause distinguishes this case from *R. J. Reynolds* and demonstrates that the trial court properly found a likelihood of success on the merits.

---

## II. THE APPELLANT'S MATERIAL BREACH DEFENSE RAISES GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT THAT PRECLUDE RESOLUTION AT THIS STAGE

The appellant contends that the trial court improperly "resolved material factual disputes" in favor of Health Coalition. This argument is self-defeating. If, as the appellant claims, factual disputes exist regarding the alleged non-payment of commissions and the alleged instruction to alter invoices, then these disputes cannot be resolved in the appellant[']s favor at the temporary injunction stage either. The appellant cannot have it both ways: he cannot claim that factual disputes preclude the employer's success while simultaneously asking the Court to find that those same disputes prove material breach.

The proper approach, which the trial court employed, is to recognize that the appellant has raised defenses that must be litigated at trial but that do not preclude provisional relief. The covenant not to compete is presumptively enforceable, and the appellant has not overcome this presumption. The evidence presented by Health Coalition—showing that the appellant signed the agreement, that he voluntarily resigned, and that he subsequently went to work for a direct competitor selling the same products—establishes a prima facie case for enforcement.

The appellant[']s assertion that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in "improper business practices" is unsupported by the record. The appellant has presented no evidence, beyond his own self-serving affidavit, that such instructions were given. The employer expressly denies that any such instruction was provided. Under these circumstances, the trial court properly found that Health Coalition demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits.

---

## III. HEALTH COALITION PROPERLY DEMONSTRATED IRREPARABLE HARM

The appellant argues that Health Coalition cannot demonstrate irreparable harm because the covenant may be unenforceable. This argument is circular and unsupported by Florida law. The very question before the trial court was whether the covenant was likely enforceable—and Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood that it is.

The harm alleged by Health Coalition—the loss of customer relationships and confidential information—is recognized under Florida law as inherently irreparable. *Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc.*, 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995). Once an employee goes to work for a competitor, the employer cannot un-ring that bell. Customer relationships, once solicited, are difficult if not impossible to recover. Confidential information, once disclosed, cannot be un-disclosed. The appellant[']s argument that "actual harm" must be proven before an injunction can issue misreads the applicable precedent. *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d 268, 270 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), stands for the proposition that an injunction may issue to prevent irreparable harm—not that the harm must have already occurred.

The appellant[']s attempt to distinguish *Personnel One* is unavailing. That case recognizes that loss of customer relationships is inherently irreparable *when the covenant is likely enforceable*. Here, the trial court found the covenant likely enforceable. The appellant cannot bootstrap his way out of an injunction by simply claiming the covenant might be unenforceable; he must prove at trial that it is not enforceable.

## IV. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS AND PUBLIC INTEREST SUPPORT THE INJUNCTION

The appellant argues that the balance of hardships favors him because he was "forced to resign" and denied commissions. These allegations, even if true, do not outweigh the harm to Health Coalition. The appellant voluntarily signed a covenant not to compete, accepted employment with Health Coalition knowing the terms, and then chose to go to work for a direct competitor. If the appellant believed he was wronged, his remedy was to litigate his claims—not to breach the covenant and then hide behind a self-serving material breach defense.

The public interest supports the enforcement of reasonable covenants not to compete. Such covenants protect legitimate business interests, encourage investment in employee training and development, and promote commercial stability. The appellant[']s position—that any employee can escape a valid covenant by simply alleging employer misconduct—would eviscerate these important policies.

## V. THE APPELLANT'S RELIANCE ON THE PRELIMINARY ROUND 1 RULING IS MISPLACED

The appellant[']s rebuttal relies heavily on the Court's preliminary Round 1 ruling. However, that ruling does not constitute binding precedent on the merits of Health Coalition's claims. The preliminary ruling was issued *sua sponte* by this Court in response to the opening arguments, without the benefit of full briefing or oral argument on the issues. The ruling itself acknowledges that the case must be "remanded for consideration of former employee Bradley's defenses to the employer's claims"—demonstrating that the Court recognized the existence of disputed factual issues that require resolution at trial.

The preliminary ruling's analysis of the material breach issue is preliminary, not final. It does not bind this Court or the trial court on remand. The defense is entitled to fully develop the factual record regarding the alleged commission dispute and the circumstances of the appellant[']s resignation, and to demonstrate at trial that the covenant not to compete is enforceable.

**UPDATED RULING**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** (As Assigned)

**Pursuant to the review of the rebuttals submitted by both the Appellant and the Appellee, this Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows:**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case arises from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Martin J. Bradley, III appeals that order, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction because Health Coalition, Inc. materially breached the employment agreement. Health Coalition contends the trial court properly resolved the motion for temporary injunction by finding a prima facie case for enforcement.

A trial court’s ruling on a request for a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would not be adverse to the public interest. See *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305, 307 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The primary issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion in determining that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. Bradley argues that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement by failing to pay earned commissions, thereby excusing his performance under the noncompetition covenant. He relies on *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993), to support the proposition that an employer’s failure to pay commissions constitutes a material breach.

However, Health Coalition correctly distinguishes *R. J. Reynolds*. In that case, the employer’s failure to pay commissions was established as fact, whereas here, the non-payment of commissions is merely alleged by Bradley and disputed by Health Coalition. At the temporary injunction stage, the trial court is not required to resolve conflicting factual assertions or determine the ultimate truth of the parties’ claims. See *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d at 1067. The trial court need only determine whether the movant has established a prima facie case.

Furthermore, the presence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement is a significant factor that distinguishes this case from *R. J. Reynolds*. The clause indicates that the parties contemplated that breaches related to compensation would be remedied monetarily rather than by rescinding the entire agreement, including the noncompetition covenant. See *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d 268, 270 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991) (noting that contractual remedies for breach are relevant to the enforceability of restrictive covenants). Because Bradley has not proven the material breach at this stage, and the contract provides a specific remedy for the alleged dispute, Health Coalition established a likelihood of success on the merits of enforcing the covenant.

**B. Irreparable Harm**

Health Coalition was required to demonstrate irreparable harm. The loss of established customer relationships and confidential information is recognized as irreparable harm in the context of noncompetition agreements. See *Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc.*, 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995). Bradley argues that because the covenant may be unenforceable due to the alleged breach, the harm is not irreparable. However, as Health Coalition established a prima facie case for the covenant’s enforceability, the threat of irreparable harm to the employer’s legitimate business interests is sufficient to support the injunction. See *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d at 270.

**C. Balance of Hardships and Public Interest**

The balance of hardships and public interest considerations support the injunction. While Bradley faces a temporary restriction on his employment, Health Coalition faces the potential permanent loss of customer goodwill and confidential information if the covenant is violated during the pendency of the litigation. The public interest is served by maintaining the stability of contractual agreements and allowing the employer to protect its legitimate business interests while the factual disputes regarding the alleged breach are resolved at trial. See *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d at 308.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, and that the balance of hardships and public interest favored the issuance of the injunction. The Appellant’s defenses, including the material breach defense, raise genuine issues of material fact that are more appropriately resolved at a full trial on the merits rather than at the temporary injunction stage. Accordingly, it is ORDERED that the temporary injunction be affirmed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR PLAINTIFF/APPELLANT**  
*Martin J. Bradley, III v. Health Coalition, Inc.*  

Your Honor, the trial court’s order modifying and extending the temporary injunction was a profound legal error. It resolved disputed factual issues at the preliminary injunction stage—a violation of Florida’s settled law—and improperly assumed the noncompetition covenant’s enforceability when material breaches by Health Coalition directly undermined it. This Court must reverse.  

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY RESOLVING DISPUTED FACTS ON MATERIAL BREACH  
Florida law is unequivocal: *at the temporary injunction stage, courts must not resolve factual disputes or determine the ultimate merits of a case.* **Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc., 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995)**. Here, Bradley alleged two material breaches: (1) Health Coalition’s refusal to pay earned commissions, and (2) instructions to alter invoices to defraud customers. Health Coalition denies these allegations, but that is irrelevant at this stage. The mere existence of a colorable defense to enforcement *precludes* a temporary injunction.  

The trial court’s ruling misapplied **R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown, 617 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993)**. In *R.J. Reynolds*, this Court held that an employer’s material breach—specifically, *proven* non-payment of commissions—excuses an employee’s performance under a noncompetition covenant. Here, Bradley presented sworn allegations of unpaid commissions and invoice fraud. Health Coalition’s denial does not negate the *possibility* of material breach; it confirms a factual dispute that must be resolved at trial. By ignoring this, the trial court violated *Wells* and effectively adjudicated the merits prematurely.  

The trial court also misread **F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S.S. & Co., 580 So. 2d 268 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991)**. While the contract contains a liquidated damages clause for compensation disputes, *Hoffman-La Roche* explicitly holds that such clauses *do not automatically prevent a finding of material breach for other contractual violations*. The liquidated damages provision addresses monetary remedies for specific breaches but does not insulate the entire agreement from rescission when the employer’s conduct goes to the core of the contract. If Health Coalition wrongfully withheld earned commissions—a breach of the employment agreement’s fundamental terms—the entire covenant may be unenforceable. The trial court’s attempt to distinguish *R.J. Reynolds* based on the liquidated damages clause was legally unsound.  

### II. IRREPARABLE HARM CANNOT BE SHOWN WHEN ENFORCEABILITY IS IN DOUBT  
Health Coalition’s claim of "irreparable harm" is circular and legally untenable. **Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc., 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995)**, requires that irreparable harm exist *only if the covenant is enforceable*. But the trial court assumed enforceability to find irreparable harm—exactly the question in dispute.  

If Bradley’s material breach defense succeeds at trial, the covenant is void ab initio. In that event, Health Coalition’s alleged "loss of customer relationships" is not "irreparable harm" but the natural consequence of competing in the marketplace. Florida law does not permit courts to grant injunctions based on hypothetical harm when the very foundation of that harm—the covenant’s validity—is contested. The trial court’s reasoning would allow employers to evade trial on the merits simply by alleging breach of contract, a result directly contrary to **Wells**.  

### III. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS AND PUBLIC INTEREST FAVOR REVERSAL  
The trial court’s analysis of the balance of hardships was one-sided. Bradley faces immediate loss of livelihood and professional reputation if forced to comply with an unenforceable covenant. Health Coalition, by contrast, claims only speculative harm contingent on the covenant’s validity. Where a material breach defense exists, the balance *must* tilt in favor of the employee.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENDANT/APPELLEE

## *Health Coalition, Inc. v. Martin J. Bradley, III*

---

### INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the Appellant's closing argument mischaracterizes both the applicable law and the record before this Court. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting the temporary injunction, and this Court should affirm that ruling. The Appellant asks this Court to reverse well-settled precedent by requiring a trial on the merits before a court may issue injunctive relief to preserve the *status quo*—a requirement that finds no support in Florida law. The defense respectfully requests that the Court affirm the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

---

### I. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE PRIMA FACIE STANDARD FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTIONS

The Appellant conflates the preliminary injunction standard with the burden of proof at trial. As this Court held in *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995), a trial court at the temporary injunction stage "is not required to resolve conflicting factual assertions or determine the ultimate truth of the parties' claims." The Appellant's argument that the mere allegation of material breach precludes injunctive relief turns the preliminary injunction standard on its head.

The Appellant cites *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993), for the proposition that an employer's failure to pay commissions constitutes material breach. However, as the trial court correctly observed, *R. J. Reynolds* is distinguishable on its facts. In that case, the employer's non-payment of commissions was established as fact through evidentiary proceedings. Here, by contrast, the non-payment of commissions is "merely alleged by Bradley and disputed by Health Coalition." The Appellant cannot convert a disputed allegation into an established fact simply by asserting it in his briefing.

The Appellant's reliance on *Wells* is particularly misplaced. In *Wells*, this Court explicitly held that a trial court may grant a temporary injunction based on a prima facie showing, without resolving contested factual issues. The Appellant asks this Court to hold that any colorable defense to enforcement automatically defeats a preliminary injunction—but such a rule would render temporary injunctions impossible in any case where the defendant raises a defense. This cannot be the law, and it is not the law under *Wells*.

---

### II. THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DISTINGUISHES THIS CASE FROM *R. J. REYNOLDS*

The employment agreement between Bradley and Health Coalition contains a liquidated damages clause specifically addressing compensation disputes. This contractual provision is not mere "boilerplate"—it represents the parties' negotiated agreement that breaches relating to compensation would be remedied through monetary damages rather than through rescission of the entire agreement, including the noncompetition covenant.

In *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d 268, 270 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), this Court recognized that "contractual remedies for breach are relevant to the enforceability of restrictive covenants." The parties in this case specifically contemplated that compensation disputes would be resolved through the liquidated damages mechanism, not through voiding the noncompetition covenant. The trial court properly considered this provision in finding that Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

The Appellant argues that *Hoffman-La Roche* stands for the proposition that liquidated damages clauses "do not automatically prevent a finding of material breach." This reading misappreends the Court's holding. *Hoffman-La Roche* addressed whether a liquidated damages clause could *override* a finding of material breach—which it cannot do for certain types of breaches. Here, however, the question is different: whether the existence of a liquidated damages clause for compensation disputes demonstrates that the parties did not intend for such disputes to excuse performance under the noncompetition covenant. The trial court correctly concluded that it does.

---

### III. IRREPARABLE HARM IS PROPERLY ESTABLISHED IN THIS CASE

The Appellant's argument that irreparable harm cannot be shown when the covenant's enforceability is "in doubt" misstates the applicable standard. The relevant inquiry is whether the employer has demonstrated irreparable harm *assuming the covenant is enforceable*—which is precisely what the prima facie showing requires.

In *Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc.*, 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995), the court held that "the loss of established customer relationships and confidential information is recognized as irreparable harm in the context of noncompetition agreements." Health Coalition has alleged that Bradley is soliciting its "active, paid up customers" while working for Bio-Med, Inc. If this occurs during the pendency of litigation, the harm is irreparable because once customer relationships are transferred, they cannot be recovered.

The Appellant's argument that harm is "speculative" ignores the purpose of temporary injunctive relief. The whole point of such relief is to preserve the *status quo* pending resolution of the underlying dispute. If the Appellant were correct that irreparable harm requires proof that the covenant will ultimately be found enforceable, temporary injunctions would never issue in covenant cases—exactly the result Florida law seeks to avoid.

---

### IV. THE BALANCE OF HARDSHIPS AND PUBLIC INTEREST SUPPORT THE INJUNCTION

The Appellant contends that the balance of hardships "must tilt in favor of the employee" when a material breach defense exists. This argument proves too much. Under the Appellant's reasoning, any employee accused of violating a noncompetition covenant could defeat injunctive relief simply by alleging that the employer breached the agreement. This would render noncompetition covenants virtually unenforceable through preliminary injunction—contrary to the well-settled principle that such covenants are valid and enforceable when properly limited in scope and duration.

Here, the covenant restricts Bradley from soliciting "any active, paid up customers of the corporation" for a period of two years. This is a limited restriction that protects Health Coalition's legitimate business interests without unduly burdening Bradley's ability to earn a living. The public interest is served by enforcing valid contracts and allowing employers to protect their confidential information and customer relationships while litigation proceeds.

The Appellant emphasizes the harm to Bradley from being "forced to comply with an unenforceable covenant." But this argument assumes the very question in dispute—whether the covenant is unenforceable. At the preliminary injunction stage, the Court must assume the covenant is enforceable (for purposes of evaluating the employer's prima facie case) and preserve the *status quo* until that question can be resolved at trial.

---

### V. THE APPELLANT'S POLICY ARGUMENTS DO NOT SUPPORT REVERSAL

The Appellant warns that allowing preliminary injunctions in covenant cases "would empower employers to weaponize temporary injunctions to silence employees who raise good-faith disputes." This Court should reject this alarmist rhetoric. The standard for temporary injunctions is rigorous: the employer must demonstrate substantial likelihood of success, irreparable harm, balance of hardships, and public interest. An employer cannot obtain a temporary injunction simply by filing a lawsuit—the employer must make a colorable showing on each element.

Moreover, the Appellant's proposed rule would produce the opposite result from what he suggests. Under his theory, an employer could never obtain injunctive relief pending trial, meaning that an employee could immediately begin soliciting the employer's customers while litigation proceeds. By the time a trial occurs, the damage would be done—customer relationships transferred, confidential information disclosed, and the employer's business permanently harmed. This is precisely the harm that preliminary injunctions are designed to prevent.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings. The trial court properly applied the prima facie standard for temporary injunctions, correctly distinguished *R. J. Reynolds* based on the presence of a liquidated damages clause, and properly found that Health Coalition demonstrated irreparable harm, a favorable balance of hardships, and that the injunction serves the public interest. The Appellant's defenses—however colorable—raise issues of fact that are properly resolved at trial, not at the preliminary injunction stage.

The trial court's ruling was well-supported by precedent and within its discretion. This Court should affirm.

**FINAL RULING**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** (As Assigned)

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments submitted by both the Appellant and the Appellee, this Court issues its final ruling as follows:**

**I. PROCEDURAL HISTORY AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case is an appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction that sought to enforce a covenant not to compete. Martin J. Bradley, III, appeals the order, asserting that the trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction because Health Coalition, Inc. allegedly materially breached the employment agreement. Health Coalition, Inc., cross-argues that the trial court properly applied the law governing temporary injunctions.

The standard of review for a trial court’s ruling on a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. See *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d 1064, 1067 (Fla. 1st DCA 1995). To obtain a temporary injunction, the movant must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that irreparable harm will result if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the potential harm to the respondent; and (4) that the injunction would not be adverse to the public interest. See *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305, 307 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and the Material Breach Defense**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by granting the injunction despite his defense of material breach. Specifically, he contends that Health Coalition’s refusal to pay earned commissions and its alleged instruction to alter invoices constitute a material breach that excuses his performance under the noncompetition covenant. The Appellant relies on *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d 305 (Fla. 1st DCA 1993), for the proposition that an employer’s failure to pay commissions excuses an employee from adhering to a noncompetition agreement.

The Appellee counters that *R. J. Reynolds* is distinguishable because, in that case, the employer’s failure to pay commissions was established as fact, whereas here, the non-payment is merely alleged and disputed. This Court agrees with the Appellee. The trial court at the temporary injunction stage is not required to resolve conflicting factual assertions or determine the ultimate truth of the parties’ claims. See *Wells v. Tallahassee Memorial Regional Medical Center, Inc.*, 659 So. 2d at 1067. The Appellant’s defense of material breach presents a genuine issue of material fact that cannot be resolved on the existing record. The trial court did not abuse its discretion by finding that Health Coalition established a prima facie case for enforcement despite the existence of this disputed defense.

Furthermore, the presence of a liquidated damages clause in the employment agreement supports the trial court’s finding. The Appellant argues that *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d 268 (Fla. 1st DCA 1991), does not insulate the employer from a finding of material breach. While this is true, the clause is relevant to the intent of the parties regarding remedies. The clause indicates that the parties contemplated that breaches related to compensation would be remedied monetarily rather than by rescinding the entire agreement, including the noncompetition covenant. See *F. Hoffman-La Roche Ltd. v. S. S. & Co.*, 580 So. 2d at 270. This contractual provision strengthens Health Coalition’s likelihood of success on the merits, as it suggests the parties did not intend for compensation disputes to automatically void the restrictive covenant.

**B. Irreparable Harm**

The Appellant contends that irreparable harm cannot be shown when the enforceability of the covenant is in doubt. However, the Appellee correctly notes that the relevant inquiry is whether the employer has demonstrated irreparable harm *assuming the covenant is enforceable*—which is the essence of the prima facie showing.

The loss of established customer relationships and confidential information is recognized as irreparable harm in the context of noncompetition agreements. See *Personnel One, Inc. v. Service Alternatives, Inc.*, 664 So. 2d 1012, 1015 (Fla. 2d DCA 1995). Health Coalition alleged that Bradley was soliciting its "active, paid up customers" while working for a direct competitor. If this occurs during the pendency of the litigation, the harm would be irreparable because customer relationships, once lost, are difficult to recover. The Appellant’s argument that the harm is speculative relies on assuming his defense is valid, a factual determination reserved for trial. Therefore, the trial court properly found irreparable harm.

**C. Balance of Hardships and Public Interest**

The Appellant argues that the balance of hardships favors him because he faces the loss of his livelihood. The Appellee responds that the balance actually favors the employer because the injunction merely preserves the status quo pending trial, whereas allowing Bradley to compete could cause permanent damage to Health Coalition’s business interests.

This Court finds that the balance of hardships does not favor the Appellant. The temporary injunction restricts Bradley’s ability to solicit specific customers for a limited time; it does not prevent him from working entirely. Conversely, Health Coalition faces the potential permanent loss of customer goodwill and confidential information. The public interest is served by maintaining the stability of contractual agreements and allowing the employer to protect its legitimate business interests while the factual disputes regarding the alleged breach are resolved at trial. See *R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Brown*, 617 So. 2d at 308.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, and that the balance of hardships and public interest favored the issuance of the injunction. The Appellant’s defenses, including the material breach defense, raise genuine issues of material fact that are more appropriately resolved at a full trial on the merits rather than at the temporary injunction stage. The Appellant’s request to reverse the injunction based on unproven allegations of breach would require this Court to resolve factual disputes in his favor, which is not the function of an appellate review of a temporary injunction.

Accordingly, it is ORDERED that the temporary injunction be affirmed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**DONE and ORDERED.**
