The Defense submits that the State has failed to carry this heavy burden.

The State's argument relies heavily upon *State v. Johnson*, 583 So.2d 361 (Fla. 1991), however, this reliance is misplaced. In *Johnson*, the Florida Supreme Court addressed a situation where the improperly admitted evidence was merely cumulative of other properly admitted evidence. Here, by contrast, Officer Shugrue's comment struck at the very heart of the Defendant's constitutional privilege against self-incrimination—a privilege that lies at the foundation of our justice system.

The comment by Officer Shugrue, as described in the record, was not an isolated or fleeting reference. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a judgment of conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial following the arresting officer's comment on the Defendant's silence; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error as to the third point. Upon review of the record and the arguments presented by counsel, the Court affirms in part, reverses in part, and remands for further proceedings.

**I. The Officer’s Comment on the Defendant’s Silence Was Harmless Error**

The Appellant first argues that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial after Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. The State properly concedes that this conduct was improper under *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976), and *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965). The sole question, therefore, is whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and its Florida progeny *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. The State argues that the error was harmless because the evidence of guilt was overwhelming, citing the Defendant’s blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of .12 and his failure to perform field sobriety tests. Under Section 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), a BAC exceeding .10 establishes prima facie evidence that the defendant was under the influence.

The Defense contends that the State failed to meet its *DiGuilio* burden, arguing that the officer’s comment was not merely cumulative evidence but a direct attack on the Defendant’s constitutional privilege. While the Court agrees with the Defense that such comments by law enforcement are concerning and strike at the foundation of the right against self-incrimination, we cannot conclude that the error was reversible in light of the record. The evidence of intoxication was objective and substantial. As noted in *State v. Johnson*, 583 So.2d 361 (Fla. 1991), overwhelming scientific and behavioral evidence can sustain a conviction even in the presence of other errors. Here, the .12 BAC reading provided the jury with prima facie evidence of impairment independent of the officer's improper comment. Consequently, the State has satisfied its burden to show that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The trial court’s denial of the motion for mistrial is affirmed.

**II. The State Committed a Reversible Discovery Violation**

The Appellant next argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to produce the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Defense contends that these reports were material to the reliability of the .12 BAC reading and that the State’s failure to disclose them violated the principles established in *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), and *Strickler v. Greene*, 527 U.S. 263 (1999).

The lower court found no discovery violation, reasoning that the administrative rules do not require the State to prove the machinery’s fitness to establish a prima facie case, and that a duty to produce documentation arises only upon a specific challenge by the defendant. The Defense correctly argues that this reasoning creates a catch-22: a defendant cannot meaningfully challenge the condition of the equipment without first seeing the inspection reports, yet the State refuses to produce the reports until such a challenge is made.

The State’s obligation to disclose evidence favorable to the accused is not contingent on the defendant’s ability to guess at potential defects. Under *Brady*, suppression of material evidence is a violation of due process. The inspection reports are directly relevant to the accuracy and maintenance of the breathalyzer machine, which produced the sole scientific evidence of guilt in this case. By withholding these reports, the State denied the Appellant the opportunity to prepare a meaningful defense regarding the reliability of the .12 reading.

The State’s opening argument failed to address this discovery violation or defend the lower court’s ruling on this issue. In the absence of a counter-argument, and given the Defense’s sound application of *Brady* and *Strickler*, the Court finds that the State’s failure to disclose the inspection reports constituted a reversible error. The Defense was entitled to this information to assess the machine's reliability, and the State’s withholding of it prejudiced the Appellant’s right to a fair trial.

**III. Court Costs**

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs on the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Accordingly, the cause must be remanded to the trial court to vacate the order taxing costs.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's denial of the motion for mistrial but reverses the conviction based on the discovery violation. The judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial. The trial court is further directed to vacate the order taxing costs.

**SO ORDERED.**

By withholding these reports, the State denied the Appellant the opportunity to assess the machine's reliability. Consequently, the conviction is reversed on this ground.

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs on the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Accordingly, the cause must be remanded to the trial court to vacate the order taxing costs.

**FINAL RULING**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a judgment of conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial following the arresting officer's comment on the Defendant’s silence; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. Upon review of the closing arguments and the applicable law, the Court affirms in part, reverses in part, and remands.

**I. The Officer’s Comment on the Defendant’s Silence Was Harmless Error**

The Appellant argues that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial after Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s exercise of his Fifth Amendment right to remain silent. It is well established that the use of a defendant's silence to impeach their credibility or imply guilt violates the Due Process Clause. *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976); *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965). The State properly concedes that this conduct was improper.

The sole question is whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Under *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the State bears the burden of proving that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. The State argues that the error was harmless because the evidence of guilt was overwhelming, citing the Defendant’s blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of .12 and his failure to perform field sobriety tests. The State relies on *State v. Johnson*, 583 So.2d 361 (Fla. 1991), for the proposition that overwhelming scientific evidence of intoxication renders other errors harmless.

In its closing statement, the Defense explicitly concedes that the State met its burden under *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), to demonstrate that the officer's comment did not contribute to the verdict. Specifically, the Defense acknowledges that the .12 BAC reading provided prima facie evidence of impairment under Section 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), and that this evidence supported the conviction. Given the Defense's concession and the presence of uncontested scientific evidence of impairment, the Court finds that the State has proven that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Therefore, the trial court’s denial of the motion for mistrial is affirmed.

**II. The State Committed a Reversible Discovery Violation**

The Appellant argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to produce the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The Defense contends that these reports were material to the reliability of the .12 BAC reading and that the State’s failure to disclose them violated the principles established in *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), and *Strickler v. Greene*, 527 U.S. 263 (1999).

In its rebuttal, the State explicitly "respect[s] the State’s concession on the discovery violation." The State further acknowledges that the violation "requir[es] reversal." A concession of error by the State is binding on the court. The failure to disclose evidence that is material to the reliability of the State's primary scientific evidence undermines the fairness of the trial. *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963). Because the State has conceded the discovery violation and the necessity of reversal, the Court must reverse the conviction on this ground.

**III. Court Costs**

The State concedes that the trial court erred in imposing court costs on the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. Accordingly, the cause must be remanded to the trial court to vacate the order taxing costs.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's denial of the motion for mistrial but reverses the conviction based on the State's concession of the discovery violation. The judgment is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial. The trial court is further directed to vacate the order taxing costs.
