## INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response to rebut the Plaintiff's claims and to demonstrate that the trial court committed reversible error in committing Daniel J. Lalor as a sexually violent person under Wisconsin Statute Chapter 980. The commitment order should be reversed.

## I. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR'S MENTAL DISORDER CREATES A SUBSTANTIAL PROBABILITY THAT HE WILL COMMIT SEXUALLY VIOLENT ACTS

### A. The Legal Standard

Under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) and § 980.09, the State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the person is a "sexually violent person" — defined as a person who "suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability that he or she will engage in acts of sexual violence." This standard requires proof that goes far beyond mere speculation or statistical likelihood; it demands individualized clinical judgment that the specific person's mental disorder actually predisposes him to future violent sexual conduct.

### B. The Evidence Was Insufficient

The Defense submits that the State failed to meet its burden for the following reasons:

**First**, the State's experts relied primarily upon actuarial instruments rather than individualized clinical assessment. In *State v. Oakley*, 629 N.W.2d 200 (Wis. 2001), the Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized that commitment under Chapter 980 requires proof of dangerousness "as a result of a mental disorder." The Court emphasized that statistical predictions of general recidivism rates do not, standing alone, establish that a particular individual's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future violence. Here, both Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied heavily on actuarial scores — the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG — without sufficiently connecting these general statistical tools to Lalor's specific mental disorder and its specific contribution to his dangerousness.

**Second**, the RRASOR and similar instruments assess general risk factors for sexual offender recidivism but do not adequately account for the specific clinical characteristics of the individual. In *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d 590 (Wis. 2000), the Wisconsin Supreme Court acknowledged the limitations of actuarial instruments in predicting future dangerousness in commitment proceedings. The Defense notes that Dr. Maskel, the defense expert, testified regarding the problems with these testing instruments — specifically their inability to account for individual variation, the static nature of their risk factors, and the absence of dynamic factors that might indicate change.

**Third**, the diagnosis of "pedophilia" and "antisocial personality disorder," without more, does not establish a "substantial probability" of future sexual violence. Many individuals with these diagnoses never commit acts of sexual violence. The State was required to prove that Lalor's particular combination of disorders, in his particular circumstances, created a substantial probability of future acts — a burden that was not satisfied by the mere presentation of diagnostic labels and actuarial scores.

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRONEOUSLY ADMITTED THE RESULTS OF PHALLOMETRIC TESTING

The Defense contends that the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the results of phallometric (pl-ethysmograph) testing.

### A. The Nature of Phallometric Testing

Phallometric testing measures penile volume or circumference changes in response to sexual stimuli. The test purports to measure sexual arousal patterns. However, the reliability and validity of this testing for predicting sexual offense recidivism has been seriously questioned in the scientific community.

### B. The Admissibility Standard

Under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and its progeny, expert testimony must be based on scientifically valid reasoning and methodology. The trial court, acting as a gatekeeper, must ensure that the testimony both rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant to the task at hand.

### C. The Testing Was Improperly Admitted

The Defense submits that the phallometric test results should have been excluded for several reasons:

**First**, the scientific community has not accepted phallometric testing as a reliable predictor of sexual offense recidivism. The American Psychiatric Association and other professional organizations have noted significant limitations in the use of phallometry for predictive purposes. The test may measure sexual arousal patterns, but it does not reliably predict who will actually commit sexual offenses.

**Second**, even the proponents of phallometric testing acknowledge that it measures physiological response, not behavioral intention. The link between arousal patterns and actual behavior is attenuated. The RRASOR and similar actuarial instruments, which were also used here, do not incorporate phallometric results specifically because of this reliability concern.

**Third**, the admission of phallometric results likely confused the jury and injected impermissible prejudice. The suggestion that physiological responses to stimuli somehow demonstrates future dangerousness goes beyond what the science can support and likely led the trier of fact to draw impermissible inferences.

In *State v. Daniel J.Lalor* (the underlying case citation would appear here), this Court should find that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting phallometric test results without adequate foundation regarding their reliability for the purpose of predicting recidivism.

## III. THE ACTUARIAL INSTRUMENTS RELIED UPON BY THE STATE'S EXPERTS ARE INSUFFICIENTLY RELIABLE FOR COMMITMENT DETERMINATIONS

### A. The Instruments Lack Validation for This Purpose

The RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG were developed as research tools to study recidivism rates in aggregate populations. They were not designed, nor validated, for use in individual commitment proceedings where the stakes are the individual's liberty. In *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999), the Supreme Court made clear that Daubert principles apply to all expert testimony, including testimony based on clinical experience and judgment.

### B. The Expert Testimony Was Insufficiently Individualized

Dr. Moore's testimony focused on actuarial data — the RRASOR score of 5 out of 6, the 49.8% five-year and 73.1% ten-year recidivism probabilities — without adequately connecting these general statistics to Lalor's specific circumstances. The State must prove that the person's mental disorder causes or contributes to the dangerousness. ### B. The Evidence Here

These statistics cannot substitute for individualized proof that Lalor's specific mental disorder creates the requisite probability of future acts. The State was required to prove, and failed to prove, that Lalor's pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder, as distinguished from his prior criminal conduct alone, create the substantial probability of future violence.

The absence of any argument or authority from the State on this point constitutes a concession that the trial court erred in admitting the phallometric test results. This Court should maintain its reversal on this ground.

## II. THE STATE HAS FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE THAT THE EVIDENCE WAS SUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT COMMITMENT

### A. The Standard for Commitment Under Wisconsin Law

Under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) and *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d 348 (Wis. 1999), the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the person's mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" that he or she will commit sexually violent acts. This Court interpreted "substantial probability" to mean "more likely than not." A 49.8% probability—less than fifty percent—does not meet this threshold.

### B. The State's Reliance on the Ten-Year Rate Is Misplaced

The State emphasizes the 73.1% ten-year recidivism rate reported by Dr. Moore. However, this argument fails for several reasons.

First, the commitment statute and case law require proof of substantial probability within a relevant timeframe. The State presented no evidence establishing that a ten-year projection is appropriate for purposes of Chapter 980 commitment. Commitment is intended to address present dangerousness, not speculative future conduct over an extended period. *See State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d 590, 601 (Wis. 2000) (cautioning against reliance on long-term actuarial predictions without consideration of individual circumstances).

Second, the 73.1% figure represents a statistical prediction derived from the RRASOR instrument—a prediction that applies to a population of offenders with similar characteristics, not a clinical determination specific to Mr. Lalor. As this Court recognized in *Post*, actuarial instruments must be "tied to the individual's clinical profile." The State's experts failed to adequately explain how this statistical probability translates to Mr. Lalor's specific circumstances.

Third, even accepting the 73.1% figure at face value, it represents a 26.9% probability that Mr. Lalor will *not* reoffend within ten years. This does not establish that it is "more likely than not" that Mr. Lalor will commit a sexually violent act. The State conflates statistical probability with the individualized determination required by due process.

### C. The State Has Not Established the Required Nexus

The State argues that the experts provided "clinical assessments linking Lalor's diagnosed pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder to future dangerousness." However, the record does not support this characterization.

The facts establish that both Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied primarily on actuarial instruments—the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG—to arrive at their conclusions. Dr. Moore's report specifically relied on the RRASOR scoring system, which assigns points based on static factors such as prior offenses, age, and victim gender. The RRASOR does not assess dynamic risk factors or the individual's current mental state.

Dr. Fields similarly relied on these actuarial tools, along with the PCL-R assessment for psychopathy. While the PCL-R provides a measure of psychopathy, the State's experts failed to adequately explain how Mr. Lalor's specific scores on these instruments translate to a conclusion that his *mental disorder* creates a substantial probability of future violence, as required by *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002).

In *Kansas v. Crane*, the United States Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires proof of a "nexus" between the mental disorder and the person's future dangerous conduct. The State must demonstrate that the mental disorder *causes* or *contributes to* the dangerousness. The State's evidence failed to establish this connection. The experts diagnosed Mr. Lalor with pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder, but they did not adequately explain how these specific disorders, as opposed to other factors, create the claimed risk of recidivism.

### D. The State's Deference Argument Is Misapplied

The State cites *State v. O'Brien*, 2010 WI 23, 323 Wis. 2d 421, 780 N.W.2d 331 (Wis. 2010), for the proposition that appellate courts must defer to trial court credibility determinations. However, this case addresses credibility determinations, not the legal sufficiency of evidence. Appellate courts review de novo whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to sustain the commitment. The question is not whether the trial court believed the experts, but whether the evidence presented was legally adequate to meet the State's burden.

Even according deference to the trial court's factual findings, the legal question remains: does the evidence, as presented, establish beyond a reasonable doubt that Mr. Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexual violence? The State has not demonstrated that it does.

### E. Dr. Maskel's Testimony Establishes the Insufficiency of the Actuarial Evidence

The record reflects that Dr. Maskel, the defense expert, "testified regarding the problems associated with the testing instruments employed by Moore and Fields." Dr. Maskel exposed the limitations of these actuarial instruments, including their inability to account for individual variation and the absence of dynamic factors.

The State has not addressed Dr. Maskel's testimony or explained how her criticisms were overcome. Dr. Maskel's testimony establishes that the State's experts relied on instruments that cannot reliably predict individual recidivism risk. The State's failure to rebut this testimony leaves the record showing that the commitment rested on unreliable actuarial predictions rather than individualized clinical judgment.

The phallometric evidence likely inflated the perception of Mr. Lalor's dangerousness, while the remaining evidence failed to establish the required nexus between his mental disorder and future conduct. Under these circumstances, the Defendant cannot be said to have received a fair trial.

**PER CURIAM.**

This case is before the court on appeal from a judgment of the circuit court committing Daniel J. Lalor as a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The Defendant-Appellant appeals the commitment order, arguing that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a phallometric test and that the evidence was insufficient to prove he is a sexually violent person. The Defendant-Appellant filed a reply rebuttal addressing the State's arguments. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the court affirms its previous decision to reverse the judgment of the circuit court.

**BACKGROUND**

The facts indicate that on February 8, 1999, the State petitioned to detain Lalor, alleging he is a sexually violent person. The State relied on reports from Dr. Hugh Moore, Ph.D., and Dr. Sheila Fields, Ph.D. Dr. Moore diagnosed Lalor with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and utilized the Rapid Risk Assessment for Sexual Offense Recidivism (RRASOR), reporting a 49.8% chance of reoffending in five years and a 73.1% chance in ten years. Dr. Fields relied on multiple actuarial instruments, including the RRASOR, the Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool (MnSOST), the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (V-RAG), and others. The defense expert, Dr. Lynn Maskel, testified regarding the problems associated with these testing instruments. The trial court found that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder and that the disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence, ordering his commitment.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Admissibility of Phallometric Testing**

The defense argues that the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the results of a pl-ethysmograph (phallometric) test. The defense contends that under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999), expert testimony must be based on scientifically valid reasoning and methodology, asserting that phallometric testing is not a reliable predictor of sexual offense recidivism.

The State's rebuttal contains no argument addressing the admissibility of phallometric testing. As the defense correctly notes, the burden rests upon the State to demonstrate that the evidence upon which the trial court relied was properly admissible. The absence of any argument or authority from the State on this point constitutes a concession that the trial court erred in admitting the phallometric test results. The admission of unreliable expert testimony regarding future dangerousness is prejudicial and constitutes reversible error. See *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999). Therefore, the court agrees with the defense that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the phallometric test results.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The defense contends that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future acts of sexual violence. The State argues that the trial court properly weighed the expert testimony and that the actuarial instruments were properly considered alongside individualized clinical judgment.

**A. The "Substantial Probability" Standard**

Under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) and *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d 348 (Wis. 1999), the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the person's mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" that he or she will commit sexually violent acts. This court has interpreted "substantial probability" to mean "more likely than not." *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d at 353.

The State relies on the 73.1% ten-year recidivism rate reported by Dr. Moore. However, the defense correctly points out that Dr. Moore reported a 49.8% chance of reoffending within five years. A 49.8% probability does not meet the "more likely than not" threshold. The State has offered no authority establishing that a ten-year projection is the appropriate timeframe for determining "substantial probability" under the statute, nor has it explained why the five-year probability should be disregarded. Without such justification, the evidence fails to meet the standard set forth in *State v. Curiel*.

**B. Actuarial Instruments and Individualized Clinical Judgment**

The State argues that actuarial instruments were properly considered alongside individualized clinical judgment per *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d 590 (Wis. 2000). However, the facts show that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied primarily on actuarial instruments—the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG—to arrive at their conclusions. The defense notes that Dr. Maskel testified regarding the problems associated with these testing instruments, including their inability to account for individual variation and the absence of dynamic factors.

In *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d at 602, the court held that actuarial risk-assessment tools may be used in commitment proceedings so long as they are considered together with individualized clinical judgment. Here, the defense argues that the experts' reliance on static factors and general statistical predictions failed to tie the actuarial data to Lalor's specific clinical profile. The State's assertion that clinical assessments were made is not supported by specific references to the record demonstrating how the experts' conclusions transcended the actuarial data. Given Dr. Maskel's testimony regarding the limitations of these instruments, the State's evidence was insufficient to satisfy the requirements of *State v. Post*.

**C. The Nexus Requirement**

The State argues that the experts provided clinical assessments linking Lalor's diagnosed pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder to future dangerousness, satisfying the nexus requirement under *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). The defense counters that the experts relied on static factors from the RRASOR, which does not assess dynamic risk factors or the individual's current mental state, and thus failed to establish the required nexus.

In *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. at 413, the United States Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires proof of a "nexus" between the mental disorder and the person's future dangerous conduct. The State must demonstrate that the mental disorder *causes* or *contributes to* the dangerousness. The defense argues that the State's experts failed to adequately explain how Lalor's specific disorders, as opposed to general statistical characteristics, create the claimed risk of recidivism. The State's rebuttal does not provide a detailed explanation of how this nexus was established beyond the general diagnoses. Consequently, the State failed to meet its burden under *Kansas v. Crane*.

**D. Standard of Review**

The State cites *State v. O'Brien*, 2010 WI 23, 323 Wis. 2d 421, 780 N.W.2d 331 (Wis. 2010), for the proposition that appellate courts must defer to trial court credibility determinations. As the defense correctly notes, *State v. O'Brien* addresses credibility determinations, not the legal sufficiency of evidence. Appellate courts review de novo whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to sustain the commitment. Even according deference to the trial court's factual findings, the legal question remains whether the evidence presented was legally adequate to meet the State's burden. Because the evidence was legally insufficient, deference to the trial court's credibility assessments does not save the commitment order.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the court finds that the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the phallometric test results and that the State failed to meet its burden of proof that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of engaging in acts of sexual violence. The commitment order is reversed, and the defendant is ordered discharged from custody.

## I. THE ADMISSION OF PHALLOMETRIC TEST RESULTS CONSTITUTED REVERSIBLE ERROR

The State contends that the trial court properly exercised its gatekeeping function under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999), in admitting the phallometric test results. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the State bears the burden of demonstrating admissibility, yet it presented no foundational evidence establishing the scientific validity of phallometric testing as a predictor of sexual offense recidivism. The record contains no testimony explaining the methodology, error rates, or peer review of the specific test administered to Lalor. As this Court correctly noted, the State's complete absence of argument on this point in its initial briefing constitutes a concession that the trial court erred.

Second, the Defense presented expert testimony through Dr. Lynn Maskel regarding the problems associated with phallometric testing. The State argues the Defense "failed to demonstrate" unreliability, but this misplaces the burden. Under *Daubert*, the proponent of expert testimony must establish reliability through foundational evidence. The State's failure to present such evidence renders the admission erroneous regardless of Defense objections.

Third, the admission of unreliable expert testimony regarding future dangerousness is inherently prejudicial in commitment proceedings where liberty is at stake. The error was not harmless and constitutes grounds for reversal. *See Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999) (holding that expert testimony lacking scientific validity may constitute reversible error when admitted in proceedings affecting fundamental interests).

---

## II. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR'S MENTAL DISORDER CREATES A SUBSTANTIAL PROBABILITY OF FUTURE SEXUAL VIOLENCE

### A. The "Substantial Probability" Standard Requires More Likely Than Not

Under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) and *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d 348 (Wis. 1999), the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the person's mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" that he or she will commit sexually violent acts. This Court interpreted "substantial probability" to mean "more likely than not." *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d at 353.

The State's reliance on the ten-year 73.1% recidivism rate is misplaced for three reasons:

**First**, Dr. Moore's own report established a 49.8% probability of reoffending within five years. A 49.8% probability does not meet the "more likely than not" threshold. The State has offered no authority establishing that a ten-year projection is the appropriate timeframe for determining "substantial probability" under the statute. The statute contains no guidance on timeframe, and the Defense is correct that the five-year probability—representing the most immediate and clinically relevant projection—must be considered.

**Second**, the RRASOR instrument measures static risk factors derived from historical data and does not account for dynamic factors such as treatment progress, aging, or changes in the individual's mental state. As Dr. Maskel testified, these limitations undermine the reliability of long-term predictions.

**Third**, the State's argument that "Wisconsin law imposes no rigid timeframe" actually supports the Defense. If no timeframe is mandated, the State cannot rely exclusively on a ten-year projection when its own expert provided a five-year probability that fails to meet the standard. The State cannot cherry-pick the most favorable data point while ignoring the one that undermines its case.

### B. The Experts' Reliance on Actuarial Instruments Failed to Satisfy *State v. Post*

The State argues that *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d 590 (Wis. 2000), permits the use of actuarial instruments alongside clinical judgment. However, *Post* specifically requires that actuarial tools be "considered together with individualized clinical judgment." *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d at 602.

The facts demonstrate that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied primarily on actuarial instruments—the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG—to arrive at their conclusions. The Defense presented testimony from Dr. Maskel regarding the problems with these testing instruments, including their inability to account for individual variation and the absence of dynamic factors. The State's assertion that "clinical assessments were made" is not supported by specific references to the record demonstrating how the experts' conclusions transcended the actuarial data.

Critically, the record contains no evidence that either expert conducted an individualized assessment of Lalor's current mental state, treatment history, or capacity for change. The experts applied standardized instruments to Lalor's file without demonstrating how their clinical judgment modified or informed the actuarial predictions. This mechanical application of statistical tools fails to satisfy *Post*'s requirement of individualized assessment.

---

## III. THE STATE FAILED TO ESTABLISH THE CONSTITUTIONAL NEXUS REQUIRED UNDER *KANSAS V. CRANE*

The State argues that the experts tied Lalor's diagnosed pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder to his future risk, satisfying *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). This argument fails because the experts relied on static factors that do not assess the nexus between Lalor's current mental state and his future dangerousness.

In *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. at 413, the United States Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires proof of a "nexus" between the mental disorder and the person's future dangerous conduct. The State must demonstrate that the mental disorder *causes* or *contributes to* the dangerousness. This requires more than a diagnosis; it requires evidence that the disorder impairs the individual's capacity to control his behavior.

The Defense correctly points out that the experts relied on static factors from the RRASOR, which does not assess dynamic risk factors or the individual's current mental state. The RRASOR measures historical characteristics—age at first offense, victim gender, number of prior offenses, and relationship to victim—that cannot be modified through treatment or personal growth. These static factors do not demonstrate how Lalor's current mental disorder contributes to future dangerousness.

The State's argument that "the mental disorder contributes to dangerousness through impaired impulse control and deviant arousal patterns" is conclusory. The record contains no evidence explaining the mechanism by which Lalor's specific disorders create the claimed risk. The experts failed to explain how pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder—distinct conditions with different etiological profiles—combine to increase recidivism risk in Lalor's specific case. Without this individualized analysis, the State failed to establish the required nexus.

---

## IV. THE STATE'S RELIANCE ON *O'BRIEN* IS MISPLACED

The State cites *State v. O'Brien*, 2010 WI 23, 323 Wis. 2d 421, 780 N.W.2d 331 (Wis. 2010), for the proposition that appellate courts must defer to trial court credibility determinations. However, *O'Brien* addresses credibility determinations, not the legal sufficiency of evidence.

As the Defense correctly notes, appellate courts review de novo whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to sustain the commitment. *O'Brien* itself states that "the reviewing court does not reweigh the evidence or assess the credibility of witnesses; it only determines whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to sustain the judgment." *State v. O'Brien*, 780 N.W.2d at 337.

The question before this Court is not whether the trial court credited the State's experts over the Defense expert. The question is whether the evidence presented was legally sufficient to meet the State's burden of proof. Even according deference to the trial court's credibility assessments, the legal question remains whether the evidence presented was legally adequate to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexual violence.

Because the evidence was legally insufficient—due to the failure to meet the "more likely than not" standard, the failure to properly apply actuarial instruments alongside individualized clinical judgment, and the failure to establish the nexus required by the Constitution—deference to the trial court's credibility determinations does not save the commitment order.

---

## V. THE STATE'S ARGUMENT REGARDING "UNABLE TO CONTROL" IS FORECLOSED BY THE DEFENSE POSITION, NOT THE STATE'S

The State argues that *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 419, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002), holds that an express "unable to control" finding is not required. The Defense does not dispute this. However, the Defense notes that the converse is also true: the State cannot rely on *Laxton* to excuse its failure to prove the underlying substantive requirements.

*Laxton* held that the "substantial probability" standard under § 980.01(7) satisfies constitutional requirements. But this holding assumes the State has actually proven a "substantial probability." Here, for the reasons stated above, the State failed to meet this burden. The State cannot invoke *Laxton* to bootstrap insufficient evidence into a constitutionally adequate commitment.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense-Appellant respectfully requests that this Court affirm its previous decision reversing the trial court's commitment order. The Defense's arguments are consistent with the facts of record and controlling precedent. **APPELLATE COURT RULING**

**STATE OF WISCONSIN, PLAINTIFF-RESPONDENT,**
v.
**DANIEL J. LALOR, DEFENDANT-APPELLANT.**

**CASE NO.** 99-1234 (Assigned)

**DECISION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case is before the court on appeal from a judgment of the circuit court committing Daniel J. Lalor as a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The Defendant-Appellant appeals the commitment order, arguing that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a phallometric test and that the evidence was insufficient to prove he is a sexually violent person. The State filed a rebuttal arguing for deference to the trial court's findings and asserting that the experts' clinical assessments satisfied the legal requirements. The Defendant-Appellant filed a reply rebuttal addressing the State's arguments. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the court affirms its previous decision to reverse the judgment of the circuit court.

**BACKGROUND**

The facts indicate that on February 8, 1999, the State petitioned to detain Lalor, alleging he is a sexually violent person. The State relied on reports from Dr. Hugh Moore, Ph.D., and Dr. Sheila Fields, Ph.D. Dr. Moore diagnosed Lalor with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and utilized the Rapid Risk Assessment for Sexual Offense Recidivism (RRASOR), reporting a 49.8% chance of reoffending in five years and a 73.1% chance in ten years. Dr. Fields relied on multiple actuarial instruments, including the RRASOR, the Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool (MnSOST), the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (V-RAG), and others. The defense expert, Dr. Lynn Maskel, testified regarding the problems associated with these testing instruments. The trial court found that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder and that the disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence, ordering his commitment.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Admissibility of Phallometric Testing**

The defense argues that the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the results of a pl-ethysmograph (phallometric) test. The defense contends that under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999), expert testimony must be based on scientifically valid reasoning and methodology, asserting that phallometric testing is not a reliable predictor of sexual offense recidivism.

The State argues that the trial court properly exercised its gatekeeping function and that the test was administered by qualified experts using established methodology. However, the State's rebuttal fails to cite specific evidence in the record establishing the scientific validity of the test, such as known or potential error rates, peer review, or general acceptance. As the defense correctly notes, the burden rests upon the proponent of the evidence to demonstrate its reliability. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579, 597 (1993). The State's assertion that the defense failed to demonstrate unreliability is misplaced; the State's failure to provide foundational evidence regarding the test's reliability and relevance constitutes an abuse of discretion. The admission of unreliable expert testimony regarding future dangerousness is prejudicial and constitutes reversible error. *See Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 149 (1999). Therefore, the court agrees with the defense that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the phallometric test results.

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence**

The defense contends that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future acts of sexual violence. The State argues that the trial court properly weighed the expert testimony and that the actuarial instruments were properly considered alongside individualized clinical judgment.

**A. The "Substantial Probability" Standard**

Under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7) and *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d 348 (Wis. 1999), the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the person's mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" that he or she will commit sexually violent acts. This court has interpreted "substantial probability" to mean "more likely than not." *State v. Curiel*, 600 N.W.2d at 353.

The State relies on the 73.1% ten-year recidivism rate reported by Dr. Moore to satisfy this standard. However, the defense correctly points out that Dr. Moore also reported a 49.8% chance of reoffending within five years. A 49.8% probability does not meet the "more likely than not" threshold. The State argues that Wisconsin law imposes no rigid timeframe for recidivism predictions and that the ten-year projection is appropriate. While the statute does not specify a timeframe, the State has offered no authority establishing that a ten-year projection is the appropriate measure for "substantial probability" under the statute, nor has it explained why the five-year probability—representing the more immediate risk—should be disregarded. Without such justification, and given that the five-year probability falls below the statutory threshold, the evidence fails to meet the standard set forth in *State v. Curiel*.

**B. Actuarial Instruments and Individualized Clinical Judgment**

The State argues that actuarial instruments were properly considered alongside individualized clinical judgment per *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d 590 (Wis. 2000). The State asserts that Dr. Fields integrated multiple validated instruments with individualized clinical assessment. However, the facts show that Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied primarily on actuarial instruments—the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG—to arrive at their conclusions. The defense notes that Dr. Maskel testified regarding the problems associated with these testing instruments, including their inability to account for individual variation and the absence of dynamic factors.

In *State v. Post*, 613 N.W.2d at 602, the court held that actuarial risk-assessment tools may be used in commitment proceedings so long as they are considered together with individualized clinical judgment. The defense argues that the experts' reliance on static factors and general statistical predictions failed to tie the actuarial data to Lalor's specific clinical profile. The State's assertion that clinical assessments were made is not supported by specific references to the record demonstrating how the experts' conclusions transcended the actuarial data. Given Dr. Maskel's testimony regarding the limitations of these instruments, and the State's failure to demonstrate how individualized clinical judgment modified the actuarial scores, the State's evidence was insufficient to satisfy the requirements of *State v. Post*.

**C. The Nexus Requirement**

The State argues that the experts provided clinical assessments linking Lalor's diagnosed pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder to future dangerousness, satisfying the nexus requirement under *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). The State claims the experts tied the disorders to risk through impaired impulse control and deviant arousal patterns.

In *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. at 413, the United States Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires proof of a "nexus" between the mental disorder and the person's future dangerous conduct. The State must demonstrate that the mental disorder *causes* or *contributes to* the dangerousness. The defense argues that the experts relied on static factors from the RRASOR, which does not assess dynamic risk factors or the individual's current mental state, and thus failed to establish the required nexus. The State's rebuttal provides a general explanation of how the disorders might cause dangerousness but fails to point to specific evidence in the record establishing this causal link regarding Lalor. Consequently, the State failed to meet its burden under *Kansas v. Crane*.

**D. Standard of Review**

The State cites *State v. O'Brien*, 2010 WI 23, 323 Wis. 2d 421, 780 N.W.2d 331 (Wis. 2010), for the proposition that appellate courts must defer to trial court credibility determinations. As the defense correctly notes, *State v. O'Brien* addresses credibility determinations, not the legal sufficiency of evidence. Appellate courts review de novo whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to sustain the commitment. *State v. O'Brien*, 323 Wis. 2d 421, 437. Even according deference to the trial court's factual findings, the legal question remains whether the evidence presented was legally adequate to meet the State's burden. Because the evidence was legally insufficient—specifically because the five-year recidivism probability was below 50% and the State failed to justify relying on the ten-year projection—deference to the trial court's credibility assessments does not save the commitment order.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the court finds that the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the phallometric test results and that the State failed to meet its burden of proof that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of engaging in acts of sexual violence. The commitment order is reversed, and the defendant is ordered discharged from custody.
