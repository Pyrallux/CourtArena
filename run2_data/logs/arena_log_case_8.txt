### III. ARGUMENT

**A. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews questions of law de novo. Questions of fact are reviewed under a clearly erroneous standard. The characterization of property as marital or nonmarital is a question of law reviewed de novo.

**B. The District Court Correctly Classified the Death Benefits as Loretta Angell's Nonmarital Property**

Minnesota law provides a clear statutory framework for classifying property in dissolution proceedings. Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5, defines nonmarital property as including "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse."

The death benefits at issue here fall squarely within this statutory definition.

*1. The Benefits Were Acquired by Inheritance from a Third Party*

Levi Angell was Respondent's son—not her spouse. He is therefore a "third party" to the marital estate. The benefits were paid to Respondent upon Levi's death, triggered solely by her status as his survivor. This constitutes acquisition by inheritance under Minnesota law.

This Court has consistently held that death benefits paid to a spouse upon the death of another constitute nonmarital property. In *In re Marriage of Smith*, 477 N.W.2d 634 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), this Court held that life insurance proceeds paid to a spouse as survivor constitute nonmarital property because they are "acquired by inheritance from the decedent." Similarly, in *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 579 N.W.2d 421 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), the court classified military death gratuity benefits as nonmarital property when received by a survivor, emphasizing that such benefits are "inherently acquired through inheritance" regardless of the specific statutory source.

The seminal case on this issue is *In re Marriage of Lohr*, 304 N.W.2d 768 (Minn. 1981), in which the Minnesota Supreme Court stated unequivocally that "benefits received by a spouse as a result of the death of another are acquired by inheritance and thus nonmarital property."

*2. Appellant's Arguments Lack Merit*

Appellant contends that Respondent did not acquire the benefits "as a gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance" and that she failed to overcome the presumption that property accumulated during marriage is marital property. This argument misapprehends the nature of the benefits and the applicable legal framework.

First, the presumption of marital property applies to property acquired during the marriage through the efforts of either spouse or through joint acquisition. *See* Minn. Stat. § 518.54, subd. 4 (defining marital property as "property acquired by either spouse before or during the marriage"). This presumption does not apply to property acquired by inheritance, which is explicitly excluded from the definition of marital property. *See* Minn. Stat. § 518.54, subd. 5.

Second, Appellant's argument that the benefits were not "acquired by inheritance" because they derive from statutory programs rather than a traditional testamentary instrument fails. This Court has explicitly rejected such a narrow interpretation. In *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 579 N.W.2d 421 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), the Court held that military death gratuity benefits, though statutory in origin, are "inherently acquired through inheritance" when received by a survivor. The nature of the benefit—payment upon death to a designated survivor—determines its character, not the technical source of the authorization.

Third, the fact that Levi was a child rather than a spouse does not alter the analysis. The statutory definition of nonmarital property encompasses inheritance from "a third party"—that is, anyone other than the spouse. Levi, as the parties' son, is unquestionably a third party.

*3. The Funds Were Properly Segregated*

Minnesota law requires clear and convincing evidence to transform nonmarital property into marital property. *See* Minn. Stat. § 518.54, subd. 5. Here, the undisputed evidence shows that Respondent deposited the death benefits into a separate bank account in her name alone, and the funds remained segregated throughout the dissolution proceedings. There is no evidence of commingling or any intent to convert these funds to marital property. This segregation reinforces the nonmarital character of the benefits.

**C. Federal Law Prohibits the Division of Military Death Benefits**

Although the district court correctly classified the benefits as Respondent's nonmarital property, federal law prohibits the division of these benefits between the parties, even under state law theories of unfair hardship.

*1. The Anti-Attachment Provisions*

Federal law provides specific protections for military death benefits against attachment, garnishment, or other legal process. Under 10 U.S.C. § 1475, death-gratuity benefits are payable "to the person or persons entitled to receive them" and "may not be assigned, transferred, or pledged as security for any purpose." Similarly, the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance program contains protections against the benefits being subject to the claims of creditors. *See* 38 U.S.C. § 1970(g).

These provisions reflect Congress's intent to ensure that death benefits reach the intended survivors without interference from creditors or other claimants. As the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit held in *In re Marriage of Wilson*, 176 F.3d 449 (8th Cir. 1999), federal law preempts state marital property laws that would allow division of military retired pay. While this case involves death benefits rather than retired pay, the same principles of federal preemption apply.

*2. Application to This Case*

The district court relied on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to award Appellant $150,000 from Respondent's nonmarital property. However, this state-law authority cannot override federal anti-attachment protections. The death-gratuity benefits under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 and the Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance proceeds are protected federal benefits that cannot be reached by Appellant's claims, regardless of the district court's equitable concerns.

This Court should therefore affirm the classification of the benefits as Respondent's nonmarital property but reverse the award of $150,000 to Appellant, as such an award violates federal law.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Classification of Property**

The Defense argues that the district court erred in classifying the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property, contending that statutory death benefits do not constitute "inheritance" under Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5, and that the marital property presumption applies. The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive.

Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5, defines nonmarital property to include "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The Defense attempts to distinguish the precedent cited by the Prosecution, specifically *In re Marriage of Smith*, 477 N.W.2d 634 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), by noting that *Smith* involved a private life insurance policy rather than a government-provided benefit. However, the Prosecution correctly relies on *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 579 N.W.2d 421 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), which directly addressed military death gratuity benefits. In *Johnson*, the court held that such benefits are "inherently acquired through inheritance" regardless of their statutory origin. The Defense’s assertion that *Johnson* was wrongly decided or should be limited is without merit; as an appellate court, we are bound by the precedent of the Minnesota Court of Appeals absent controlling authority to the contrary.

Furthermore, the Defense’s "marital economic partnership" theory—that the benefits compensate for the loss of the son’s economic value to the marriage—cannot override the statutory definition. The benefits were paid to Loretta Angell solely by virtue of her status as the survivor of Levi Angell, a third party to the marriage. As established in *In re Marriage of Lohr*, 304 N.W.2d 768 (Minn. 1981), benefits received by a spouse as a result of the death of another are acquired by inheritance and are thus nonmarital property. The fact that the benefits were received during the marriage does not trigger the marital presumption of Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 4, because the property falls squarely within the nonmarital categories of subdivision 5.

The undisputed fact that the funds were deposited into a separate bank account and never commingled further supports the conclusion that the property retained its nonmarital character. Therefore, the district court’s classification of the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed.

**II. Division of Nonmarital Property**

The Defense contends that even if the benefits are classified as nonmarital, the district court’s award to Gordon Angell should be upheld under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2 (the hardship provision), and that federal anti-attachment statutes do not prohibit such an award in a dissolution proceeding. The Court disagrees.

Federal law explicitly protects military death benefits from attachment and transfer. Under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80, death-gratuity benefits "may not be assigned, transferred, or pledged as security for any purpose." Similarly, 38 U.S.C. § 1970(g) shields Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance proceeds from the claims of creditors. The Defense argues that these provisions are intended to protect against creditors, not against equitable division in a dissolution proceeding. However, the statutory language is broad, prohibiting assignment or transfer "for any purpose."

The Defense’s reliance on the distinction between creditor proceedings and dissolution proceedings is unpersuasive in light of the broad protective language of the federal statutes. A court order directing a spouse to pay a portion of these federal benefits to the other spouse constitutes a transfer of those benefits. The principle of federal preemption regarding military benefits, supported by cases such as *In re Marriage of Wilson*, 176 F.3d 449 (8th Cir. 1999), dictates that state courts cannot utilize state law equitable powers to circumvent federal anti-attachment protections.

Because the federal statutes prohibit the transfer of these benefits, the district court lacked the authority to utilize Minnesota’s hardship statute to award a portion of Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell. The award of $150,000 is therefore reversed.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## Introduction and Statement of Position

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in support of our position that the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits at issue should be classified as marital property, not as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property. ## I. The Death Benefits Do Not Constitute "Inheritance" Under Minnesota Law

The prosecution relies heavily on *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 579 N.W.2d 421 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), to argue that military death gratuity benefits are "inherently acquired through inheritance." However, this Court should decline to follow *Johnson* because it incorrectly interprets the statutory term "inheritance" and conflates distinct legal concepts.

The Minnesota Legislature, in enacting Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5, used the term "inheritance" in its traditional probate context—a transfer of property from a decedent's estate through testate or intestate succession. See Black's Law Dictionary 828 (9th ed. 2009) (defining inheritance as "[p]roperty that a person receives by will or succession under the law"). Death gratuity benefits, by contrast, are statutory payments created by Congress pursuant to its war powers, not assets passing from a decedent's estate. They are more properly characterized as gratuitous payments made by the government to survivors of service members who die in the line of duty—payments that bear no resemblance to traditional inheritances.

The Defense distinguishes *Johnson* on the basis that it involved a different type of benefit and that the court failed to properly analyze the statutory origin of the payments. The death gratuity program established under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480 is a federal statutory program designed to provide immediate financial assistance to survivors, not a bequest or devise from the deceased service member. The money does not come from Levi's estate; it comes from the federal treasury pursuant to congressional appropriation. To classify these government-funded benefits as "inheritance" stretches the statutory language beyond any reasonable interpretation.

## II. The "Marital Economic Partnership" Theory Supports Classification as Marital Property

The Defense further argues that even if the benefits could theoretically constitute inheritance, they should be treated as marital property under the "marital economic partnership" theory recognized in Minnesota law. During their 27-year marriage, Gordon and Loretta Angell raised five children together, including Levi. Levi's death deprived both spouses of the economic value that their son would have contributed to the family unit had he survived.

The prosecution dismisses this argument as "legally irrelevant," citing *In re Marriage of Lohr*, 304 N.W.2d 768 (Minn. 1981). However, *Lohr* is distinguishable. In *Lohr*, the Minnesota Supreme Court addressed survivor benefits paid to a widow under the Railroad Retirement Act. The court held that because the benefits were "derived from the deceased husband's earnings," they constituted marital property. The *Lohr* court explicitly recognized that benefits "derived from" a spouse's earnings during marriage are marital property, even if received after the spouse's death.

Here, Levi's potential earnings—the economic value he would have contributed to his parents' marriage—were never realized because of his death in service. The death benefits, while technically paid to Loretta as survivor, represent compensation for the loss of Levi's future economic contributions to the family. Both Gordon and Loretta suffered this loss; both should be deemed to have acquired the benefits. The marital economic partnership theory recognizes that marriages create a shared economic enterprise, and the loss of a child's future earnings capacity harms both spouses equally.

The prosecution's argument that "Minnesota law does not classify property based on its economic impact" ignores the foundational principle underlying the entire marital property framework. Minnesota adopted the marital property system specifically because marriage creates an economic partnership in which both spouses contribute to the accumulation of assets, whether through direct earnings, homemaking, child-rearing, or other means. The loss of a child's economic value to this partnership is precisely the type of event that should affect property classification.

## III. The Marital Presumption Applies and Was Not Rebutted

The prosecution incorrectly argues that the marital presumption does not apply because the property falls within the "inheritance" exception. The question before the Court is whether the property qualifies as inheritance in the first instance—a question the prosecution merely assumes rather than proves.

Under Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 4, "property acquired during marriage is presumed to be marital property regardless of whether it is held in the name of one or both parties." This presumption applies to all property acquired during the marriage unless it falls within one of the specific exceptions enumerated in subdivision 5. The exceptions must be proven by the party claiming the property is nonmarital.

Here, the death benefits were received during the marriage. The marital presumption therefore applies. The burden shifted to Loretta to prove that the benefits fall within the inheritance exception. She has not met this burden because the benefits are not traditional inheritances—they are government-funded statutory payments that bear no resemblance to bequests, devises, or inheritances from a decedent's estate.

The prosecution's reliance on the fact that the funds were deposited in a separate account and never commingled is unavailing. Commingling is relevant only after property has been initially classified as nonmarital; it is not relevant to the initial classification analysis. The question is whether the property was nonmarital at the moment of acquisition. Because Loretta has not proven the benefits constitute inheritance, the marital presumption controls, and the property must be classified as marital.

## IV. Even If the Benefits Are Nonmarital, the Hardship Award Should Be Upheld

The purpose of the anti-attachment provisions is to ensure that surviving family members receive financial support without fear that the benefits will be seized by creditors or other parties seeking to enforce debts. Congress enacted these provisions to protect military families from predatory lending and financial exploitation—not to carve out a special exemption for military death benefits from state family law equitable division principles.

The Defense's argument finds support in the well-established principle that states have broad authority to regulate marriage dissolution and property division within their borders. The federal government, in creating the military death benefit programs, did not expressly preempt state family law authority. The anti-attachment statutes use broad language ("may not be assigned, transferred, or pledged as security for any purpose"), but this language must be interpreted in context. It was designed to address creditor claims, not to preclude spouses from sharing in the economic losses resulting from a service member's death.

The prosecution's reliance on *In re Marriage of Wilson*, 176 F.3d 449 (8th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. *Wilson* addressed whether a state court could garnish military retirement pay to satisfy a judgment, not whether military death benefits could be divided in a dissolution proceeding. **FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both parties. Upon consideration of the statutory framework, binding precedent, and the federal anti-attachment statutes cited, the Court affirms in part and reverses in part the district court’s judgment.

The Court **affirms** the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The Court **reverses** the district court’s award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from these nonmarital funds. The case is **remanded** for entry of judgment consistent with this opinion.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Classification of Property**

The central dispute on appeal is whether the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits received by Loretta Angell following the death of her son, Levi Angell, constitute marital property or her exclusive nonmarital property. The Defense argues that the benefits should be classified as marital property because they do not constitute "inheritance" under Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5, and because they represent the loss of the "marital economic partnership." The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive.

Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 5, defines nonmarital property to include "property acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The Defense contends that "inheritance" is limited to traditional probate transfers from a decedent's estate and cannot encompass statutory payments from the federal treasury. This argument is directly contrary to binding precedent.

In *In re Marriage of Johnson*, 579 N.W.2d 421 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), this Court specifically addressed the classification of military death gratuity benefits under the same statutory framework. The court held that such benefits are "inherently acquired through inheritance" regardless of the fact that they are statutory payments rather than proceeds from a probate estate. The Defense’s request to disregard *Johnson* or to distinguish it based on the source of the funds is rejected; as an appellate court, we are bound by the precedent of *Johnson* absent controlling authority to the contrary. The source of the funds—the federal treasury—does not alter the legal nature of the acquisition, which flows from the death of a third party (the son) to the survivor (the mother).

The Defense’s reliance on *In re Marriage of Smith*, 477 N.W.2d 634 (Minn. Ct. App. 1991), is misplaced. While *Smith* involved a private life insurance policy, its holding reinforces the principle that proceeds payable to a beneficiary upon the death of another are acquired by inheritance. The distinction drawn by the Defense between private insurance and government-provided SGLI is irrelevant to the classification analysis; both are acquired by virtue of the decedent’s death.

Furthermore, the Defense’s "marital economic partnership" theory—that the benefits compensate for the loss of the son’s potential economic contributions to the marriage—cannot override the plain language of the statute. Minnesota law classifies property based on the source of acquisition, not the economic impact of the asset on the marriage. As established in *In re Marriage of Lohr*, 304 N.W.2d 768 (Minn. 1981), benefits received by a spouse as a result of the death of another are acquired by inheritance and are thus nonmarital property. The marital presumption under Minnesota Statutes section 518.54, subdivision 4, is rebutted by the specific exception in subdivision 5. The undisputed fact that the funds were deposited into a separate account and never commingled further supports their nonmarital character. Therefore, the district court’s classification of the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed.

**II. Division of Nonmarital Property**

The Defense alternatively argues that even if the benefits are classified as nonmarital, the district court’s award to Gordon Angell should be upheld under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, and that federal anti-attachment statutes do not prohibit such an award. The Court disagrees.

Federal law explicitly protects military death benefits from attachment and transfer. Under 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80, death-gratuity benefits "may not be assigned, transferred, or pledged as security for any purpose." Similarly, 38 U.S.C. § 1970(g) shields Servicemembers’ Group Life Insurance proceeds from the claims of creditors. The Defense argues that these provisions are intended to protect against creditors, not against equitable division in a dissolution proceeding. However, the statutory language is broad, prohibiting assignment or transfer "for any purpose."

The Defense’s reliance on the distinction between creditor proceedings and dissolution proceedings is unpersuasive in light of the broad protective language of the federal statutes. A court order directing a spouse to pay a portion of these federal benefits to the other spouse constitutes a transfer of those benefits. The principle of federal preemption dictates that state courts cannot utilize state law equitable powers to circumvent federal anti-attachment protections. See *In re Marriage of Wilson*, 176 F.3d 449 (8th Cir. 1999) (recognizing the preemptive effect of federal law regarding military benefits).

Because the federal statutes prohibit the transfer of these benefits, the district court lacked the authority to utilize Minnesota’s hardship statute to award a portion of Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell. The award of $150,000 is therefore reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed. The award of $150,000 from these funds to Gordon Angell is reversed. The case is remanded for entry of judgment consistent with this ruling.
