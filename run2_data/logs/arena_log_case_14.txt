**I. The Standard of Review Does Not Foreclose Meaningful Appellate Inquiry**

---

**II. The State Failed to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That Lalor's Mental Disorder Creates a Substantial Likelihood of Future Sexually Violent Conduct**

The prosecution relies heavily on Dr. Fields' conclusion that Lalor poses a "substantial probability" of reoffending. However, the Defense submits that the evidence presented was legally insufficient to satisfy the beyond a reasonable doubt standard for several reasons.

First, the record reveals that Dr. Fields' conclusion relied substantially on actuarial instruments that measure general recidivism rates among populations of sex offenders, not Lalor's individual characteristics. As Dr. Maskel's testimony established—and the prosecution's argument implicitly concedes—these testing instruments have significant limitations when applied to individual prediction. The trial court was entitled to weigh this testimony, but the State bears the burden of proving its case beyond a reasonable doubt, not merely by a preponderance of the evidence.

Second, the prosecution conflates the "substantial probability" language used by the experts with the statutory "substantial likelihood" standard articulated in *State v. Thiel*, 2001 WI 111, 246 Wis.2d 477, 631 N.W.2d 587 (Wis. 2001). While *Thiel* held that "substantial likelihood" requires "more than a mere possibility" but "less than certainty," the Court also emphasized that commitment under Chapter 980 is a significant deprivation of liberty that requires clear, convincing evidence. Here, the actuarial tools employed—including the PCL-R, MnSOST, and V-RAG—provide probability estimates that, while elevated, do not establish with the requisite certainty that Lalor specifically will commit future acts of sexual violence.

Third, the Defense notes that the trial court's own findings, as articulated in the record, focused primarily on Lalor's prior convictions and the expert opinions, without sufficient individualized analysis of how Lalor's specific mental disorder relates to his likelihood of future conduct. The statutory requirement that the mental disorder "creates" a substantial probability of future acts demands a causal connection that the State did not adequately establish.

---

**III. The Admission of the Pl-Ethysmograph Test Results Constituted Reversible Error**

The Defense maintains that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of the pl-ethysmograph test. In *State v. Denny*, 120 Wis.2d 614, 357 N.W.2d 555 (Wis. Ct. App. 1984), this Court recognized that expert testimony must be based on reliable scientific methods. The pl-ethysmograph results do not meet this standard, and their admission likely affected the trial court's assessment of Lalor's dangerousness. The Defense submits that this evidentiary error was not harmless and requires reversal.

**IV. The Lack of an Express Finding Regarding Lalor's Inability to Control His Behavior Raises Substantive Due Process Concerns**

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL BRIEF

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF WISCONSIN

---

**LALOR, Defendant-Appellant, v. STATE OF WISCONSIN, Plaintiff-Respondent.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

The Defense submits this rebuttal to address the fundamental deficiencies in the State's response and to demonstrate that the trial court's commitment order must be reversed. While the State correctly cites *Post*, *Thiel*, and *Laxton*, it misapplies these precedents and ignores the core constitutional and evidentiary problems that warrant reversal. The Defense establishes that: (1) the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard requires more than actuarial predictions unsupported by individualized clinical judgment; (2) the admission of pl-ethysmograph evidence was prejudicial and not harmless; and (3) the constitutional requirement of a nexus between mental disorder and dangerousness was not satisfied.

---

## I. THE STANDARD OF REVIEW DOES NOT PRECLUDE APPELLATE EXAMINATION OF LEGAL SUFFICIENCY

The State mischaracterizes the Defense's argument by suggesting we seek to "reweigh evidence." To the contrary, the Defense acknowledges that appellate courts must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations. However, the ultimate question of whether the evidence satisfies the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt is a question of law subject to independent review. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis.2d 279, 303, 541 N.W.2d 115, 125 (Wis. 1995).

The State conflates two distinct concepts: the weight of evidence (a factual matter) and the legal sufficiency of evidence to meet a constitutional burden (a legal question). While *Post* prohibits appellate courts from reweighing testimony, it does not preclude this Court from examining whether the evidence, as a matter of law, is sufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial likelihood of future violence. The question before this Court is not whether this Court *would* have credited Dr. Fields' testimony over Dr. Maskel's, but whether any rational trier of fact could have found beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person based on the evidence presented.

---

## II. THE ACTUARIAL EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT

### A. Population-Based Instruments Cannot Satisfy the Constitutional Burden

The State contends that Dr. Fields' use of multiple actuarial instruments, coupled with a review of Lalor's records, satisfies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. This argument fails because actuarial tools like the PCL-R, MnSOST, and V-RAG generate probability assessments based on population data—not individualized predictions of Lalor's future conduct. These instruments measure static risk factors (prior offenses, age, victim characteristics) rather than the dynamic clinical question: whether Lalor's specific mental disorder, as distinct from his criminal history, creates a substantial likelihood of future violence.

The Defense does not argue that actuarial tools are inadmissible. Rather, the Defense contends that when such tools form the sole basis for a finding of substantial probability, the evidence is legally insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the *mental disorder*—rather than merely the criminal history—drives the predicted future dangerousness. The statutory scheme requires proof that the mental disorder creates the substantial likelihood. Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). Actuarial instruments that measure general recidivism risk do not isolate the causal contribution of the mental disorder.

### B. Dr. Maskel's Testimony Established Reasonable Doubt

The State argues that a "battle of experts" does not invalidate the State's case. While *Post* acknowledges that trial courts may resolve conflicting expert testimony, it does not stand for the proposition that such resolution satisfies the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt burden when the defense expert's testimony raises fundamental doubts about the reliability of the State's evidence.

Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed critical limitations in the actuarial instruments: that they are population-based rather than individualized; that they fail to account for treatment progress or behavioral change; and that they cannot distinguish between statistical risk and clinical prediction of dangerousness. When the defense expert demonstrates that the State's evidence is fundamentally unreliable, the trial court cannot simply credit the State's expert and dismiss the defense's criticisms without explanation. Here, the trial court failed to explain why it credited Dr. Fields' conclusions despite the well-documented limitations of her instruments.

The trial court's bare assertion that Dr. Fields' testimony was credible does not satisfy the constitutional requirement that commitment be based on proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, does not exclude every reasonable hypothesis except that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffense. The defense expert's testimony, which the State dismisses as mere "criticism," actually established that the State's evidence was insufficient to carry its burden.

---

## III. THE ADMISSION OF PLETHYSMOGRAPH EVIDENCE WAS PREJUDICIAL AND NOT HARMLESS

The State argues that even if the pl-ethysmograph was improperly admitted, the error was harmless because the trial court claimed it did not rely on that evidence. This argument fails for three reasons.

### A. The Trial Court's Statement Is Not Dispositive

The trial court's written decision states that its commitment order was "based on the expert testimony of Dr. Fields and Dr. Moore, the actuarial data, and Lalor's prior convictions." However, the record reflects that the pl-ethysmograph results were admitted into evidence and were part of the trial court file. The trial court's statement that it did not rely on the pl-ethysmograph is not supported by any explicit analysis of that evidence. When a court has access to improperly admitted evidence, the presumption is that the evidence influenced the decision unless the record affirmatively demonstrates otherwise. The trial court here made no such demonstration.

### B. The Plethysmograph Evidence Was Highly Prejudicial

The pl-ethysmograph measures sexual arousal patterns and is notoriously unreliable for predicting future dangerousness. In *State v. Denny*, 120 Wis.2d 614, 357 N.W.2d 555 (Wis. Ct. App. 1984), this Court recognized that the admissibility of novel scientific evidence requires a threshold showing of reliability. The pl-ethysmograph fails this test because there is no accepted scientific consensus that it reliably predicts sexual violence propensity. Its admission created a substantial risk that the trial court relied on improper evidence to find Lalor dangerous.

### C. The Error Was Not Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The State bears the burden of proving that the admission of the pl-ethysmograph was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The State cannot meet this burden merely by pointing to other evidence in the record. The question is not whether sufficient *other* evidence supported the verdict, but whether the improperly admitted evidence contributed to the verdict. Here, the pl-ethysmograph was the only "direct" evidence of Lalor's sexual dangerousness—unlike the actuarial instruments, which measure statistical probability. The trial court's finding of dangerousness was likely influenced by this visceral evidence of Lalor's sexual responses. The error was not harmless.

---

## IV. THE CONSTITUTIONAL REQUIREMENT OF A NEXUS BETWEEN MENTAL DISORDER AND DANGEROUSNESS WAS NOT SATISFIED

### A. Laxton Does Not Foreclose This Argument

The State argues that *Laxton* forecloses any due process argument regarding the lack of an express finding of inability to control. The Defense does not argue that an express finding is required. Rather, the Defense argues that the trial court's finding of "substantial probability" of reoffending, standing alone, does not satisfy the constitutional requirement articulated in *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), that civil commitment be tied to a demonstrated inability to control dangerous behavior.

In *Crane*, the Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires "some relationship" between the mental disorder and the predicted future dangerousness. The Court emphasized that the State must prove "the serious difficulty in controlling behavior" that "differentiates the dangerous sexually violent predator who can be civilly committed from the dangerous but typical recidivist convicted of an offense." *Crane*, 534 U.S. at 413.

The trial court found that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that his "mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." This finding does not establish that Lalor's mental disorder impairs his capacity for behavioral control. The finding merely recites the statutory language without explaining how the mental disorder specifically affects Lalor's ability to control his behavior.

### B. The Record Lacks Evidence of Lalor's Inability to Control

The State's experts testified that Lalor scored high on actuarial instruments and therefore presents a substantial probability of reoffending. They did not testify that Lalor cannot control his behavior. The distinction is critical: high actuarial scores indicate statistical risk; they do not establish that Lalor's mental disorder impairs his volitional control.

Dr. Fields' report concluded that Lalor's "mental disorders create a substantial probability that he will commit a sexually violent act." This conclusion conflates statistical risk with volitional impairment. The record contains no evidence that Lalor's mental disorder affects his ability to refrain from sexual violence—not in the expert reports, not in the testimony, and not in the trial court's findings. The constitutional requirement of a nexus between disorder and dangerousness was not satisfied.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's commitment order. The evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial likelihood of future sexual violence. The admission of pl-ethysmograph evidence was prejudicial and not harmless. And the trial court's findings fail to satisfy the constitutional requirement of a nexus between Lalor's mental disorder and his alleged dangerousness.

The Defense does not ask this Court to reweigh the evidence. Rather, the Defense asks this Court to apply the law to the facts and hold that the State failed to meet its constitutional burden. The commitment order must be reversed.

The defense expert’s criticisms of the instruments do not transform the State’s evidence into legal insufficiency; they merely present factual disputes the trial court resolved in the State’s favor. *Post* prohibits this Court from reweighing those disputes. *Post*, 197 Wis.2d at 304. The record contains ample evidence—Dr. Fields’ clinical assessment, her use of multiple validated tools, and Lalor’s extensive history of sexually violent offenses—to support the trial court’s finding beyond a reasonable doubt.

In *Laxton*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that the Sexually Violent Person statute "does not require an express finding of inability to control behavior" because the statutory requirement of a "substantial likelihood" of engaging in sexually violent acts "necessarily incorporates the concept of lack of control." *Laxton*, 254 Wis.2d at 201–02. The court further explained that *Crane*’s constitutional requirement of a nexus between mental disorder and dangerousness is satisfied by the statutory standard itself. *Laxton*, 254 Wis.2d at 202. The trial court’s finding that Lalor’s mental disorder creates a "substantial probability" of reoffending—coupled with its determination that he suffers from a mental disorder—fully satisfies *Crane* and *Laxton*. ## STATE OF WISCONSIN v. LALOR

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits that this Court must reverse the trial court's commitment order. The State has failed to meet its constitutional burden, and the trial court committed reversible error in its evidentiary rulings. The Defense addresses each of the State's arguments in turn.

## I. THE STATE FAILED TO PROVE THE REQUIRED NEXUS BETWEEN MENTAL DISORDER AND DANGEROUSNESS

The State's closing statement mischaracterizes the nature of the Defense's sufficiency argument. The Defense does not ask this Court to "reweigh" evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court. Rather, the Defense contends that the State failed to present legally sufficient evidence to establish a constitutional violation under **Kansas v. Crane**, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), and its progeny.

In **Crane**, the United States Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires the State to prove "a nexus between the mental disorder and the inability to control behavior." **Crane**, 534 U.S. at 413. This requirement exists because civil commitment of sexually violent persons is a massive curtailment of liberty that cannot be justified absent a showing that the person's mental disorder actually causes dangerousness. The Defense does not dispute that Lalor has a mental disorder or that he has committed prior offenses. The critical constitutional deficiency is the absence of evidence demonstrating that his mental disorder *causes* his dangerousness.

The State's experts, Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, relied exclusively on actuarial instruments to predict Lalor's future dangerousness. These instruments—the PCL-R, the MnSOST, the MnSOST-R, the V-RAG, and the RRASOR—are designed to assess *risk* based on *static* factors such as criminal history, age at first offense, and victim characteristics. As Dr. Maskel testified, these instruments measure correlation in population samples rather than causation in any individual case. They cannot distinguish whether Lalor's predicted dangerousness stems from his mental disorder, his criminal history, or some other factor entirely.

This distinction is constitutionally significant. In **Kansas v. Hendricks**, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), the Supreme Court upheld the Kansas sexually violent person statute partly because it required proof that the person's "mental abnormality" predisposes them to commit sexually violent acts. This "predisposition" requirement necessarily implies a causal connection—the mental disorder must be the *reason* the person is dangerous, not merely correlated with dangerousness. The State's evidence fails this requirement.

The State argues that Dr. Fields' conclusion that Lalor presents a "substantial probability" of reoffending satisfies the standard. However, this conclusion, standing alone, conflates prediction with causation. A weather forecaster can predict rain with substantial probability without demonstrating that clouds *cause* rain. Similarly, the State's experts can predict reoffending without establishing that Lalor's mental disorder *causes* his predicted reoffending. The record contains no evidence establishing this causal nexus.

The Defense acknowledges that **State v. Thiel**, 2001 WI 111, 246 Wis.2d 477, 631 N.W.2d 587 (Wis. 2001), defines "substantial likelihood" as "more than a mere possibility but less than certainty." However, Thiel did not address the constitutional requirement articulated in Crane—the causal nexus between mental disorder and dangerousness. The State's reliance on Thiel is therefore misplaced. Even assuming the State proved a "substantial likelihood" of reoffending, it failed to prove that Lalor's mental disorder creates that likelihood.

---

## II. THE ADMISSION OF PLETHYSMOGRAPH EVIDENCE WAS REVERSIBLE ERROR

The State contends that any error in admitting the pl-ysmograph evidence was harmless because the trial court stated it did not rely on that evidence. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the trial court's statement that it "did not rely on" the pl-ysmograph evidence is not dispositive. The trial court made this statement in its written decision, but the record does not reflect whether the trial court was exposed to the evidence during the evidentiary hearing itself. If the trial court heard the evidence and later attempted to disregard it, the presumption of tainted influence applies. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "it is practically impossible for a judge to erase from his mind memory of evidence that he has heard." **Bruton v. United States**, 391 U.S. 123, 128 (1968). The trial court's post-hoc assertion of purity cannot cure the constitutional injury of admitting unreliable, prejudicial evidence.

Second, the pl-ysmograph test is an extraordinarily unreliable measure of dangerousness. The test measures penile responsivity to sexual stimuli, but the relationship between such responsivity and the likelihood of future sexual violence is tenuous at best. No validated scientific evidence establishes that pl-ysmograph results predict future criminal behavior. The trial court's admission of this evidence violated **State v. Denny**, 120 Wis.2d 614, 357 N.W.2d 555 (Wis. Ct. App. 1984), which requires a threshold showing of reliability before admitting scientific evidence.

Third, even if this Court accepts the State's harmless error argument, the error was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The State's case rested entirely on expert testimony predicting future dangerousness. The pl-ysmograph evidence was the only "direct" evidence of Lalor's sexual propensities—the only evidence purporting to measure his actual sexual arousal patterns rather than statistical risk factors. The trial court's commitment order found that Lalor is "dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." This finding tracks the precise language of the pl-ysmograph evidence. The inference that the trial court relied on this evidence is unavoidable.

The Defense does not "speculate" about the trial court's reasoning. The Defense draws the reasonable inference that a trial court confronted with unreliable evidence of sexual dangerousness would be influenced by that evidence when making a finding about sexual dangerousness. The State's attempt to shift the burden to the Defense to prove "no influence" inverts the harmless error analysis. The State bears the burden of proving harmlessness beyond a reasonable doubt, and it has not met that burden.

---

## III. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS VIOLATE DUE PROCESS

The State's closing statement correctly notes that **State v. Laxton**, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis.2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002), holds that the Wisconsin Sexually Violent Person statute does not require an express finding of inability to control behavior. However, the Defense does not argue for an express finding. The Defense argues that the *evidence* must demonstrate such an inability.

In **Crane**, the Supreme Court explained that the constitutional requirement of a nexus between mental disorder and dangerousness "serve[s] the essential due process purpose of 'ensuring that the confinement does not exceed the need for security.'" **Crane**, 534 U.S. at 411 (quoting **Hendricks**, 521 U.S. at 350). Without evidence that Lalor cannot control his behavior, the State's justification for indefinite civil commitment evaporates. The commitment becomes punitive in nature, detaining Lalor not for what he might do but for who he is—a person with a mental disorder and a criminal history.

The record contains no such evidence. Dr. Fields testified that Lalor meets the diagnostic criteria for psychopathy, but psychopathy is not synonymous with inability to control behavior. Many individuals with psychopathic traits retain the capacity to conform their conduct to the law; they simply lack the motivation to do so. Dr. Fields' conclusion that Lalor presents a "substantial probability" of reoffending says nothing about his capacity to control his behavior. It addresses only the statistical likelihood of recurrence.

The State's expert testimony establishes, at most, that Lalor is *likely* to reoffend. It does not establish that Lalor *cannot* refrain from reoffending. This distinction is constitutionally significant. Civil commitment for dangerousness requires proof that the person is *unable* to control their behavior, not merely that they *will* engage in dangerous conduct if released. The trial court's findings, which mirror the statutory language without additional evidentiary support, do not satisfy this requirement.

---

## IV. THE DEFENSE PROPERLY PRESERVED ALL ARGUMENTS

The State suggests that the Defense's arguments are improper because they seek to "re-litigate" issues or "reweigh" evidence. This characterization is incorrect. The Defense has raised pure questions of legal sufficiency and constitutional adequacy. The Defense does not ask this Court to reassess the credibility of witnesses or to substitute its judgment for the trial court's. The Defense asks this Court to determine whether the State met its constitutional burden—specifically, whether the evidence is sufficient to establish a causal nexus between Lalor's mental disorder and his dangerousness.

This Court has an independent obligation to conduct such a review. In **Jackson v. Virginia**, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), the Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause requires appellate courts to ensure that "any rational trier of fact" could have found the essential elements of the crime proven beyond a reasonable doubt. This standard applies with equal force to civil commitment proceedings, which deprive individuals of their liberty. The Defense's arguments invoke this constitutional obligation, not a request to reweigh evidence.

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's commitment order. The State failed to prove the constitutionally required nexus between Lalor's mental disorder and his dangerousness. The trial court erroneously admitted unreliable pl-ysmograph evidence, and that error was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court's findings do not satisfy the due process requirements articulated in **Kansas v. Crane**, 534 U.S. 407 (2002).

The Defense does not minimize the seriousness of Lalor's prior offenses or suggest that he poses no risk to the community. However, the Constitution prohibits indefinite civil commitment based on nothing more than predictions of future dangerousness drawn from population-based instruments. The State must prove that the person's mental disorder *causes* their dangerousness—that the person cannot control their behavior due to their mental disorder. The State failed to carry this burden.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the circuit court and remand with instructions to discharge Lalor from commitment.

**STATE OF WISCONSIN, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. LALOR, Defendant-Appellant.**

**CASE NO. 00-XXXX**

**DECISION**

The appeal is taken from the judgment of the circuit court committing Lalor to the custody of the Department of Social Services as a sexually violent person pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). Lalor argues that the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt, that the trial court erroneously admitted evidence, and that the trial court’s findings violated due process. Upon review of the closing arguments submitted by both parties and the record provided, the court affirms the trial court’s order.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review in a sexually violent person commitment case is governed by *State v. Post*, 197 Wis.2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995). As established in *Post*, while the ultimate question of whether the evidence satisfies the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt is a question of law, appellate courts must defer to the trial court’s credibility determinations unless a witness is incredible as a matter of law. *Post*, 197 Wis.2d at 303. The Defense argues that this Court must independently examine the legal sufficiency of the evidence to ensure the State met its burden, citing *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979). While *Jackson* requires appellate courts to ensure that "any rational trier of fact" could have found the essential elements proven beyond a reasonable doubt, this standard does not permit the Court to reweigh the conflicting expert testimony or substitute its judgment for that of the trial court regarding the weight given to that testimony. *Post*, 197 Wis.2d at 304.

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE AND CONSTITUTIONAL NEXUS**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove the required nexus between his mental disorder and his dangerousness, arguing that the actuarial tools used by the State’s experts were population-based rather than individualized. The Defense relies on *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), and *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997), to assert that the State must prove the mental disorder *causes* the dangerousness, not merely that it correlates with it.

The trial court heard testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, both of whom concluded that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder creating a substantial probability of reoffending. Dr. Fields utilized multiple validated instruments, including the PCL-R, the MnSOST, and the V-RAG, in conjunction with a review of Lalor’s Department of Corrections records. The defense expert, Dr. Maskel, testified regarding the limitations of these instruments, specifically arguing that they measure static risk factors rather than the causal contribution of the mental disorder.

The Defense’s argument that the actuarial evidence is legally insufficient because it is population-based goes to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility or legal sufficiency. The trial court, as the trier of fact, was entitled to credit the testimony of Dr. Fields over the criticisms offered by Dr. Maskel. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis.2d at 304. The experts testified that the results of these instruments, when combined with their clinical judgment and review of Lalor’s records, supported the conclusion that Lalor’s mental disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffending. The trial court’s acceptance of this methodology is not clearly erroneous.

Furthermore, the Defense’s argument that the record lacks evidence of a "nexus" between the mental disorder and dangerousness is foreclosed by binding precedent. In *State v. Laxton*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis.2d 185, 647 N.W.2d 784 (Wis. 2002), the Wisconsin Supreme Court explicitly held that the Sexually Violent Person statute does not require an express finding of inability to control behavior. The court reasoned that the statutory requirement of a "substantial likelihood" of engaging in sexually violent acts "necessarily incorporates the concept of lack of control" and satisfies the constitutional requirements outlined in *Crane*. *Laxton*, 254 Wis.2d at 201–02. The trial court’s finding that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability of reoffending is sufficient to meet the constitutional burden. The experts’ testimony regarding Lalor’s psychopathy and high risk of recidivism provided the necessary evidentiary basis for this finding.

The statutory standard requires proof of a "substantial likelihood" of future sexually violent conduct. In *State v. Thiel*, 2001 WI 111, 246 Wis.2d 477, 631 N.W.2d 587 (Wis. 2001), the court explained that this standard requires "more than a mere possibility" but "less than certainty." *Thiel*, 246 Wis.2d at 493. Dr. Fields’ conclusion that Lalor presents a "substantial probability" of reoffending aligns with this standard. The trial court’s finding that Lalor’s mental disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffending is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous.

**III. ADMISSIBILITY OF PLETHYSMOGRAPH EVIDENCE**

Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a pl-ethysmograph test and that the error was not harmless. The Defense contends that the trial court’s statement that it did not rely on the test is not dispositive, citing *Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123 (1968), for the proposition that a judge cannot erase evidence from their mind.

The trial court’s written decision indicates that its commitment order was based on the expert testimony of Dr. Fields and Dr. Moore, the actuarial data, and Lalor’s prior convictions. The facts provided state that Dr. Fields relied on the PCL-R, RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG, but do not indicate that the pl-ethysmograph was a basis for her conclusion. Under *State v. Denny*, 120 Wis.2d 614, 357 N.W.2d 555 (Wis. Ct. App. 1984), when a trial court’s findings affirmatively show it did not rely on challenged evidence, any error in admission is harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *Denny*, 120 Wis.2d at 625.

The Defense’s reliance on *Bruton* is misplaced. *Bruton* involved a jury trial where a co-defendant's confession implicating the defendant was admitted, violating the Confrontation Clause. Here, the case was tried by the court, and the judge explicitly stated in the written decision that the pl-ethysmograph evidence was not relied upon. The presumption of regularity supports the conclusion that the trial court followed its own stated reasoning. The Defense’s speculation that the trial court was influenced by the pl-ethysmograph despite its written findings to the contrary is unpersuasive. Even if the admission of the pl-ethysmograph evidence was erroneous, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt because the trial court’s findings were supported by substantial, competent evidence independent of the challenged test.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court’s findings of fact are not clearly erroneous, and the court correctly applied the law to those facts. The State met its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person. The arguments raised by the Defense regarding the sufficiency of the evidence and the admission of the plethysmograph are without merit under controlling precedent. Therefore, the order of commitment is affirmed.
