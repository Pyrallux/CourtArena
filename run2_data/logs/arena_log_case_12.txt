FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1

The letters to the editor, on the other hand, are more opinionated. They do contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt. Some are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race. We refuse, however, to find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society. But see Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537. These few letters do not represent a society scornful of outsiders, as counsel Reardon would have us believe. Instead, by attempting to influence the public, these letters affirm that race-based accusations lack merit and credence.
One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the High Court of American Samoa

**Defendants-Appellants Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang** respectfully submit this opening response in opposition to the prosecution's argument and in support of their motion for change of venue.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution asks this Court to affirm the denial of the defendants' motion for change of venue by minimizing the extent of pre-trial publicity and mischaracterizing the applicable legal standard. However, the prosecution ignores critical facts establishing that the pre-trial publicity in this case created a reasonable likelihood of prejudice that cannot be cured through ordinary voir dire procedures. The defendants respectfully submit that the trial court erred in denying their motion and that this Court should reverse and remand with instructions to grant a change of venue.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The defendants face serious charges: Wei Ki Fang with one count of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree, Siituu Sanerivi with six counts, and Wei Li Fang with nine counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree plus one count of assault in the second degree. The charges arise from allegations of prostitution activities involving Chinese nationals, with Wei Ki Fang and Wei Li Fang being Chinese nationals and Sanerivi having been born in Western Samoa.

The pre-trial publicity at issue included newspaper articles and letters to the editor published in the American Samoa media. While the prosecution characterizes this coverage as limited to "only six newspaper articles," this characterization obscures the nature of the publicity. The trial court itself acknowledged that the letters to the editor "contain conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and that "[s]ome are indeed embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race."

The trial court further noted that the defendants submitted evidence "that both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals" and that counsel for Wei Ki Fang argued that "the publicity is the product of bigotry from a 'culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here.'" This evidence of racial bias in the community, combined with the nature of the charges involving foreign nationals, presents a distinct prejudice concern not adequately addressed by the trial court.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Trial Court Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard for Presumed Prejudice

The prosecution argues that presumed prejudice requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage" and that the defendants failed to meet this burden. However, the prosecution conflates the standard for presumed prejudice with the facts of this case. The correct standard, as established in *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), requires that the defendant show "that the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993).

The prosecution's reliance on the number of articles as the primary metric is misguided. The relevant inquiry is not merely the quantity of coverage but the nature and content of that coverage, particularly in a small island community where media penetration is disproportionately high. In *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), the Supreme Court reversed a conviction where the defendant "was entitled to have his guilt or innocence determined solely by the evidence" presented at trial, not influenced by pre-trial publicity. The Court held that when the community has been exposed to prejudicial material, the proceedings become "but a hollow formality."

Here, the letters to the editor containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and expressions of racial bias against the defendants present precisely the type of prejudicial influence that *Rideau* addressed. The trial court acknowledged these letters but erroneously characterized them as representing only "a very limited section of society." This finding ignores the reality of media influence in small communities where a single publication reaches a substantial portion of the population.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Its Assessment of Actual Prejudice

The prosecution argues that actual prejudice cannot be assessed without voir dire and that the trial court properly deferred this inquiry. However, this argument ignores the defendants' burden at this stage and the nature of the prejudice alleged.

In *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), the Supreme Court held that actual prejudice may be shown when "it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). The defendants need not prove with certainty that no impartial juror exists; they need only show that the pre-trial publicity has made it reasonably likely that such a jury cannot be secured.

The trial court acknowledged that "if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors." However, this finding ignores the practical reality that in a small island community, voir dire may be insufficient to uncover the subtle effects of pre-trial publicity. Jurors may be reluctant to admit bias in front of the court, or they may themselves be unaware of how pre-trial publicity has influenced their views.

The prosecution cites *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999), for the proposition that actual prejudice requires voir dire. However, *Nevers* also recognizes that "the responses of prospective jurors in voir dire" are relevant to this inquiry, suggesting that the nature of the publicity itself is a factor to be considered. The trial court erred in deferring all inquiry into actual prejudice until after voir dire without first assessing whether the pre-trial publicity was of such a character as to likely taint the jury pool.

### C. The Cultural Bias Claim Was Not Adequately Considered

The prosecution dismisses the defendants' claim that the publicity stems from cultural bias against non-native-born individuals as "unsupported." However, the trial court acknowledged this claim in its order, noting that counsel for Wei Ki Fang "submits that the publicity is the product of bigotry from a 'culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here.'"

The trial court responded to this claim by stating that "we refuse...to find that a few rogue letters to the editor represent the beliefs of an entire society." However, this response conflates the question of whether the letters represent the beliefs of the entire society with the separate question of whether the letters, combined with other pre-trial publicity, created a reasonable likelihood of prejudice.

The defendants presented evidence that they are Chinese nationals facing charges related to prostitution activities. The letters to the editor, which the trial court acknowledged were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," directly connected the defendants' nationality to the charges against them. This type of prejudicial publicity is particularly harmful because it appeals to pre-existing biases in the community, making it more difficult to seat an impartial jury.

The trial court erred in dismissing this claim without adequate consideration of how the combination of the defendants' nationality, the nature of the charges, and the racial bias expressed in the letters to the editor created a reasonable likelihood of prejudice.

### D. The Inadequacy of Alternative Remedies

The prosecution argues that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa and that alternative remedies are appropriate. However, this argument puts the cart before the horse. The question before this Court is whether the defendants have established a right to a change of venue; only if such a right is established does the question of remedy become relevant.

The trial court stated that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives" and that "a change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial." However, this reasoning is flawed. The Constitution requires that the defendant receive a fair trial in a fair tribunal. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 722. If a change of venue is the only means of securing a fair trial, then it is constitutionally required.

The trial court's reliance on *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), is misplaced. In *Moreno*, the First Circuit addressed the specific circumstances of Puerto Rico and noted that "alternative remedies—not venue transfer—are appropriate in such contexts." However, the *Moreno* court made this observation in the context of a case where the defendant had not established actual prejudice. Here, the defendants have presented evidence of both presumed and actual prejudice that was not adequately addressed by the trial court.

Furthermore, the trial court's conclusion that "transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue" may not be accurate. While American Samoa may have a single jury roll, moving the trial to a different location within the territory could provide some measure of relief from the most pervasive effects of pre-trial publicity, particularly if the publicity was concentrated in specific geographic areas.

The prosecution's argument that *A.S.C.A. § 46.0601* does not contemplate a change of venue is similarly unavailing. The statute provides that the Chief Justice or Associate Justice "may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa." While the trial court interpreted this statute as being "geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient location," the statute's language is broad enough to encompass a change of venue for the purpose of securing a fair trial.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument rests on several key assertions that the defense respectfully submits are incorrect:

First, the prosecution argues that "only six newspaper articles" were presented and that these were "not deemed inflammatory by this Court." However, the trial court's characterization of the articles as non-inflammatory does not end the inquiry. The letters to the editor, which the trial court acknowledged contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," present a different type of prejudice concern. The prosecution's failure to address the nature of these letters undermines its argument that the publicity was not prejudicial.

Second, the prosecution argues that the letters to the editor "do not represent societal views" and that "isolated commentary cannot be attributed to an entire community." However, the prosecution ignores the reality that in a small island community, published letters to the editor may represent a more significant portion of public opinion than they would in a larger jurisdiction. The trial court itself acknowledged that "the island's media outlets are virtual monopolies," suggesting that the published material had disproportionate influence in the community.

Third, the prosecution argues that "actual prejudice cannot be assessed without voir dire." While voir dire is an important tool for assessing actual prejudice, the trial court erred in deferring all inquiry until after voir dire without first determining whether the pre-trial publicity was of such a character as to likely taint the jury pool. The defendants presented evidence of prejudicial publicity; the trial court should have at least tentatively assessed whether this evidence was sufficient to establish a reasonable likelihood of prejudice before deferring the matter.

Fourth, the prosecution argues that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa. However, as the defense has argued above, the question of remedy is secondary to the question of whether the defendants have established a right to relief. If the defendants have established such a right, the Court must fashion an appropriate remedy, which may include transferring the case to another location within American Samoa or, if necessary, to another jurisdiction.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The defendants respectfully submit that the trial court erred in denying their motion for change of venue. The pre-trial publicity in this case, including newspaper articles and letters to the editor containing "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and expressions of racial bias against the defendants, created a reasonable likelihood that an impartial jury cannot be secured in American Samoa. The trial court applied an incorrect legal standard by requiring "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage" rather than assessing whether the publicity, considering its nature and the circumstances of the community, created a reasonable likelihood of prejudice.

The prosecution has not met its burden of demonstrating that the trial court's denial of the motion was correct. Rather, the record establishes that the defendants are entitled to a change of venue to secure their constitutional right to a fair trial.

**Before the Court:** Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang (Defendants-Appellants) v. American Samoa Government (Appellee).

**Issue:** Whether the trial court erred in denying the defendants’ motion for a change of venue based on alleged pre-trial publicity and community prejudice.

**Standard of Review**
The right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is a fundamental requirement of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either presumed prejudice or actual prejudice. Presumed prejudice is a "rare" phenomenon reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); see also *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963). Actual prejudice, conversely, is discerned by reviewing both the extent of the publicity and the responses of prospective jurors during voir dire. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999).

**Analysis**

**I. Presumed Prejudice**
The defendants argue that the trial court applied an incorrect standard by focusing on the quantity of publicity rather than its nature, specifically the racial bias evident in letters to the editor. While the defense correctly notes that the standard for presumed prejudice focuses on whether the media influence "pervades the community," *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 728, they have not met the heavy burden required to establish such prejudice.

The Supreme Court has limited presumed prejudice to extreme circumstances, such as in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where a defendant’s coerced confession was televised multiple times to the local community. Here, the record reflects only six newspaper articles and a limited number of letters to the editor. While the trial court acknowledged that some letters contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race," the court found that these represented a "very limited section of society" rather than a pervasive community sentiment.

The defendants’ reliance on the "nature" of the publicity over the "quantity" is unpersuasive in this context. Even assuming the letters were highly prejudicial, isolated instances of bias do not equate to the "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage" required to presume prejudice. *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985); *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002). The trial court’s finding that the letters do not represent the beliefs of the entire society is a factual determination that is not clearly erroneous on this record. Therefore, the defendants have failed to show that the publicity created an atmosphere of inherent prejudice that would render the trial a "hollow formality." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726.

**II. Actual Prejudice**
The defendants contend that the trial court erred by deferring the inquiry into actual prejudice until voir dire. However, this position is inconsistent with established precedent. Actual prejudice cannot be established solely by the content of the news articles; it requires a showing that it is "reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured." *Bell v. Lynbaugh*, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). This determination is "discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers*, 169 F.3d at 362.

The defense argues that voir dire is insufficient because jurors may be unaware of their own bias or may be reluctant to admit it. While this is a theoretical concern, it does not justify bypassing the standard procedure for assessing actual prejudice. The trial court acted within its discretion by allowing the defendants to submit a detailed voir dire questionnaire to probe for media exposure and bias. Until the jury pool is examined, the defendants cannot prove that an impartial jury cannot be seated.

**III. Remedy and Change of Venue**
Finally, the defendants argue that the trial court erred in concluding that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa. The defense asserts that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 provides the statutory authority to move the trial to a different location to ensure fairness.

The trial court correctly observed that American Samoa has a single jury roll comprising all registered voters on the island. Consequently, transferring the trial to a different physical location within the territory would not change the composition of the jury pool. As the First Circuit noted in *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), a change of venue is often unsuitable in small, insular jurisdictions where the community is compact and the jury pool is uniform. See also *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981).

The defendants’ interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 is unpersuasive. The statute allows the court to adjourn to a different location for the "convenience of parties, witnesses or the court." It does not contemplate a change of venue to escape prejudice, particularly when such a move would not result in a different jury pool. The trial court’s determination that a physical move would be a "pipe dream" that fails to address the core issue of juror impartiality is sound.

**Conclusion**
The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for a change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate the "exceptional" circumstances necessary for presumed prejudice, and the inquiry into actual prejudice was properly deferred to voir dire. Furthermore, the logistical realities of the American Samoa judiciary render a change of venue an ineffective remedy. The trial court’s order allowing for a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire provides sufficient protection for the defendants' right to a fair trial.

The prosecution argues that actual prejudice can only be determined through voir dire. While this is the general rule, the prosecution ignores the practical limitations of voir dire in a small community context.

In American Samoa's compact social structure, potential jurors may be reluctant to admit prejudice due to social pressure, community ties to the defendants or victims, or fear of social repercussions.

The prosecution cites *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), for the proposition that voir dire is sufficient. A juror who has been exposed to prejudicial publicity may honestly answer "yes" to media exposure questions without revealing the impact of that exposure on their impartiality.

**II. Actual Prejudice and Voir Dire**
The defendants contend that voir dire is an inadequate remedy in this context because the social pressure of a small community will prevent jurors from candidly admitting bias. The defense argues that *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), which found voir dire sufficient to uncover bias, is distinguishable because it involved a larger community with multiple media sources.

While the Court recognizes the theoretical validity of the defense's concern regarding social pressure in a small island community, this concern does not render the voir dire process *per se* ineffective or justify bypassing the standard procedure for assessing actual prejudice. The trial court acted within its discretion by allowing the defendants to submit a detailed voir dire questionnaire and by indicating a willingness to conduct individual voir dire if necessary. The restrictions placed on the questionnaire—prohibiting questions about substantive arguments or hypothetical verdicts—are standard safeguards designed to prevent juror conditioning and do not constitute an abuse of discretion. Until the jury pool is examined, the defendants cannot prove that an impartial jury cannot be seated. *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728.

**III. Remedy and Change of Venue**
Finally, the defendants argue that the trial court erred in concluding that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa. They assert that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 provides statutory authority to move the trial to ensure fairness and distinguish *United States v. The fact that American Samoa is smaller than Puerto Rico only strengthens the trial court’s conclusion that a physical move would be a "pipe dream" that fails to address the core issue of juror impartiality. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for a change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate the "exceptional" circumstances necessary for presumed prejudice, and the inquiry into actual prejudice was properly deferred to voir dire. The logistical realities of the American Samoa judiciary render a change of venue an ineffective remedy, as it would not alter the composition of the jury pool. The trial court’s order allowing for a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire provides sufficient protection for the defendants' right to a fair trial at this stage.

## I. The Nature of the Publicity, Not Merely Its Volume, Creates Presumed Prejudice

The appellee's argument rests heavily on the notion that presumed prejudice requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage," citing *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). However, this Court in *Coleman* expressly recognized that the nature of publicity is equally determinative, noting that courts must examine "the nature of the publicity" and not merely count articles or column inches. The defense contends that the trial court erred by failing to give adequate weight to the character of the publicity in this case.

The record establishes that letters to the editor contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." This Court acknowledged these facts but characterized the letters as representing "a very limited section of society." With respect, this characterization ignores the reality of small island communities where media outlets function as virtual monopolies and where published opinions carry outsized influence. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 728 (1961), the question is whether the community atmosphere is one in which "passion, hatred, or terror" has been stirred such that the trial cannot be fair.

The trial court acknowledged that some letters were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." This is not a matter of mere bias or opinion—it is a direct attack on the defendants' character based solely on their national origin. In *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963), the Supreme Court found presumed prejudice where a televised confession "pervaded the community." Here, the poisoning of the community wellspring is no less real simply because it manifests through letters to the editor rather than broadcast media. The distinction is one of mechanism, not impact.

We further note that the appellee's reliance on the limited number of articles (six) ignores the multiplier effect in small communities. In *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d at 1543, the Eleventh Circuit recognized that "in a small community, even limited publicity may have a pervasive effect." American Samoa's population is minuscule compared to even the smallest mainland jurisdictions, and the impact of any negative publicity is correspondingly amplified.

---

## II. Actual Prejudice Need Not Wait for Voir Dire; The Circumstances Here Warrant Preemptive Relief

The appellee argues that actual prejudice "is discerned only by reviewing...the responses of prospective jurors in voir dire," citing *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999). While this is generally true, the defense contends that the circumstances here present an exceptional case where voir dire cannot adequately remedy the demonstrated prejudice.

The defense has raised a legitimate concern that in American Samoa's compact social structure, prospective jurors may be unwilling to candidly admit bias, particularly when that bias is rooted in community attitudes toward foreigners. The trial court itself acknowledged the "compact social structure of this island community" and that "if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest corners of the Territory." Yet despite this recognition, the court below placed faith in a voir dire process that cannot function effectively when potential jurors have every incentive to conceal their true sentiments.

In *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), the Supreme Court found voir dire sufficient to uncover bias, but that case involved a larger community with multiple media sources and diverse juror backgrounds. The unique circumstances of American Samoa—where everyone knows everyone, where social pressure to conform is immense, and where the jury roll comprises all registered voters—render the traditional voir dire process inadequate. The defense is not asking this Court to assume prejudice; we are asking the Court to recognize that the mechanism for discovering prejudice is structurally compromised in this jurisdiction.

Furthermore, the trial court's restrictions on the voir dire questionnaire—requiring yes or no responses and prohibiting inquiries into anticipated instructions or hypothetical verdicts—significantly limit its effectiveness. While these restrictions may be appropriate in ordinary cases, they further undermine the ability to detect deeply held biases in a community where prejudice may be subtle but pervasive.

---

## III. The Change of Venue Remedy Is Not Impractical; Alternative Relief Is Warranted

The appellee argues that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa because the territory has a single jury roll. The defense respectfully disagrees and contends that the trial court erred in its interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601.

The statute authorizes the Chief Justice to "order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa" when "the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires." While the trial court interpreted this as merely authorizing physical relocation for witness convenience, the statute's language is broader and encompasses the "interest of justice." Precedent from *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981), recognizes that in small insular jurisdictions, alternative remedies may be appropriate when change of venue is impossible. However, the question is not merely whether physical relocation is possible, but whether any remedy can ensure a fair trial.

The defense does not suggest that moving the trial to another village within American Samoa would solve the problem. Rather, we argue that the trial court should have considered more robust protective measures, including extended voir dire beyond the restricted questionnaire format, juror sequestration, or even a transfer to a federal court in Hawaii or another jurisdiction where the defendants could receive a truly impartial trial. The trial court's conclusion that "we need only assure a fair trial" is correct, but its failure to take meaningful steps to achieve that goal constitutes an abuse of discretion.

## IV. The Defendants' Right to Due Process Requires Greater Protection

At its core, this case presents a question of fundamental fairness. The defendants are charged with serious offenses carrying significant penalties. They are entitled to a trial before an impartial jury—a right guaranteed by both the United States Constitution and the American Samoa Constitution. The trial court acknowledged that "a fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process," citing *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 722, and *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966).

The publicity in this case has tainted the defendants' reputation in the community before any evidence has been presented. The letters to the editor have pronounced guilt without trial, and the community has been invited to judge the defendants based on their nationality rather than the evidence. This is precisely the "tyrannical power" against which due process protects. *Sheppard*, 384 U.S. at 350.

The defense recognizes that not every case with pre-trial publicity warrants a change of venue. However, the circumstances here are exceptional. The combination of racially charged commentary, the insular nature of the community, and the structural limitations of voir dire create a situation where the probability of prejudice is not merely speculative—it is practically certain. Waiting to discover that prejudice through voir dire, after the jury has already been exposed to prejudicial material, puts the defendants in the position of having to prove actual bias after the damage is done.

**Before the Court:** Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang (Defendants-Appellants) v. American Samoa Government (Appellee).

**Issue:** Whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying the defendants’ motion for a change of venue based on alleged pre-trial publicity, community prejudice, and the inadequacy of proposed remedies.

**Standard of Review**
The denial of a motion for a change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. A defendant’s right to a fair trial by an impartial jury is a fundamental requirement of due process. *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961). To warrant a change of venue based on pre-trial publicity, a defendant must demonstrate either presumed prejudice or actual prejudice. Presumed prejudice is a "rare" phenomenon reserved for "exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993); see also *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963). Actual prejudice is discerned by reviewing both the extent of the publicity and the responses of prospective jurors during voir dire. *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999).

**Analysis**

**I. Presumed Prejudice**
The defendants argue that the trial court erred by focusing on the quantity of publicity rather than its nature, specifically the racial bias evident in letters to the editor. They contend that in the insular community of American Samoa, the "nature" of the publicity—racially charged conclusory statements of guilt—creates a presumption of prejudice regardless of volume.

While the defense correctly identifies that the analysis of presumed prejudice must consider the character of the publicity, *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. at 728, they have not met the heavy burden required to establish such prejudice. The Supreme Court has limited presumed prejudice to extreme circumstances, such as in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), where a defendant’s coerced confession was televised multiple times to the local community, rendering the trial a "hollow formality." *Rideau*, 373 U.S. at 726. Similarly, in *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985), the court required "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage."

The trial court acknowledged that the letters to the editor contained "conclusory, matter-of-fact pronouncements of guilt" and were "embarrassingly slanted against the defendants on account of their race." However, the court found that these represented a "very limited section of society" rather than a pervasive community sentiment. This factual finding is not clearly erroneous on this record. The record reflects only six newspaper articles and a limited number of letters. The defendants’ reliance on the "multiplier effect" in a small community is noted, but the legal standard for presumed prejudice requires a showing that the community atmosphere is so pervasively prejudiced that the trial is inherently unfair. *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 351 (1966). Isolated instances of bias, however troubling, do not equate to the "oversaturation" required to presume prejudice. *Coleman*, 778 F.2d at 1490. The trial court’s determination that the letters do not represent the beliefs of the entire society is a reasonable application of the law to the facts.

**II. Actual Prejudice and Voir Dire**
The defendants contend that voir dire is an inadequate remedy in this context because the social pressure of a small community will prevent jurors from candidly admitting bias. They further argue that the trial court’s limitations on the voir dire questionnaire—restricting it to "yes or no" responses and prohibiting inquiries into anticipated instructions or verdicts—neuter its effectiveness.

It is well established that actual prejudice "is discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire." *Nevers*, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 725-28). The defense argues that *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794 (1975), which found voir dire sufficient to uncover bias, is distinguishable because it involved a larger community.

While the Court recognizes the theoretical validity of the defense's concern regarding social pressure in a small island community, this concern does not render the voir dire process *per se* ineffective or justify bypassing the standard procedure for assessing actual prejudice. The trial court acted within its discretion by allowing the defendants to submit a detailed voir dire questionnaire and by indicating a willingness to conduct individual voir dire if necessary. The restrictions placed on the questionnaire—prohibiting questions about substantive arguments or hypothetical verdicts—are standard safeguards designed to prevent juror conditioning and do not constitute an abuse of discretion. *Murphy*, 421 U.S. at 799-800. Until the jury pool is examined, the defendants cannot prove that an impartial jury cannot be seated. *Irvin*, 366 U.S. at 728.

**III. Remedy and Change of Venue**
Finally, the defendants argue that the trial court erred in concluding that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa. They assert that A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 provides statutory authority to move the trial to ensure fairness and distinguish *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725 (1st Cir. 1987), on the grounds that Puerto Rico is a larger jurisdiction.

The trial court correctly observed that American Samoa has a single jury roll comprising all registered voters on the island. Consequently, transferring the trial to a different physical location within the territory would not change the composition of the jury pool. The defendants’ interpretation of A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 is unpersuasive; the statute authorizes the court to adjourn to a different location for the "convenience of parties, witnesses or the court," not to escape prejudice. As the First Circuit noted in *Moreno*, a change of venue is often unsuitable in small, insular jurisdictions where the community is compact and the jury pool is uniform. *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731. The fact that American Samoa is smaller than Puerto Rico only strengthens the trial court’s conclusion that a physical move would be a "pipe dream" that fails to address the core issue of juror impartiality. See also *In re San Juan Star Co.*, 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1st Cir. 1981).

The defendants suggest that the trial court failed to explore meaningful alternatives, such as sequestration or extended voir dire, or transfer to a federal court in Hawaii. However, the trial court’s order explicitly provided for a comprehensive questionnaire and individual voir dire. Regarding transfer to Hawaii, the provided statute, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601, limits the court's power to adjourn to "any appropriate place in American Samoa." The court is without statutory authority to transfer the case to a different jurisdiction. The court is not required to grant a change of venue to explore these alternatives; rather, it must ensure a fair trial, which the current procedural safeguards are designed to protect.

**Conclusion**
The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for a change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate the "exceptional" circumstances necessary for presumed prejudice, and the inquiry into actual prejudice was properly deferred to voir dire. The logistical realities of the American Samoa judiciary render a change of venue an ineffective remedy, as it would not alter the composition of the jury pool, and the applicable statute does not authorize transfer outside the territory. The trial court’s order allowing for a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire provides sufficient protection for the defendants' right to a fair trial at this stage.

**Accordingly, the ruling is to affirm the denial of the motion for change of venue.**
