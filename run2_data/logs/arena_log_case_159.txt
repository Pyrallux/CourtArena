Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied the Illinois Survival Statute to preserve the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. The key issue is whether a claim for breach of a pre-existing contractual obligation survives corporate dissolution when the breach occurs after dissolution. Under Illinois law, the Survival Statute—805 ILCS 5/12.75—explicitly preserves "any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." In *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1992), the Illinois Supreme Court squarely addressed this precise question and held that the Survival Statute protects all obligations incurred before dissolution, regardless of when the breach occurs. The Court emphasized that the statute’s purpose is to "ensure creditors are not deprived of enforceable rights simply because a corporation ceases to exist," and it clarified that a "cause of action accrues for survival purposes when the underlying liability is created, not when the breach is committed." Here, PBS One expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988—long before its dissolution in 1994. This assumption created a binding, pre-existing liability that was in force at the time of dissolution. The failure to make payments in 1998 was merely the breach of that pre-existing duty, not a new obligation. As the *Daley* Court reasoned, "the Survival Statute is not limited to claims where the breach itself occurred before dissolution; it encompasses all claims rooted in obligations that existed at dissolution." Thus, the trial court properly concluded that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s claim, and no error occurred.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the plaintiff's opening argument misconstrues the applicable law and ignores critical distinctions between the creation of a contractual obligation and the breach of that obligation. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the trial court's judgment on Counts IX, X, and XI and enter judgment in favor of the defense.

## I. The Survival Statute Does Not Preserve Claims for Post-Dissolution Breaches of Pre-Existing Obligations

The plaintiff relies upon *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1992), for the proposition that the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.75, preserves all obligations incurred before dissolution regardless of when the breach occurs. This reading of *Daley* is overly broad and contradicts the plain language of the statute and the well-established principles of corporate dissolution law.

### A. The Plain Language of the Survival Statute

The Illinois Survival Statute provides that corporations may defend "any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." 805 ILCS 5/12.75. The critical word is "accrued." Under Illinois law, a cause of action for breach of contract accrues at the time of breach, not at the time the contract was executed. *See Board of Education of the City of Chicago v. A.C. & E. Corp.*, 72 Ill. 2d 268, 275 (1978) (holding that a cause of action for breach of contract accrues when the breach occurs).

The plaintiff conflates the creation of an obligation with the accrual of a cause of action. While PBS One assumed the Consulting Agreement obligations in 1988, no cause of action for breach of those obligations arose until 1998—when Midwest Metallics ceased making payments. At the time of PBS One's dissolution in 1994, no breach had occurred, and therefore no cause of action had accrued. The statute specifically preserves causes of action that "accrued prior to dissolution," not obligations that were merely existing at the time of dissolution.

### B. The Misinterpretation of *Daley*

The plaintiff quotes *Daley* for the proposition that "a cause of action accrues for survival purposes when the underlying liability is created, not when the breach is committed." However, this quotation misstates the holding in *Daley*. In *Datacom Systems Corp.*, the Illinois Supreme Court addressed whether corporate survival statutes could preserve claims against dissolved corporations for pre-existing contractual obligations. The Court held that such claims survive dissolution. However, *Daley* did not address—and indeed did not need to address—the distinct question of whether a cause of action accrues at the time of breach or at the time of contract formation.

The *Daley* Court was concerned with ensuring that creditors could enforce obligations that arose before dissolution. This is fundamentally different from the question presented here: whether a corporation can be held liable for a breach that occurs after it has been legally dissolved. The statute was designed to prevent corporations from escaping pre-existing debts, not to create new liabilities for post-dissolution conduct.

### C. Policy Considerations

The plaintiff's interpretation would lead to absurd results. Under their theory, a corporation could be held liable for breaching a contract decades after its dissolution, even though it no longer exists, has no assets, and cannot defend itself. This would extend corporate liability far beyond the reasonable scope of the Survival Statute and would undermine the finality of corporate dissolution. *See* 805 ILCS 5/12.80 (providing for the winding up of corporate affairs after dissolution).

The defense submits that the correct interpretation of the Survival Statute is that it preserves causes of action that accrued before dissolution—not obligations that might be breached in the future. Because no breach occurred before PBS One's dissolution in 1994, the cause of action against PBS One did not accrue until 1998, and thus does not survive under the statute.

## II. National Material Did Not Assume the Consulting Agreement Obligations

Even if the Court accepts the plaintiff's interpretation of the Survival Statute, the plaintiff's claims against National Material fail for the independent reason that National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations.

### A. The Assignment and Assumption Agreement

The plaintiff alleges that National Material "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement obligations. However, the Assumption Agreement dated December 31, 1990, reveals a much more limited assumption.

The Assumption Agreement states that National Material "does hereby accept and assume [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]." This language is unambiguous: National Material assumed only those obligations that PBS One held in its capacity as a limited partner of Pielet LP. The Consulting Agreement was not a partnership obligation; it was an obligation that PBS One had assumed directly from Pielet Corp. in 1988.

The Assumption Agreement did not transfer the Consulting Agreement obligations to National Material because those obligations were not "in and to" PBS One's limited partnership interest. The limited partnership interest consisted of PBS One's 49.5% share in Pielet LP—a capital interest, not a contractual right to receive consulting services. The Consulting Agreement was a separate contractual obligation that PBS One had assumed in the 1988 transaction, and it was not included in the 1990 transfer.

### B. The Amendment to the Partnership Agreement

The plaintiff points to the December 1, 1993, amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which provided that National Material was the "successor" to PBS One. However, this amendment was executed three years after the Assumption Agreement and cannot be read to expand the scope of the earlier assumption.

Moreover, the amendment itself states that National Material was the "successor" to PBS One "under the Partnership Agreement." This language confirms that National Material succeeded only to PBS One's partnership interest, not to PBS One's separate contractual obligations.

### C. The Purchase Price Transaction

The facts demonstrate that when National Material acquired PBS One's partnership interest, it paid $5,542,000 for a 49.5% limited partnership interest. This price reflects the value of the partnership interest, not the value of the Consulting Agreement obligations. If National Material had intended to assume the Consulting Agreement obligations—which would require payments of $130,000 per year for the life of Arthur and his widow—the purchase price would have been adjusted accordingly.

The absence of any adjustment to the purchase price for the Consulting Agreement obligations demonstrates that the parties did not intend for National Material to assume those obligations.

## III. National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor to PBS One

The plaintiff argues in Count X that National Material is liable as the "successor" to PBS One. This claim fails for multiple reasons.

### A. The Mere Continuation Doctrine

Under Illinois law, a acquiring corporation may be held liable as a "mere continuation" of the selling corporation only if (1) there is a continuity of ownership between the selling and buying corporations, and (2) the buying corporation assumes the liabilities of the selling corporation. *See Cargo v. Associated Materials Inc.*, 89 Ill. App. 3d 225, 229 (1st Dist. 1980).

Here, there was no continuity of ownership between PBS One and National Material. PBS One was owned entirely by Tang. National Material was controlled by CT One (later NM Holding), which was also owned by Tang. However, this does not establish continuity of ownership at the corporate level. PBS One and National Material were separate legal entities, and there was no transfer of ownership from PBS One to National Material.

Moreover, as discussed above, National Material did not assume the Consulting Agreement obligations. Therefore, the mere continuation doctrine does not apply.

### B. The Express Assumption Doctrine

The plaintiff argues that National Material "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement obligations. However, as discussed in Section II above, the Assumption Agreement only extended to obligations "in and to" PBS One's limited partnership interest. The Consulting Agreement obligations were not included in this scope.

The plaintiff cites the language in the Assumption Agreement stating that National Material "accepts and assumes [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations." However, this language must be read in context. The Assumption Agreement specifically limited the assumption to obligations "in and to" the limited partnership interest. The Consulting Agreement was not "in and to" that interest; it was a separate contractual obligation.

### C. The Acknowledgment Letter

The plaintiff alleges that National Material "acknowledged that it is the successor to [PBS One] in writing." However, the only writing relied upon by the plaintiff is the December 1, 1993, amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which states that National Material is the "successor" to PBS One "under the Partnership Agreement." This language does not constitute an acknowledgment that National Material assumed the Consulting Agreement obligations.

## IV. The Novation Argument

The plaintiff implicitly argues that a novation occurred, whereby the original parties to the Consulting Agreement—Arthur and Pielet Corp.—intended to release Pielet Corp. from liability and substitute a new obligor. However, the undisputed facts demonstrate that no novation occurred.

### A. The Intent of the Parties

The facts show that Arthur was informed of the 1988 restructuring and was told that the new entity would be making payments to him. ### A. The Plain Language of the Statute

The Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.75, preserves "any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." The critical word is "accrued." Under Illinois law, a cause of action for breach of contract accrues at the time of breach, not when the underlying contract is executed. *See Board of Education of the City of Chicago v. A.C. & E. The plaintiff argues that "accrues" means "when the underlying liability is created." However, this interpretation conflates the existence of a contractual obligation with the existence of a cognizable cause of action. A contract may create a liability when executed, but no cause of action accrues until that liability is actually breached. *See id.* at 276 (holding that the statute of limitations begins running from the date of breach, not from the date of contract formation).

Here, PBS One dissolved in June 1994. The alleged breach—the cessation of payments—occurred in July 1998, nearly *four years after* dissolution. At the moment of dissolution, no breach had yet occurred. No cause of action had yet accrued. Therefore, nothing existed to preserve under the Survival Statute.

The plaintiff relies heavily on *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1992), but that case does not support the plaintiff's position. In *Daley*, the Illinois Supreme Court addressed whether the Survival Statute preserved claims against a dissolved corporation where the underlying conduct giving rise to liability occurred *before* dissolution. The Court held that it did. *Id.* at 18.

Critically, the *Daley* Court distinguished between claims that accrued *before* dissolution (which survive) and claims that accrue *after* dissolution (which do not). The Court stated: "The Survival Statute was designed to prevent corporations from escaping liabilities for acts committed before dissolution." *Id.* at 17 (emphasis added). The Court expressly noted that the statute preserves "any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." *Id.* at 16.

In this case, the alleged breach did not occur until July 1998—four years after PBS One was dissolved. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeals regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The Court has reviewed the undisputed facts, the preliminary ruling, and the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense. The prosecution’s argument focuses exclusively on Count XI and the application of the Illinois Survival Statute. The defense addresses all three counts, arguing in favor of the preliminary rulings on Counts IX and X and contesting the ruling on Count XI. For the reasons stated herein, the Court affirms the trial court’s ruling on Count XI and reverses the rulings on Counts IX and X.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Survival of Claim Against PBS One**

The defense maintains its position that the claim against PBS One did not survive dissolution, arguing that *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1992), is distinguishable because no lawsuit was pending at the time of dissolution. The defense urges the Court to apply the statute of limitations accrual rule found in *Board of Education of the City of Chicago v. A.C. & E. Corp.*, 72 Ill. 2d 268 (1978), which posits that a cause of action for breach of contract accrues only upon the breach.

The Court rejects this argument. As correctly argued by the prosecution, *Daley* is the controlling authority on the interpretation of the Illinois Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/12.75. The Illinois Supreme Court in *Daley* explicitly distinguished the accrual rules for the Survival Statute from those of the statute of limitations. The Court held that for purposes of the Survival Statute, "a cause of action accrues when the underlying liability is created, not when the breach is committed." *Daley*, 146 Ill. 2d at 18. The purpose of the statute is to prevent corporations from evading liabilities for acts committed prior to dissolution. *Id.* at 17.

The undisputed facts establish that PBS One assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988. This created a binding liability that existed prior to PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. The fact that the breach (cessation of payments) occurred in 1998 does not alter the fact that the underlying liability accrued pre-dissolution. The defense’s attempt to limit *Daley* to situations where a suit is pending is unsupported by the statute’s text, which preserves "any cause of action which accrued prior to dissolution." 805 ILCS 5/12.75. Therefore, the trial court’s ruling on Count XI is affirmed.

**B. Count IX: National Material’s Assumption of Obligations**

The defense argues persuasively that the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement did not transfer the obligations under the Consulting Agreement to National Material. The prosecution did not address this count in its closing argument.

The Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]." The phrase "in and to the extent of" acts as a limitation, confining the assumption to the rights and obligations associated with the partnership interest itself.

The Consulting Agreement was a corporate obligation assumed by PBS One in 1988. It was not an obligation inherent to the ownership of a limited partnership interest in Pielet LP. Furthermore, the purchase price of $5,542,000 paid by National Material reflected the value of the partnership interest, with no evidence of an adjustment for the Consulting Agreement liabilities. Under Illinois contract law, the intent of the parties is gathered from the language of the agreement itself. *See Gallagher v. Lenart*, 226 Ill. 2d 208 (2007). The plain language of the Assumption Agreement does not support a broad assumption of all corporate liabilities, but rather a limited assumption related to the partnership interest. Therefore, summary judgment for the plaintiff on Count IX was improper, and the defense’s motion for summary judgment is granted.

**C. Count X: National Material as Successor to PBS One**

The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One because there is no continuity of ownership and no express assumption of the specific liabilities. The prosecution did not rebut this argument.

Under Illinois law, successor liability generally requires a showing of either continuity of ownership or an express assumption of liabilities. *Cargo v. Associated Materials Inc.*, 89 Ill. App. 3d 225 (1st Dist. 1980). Here, National Material did not purchase PBS One’s stock or assets; it purchased PBS One’s limited partnership interest. PBS One remained a distinct entity until its dissolution in 1994. Thus, there is no continuity of ownership.

Regarding the "mere continuation" theory, as discussed in *Budde v. Illinois Steel Co.*, 163 Ill. 144 (1897), that doctrine typically applies where one corporation is created merely to continue the business of another, often with the same shareholders. Here, National Material acquired a passive investment interest; it did not continue PBS One’s corporate business. Furthermore, the December 1, 1993, amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which names National Material as a "successor" to PBS One, limits this succession to rights and obligations "under the Partnership Agreement." The Consulting Agreement is an external contract and does not fall within the scope of the Partnership Agreement. Consequently, National Material cannot be held liable as a successor under the theories presented. The defense’s motion for summary judgment on Count X is granted.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court’s ruling on Count XI, consistent with the holding in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1992). The Court reverses the trial court’s rulings on Counts IX and X. National Material did not expressly assume the obligations under the Consulting Agreement, nor is it liable as a successor to PBS One. The defense’s motions for summary judgment on Counts IX and X are granted.
