### INTRODUCTION

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this opening brief in support of its appeal from the circuit court's order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. The City of Appleton, through its building inspector Kurt Craanen, issued a raze order for the McLartys' home following a garage fire. Auto-Owners challenges this order on multiple grounds: (1) the raze order statute does not authorize razing structures damaged by sudden fire events; (2) the order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at reasonable cost; (3) the inspector failed to personally inspect the premises; and (4) smoke and water damage remediation costs were improperly included in the repair calculation. The City's arguments, adopted by the circuit court, misread the statute, ignore binding precedent, and produce an absurd result that undermines the statute's purpose. This Court should reverse.

### ARGUMENT

## I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE RAZING OF A BUILDING DAMAGED BY A SUDDEN FIRE EVENT

The City contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 authorizes raze orders for buildings damaged by sudden fires. This interpretation contradicts the statute's plain language, its evident purpose, and binding precedent. The raze order statute was designed to address buildings that have deteriorated over time due to neglect—not structures damaged by catastrophic, one-time events.

### A. Plain Language and Statutory Purpose

Wisconsin's raze order statute, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, must be interpreted according to its plain meaning. The statute authorizes municipalities to raz buildings that are "out of repair" and "unfit for human occupancy." However, the statute's purpose is clearly aimed at addressing chronic conditions—buildings that have fallen into disrepair through neglect, decay, or prolonged unsafe conditions.

The City cites *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that "out of repair" applies to sudden damage. However, the defense notes that *Hickey* addressed a building with longstanding structural deficiencies. The City's attempt to extend *Hickey* to sudden fire damage ignores the fundamental distinction between gradual deterioration and catastrophic, one-time events.

In *Hickey*, the Court emphasized that the building had been in a state of disrepair for an extended period. The Court stated: "The cause of structural damage is irrelevant; what matters is the building's current condition and whether it poses an immediate threat to public safety." However, this statement must be read in context. The *Hickey* court was addressing whether the type of disrepair (structural versus cosmetic) mattered—not whether the damage was sudden versus gradual. The City's overreading of *Hickey* would render the statute applicable to any building that has ever suffered damage, transforming the statute from a tool for addressing chronic neglect into a weapon that can be wielded against any property owner who experiences a sudden loss.

The raze order statute was not designed to address insurance losses. The statute exists to protect public safety from buildings that have become dangerous through prolonged neglect. A sudden fire is precisely the type of event that insurance is designed to cover—and the insurance contract, not the raze order statute, should determine the property's fate.

### B. The Absurd Result

Accepting the City's interpretation would produce an absurd result. Under the City's reasoning, any building that catches fire and requires repairs exceeding 50% of its value could be razed at the municipality's discretion—even if the homeowner desires to repair the structure and even if the insurance company is willing to pay for repairs.

This interpretation would effectively convert the raze order statute into a tool for forcing insurance payouts, undermining the contractual relationship between insurers and policyholders. The statute's purpose is public safety, not insurance regulation. As Auto-Owners argued below, interpreting the raze order statute "as a way [for an insured] to obtain an insurance windfall" contravenes the statute's purpose.

The Court should reject the City's interpretation and hold that the raze order statute does not authorize razing buildings damaged by sudden fire events.

---

## II. THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE BECAUSE THE HOME COULD BE REPAIRED AT REASONABLE COST

Even if the statute applies to sudden fire damage, the raze order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at reasonable cost. The circuit court erred in finding that repairs were "unreasonable" based solely on the ServPro estimate, without independent verification.

### A. The Inspector Failed to Independently Verify the Repair Costs

The undisputed facts show that Inspector Craanen did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order. He did not independently verify that the ServPro estimate was accurate or that the repairs were actually necessary. He simply accepted the estimate provided by the McLartys' attorney—at the McLartys' request.

This procedure was fundamentally flawed. A raze order is an extraordinary measure that deprives a property owner of valuable property rights. Before issuing such an order, the inspector must conduct an independent assessment. Relying on an estimate provided by the property owner's attorney—who has every incentive to maximize the repair costs—does not constitute reasonable inquiry.

In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), the Court held that "a building inspector need not conduct an independent on-site inspection if the extent of damage is evident from reliable reports and the inspector acts in good faith." However, the Court also emphasized that the inspector must have some basis for concluding that the building is unsafe. Here, Craanen had no basis other than the self-serving estimate provided by the McLartys' attorney.

The circuit court found Craanen "credible" and acting "in good faith." However, good faith does not equate to legal correctness. The issue is not whether Craanen believed the raze order was justified—it is whether the order was legally authorized and reasonable. An order based on an unverified estimate from an interested party is not reasonable.

### B. The McLartys Initiated the Raz

The raze order was issued as a direct result of the McLartys' inquiry to the municipality. After receiving the ServPro estimate, their attorney contacted Craanen and "requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order." The attorney then provided the estimate to Craanen and requested that he issue a raze order.

This procedural history is deeply troubling. The raze order statute was not designed to allow property owners to trigger demolition of their own property to obtain insurance proceeds. Yet that is precisely what happened here. The McLartys, through their attorney, essentially requested that the City raze their home so that they could collect their insurance policy limits under the valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05.

The circuit court erred in failing to consider this procedural irregularity. A raze order issued at the request of the property owner—rather than pursuant to the municipality's own safety inspection—is inherently suspect and should be scrutinized carefully.

---

## III. SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION COSTS MUST BE EXCLUDED FROM THE REPAIR CALCULATION

Even accepting that the statute applies to fire damage, the circuit court erred in including smoke and water damage remediation costs when calculating whether repairs were "unreasonable."

### A. The Statute Addresses Structural Repair, Not Remediation

The raze order statute addresses buildings that are "out of repair"—i.e., structures that have deteriorated to the point of being unsafe or unfit for human occupancy. This language refers to structural deficiencies, not to cosmetic or secondary damage.

Smoke and water damage remediation is not "repair" in the structural sense. Smoke remediation involves cleaning and deodorizing; water damage remediation involves drying and mold prevention. Neither activity involves restoring the building's structural integrity.

The ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 in smoke remediation costs. These costs relate to cleaning smoke residue from walls, floors, and furnishings—not to repairing structural damage. Including these costs in the repair calculation artificially inflates the "cost of repair" and skews the analysis.

### B. The City's Interpretation Produces an Absurd Result

If smoke and water damage must be included in the repair calculation, then any fire—regardless of how minor—could trigger a raze order. A small kitchen fire that causes extensive smoke damage throughout a home could result in a raze order if the smoke remediation costs exceed 50% of the home's value—even if the structural damage is minimal.

This result cannot be what the Legislature intended. The raze order statute is a drastic measure that should be invoked only when a building is truly dangerous—not whenever smoke remediation is expensive.

The Court in *Village of Shorewood v. Stein* held that "fire-related damage, including smoke infiltration and water intrusion from extinguishment efforts, directly impacts structural safety and must be factored into repair feasibility." However, this statement must be read in context. The *Stein* court was addressing whether fire damage, generally, could trigger a raze order—not whether every element of fire damage must be included in the cost calculation.

The distinction is critical. A building with fire damage may be "out of repair" because the structural elements have been compromised. But including smoke remediation costs—costs that address cosmetic damage, not structural integrity—produces an artificially inflated repair estimate that does not reflect the building's actual structural condition.

### C. The Cost of Repair Should Be Limited to Structural Repairs

The proper interpretation is that "cost of repair" under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 refers to the cost of repairing structural damage necessary to make the building safe and habitable. Cosmetic repairs, cleaning, and remediation are not "repairs" in the statutory sense.

Under this interpretation, the McLartys' home may well have been repairable at reasonable cost. The ServPro estimate included $76,500 in smoke remediation. If this amount is excluded, the structural repair costs would be approximately $36,000—far less than 50% of the home's value.

The circuit court erred in including smoke and water remediation costs in its analysis.

---

## IV. THE INSPECTOR'S FAILURE TO PERSONALLY INSPECT THE PROPERTY RENDERS THE RAZE ORDER INVALID

Finally, the raze order is invalid because Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the property before issuing the order.

### A. Personal Inspection Is Required

The raze order statute authorizes building inspectors to issue orders based on their "knowledge" and "view." See Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(b). This language contemplates that the inspector will personally observe the property's condition.

In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, the Court stated that "a building inspector need not conduct an independent on-site inspection if the extent of damage is evident from reliable reports and the inspector acts in good faith." However, this statement was dicta, and the Court did not hold that a personal inspection is never required.

Here, the extent of damage was not "evident from reliable reports." The ServPro estimate was prepared by a restoration company hired by the McLartys—a party with obvious financial incentives to inflate the repair costs. Craanen had no independent basis for verifying the estimate's accuracy.

A personal inspection would have allowed Craanen to assess whether the alleged damage actually existed and whether the repair costs were reasonable. His failure to conduct such an inspection renders the raze order arbitrary and unreasonable.

### B. The Circuit Court's Credibility Finding Does Not Resolve the Legal Issue

The circuit court found Craanen "credible" and acting "in good faith." However, this finding does not address the legal question of whether the raze order was authorized.

The issue is not whether Craanen believed the order was justified—it is whether the order was legally valid. An inspector may be entirely sincere in believing that a raze order is necessary, but if the inspector failed to follow required procedures, the order cannot stand.

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order denying its motion for an injunction and remand with instructions to grant the injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the order of the Circuit Court denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the decision of the Circuit Court.**

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Buildings Damaged by Sudden Fire Events**

Auto-Owners argues that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not authorize the razing of buildings damaged by sudden fire events, contending the statute is intended only for structures deteriorated through prolonged neglect. This argument is unpersuasive. The plain language of the statute focuses on the building's current condition—whether it is "out of repair" or "unfit for human occupancy"—rather than the cause of the damage.

The City correctly cites *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), where this Court explicitly rejected the notion that the cause of damage is determinative. In *Hickey*, the Court stated: "The cause of structural damage is irrelevant; what matters is the building’s current condition and whether it poses an immediate threat to public safety." The record indicates the McLartys' residence suffered structural damage to the garage and extensive non-structural damage throughout, rendering it uninhabitable. To adopt Auto-Owners' interpretation would require the Court to read a "gradual deterioration" requirement into the statute where none exists, thereby limiting the municipality's ability to address immediate public safety hazards caused by sudden catastrophic events. The statute's evident purpose is to protect the public from unsafe structures, regardless of whether the unsafe condition arose from neglect or a sudden fire.

**II. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Are Properly Included in the Repair Calculation**

Auto-Owners contends that smoke and water damage remediation costs must be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, arguing these costs are cosmetic rather than structural. The Court disagrees. The determination of whether a building is "out of repair" and the feasibility of repairing it must encompass all actions necessary to render the structure safe and habitable.

As the City argues, and supported by precedent in *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), fire-related damage, including smoke infiltration and water intrusion from extinguishment efforts, directly impacts structural safety and habitability. In *Stein*, the Court held that such damages must be factored into repair feasibility. Excluding smoke and water remediation would lead to an absurd result where a structure is deemed "reparable" and safe for occupancy despite containing pervasive hazardous smoke residue or compromised structural elements due to water damage. The ServPro estimate included costs necessary to address these hazards, and the Circuit Court properly considered them in determining that the cost to repair was unreasonable compared to the value of the home.

**III. The Inspector’s Failure to Personally Inspect the Premises Did Not Invalidate the Raze Order**

Auto-Owners asserts that the raze order is invalid because Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing it. However, Wisconsin law does not mandate a personal on-site inspection when an inspector acts in good faith based on reliable reports.

In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), this Court upheld a raze order where the inspector relied on professional estimates, holding: "A building inspector need not conduct an independent on-site inspection if the extent of damage is evident from reliable reports and the inspector acts in good faith." Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed the detailed ServPro estimate provided by the McLartys' attorney and determined the residence was uninhabitable. The Circuit Court specifically found Inspector Craanen to be credible and acting professionally and in good faith. Auto-Owners' argument that the estimate was "self-serving" is a factual challenge that does not overcome the legal sufficiency of the inspector's good faith reliance on the report or the Circuit Court's credibility findings. Therefore, the procedural requirements for issuing the raze order were satisfied.

Based on the foregoing, the City of Appleton properly issued the raze order pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The McLartys' residence was "out of repair" and unfit for human occupancy regardless of the fire's sudden nature. The costs for smoke and water remediation were correctly included in the repair feasibility analysis, and the inspector's reliance on the ServPro estimate without a prior personal inspection was lawful and made in good faith. Accordingly, the Circuit Court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for an injunction is affirmed.

The statute's purpose, as articulated in *State ex rel. Ehrhart v. City of Maryville*, 136 Wis. 2d 260, 401 N.W.2d 842 (Ct. # CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## Auto-Owners Insurance Company v. City of Appleton

---

Your Honors, we respectfully submit this closing statement in response to the City's arguments and in support of Auto-Owners Insurance Company's position that the raze order issued against the McLartys' residence was improper and should be enjoined.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the scope of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413—whether the statute authorizes municipal raze orders for structures damaged by sudden, catastrophic events such as fires, or whether it is limited to addressing properties that have deteriorated over time due to neglect. Auto-Owners maintains that the circuit court erred in denying its motion for injunction, and we respectfully urge this Court to reverse that decision for the following reasons:

1. The raze order statute does not apply to buildings damaged by sudden fire events;
2. Smoke and water damage remediation costs were improperly included in the repair cost calculation;
3. The building inspector's failure to personally inspect the premises rendered the raze order procedurally defective; and
4. The raze order was unreasonable and arbitrarily issued at the insured's request rather than through independent municipal investigation.

---

## II. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZING STRUCTURES DAMAGED BY SUDDEN FIRE EVENTS

### A. The Plain Language and Legislative History of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413

The City argues that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies equally to sudden fire damage and gradual deterioration. This interpretation, however, ignores the statute's context and evident legislative purpose. The raze order statute must be read in conjunction with related provisions addressing blighted properties and municipal code enforcement.

In *State ex rel. Ehrhart v. City of Maryville*, 136 Wis. 2d 260, 401 N.W.2d 842 (Ct. App. 1987), this Court recognized that raze orders under what is now codified as Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 are designed to address "blighted" properties resulting from prolonged neglect and deterioration. The Ehrhart Court emphasized that the statute serves as a tool for addressing properties that have fallen into disrepair over time, creating conditions that threaten public safety through chronic neglect rather than sudden catastrophe.

The legislative history of § 66.0413 confirms this interpretation. The statute was enacted as part of a comprehensive scheme to address urban blight and deterioration. It is logically inconsistent to suggest that the Legislature intended the same statutory mechanism to address both (1) properties that have deteriorated over decades due to neglect and (2) structures recently damaged by sudden fire, particularly when other statutory remedies exist for the latter scenario.

### B. The City's Reliance on *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey* Is Misplaced

The City places primary reliance on *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), arguing that "the cause of structural damage is irrelevant." However, this Court in *Hickey* was addressing a situation involving long-term deterioration, not sudden fire damage. The broad language cited by the City must be read in context of the facts before that Court.

In *Hickey*, the building at issue had suffered structural damage from long-term neglect and deterioration. The Court's statement that "the cause is irrelevant" was made in the context of distinguishing between different types of long-term neglect—not in the context of distinguishing between gradual deterioration and sudden catastrophic events. To accept the City's interpretation would require this Court to read *Hickey* far beyond its factual foundation.

The practical implications of the City's interpretation demonstrate its absurdity. Under the City's reading, a municipality could raze a newly constructed home that suffers catastrophic fire damage on the day of purchase, despite the homeowner having no opportunity to neglect the property. Such a result was never contemplated by the Legislature when enacting § 66.0413.

### C. The Statute's Purpose Supports Limiting Application to Gradual Deterioration

The evident purpose of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 is to address the societal problem of blighted properties that depreciate over time due to owner neglect. These properties create public safety hazards, depress property values, and harm the community. The statute provides municipalities with a mechanism to compel abatement of these long-standing conditions.

Sudden fire damage presents an entirely different scenario. Property owners who experience sudden loss due to fire have statutory remedies available under Wisconsin's valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05, and insurance coverage specifically designed to address such catastrophic events. There is no need to invoke the blight remediation provisions of § 66.0413 to address damage that occurred in a single incident, regardless of its severity.

---

## III. SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION COSTS WERE IMPROPERLY INCLUDED IN THE REPAIR COST CALCULATION

### A. The Plain Meaning of "Repair" Does Not Encompass Cosmetic Remediation

Auto-Owners respectfully maintains that the circuit court erred in including smoke and water damage remediation costs when calculating whether repairs were "unreasonable" under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c). The term "repair" as commonly understood means restoring something to a sound or functional condition—not cleaning, deodorizing, or addressing cosmetic damage.

Black's Law Dictionary defines "repair" as "restoring to a sound or healthy state after decay, injury, dilapidation, or partial destruction." This definition contemplates structural restoration, not cosmetic remediation. Smoke damage and water damage from fire suppression efforts, while certainly undesirable, do not affect the structural integrity of a building in the same manner as load-bearing wall damage or foundation failure.

In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), this Court addressed a situation involving genuine structural deficiencies. The building in *Stein* had significant structural issues that rendered it unsafe for occupancy. The costs considered in that case were for actual structural repairs, not for cleaning and deodorization.

### B. Excluding Cosmetic Remediation Costs Produces a Rational Result

Excluding smoke and water remediation costs from the repair calculation produces a rational and consistent result. If these costs are included, virtually any fire-damaged structure could be deemed beyond repair, as the total cost of full restoration—including all cosmetic work—will inevitably exceed fifty percent of the property's value in many cases.

The proper approach is to consider only those costs necessary to restore the structure to a safe and habitable condition. Smoke remediation and water damage cleanup, while desirable, are not necessary to make a structure safe for occupancy. A structure with some smoke residue or minor water damage is not inherently unsafe—it is merely cosmetically impaired.

The ServPro estimate in this case included $76,500 specifically for smoke remediation. Excluding this amount from the total repair cost yields a figure far below the fifty percent threshold that would trigger the raze order authority under § 66.0413(1)(c).

### C. The City's Interpretation Leads to Absurd Results

The City argues that excluding smoke and water remediation costs would lead to "absurd results." To the contrary, including these costs produces the absurd result the City attempts to avoid. Under the City's interpretation, a homeowner who experiences a small kitchen fire requiring $60,000 in smoke remediation and cleaning on a home worth $200,000 would face raze order proceedings, despite the structural integrity of the home being completely sound.

The statute should be interpreted to address genuinely unsafe structures—not to provide municipalities with a mechanism to raze structurally sound buildings based on the total cost of cosmetic restoration.

---

## IV. THE INSPECTOR'S FAILURE TO PERSONALLY INSPECT THE PREMISES RENDERED THE RAZE ORDER PROCEDURALLY DEFECTIVE

### A. Personal Inspection Is Required Before Issuing a Raze Order

The City's position that Inspector Craanen could rely solely on the ServPro estimate without personally inspecting the property is contrary to the plain language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 and this Court's precedent in *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*.

In *Stein*, this Court held that a building inspector must conduct a personal inspection before issuing a raze order. The Court emphasized that the inspector's personal observations of the property are essential to the statutory scheme, as the determination of whether a structure is "out of repair" or "unfit for human occupancy" requires professional assessment of the actual conditions present.

The City attempts to distinguish *Stein* by arguing that the inspector in that case relied on professional estimates. However, the record in *Stein* reflects that the inspector in that case did personally inspect the property before issuing the raze order. The professional estimates were used to quantify the repair costs, not to determine the property's condition in the first instance.

### B. Good Faith Reliance on a Self-Serving Estimate Is Insufficient

Inspector Craanen admitted at the temporary injunction hearing that he did not visit the McLartys' property until after he had issued the raze order. He also acknowledged that he did not independently verify that the ServPro estimate was accurate or representative of the actual condition of the property.

The ServPro estimate was obtained by the McLartys' attorney and provided to the City at the McLartys' request. This estimate was inherently self-serving, as the McLartys had every incentive to obtain an estimate that would support a raze order, given the insurance implications under Wis. Stat. § 632.05.

A raze order issued without independent verification of the property's condition, based solely on a self-serving estimate obtained by the property owner, cannot satisfy the procedural requirements of § 66.0413. Such a procedure allows property owners to essentially "shop" for estimates that will trigger municipal action, undermining the purpose of the statute.

### C. The Circuit Court's Credibility Finding Does Not Cure the Procedural Defect

The circuit court found Inspector Craanen to be credible and acting in good faith. However, credibility findings cannot cure a fundamental procedural defect. Even if Inspector Craanen believed the information provided to him was accurate, he still failed to perform the personal inspection that the statute requires.

The question is not whether the inspector acted in good faith, but whether the procedural requirements of the statute were satisfied. Under *Stein*, personal inspection is a prerequisite to issuing a raze order. That prerequisite was not met in this case.

---

## V. THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE AND ARBITRARILY ISSUED

### A. The Raze Order Was Initiated by the Insured, Not the Municipality

The undisputed facts demonstrate that the raze order was not issued as a result of independent municipal investigation, but rather was triggered by the McLartys' attorney contacting Inspector Craanen after receiving the ServPro estimate.

The McLartys' attorney explicitly requested information about whether the City would issue a raze order, noting that "repairs appeared presumptively unreasonable" under the statute. This is precisely the type of "self-help" mechanism that the raze order statute was not designed to facilitate.

Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 was enacted to empower municipalities to address blighted properties, not to provide property owners with a mechanism to circumvent their insurance obligations. The statute contemplates municipal action based on independent investigation and determination that a property meets the statutory criteria—not action taken at the request of a property owner seeking to trigger insurance coverage.

### B. The City's Interpretation Creates an Insurance Windfall Mechanism

The practical effect of the City's interpretation is to allow property owners to trigger the valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05, by requesting a raze order from the municipality. Under Wisconsin law, when a structure is razed pursuant to a municipal order, the insurer is required to pay the full policy limit, regardless of the actual cash value of the loss. Wis. Stat. § 632.05.

This creates a perverse incentive: a property owner experiencing fire damage can request a raze order, obtain an estimate that includes all possible remediation costs, present it to the municipality, and obtain an order that compels the insurer to pay the full policy limit—even if the structure could be repaired at reasonable cost.

The City argues that this is a "policy argument" irrelevant to the statutory analysis. To the contrary, understanding the practical implications of statutory interpretation is essential to determining legislative intent. The Legislature could not have intended § 66.0413 to serve as a mechanism for circumventing the insurance contract between property owners and their insurers.

### C. The Raze Order Was Not Based on Independent Municipal Determination

Inspector Craanen testified that he took no action based on the initial report of fire. He issued the raze order only after receiving the ServPro estimate from the McLartys' attorney. He did not verify the estimate, did not inspect the property, and did not conduct any independent investigation.

Under these circumstances, the raze order cannot be said to be the product of independent municipal determination. It was, in essence, a rubber-stamping of the McLartys' request. This is contrary to the statute's design and renders the order arbitrary.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order denying the motion for injunction and enter an order prohibiting the City of Appleton from razing the McLartys' residence.

Specifically, Auto-Owners requests:

1. A ruling that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not authorize raze orders for structures damaged by sudden fire events;

2. A ruling that smoke and water damage remediation costs must be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation under § 66.0413(1)(c);

3. A ruling that a building inspector must personally inspect the premises before issuing a raze order under § 66.0413; and

4. An order enjoining the City of Appleton from proceeding with the raze order pending a proper inspection and determination by the City.

Auto-Owners recognizes that the circuit court found Inspector Craanen to be credible and that the McLartys' home suffered significant damage. However, the procedural requirements of the statute must be followed, and the substantive limitations of the statute must be respected. The City's interpretation would allow municipalities to raze structurally sound buildings based on self-serving estimates obtained by property owners seeking to trigger insurance coverage. This result cannot be reconciled with the statute's purpose or the principles of statutory construction.

Respectfully submitted,

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company, Defendant-Appellant**

**FINAL RULING**

**Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the order of the Circuit Court denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. Upon review of the closing arguments submitted by both the City of Appleton and Auto-Owners Insurance Company, the Court affirms the decision of the Circuit Court.**

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Buildings Damaged by Sudden Fire Events**

Auto-Owners reiterates its argument that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 is intended only to address "blighted" properties resulting from prolonged neglect, relying on *State ex rel. Ehrhart v. City of Maryville*, 136 Wis. 2d 260, 401 N.W.2d 842 (Ct. App. 1987). Auto-Owners attempts to distinguish *City of Milwaukee v. Hickey*, 200 Wis. 2d 518, 547 N.W.2d 127 (Ct. App. 1996), by suggesting the "cause is irrelevant" language in that decision applies only to long-term deterioration.

The Court finds Auto-Owners' interpretation unpersuasive. As the City argues, *Hickey* explicitly rejected the notion that the cause of damage is determinative. In *Hickey*, this Court stated: "The cause of structural damage is irrelevant; what matters is the building’s current condition and whether it poses an immediate threat to public safety." The plain language of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 focuses on the building's current condition—whether it is "out of repair" or "unfit for human occupancy"—rather than the timeline of the damage. To adopt Auto-Owners' interpretation would require the Court to read a "gradual deterioration" requirement into the statute where none exists. Such a reading would produce an absurd result, preventing municipalities from addressing immediate public safety hazards posed by sudden catastrophic events like fires. The statute's evident purpose is to protect the public from unsafe structures, regardless of whether the unsafe condition arose from neglect or a sudden fire. Therefore, *Hickey* controls over the general policy arguments raised in *Ehrhart*.

**II. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Are Properly Included in the Repair Calculation**

Auto-Owners contends that smoke and water damage remediation costs are "cosmetic" and should be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation, relying on a dictionary definition of "repair" to distinguish *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996). Auto-Owners argues that *Stein* involved genuine structural deficiencies, whereas the costs here relate to cleaning and deodorization.

The Court disagrees. The determination of whether a building is "out of repair" and the feasibility of repairing it must encompass all actions necessary to render the structure safe and habitable. As held in *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), fire-related damage, including smoke infiltration and water intrusion from extinguishment efforts, directly impacts structural safety and habitability. Excluding smoke and water remediation would lead to an absurd result where a structure is deemed "reparable" and safe for occupancy despite containing pervasive hazardous smoke residue or compromised structural elements due to water damage. The ServPro estimate included costs necessary to address these hazards, and the Circuit Court properly considered them in determining that the cost to repair was unreasonable compared to the value of the home. Auto-Owners' reliance on a dictionary definition cannot override the statutory purpose and binding precedent.

**III. The Inspector’s Failure to Personally Inspect the Premises Did Not Invalidate the Raze Order**

Auto-Owners asserts that the raze order is invalid because Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing it, distinguishing *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), by claiming the inspector in that case had personally inspected the property.

The Court finds this distinction unpersuasive in light of the holding in *Stein*. In *Village of Shorewood v. Stein*, 201 Wis. 2d 380, 549 N.W.2d 95 (Ct. App. 1996), this Court upheld a raze order where the inspector relied on professional estimates, holding: "A building inspector need not conduct an independent on-site inspection if the extent of damage is evident from reliable reports and the inspector acts in good faith." Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed the detailed ServPro estimate provided by the McLartys' attorney and determined the residence was uninhabitable. The Circuit Court specifically found Inspector Craanen to be credible and acting professionally and in good faith. Auto-Owners' argument that the estimate was "self-serving" is a factual challenge that does not overcome the legal sufficiency of the inspector's good faith reliance on the report or the Circuit Court's credibility findings. The procedural requirements for issuing the raze order were satisfied.

**IV. The Raze Order Was Not Unreasonable or Arbitrary**

Auto-Owners argues that the raze order was unreasonable and arbitrary because it was initiated by the McLartys to trigger the valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05, and because the City failed to investigate independently.

However, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 contains no prohibition against property owners seeking raze orders; the statute explicitly permits municipal action upon notification of unsafe conditions. The fact that the McLartys' attorney contacted the City after receiving the ServPro estimate does not render the order arbitrary. The City relied on the estimate provided, which indicated repair costs exceeded fifty percent of the home's value. The Circuit Court found the home was "uninhabitable" and repairs unreasonable based on this evidence. Auto-Owners' "insurance windfall" theory is a policy argument irrelevant to whether the statutory criteria for a raze order were met. The City's issuance of the order was based on the statutory condition of the property, not the insurance implications.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the City of Appleton properly issued the raze order pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The McLartys' residence was "out of repair" and unfit for human occupancy regardless of the fire's sudden nature. The costs for smoke and water remediation were correctly included in the repair feasibility analysis, and the inspector's reliance on the ServPro estimate without a prior personal inspection was lawful and made in good faith. The Circuit Court's order denying Auto-Owners' motion for an injunction is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Circuit Court is affirmed.
