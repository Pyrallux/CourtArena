**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the plaintiff, Rita A. Wallace, and the defendants, Hector L. Haddock and Subaru Leasing Corporation. The issue on appeal is whether the trial court abused its discretion and violated statutory mandates by ordering an additur and immediately entering judgment without providing the defendants the opportunity to accept or reject the increase in damages.

Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the court finds that the trial court committed reversible error. The judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**ANALYSIS**

The central issue in this appeal is procedural. The defendants argue that the trial court violated Connecticut General Statutes §52-228b by failing to offer them the statutory option to accept or reject the additur before entering judgment. The plaintiff, while acknowledging the procedural error in her capacity as appellee, correctly argues that this error mandates reversal.

**I. The Statutory Mandate of §52-228b**

Connecticut General Statutes §52-228b governs the procedure for additur and remittitur. The statute explicitly states: "In any case where the court has ordered an additur or remittitur, the party against whom the order is made may, within the time prescribed for the filing of a motion for new trial, accept or reject the order. If the order is accepted, judgment shall be entered accordingly. If the order is rejected, the court shall grant a new trial on the issue of damages only."

The language of the statute is mandatory. It requires the trial court to stay the entry of judgment until the party against whom the additur is ordered has had a reasonable opportunity to elect between accepting the court's increased award or proceeding to a new trial on damages. In this case, the trial court ordered an additur of $15,000 on July 12, 2002, and "immediately rendered judgment for the plaintiff, including the additur." This action effectively deprived the defendants of the election rights granted by the legislature.

**II. Binding Precedent Requires Reversal**

The Connecticut Supreme Court has consistently held that the failure to provide the statutory option is reversible error.

In *Bova v. Bova*, 199 Conn. 23, 30–31 (1986), the court held: "The trial court’s failure to give the defendant the opportunity to accept or reject the additur was reversible error. The statutory requirement that the party be given the option of accepting or rejecting the additur is mandatory." This case is directly on point, as the trial court in *Bova* entered judgment without providing the required choice, just as the court did here.

Similarly, in *Cianchette v. Cianchette*, 195 Conn. 458, 463 (1985), the court reiterated: "The trial court must offer the party the opportunity to accept or reject the additur before entering judgment. Failure to do so requires reversal." The court further emphasized that the statute creates a mandatory procedural safeguard that cannot be bypassed by the trial court.

The precedent in *Gaudet v. Sweeney*, 157 Conn. 591, 593 (1969), supports this conclusion, stating: "The trial court erred in entering judgment without first giving the defendant the option of accepting the additur or rejecting it and proceeding to a new trial."

The plaintiff’s argument is entirely consistent with these authorities. The trial court’s failure to offer the defendants the choice between accepting the $15,000 additur or undergoing a new trial on damages constituted a clear violation of §52-228b and binding precedent.

**III. The Defense Arguments Cannot Overcome the Procedural Violation**

The defendants do not contest that the trial court failed to follow the procedure outlined in §52-228b. Instead, they argue that this Court should affirm the judgment on the merits because the additur was substantively reasonable and the procedural error was harmless.

The defendants rely on *Malley v. Morgenthau*, 175 Conn. 256, 260 (1978), for the proposition that additur is appropriate where the jury's award fails to do substantial justice. While the defendants assert that the $1,715 noneconomic award was inadequate, the substantive propriety of the additur amount is secondary to the mandatory procedural requirements that were ignored.

The defense’s request for this Court to "affirm the additur amount... notwithstanding the procedural deficiency" is untenable in light of the mandatory language of §52-228b and the rulings in *Bova*, *Cianchette*, and *Gaudet*. The statute grants the *party* the right to choose between a specific judgment and a new trial. By entering judgment immediately, the trial court extinguished the defendants' right to make that choice. The appellate court cannot cure this deprivation by simply affirming the judgment; doing so would effectively rewrite the statute to make the acceptance/rejection provision discretionary rather than mandatory.

Furthermore, the defendants’ claim that they were not prejudiced because they "would have rejected the additur" actually underscores the harm caused by the error. If they would have rejected it, they are entitled to a new trial on damages, a remedy they were denied by the trial court’s premature entry of judgment. The defense’s alternative request—that the case be remanded for the trial court to offer the choice—is the only legally viable remedy.

**CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion and violated Connecticut General Statutes §52-228b by entering judgment immediately after ordering an additur. The defendants were deprived of their statutory right to accept or reject the additur. Under the authority of *Bova v. Bova*, 199 Conn. 23, 30–31 (1986), *Cianchette v. Cianchette*, 195 Conn. 458, 463 (1985), and *Gaudet v. Sweeney*, 157 Conn. 591, 593 (1969), this error requires reversal.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed. The case is remanded to the trial court with directions to offer the defendants the option to accept the additur of $15,000 or, in the alternative, to reject the additur and receive a new trial on the issue of damages only.

**ANALYSIS**

Both parties now concur that the trial court committed reversible procedural error by entering judgment immediately upon ordering an additur, without providing the defendants the opportunity to accept or reject the increase in damages. The plaintiff correctly argues that this failure violates the mandatory procedures set forth in General Statutes § 52-228b. The defense explicitly concedes this point, acknowledging that the failure to offer the statutory election constituted reversible error.

As established in the preliminary analysis, the controlling precedent requires reversal in this instance. In *Bova v. Bova*, 199 Conn. 23, 30–31 (1986), the court held that "the trial court’s failure to give the defendant the opportunity to accept or reject the additur was reversible error. The statutory requirement that the party be given the option of accepting or rejecting the additur is mandatory." This principle is echoed in *Cianchette v. Cianchette*, 195 Conn. 458, 463 (1985), which states that "the trial court must offer the party the opportunity to accept or reject the additur before entering judgment. Failure to do so requires reversal." Furthermore, *Gaudet v. Sweeney*, 157 Conn. 591, 593 (1969), instructs that such procedural failure mandates reversal "irrespective of the substantive merits of the additur." Consequently, the trial court's action in immediately rendering judgment was erroneous and requires reversal.

**II. The Scope of Remand and Reservation of Rights**

The primary dispute on remand concerns the scope of the new trial should the defendants reject the additur, and the preservation of the defendants' right to challenge the substantive adequacy of the verdict.

The defense requests that the remand include clear instructions that the trial court must offer the statutory election: accept the $15,000 additur or reject it and receive a new trial on the issue of damages only. The court finds this request consistent with the statute and grants it. The defense further reserves the right to argue that the original jury verdict was supported by sufficient evidence and that the plaintiff failed to meet her burden in proving the damages were inadequate as a matter of law.

The defense contends that the evidence regarding the plaintiff's injuries may not meet this burden. The court acknowledges this reservation. On remand, if the defendants reject the additur, the trial court must address the substantive adequacy of the verdict under the standard set forth in *Malley*.

Regarding the defense's request to potentially expand the scope of a new trial to include liability, the court notes that the standard procedure under § 52-228b, following an additur, typically limits a new trial to the issue of damages. However, the defense has reserved the right to argue for a broader scope. The trial court on remand may consider the defense's arguments regarding the scope of a new trial in the first instance, should the defendants elect to reject the additur.

The trial court abused its discretion and violated Connecticut General Statutes § 52-228b by entering judgment immediately after ordering an additur. Under the authority of *Bova v. Bova*, 199 Conn. 23, 30–31 (1986), *Cianchette v. Cianchette*, 195 Conn. 458, 463 (1985), and *Gaudet v. Sweeney*, 157 Conn. 591, 593 (1969), the judgment of the trial court is reversed.
